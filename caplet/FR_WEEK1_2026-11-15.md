## What the report will argue

Money Laura has promised is owned in Treasuries before she promises it; only money not yet promised takes market risk.
That is your IPS's own rule ("only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares"), and the report applies it to
everything Wharton asks for: the operating reserve, the facility contribution, the 2031 range and how confident Laura
can be in it, the words for co-sponsors, and what to watch before 2031 and 2033. On today's figures she could tell
co-sponsors in 2031 about $95,000 to $115,000. The plan's biggest strength is that every figure she states rests on money
she already owns, so her confidence comes from what she holds, not from a forecast. Its main trade-off is a lower
certain contribution than an all-Treasury plan, in exchange for a share of world growth, half of which is promised to no
one. Your pencil-and-ink conceit carries this: Laura states a figure only once it is inked.

## The week's best findings

- The ten payments are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, barring a US default. That costs about a quarter of the
  2028 deposit, and most of it comes back to Laura after the last payment.
- Buying the floor in January 2028, not 2031, is what makes her 2031 word believable. Bought in 2031 instead, she would
  be under $95,000 in about 1 in 6 simulated futures.
- Co-sponsors count the bottom. A broken promise costs trust and an exceeded one earns almost nothing (Gneezy and Epley,
  2014), which is why she states only figures she owns.
- Keeping half the shares back is your choice, defended on its real reason: a promise can be raised later but never
  taken back. Each extra $5,000 of top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps.
- 7.5% is J.P. Morgan's figure and sits in the upper third of fifteen published forecasts, not at the top. It barely
  matters: fourteen of the fifteen give the same $115,000 top and the lowest gives $110,000. What shares actually do
  before 2031 sets the top.
- A 40% crash in world shares before half of them move lowers the top to about $105,000. The bottom and every payment
  stay where they were.
- "Only two reasons to change" works because both are cases where the plan cannot be carried out (no deposit, or a
  Treasury that does not pay), never cases where keeping it would merely hurt. University endowments write the formula
  and leave the exits to a board; Stanford named its 2009 trigger after the fall.
- Taiwan's central culture spending rose in 2009 and 2010. Public co-sponsors fall short through budget votes and rule
  changes, not recessions.
