## P: first

- **What it does.** P has the essays' pun heading, and the closing line calls the heading back, which is how the
  essays treat their own headings. The concession is real: a number said flat ("almost $200,000 ahead") and left
  standing. The turn is the essays' favourite: the "But" grabs a word from the opponent's own sentence
  ("usually") and turns it against them. Then come two short sentences in a mirrored pair (one bill against ten
  bills), which is a homely reframing. The paragraph ends on a long sentence that lands through a spaced en dash.
  That's the build-and-land rhythm, and it's the only lander of the three that a judge would carry out of the room.
- **What it misses.** The pivot sentence is squeezed. "ours, under one in sixty" is a dropped-verb fragment after
  a semicolon, the over-compression Book 10 warns about, and it's the one place where P stops sounding spoken.
  The first paragraph's long sentence carries the client's temperament, a model assumption and the rival's result
  all joined by "and". It's a list where the essays would build towards one point.
- **Clarity on one reading.** To an investment judge, "bill" can mean a Treasury bill as well as a payment due, and
  P switches between "bill" and "cheque". Separately, "a 60/40 portfolio" doesn't say that the rival keeps the
  ladder, which Q and R both say. Whether P describes the same rival is a content check for the room, not a
  question of voice.

## R: second

- **What it does.** R has the essays' strongest structural move, done honestly: it gives the rival the win in
  plain words ("it usually wins"), with a number, before the "But". The "But" sentence is a proper build. The
  close ("We'd pay the $19,000.") is short and decisive and calls back the concession's figure. That's the essays'
  blunt last line and the guide's "end on what we will do".
- **What it misses.** It's a results table read aloud. There are ten figures in four sentences, and every one
  arrives with a qualifier attached ("matched in our model", "at the median", "counting the gift and what she
  keeps", "on average", "in the twentieth of our paths"). No number is reacted to, and nothing gets the essays'
  homely image: no kitchen, car or raft equivalent. "Name the same bottom" and "the twentieth of our paths" are
  analyst phrases. Most of these hedges are the right attribution, but together they set a careful, airless
  register.
- **Clarity on one reading.** "Usually wins" next to "more on average" says the same thing twice in two different
  measures. "Leaves her over $40,000 behind" needs a moment to work out: behind ours, in the bad paths.

## Q: third

- **What it does.** Q ends well. "and we'd pay it" names a real cost and accepts it, which is the confession the
  guide asks for. "In a typical 2033" is a nice concrete touch. It also holds the passage's most human idea: the
  rival's bottom falls in the very years donors give less.
- **What it misses.** Q never concedes. The rival gets no win before the case against it, so there's no "But"
  and no turn. "Set to promise the same $145,000 ..." opens on a dangling participle that reads like a spec
  sheet. "Ours, about one in 150." is the essays' blunt short sentence with the words taken out, a number standing
  where a reaction should be. The donor idea, which could carry the paragraph, sits in the last comma clause of
  an already long sentence. Q has no image.
- **Clarity on one reading.** "Has to promise under $100,000 about one time in thirteen" needs a second pass:
  promise at the 2031 naming, or in 2033?

## The one change for P (whole sentence, every number and claim kept)

Replace the squeezed pivot sentence:

> But "usually" is the whole trick: about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word to
> co-sponsors; ours, under one in sixty.

with one sentence that builds and then lands on a spaced en dash, so that "ours" gets a verb and the reader gets a
breath:

> But "usually" is the whole trick, because about one time in three, in that same model, the 60/40 either misses
> one of Laura's cheques or breaks its word to the co-sponsors – something ours does under one time in sixty.

Why this sentence: it's where the argument turns, and it's the one place where P reads as a note instead of a voice.
The essays never compress their turning point. They let it run in full and save the short sentence for afterwards.
P already has that short sentence, in the two "bill" lines that follow. "In that same model" ties the odds to their
source (Commandments) without adding a claim.

Smaller fixes, if the room wants them later (each is a word change, not part of the one change): use "cheque"
throughout instead of "bill", or say "payment", to avoid the Treasury-bill reading. Check that "60/40" names the
same rival as Q and R.
