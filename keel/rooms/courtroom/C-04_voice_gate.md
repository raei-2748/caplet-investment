## M: first

M does almost everything the essays do. It has a pun heading, then a real concession: the rival "usually" wins,
and the number is said flat ("almost $200,000 ahead") with no hedge around it. Then comes the essays' favourite
turn, "But", hung on the rival's own word ("'usually' is the whole trick"), which is how the essays take an
opponent's phrase and turn it on them. "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills" is a
mirrored pair, but it's a real turn of thought, because it is the argument. The last sentence builds through a
dash and lands on a homely, slightly comic image (a sale at the bottom, booked on a printed timetable) that pays off
the heading. What it misses: it ends on the image rather than a decision, "60/40" arrives unexplained, and its
rival isn't quite the one K and N describe. That last point is outside a voice brief, but the room should know.

## K: second

K's best sentence is the second one. It runs clause on clause through the rival's three steps and then drops in a
homely detail ("whatever they fetch that morning"). That's the essays' move, an everyday picture inside a formal
sentence, and it shows the danger before anyone names it. The concession is real and the number is said flat
($19,000). The "But" is there, and "We'd pay the $19,000." is a short, earned close that ends on a decision. What it
misses: "our $145,000 median bottom" is a noun stack a judge has to unpack, and "after the 2031 set-aside" trails
off into in-house shorthand. "the twentieth of our paths" can be misread as path number twenty. The morning image is
set up and never paid off.

## N: third

N fixes K's two small faults. "the same $145,000 bottom as ours at the median" keeps the small words, and "leaves
her over $40,000 behind" puts a second number in Laura's own pocket. But its mechanism sentence ("buys its bottom
only in 2031, out of a 2028 deposit put mostly in stocks") turns the forced sale into a date and a passive tail. A
reader has to work out for themselves why 2031 is dangerous, and that was the one sentence that could have carried
them. It has no image, so the "But" sentence is a list of figures with nothing to feel them against. It shares K's
"twentieth of our paths" ambiguity.

## K or N

**K is better on voice.** The difference is one sentence. K shows the rival selling stocks on a set morning at
whatever price the market gives, and that is the only place in either version where a person seems to be telling
you what will happen. N says the same thing as a date. N's two wins are word-level fixes (a plainer comparison, a
felt $40,000), and the room could carry them over. K's win is the voice itself, and N would have to rebuild a sentence to get it.

## The one whole-sentence change to K

Replace the "But" sentence so that it pays off the morning image and reads one way only. Every number is kept:

> **Now:** But in the twentieth of our paths where 2028-30 is worst for stocks, it names about $90,000 to
> co-sponsors against our $140,000 after the 2031 set-aside.
>
> **Proposed:** But in the worst twentieth of our paths for stocks in 2028-30, that January morning arrives
> mid-slump, and the rival names about $90,000 to co-sponsors against our $140,000 after the 2031 set-aside.

Why this sentence: it turns the image from a detail into the argument (law 6: introduce once, call back once). It
also removes the "path number twenty" misreading, and it gives "We'd pay the $19,000." something felt to land on.
Check: "worst twentieth" assumes the original meant the worst 5% of paths. If it meant something else, keep the
original phrase and change only the middle clause.
