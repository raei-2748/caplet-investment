**Q13:** who asks a sceptic to pay for a promise, in under a hundred words, and gets believed? **Field:** how a guide tells a paying client, before the climb, that they'll be turned back at a set hour whatever it looks like then, and gets them to agree.

## Finds

- **A1. The guide names the turn-back hour before the climb, and names the case it still holds in: however close the top is.** "Into Thin Air", Jon Krakauer (Outside magazine, September 1996 issue; the copy read is a PDF on a Swedish Armed Forces course page, with no magazine header, but a printed clarification refers to "your September, 1996 issue"), https://www.forsvarsmakten.se/siteassets/english/swedint/engelska/swedint/courses/nato-ll-soc/into-thin-air.pdf, accessed 1 Nov 2026.
  Quotes (exact, from the fetched text): Krakauer reporting Rob Hall: "Our turn-around time, he said, would probably be 1 P.M., and no matter how close we were to the top, we were to abide by it."; Guy Cotter, the guide who "was guiding the peak for him in 1995", on why it can't be left to the day: "If a client sees that the summit is close and they're dead-set on getting there, they're going to laugh in your face and keep going up."
  **The move (one line):** fix the limit while nobody can see the top yet, and say ahead of time that the top looking close won't change it.
  **Mechanism:** Hall gave the hour and the "however close" clause before summit day and, by Krakauer's account, "lectured us repeatedly on this point" for a month, so on the day the decision had already been taken and only had to be kept. Cotter's line is the reason it's done early: high on the mountain, a client who can see the top won't take the call, so the call has to be made down low.
  **Breaks:** On the day the rule failed. Hall had clients paying "$65,000 a head" (Krakauer) and a season with no summits behind him, and a client reached the top "well after Hall's pre-determined turn-around time" (Krakauer). A rule that rests on one person's judgement on the day broke at the moment it was there for. The stakes differ too: a turnaround protects the climber's own life, while Laura's lock protects a promise to other people (the co-sponsors) and costs her the rises past the top. The source is a client-journalist's account, Hall's instruction is Krakauer's paraphrase rather than Hall's sentence, and Outside's own copy of the article wasn't found. What carries over to Laura is only the timing: the decision is written down before anyone knows what 2031 looks like, and it's carried out as a step on a date, not as a judgement call.
  **Could touch:** the capped-range paragraph (the promised half moves into Treasuries on the day she names the range, "whatever the market is doing"); later, the 2031 co-sponsor text in the Final Report.

- **A2. The guide service publishes the pace test in hours, and how often climbers fail it, before anyone pays a deposit.** "Denali Mountaineering & Climbing | Classic Denali West Buttress Route Expedition", Mountain Trip, page metadata modified 20 Oct 2026, https://mountaintrip.com/alaska/climb-denali/west-buttress/, accessed 1 Nov 2026.
  Quotes (exact, from the fetched text): "Therefore, we have decided to attempt to be as clear and transparent as possible about our expectations of our climbers."; "From the 7,800’ Camp to the 11,000’ Camp, it should take about 6-7 hours."; looking back over a decade of trip reports, "less than 2% of the time" a team member can't go on safely.
  **The move (one line):** give the doubter the test in their own units, and the honest rate at which it bites, before they pay.
  **Mechanism:** A climber can hold their own training times up against "6-7 hours" before booking, so being turned round later is the result of a rule they've read rather than a guide's mood on the day. The rate tells them how rare it is, and on the same page paying the deposit is stated to be acceptance of the policy, including no refund for a missed summit.
  **Breaks:** Mountain Trip's limit is set off by the climber's own performance and enforced partly in the company's interest (risk to the guides and the team; no refund), whereas Laura's lock is set off by a date, chosen by her, and serves the co-sponsors. She isn't failing a test. The "less than 2%" comes from ten years of trip reports, while Caplet's "about 97 times in 100" is a model with no track record behind it, so copying the form risks borrowing a record's authority that Caplet doesn't have. That's why "in our model" has to stay.
  **Could touch:** the range sentence (bottom = the floor, top = floor plus half her stocks, "about 97 times in 100"); the Final Report's Portfolio Analysis, where the test itself can be shown.
  **In the reading already?** Partly dry. "A range with a hit rate" is already in Synthesis 4's investing finds (FRB-56, FRB-60: Hunter, Hauser, Bullock). Not there: publishing the test itself, in the reader's own units, before they commit.

- **A3. An IFMGA guide puts the turnaround times in the night-before briefing, between the route and the morning's plan.** "Matterhorn 2-day ascent. 2-day trip. Certified guide", Tobias, an IFMGA-certified guide, on Explore-Share (no page date shown), https://explore-share.com/trip/matterhorn-2-day-guided-ascent, accessed 1 Nov 2026.
  Quotes (exact, from the fetched text): "After checking your equipment, we'll discuss the route, the latest mountain conditions and the plan for the following morning, including the turnaround times."; "Reaching the summit cannot be guaranteed."
  **The move (one line):** say the limit the night before, as one ordinary line of the plan, never as a warning on the day.
  **Mechanism:** Said at the hut along with the gear check and the route, the turnaround time becomes part of what the client is agreeing to climb, so on the ridge it's the plan being followed, not a fresh decision. The disclaimer then says the rest flat, in five words.
  **Breaks:** This is one evening's briefing for a climb of "around 4-5 hours", one guide and one client in the same room. Laura's lock comes once, years after it's written, and no one is there to brief her the night before. The page is also a sales listing on a marketplace, where "cannot be guaranteed" is legal cover as much as candour. The likeness is mostly on the surface: a briefing is spoken and can be questioned, and an IPS is read once by strangers.
  **Could touch:** where the lock sits in the IPS: inside the description of the plan, rather than being introduced as "a catch"; also the 50-word pitch's tone.
  **In the reading already?** Mostly dry: already in Synthesis 2 §1 ("bad news early, small and in the middle") and Synthesis 4 §2 ("Doubt the forecast in one sentence, then say the plan flat").

## Amara's ninety-second rule
"Rob Hall spent a month telling his Everest clients they'd turn back at a set hour however close the top was, and then on the day he let one of them go on past it – because, as his colleague Guy Cotter put it, a client who can see the summit will 'laugh in your face and keep going up'. That's why Laura's call is already written down: the half she promises moves into Treasuries the day she names the range, so who'd want to make that call in a week when stocks are rising?"
