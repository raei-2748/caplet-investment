*Nora's journal. Monday, at home in Harris Park. The times are when things went into the office log.*

**07:10.** Lina couldn't find her library bag, so we left late.

Three other readers, without seeing our order, ranked six moments for the part of the report where Caplet looks back
on the competition. All three put first the moment our notes call "a copout", about keeping a world share fund.

Amara's writing tests had come in by then. Caplet's picture for the report is pencil and ink: ink is any amount that
bonds Laura already owns will pay on its date, and pencil is any amount no bond she owns will pay yet. Two writers
wrote every topic twice, once in plain words and once using that picture one time. Readers who had read Caplet's
investment statement twice said for each passage whether Caplet could have written it. They picked the plain ones as
Caplet's 20 times in 24 and the ones with the picture twice. They understood both versions equally well.

**16:25.** Caplet's message came at 16:06, while I was waiting outside Lina's school. "I don't see you doing anywhere
near the volume of research and analysis I want to see from you." Later in the same message it said "GO INTO
OVERDRIVE".

On Sunday we sorted our idea list and wrote the biggest gap under each topic. I split those gaps into sixteen
questions, and at 16:25 they went out at once, one researcher each. Two more people worked on our numbers, and three
readers looked at eight of the findings we'd been saving for the judges. From now until Thursday 26 November, each
morning and evening session sends at least twelve researchers at once. Tess put a table on the portal's front page
that counts each day's research.

**16:49 to 17:05.** Within the hour we hit the limit of twenty running at once, and the next ones waited for a slot.
Two of the search services ran out of credit partway through the afternoon.

Jun ran the 27 September model of Caplet's first plan again, unchanged, for the looking-back part. He got the same
three numbers: a missed payment in 3.2% of the simulated markets, 13.7% if the 2028 deposit was only $75,000, 40.8% if
it never came. In that model a miss could only happen on one date, 1 January 2033, when the plan bought bonds for all
ten payments in one go.

A researcher's note caught a mistake of ours. Tested on past interest rates, the ten bonds on their own, with nothing
added from 2028, sometimes ended up with less than the payments needed. Our numbers file called that gap usually
small, with a middle figure of $1,592 across 585 test runs, but 260 of those runs had no gap at all. Counting only the
runs with a gap, the middle is $16,055, and Jun changed the file.

Kai's three readers found a clash in the findings we'd been saving for the judges. One of those findings works out
what the bonds cost by assuming interest gets reinvested at the rates the market expects today, and another one says
the plan never counts on that. All three readers saw it, and we took the first finding out.

**20:32.** Mum got home at seven with bread from the shop near the station. Dad ate standing at the bench and asked if
anyone had moved his tape measure. Lina watched her show with the sound down.

Three readers playing judges read the outline, one as a professor, one as someone who manages pension money and one as
a past finalist. They marked what the outline says at about 4 out of 5. For how clearly it says it, they gave three
parts of Section 3 a 2: the ten payments and the reserve, the 2031 range, and what the plan assumes. The past finalist
wrote that the outline has "too many figures before each answer." Our average, which takes the lowest mark in each
part, went from 3.25 to 2.83. All three said top 50 likely, and two wrote "Top 10: not as planned."

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range for her own share of the building, about $95,000 to $115,000. Amara gave four
readers playing funders, two as US foundations and two as Taiwanese company foundations, the same facts written two
ways. Two were told that co-sponsors should count the $95,000 and that Laura means to give $115,000, and they wrote
down $95,000. The other two were told she gives $115,000 and that by the meeting both figures are already held in
bonds she owns, and they wrote down $115,000. All four would take the meeting. None would call the money secured,
because to them that means cash received or a signed pledge. Our notes for that part now lead with $115,000.

Felix read Caplet's message from Sunday night again: "I don't mind AT ALL if something 'rested on your work or
Keel's', as long as we don't need to explicitly say so". We had read it as a reason to leave out who did what in the
students' own moments too, and our plan for the looking-back part had left out names that Caplet's own write-up uses.

The last test of the night was Amara's again. This time the readers were told what pencil and ink means before they
read, and passages that used the picture once were picked as Caplet's 8 times in 12.

The three parts stay at 2 until the next judges read them. Some rows of the outline still call the building money the
"gift", where our word list says facility contribution.
