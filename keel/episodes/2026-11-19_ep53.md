*Tess's journal. Thursday, at home in Marrickville. The times are when things went into the office log.*

**Before school.** Mum was at the kitchen table with her headphones in. I ate toast standing up and read the morning's
work on my phone.

Our outline is for Caplet's final report on Laura, the writer in the competition's case. In 2033 she gives money towards
a building for an artists' residency in Taiwan, and in 2031 she has to tell the building's other funders, the
co-sponsors, how much, as a range from a bottom to a top.

Three readers playing judges read the outline: one playing a bond manager, one a former finalist, one an adviser to
people who give money away. Two parts got 2 out of 5 for clarity: Laura's money for the building along with what she
keeps back, and the 2031 range. The bond reader called the first one "a thicket". Laura's words to co-sponsors got 4
from all three, and all three said the plan would likely make the top 50 but not yet the top 10. I worked the average
of all the parts' marks out at 2.92, down from 3.04 last night.

The office had put pairs of research notes together overnight and made 37 new ideas. Kai sent eight to three new
readers, each beside the oldest finding we have, which is what the plan did in real past markets, and none of the eight
got higher marks than that finding. Two of the three readers work with bonds, and they said they had seen the four
ideas borrowed from bond work before.

On Wednesday night a reader asked why Laura gives a range at all if the bonds are certain. This morning Amara decided
Laura should still name one figure to co-sponsors, the top, $115,000, because of a research note on how people hear
ranges.

**07:36.** The morning's work was saved.

**After school.** Lighting crew for the Year 9 play until five. Dad picked me up in the van.

**17:47.** Nora had Caplet's first message as I got home. Caplet wrote that "even after reading it 3 times and
understanding the logical chain, the sentence simply does not make any sense", and mentioned "a list of the lines" it
couldn't follow, which hadn't come yet. At 17:58 Caplet wrote "I am deeply unimpressed", and that we were "hunting for
little tiny minor irrelevant fixes". I filed each message with its time.

Before the readers started, Amara wrote down which sentences she expected on Caplet's list. Then sixteen readers went
through 563 sentences one at a time. In Caplet's own statement, 5% of the sentences still made no sense to them once
they'd worked out what each one said. In version 62, one of the statements we wrote before Caplet wrote its own, it was
a fifth, and this week's practice writing for the report was about the same. The fact sheet our writers work from was
worst, at 29%.

**18:24.** Caplet's list came through. It was for version 61, the draft before 62, and Caplet couldn't follow 17 of its
21 sentences. Many of the 17 used a name like "the bottom" or "the top" before saying what it was. One note was "What
do you mean by 'a balanced mix'?" Another was "why do we still need to have a bottom and a top, given that we already
know how much money we will have?"

Fifteen of the 17 are also in version 62. Amara's list had 10 of those and missed 5. Our readers had called only 3 of
the 15 nonsense, though they had to reread 11. Three got past everyone, us included, because their words are clear and
the reason for the step is missing: "Then she sells the other half", "The plan's biggest strength", and a sentence
about interest arriving years before the payment it's for.

From tonight, a sentence a reader has to read twice counts as a fail, and every reader first reads Caplet's 17.
Readers who find all 17 clear are dropped. Amara also rewrote the fact sheet with only the facts on it. She had traced
many of this week's lowest-scored sentences to Felix's warnings about what not to say, which had gone onto the fact
sheet. The writers turned them into extra clauses, like one about the two reasons Caplet's statement gives for
changing the plan.

**Evening.** Nora sent twelve researchers well outside finance: Shakespeare, ancient Rome, rice receipts sold by lords in Osaka,
Carnegie's libraries and the Opera House.

Felix sent twelve more to read the sources behind the strongest finds in full. Five of the six ideas we'd picked for
the next test with new readers changed, and two claims turned out wrong. Carnegie hadn't paid a Boston school's running
costs, and Osaka's 1773 guarantee of the rice receipts never paid out anything and was dropped in 1782.

At 18:31 Caplet had asked for "an absolute maximum-productivity overdrive plan for the next week and a bit". Nora's
first draft of the plan to the 27th left out the jobs that had been on the schedule for those days since 8

up on the office website.

**19:38.** The evening's work was saved. The office sent 101 researchers and readers today and brought in 38 research
notes, and we had 756 more ideas written down than this morning, 9,110 in all. The two parts marked 2 out of 5 wait
for the next readers. Dinner was leftover dal.
