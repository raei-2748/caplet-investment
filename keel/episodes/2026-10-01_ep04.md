### Lunch, then the Long Room

I'd been bothered since Tuesday by something Felix said: that WInS prices bonds "at end-of-day prices". End of whose
day, and from where? So I took the forty-three bonds Felix had typed up, took WInS's last price for each, and
worked out the yield each price implies. Then I compared it with what the Treasury curve says a bond of that length
should yield that day.

Most were within a few basis points. Two weren't. Two of the longer bonds showed prices that implied yields about
**one whole percentage point** away from the curve. Not 5bp. 100bp.

**Me:** Either the market has gone mad about two bonds, or WInS's prices for them are stale.

**Kai:** Or it's a bargain. Buy them.

**Me:** If the price is too low, the yield looks high. If that's real, it means nobody wants to trade it, which
means we could buy it and never be able to sell it at a fair price. If it isn't real, we're buying at a fake price
and the next update will knock it down.

**Felix:** WInS FAQ: bonds "fill at end-of-day prices". It doesn't say the prices are checked.

**Nora:** So what? What does Laura do with this?

**Me:** Nothing, in real life. Her asset manager would see the real price. But our book is supposed to *be* her
plan, scaled. If we buy a stale bond, our notes say "this bond pays for 2040", and the price we paid says we
overpaid or bought something illiquid. A judge who checks the yield would see it.

**Tess:** Would a judge check the yield?

**Me:** A good one would.

**Nora:** Rule?

**Me:** Before any bond order: work out its yield from the WInS price and compare it with the curve for that date.
If it's more than 25 basis points away, we don't buy it that day. We wait for a fresh price or pick the
neighbouring bond.

**Kai:** Twenty-five because?

**Me:** Because the real spread between neighbouring Treasuries is usually a few basis points, and the curve
moved more than 25bp in total over the last month. Anything further than that is a price problem, not a market.
It's a rule of thumb. I'll write that it's a rule of thumb.

Kai asked me to prove it on the board. I did. He nodded, which from Kai is a standing ovation.

### The spread lesson

Kai, to be fair, brought the second finding. He'd been watching the iBond term funds after the U.S. close, from his
slate, while serving customers at the shop.

**Kai:** Outside U.S. hours the quotes on these little funds go wide. Bid and ask a long way apart. If we put a
market order in at the wrong time, it fills at the open at whatever the ask is. On a small fund that could be
cents a share. On four thousand shares that's real money.

**Felix:** WInS: orders placed while the market's closed fill "at the security's opening price when the market
reopens".

**Kai:** So: limit orders. Placed during U.S. hours, when we can see the price. That's 23:30 to 06:00 here, until
Sunday, when it moves.

**Felix:** Moves?

**Kai:** Daylight saving. Our clocks go forward on Sunday. The open becomes 00:30.

Felix wrote that on his hand. Actually on his hand. He's going to own that by Sunday, you can see it.

The ideas ledger got its fourth row: **spreads and limit orders. Adopted.** And my rule went in with it.

Nora's rule says nobody works in the room alone. I told myself it didn't count because I was only checking. I
