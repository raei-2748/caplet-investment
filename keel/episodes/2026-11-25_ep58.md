**Wednesday 06:43.** We work for Caplet, a real school team in the Wharton investment competition. Their case is about
Laura, a writer who has ten $50,000 payments to make from 2033 and wants to help pay for an artists' residency
building in Taiwan. In 2031 she gives the building's other funders, its co-sponsors, a range for her share, about
$95,000 to $115,000. The bottom goes into US government bonds, called Treasuries, in January 2028. The top waits in
shares until January 2031, when half her shares move into a Treasury fund that pays out in late 2032, and the other
half stays in shares, promised to no one.

The report gets written on Friday. This morning I tried out how the drafts get checked. I took a copy of the plan
Caplet handed in on 6 November and put five mistakes in it: a wrong number of years, a word that disagrees with their
trading notes, a term nobody explains, a main point hidden at the end of a 70-word sentence, and a two-line slogan
tacked on after the last real point. Eleven test reviewers read it, each given one thing to read for, and none of them
saw my list. Each mistake was found by at least four of the eleven, so up to seven missed each one. Two of them found
all five between them: one who read it once and answered from memory, and one working through a list of seven things
the report must never get wrong. The reviewer told to read for what the case says about Laura found one and marked it
unsure. Friday's steps were rewritten after it.

**Wednesday 07:03.** Tess finished giving every one of the 11,103 ideas in our notes a verdict and a reason, 828 of
them for the first time.

No figure can change after Thursday, so Jun checked all 136 in our notes against our register, the file every figure
in the report has to come from. Seven were wrong. One of them called $399,089 the amount still owed on the payments,
when $500,000 is owed and $399,089 is what that is worth today.

Amara gave three readers practice versions of two parts of the report, each written in full by a new writer. Readers
mark every sentence they had to read twice. In the part on the ten payments they got 78% of sentences first time, and
in the part on what could go wrong 54%. Most stops were a name that turned up before anyone knew what it meant, like
"the floor". Laura's plan also buys about $36,800 of Treasuries beyond the ten payments, so they're covered even if
money waiting between bond dates earns nothing. The practice part said those extra Treasuries cost her, and two
readers said that made no sense because most of the money comes back to her by 2042. The line now says she has to hold
them.

Bà was up before me with the radio on low. Mum had a nine o'clock inspection in Cabramatta and dropped me at the
station on the way.

**Wednesday 19:13.** A practice version of Section 2, the part about what the portfolio holds, was written in full for
the first time. Amara had written down a guess of 70 to 80% of sentences first time, and readers got 63%. All three

I was at the shop from four to seven.

**Wednesday 22:23.** Caplet shared the Google Doc they're writing the report in: "This is our final report that we are
currently working on".

Since 14 November our notes have used the comparison Caplet chose for the report, pencil and ink: ink for an amount
Treasuries held for Laura will pay, pencil for one they don't pay yet. Our notes used it once in every part of the
report. Their plan keeps it to "one restrained opening reference and an optional closing echo", with pencil standing
for her uncertain income from writing, and our notes now do the same.

Their doc also questions the promise we added on Tuesday. Laura's letter to the other funders would have said that if
the late-2032 fund paid out less than $115,000, she'd make up the difference from the half of her shares kept back.

short, but a signed letter would promise it in advance. Nobody here caught that on Tuesday. Nora dropped the promise
unless Caplet wants it back, and the letter now gives the sum and the fund that holds it.

Their draft opens on a question they've marked as not final. Amara wrote three others, and the one we suggested is
"When does Laura's money stop being hers to risk?" We also asked Caplet how Friday should work with their draft. Until
they answer, Friday writes a full draft that follows their plan.
