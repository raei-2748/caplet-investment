**Sunday 09:49.** Nora's morning work went up. We'd used most of this week's computer time on Friday and it has to last

keeping to that until Caplet decides. The seven research questions cut off on Friday were done in the same run.

She also put the whole report's plan on one page and posted it for Caplet. Laura, the writer in the case, has ten
$50,000 payments to make from 2033 and wants to help pay for an artists' residency building in Taiwan. The report opens
on a test a judge can check against the table of what she holds: if every share she owned were worth nothing, all ten
payments would still be made and the building would still get $95,000. The page's last line is "Unless you say otherwise
by Wed 25 Nov, we write to this shape."

Por Por ordered for both of us at yum cha while I read it.

**Sunday 09:49.** Amara's long round went in at the same time. Two new writers each explained Laura's plan in about
2,400 words, one after studying how good long explainers are built and one without. Three readers read each piece once,
marking any paragraph where they lost track. Amara had guessed the second piece would lose its readers, but nobody lost
track anywhere. All six could say the argument back from memory, and all six named pencil and ink first or second when
asked what stuck. That's the picture the report uses: ink for an amount Laura already holds bonds to pay, pencil for one
she doesn't yet.

The piece that opened on two questions anyone can hold (how $450,000 turns into about $615,000 of promises, and why a
figure called certain has to wait until 2031) got the most "so that's why" moments. Every reader said they'd have
stopped once the range of building money Laura names in 2031 was explained, because the risks after it read like fine
print. Two of them took the line "It will matter again" as a teaser. Amara moved the risks in the plan so that each one
sits beside the claim it tests.

**Sunday afternoon.** An hour of clarinet, then Dad rang from Hong Kong while he waited for a taxi. The call dropped
twice.

**Sunday 20:00.** The evening's work went up, about half size again. My part was the four stories from outside finance
the report plans to use, each checked against the original papers. Lehman changed most. Our notes said its $10 million
pledge to Spelman College, which ended up as $3 million when the bank collapsed, was "a pledge from a fortune". The 2007
announcements say the bank pledged it itself, a five-year partnership paid out of the business, and its foundation was
separate and stayed out of the bankruptcy. We'd also written that Australia's Reserve Bank made a promise about interest
rates. The bank said what it expected to do, and people took it as a promise, so that was reworded too. The other two, a
lottery's investment rule and the 2022 British pension crisis, got smaller fixes.

**Sunday 20:00.** Kai gave four ideas to three readers who hadn't seen any of it, playing a funder, a professor and
someone in arts finance. All three picked the same one for the part written to the people helping pay for the building:
that already owning the bonds and signing a letter mean different things to someone giving money. Kai added a line the
plan didn't have. Laura's letter names her bond fund, and they see a statement of it at her 2031 meetings with them and
again before the payout in December 2032. All three readers had asked what stops her selling the fund in between.

**Sunday 20:00.** Amara's second round asked two more writers to continue Caplet's investment plan from its last line
for about 200 words first. They used "we" two to four times as often as last round's writers, about Caplet's own rate,
so on Friday every writer warms up that way. Her other test, where a reader retells each step and its reason from
memory, caught 2 of the 5 lines on Caplet's list that every earlier reader had passed. It also flagged 3 of the 4 lines
not on the list, so it can't tell them apart, and she has dropped that kind of test. Next round asks each reader one
question the passage should let them answer.

**Sunday 20:00.** Jun checked 66 figures in the report's plan a second way, re-running the scripts and working out the
bond prices again with his own code. Fifty-seven matched and one matched only roughly. Five had no second way to check,
and three were wrong, with a fourth mistake in our own number notes. Those notes said that since June 2007 there had
been only one day, in October 2023, when $300,000 was enough to buy bonds for all ten payments. His run found 13 more
days in July 2007, so that only holds from August 2007. A fall in world shares we'd written as 41% is about 42% when
measured over the same years as the building-cost figure beside it. We'd said the bond prices passed their check against
the official ones in late September. They passed on 5 October, and late September was when the check was set. And the
gap we called about $39,000 in 2027 only reaches that by January 2028; in January 2027 it's $37,499.
