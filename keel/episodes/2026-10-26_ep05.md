# FICTION. Episode 5: Who pays?

**Monday 26 October 2026, Sydney · Point of view: Amara · IPS due in 12 days (Sat 7 Nov 09:00).**

---

Amara drew it on the back of her maths homework before first period: a sign on a building, 2031, *Laura is putting in
at least this much.* Underneath, in smaller letters: *unless bond rates drop in 2036.*

She didn't like the small letters. That was the whole problem, and the mock judge had found it too: if coupons have to
be reinvested at lower rates, the ladder comes up short. Who pays?

---

### Library, 3:40 p.m.

Felix went first, which was new. He'd asked to.

**Felix:** Can I read you something? Our own draft. Principle 1: "the facility fund absorbs it." Principle 2: "The
sail is never sold to cover a payment." The facility fund is the floor plus the sail. Take the sail out and only the
floor is left.

**Tess:** And the floor is the bottom of the range.

**Felix:** Case, line 119: the range "must also protect the portfolio's ability to fund the ten-year operating
commitment." So our two sentences together make the floor pay, and the case says the floor can't be the thing at risk.
One of them goes before the 7th. Investment Policy, line 74: no revising after.

**Nora:** So what does the case say *does* pay?

**Felix:** Nothing by name. Line 91 says "the investment portfolio". Line 94 says the reserve is set aside "before
making the first operating payment or contributing to the facility". Reserve first, building second. We choose the
payer. We just have to choose now.

Jun had been waiting with a table, which meant he'd been up late.

**Jun:** Size first. Our five Treasuries pay coupons. If yields fall two points and stay there, those coupons earn less.
Shortfall: $13.5k on the bonds, $19.8k if the iBond funds' income counts too. In 2033 money, $10.5k to $16.4k. MODEL,
Friday's close.

**Kai:** In 2033 money. When does it actually hit?

**Jun:** Mostly 2038 to 2042. The late payments. That's where the coupons pile up. Almost all of it after the
2033 split.

**Kai:** So "then the sail" is a joke. In 2033 we cut the sail in half and give half to a building. You can't send a
2040 bill to a boat we've already sunk.

**Amara:** Then who?

**Jun:** Laura's kept half covers the two-point case in 91 paths out of 100. The whole sail, before it's split, covers
it in all but about one path in two thousand. The floor almost never has to.

**Amara:** Her kept half is the money line 102 is about: "committing all remaining assets could limit her financial
flexibility as the project develops." It's for overruns. In the year yields fall for good, the building might be over
budget too.

**Jun:** Joint bad case: yields down two, stocks down 40% over 2028-31, then up 7% in 2032. Her half is $12.0k. The
other $4.4k comes off the gift, which still lands above the floor.

**Kai:** Here's my motion. On 1 January 2033, before a dollar goes to the building, we reprice the reserve on that
day's prices and top it up from the sail. Whatever is already known by then gets paid while the sail still exists.
Her half only backs what's left after.

**Felix:** That matches line 94. Reserve first.

**Kai:** And it's partly our own fault. Our sentence parks pre-2033 coupons in 2033 bonds and rolls them again. We're
making our own risk.

Jun shook his head.

**Jun:** I modelled that too. Read the sentence literally: every coupon goes into a Treasury for the *next* payment.
Then the shortfall at two points is $4.8k, mostly in 2034, while the sail still exists. But it's a different ladder.
No iBond for 2033 at all, bigger bond faces, and I haven't priced the iBond income in that version yet.

**Kai:** So the sentence is *better* than the sizing?

**Jun:** Maybe. Or I'm wrong by the iBond income. Wednesday.

**Kai:** Fine. Prove me wrong properly. Wednesday.

Amara looked at her drawing.

**Amara:** Then the sign stays true either way. Whatever is known by 2031 comes off before she says the number.
Whatever comes later comes from her own half first, then the sail before it's split. Never the bottom. Can we say that
in one sentence a co-sponsor would believe?

**Tess:** In about twenty words. We have twenty-three spare.

**Nora:** Decision, owner, date.

## What Caplet can take from today
1. **Your "~$21k" coupon-shortfall figure holds up.** Keel re-ran it on the 23 Oct close and got $21.0k (cash when
   due): Treasuries $12.9k, iBond income $6.2k, bills between maturity and payment $2.0k
   (`keel/library/ips_numbers_2026-10/ips_numbers.py`, MODEL). It is a 2-point-fall-for-good case.
2. **Your IPS names a payer for a shortfall "expected in 2033", but the shortfall mostly arrives later.** IPS Version 5
   says income is "reinvested in the holding that paid it" (`caplet/google_drive/ips_report/01_version_5.md:26`). On
   that design the gap shows up mostly in 2038-42, almost all of it after the 2033 split. Check that the IPS says who covers a shortfall that
   appears after 2033.
3. **Name the payer as a rule now.** The IPS should "establish your strategic approach to ... managing the operating
   reserve's asset composition over time" (`2026_WGY_Investment_Policy-FINAL.txt:48-49`) and can't be revised after the deadline (`:73-74`). The number waits
   for the Final Report (`:68-70`).
4. **Reserve first, gift second.** The case orders it: the reserve is set aside "before ... contributing to the
   facility" (`Laura_Gao_2026_Client_Profile.txt:94-95`). A 2033 repricing before the gift is the cleanest reading.
5. **Look for two sentences that leave only the floor as the payer.** If any sentence says the floor, the bottom of the
   range, can pay a shortfall after it's announced, it fails the case's "must also protect" test (`:119-120`).
