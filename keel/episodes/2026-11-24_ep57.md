**Tuesday 06:37.** We work for Caplet, a real school team in the Wharton investment competition. Their case is about
Laura, a writer who has ten $50,000 payments to make from 2033 and wants to help pay for an artists' residency
building in Taiwan. In 2031 she tells the building's other funders a range for her share, about $95,000 to $115,000.
The $95,000 has been in US government bonds, called Treasuries, since January 2028. In January 2031 she moves half her
shares into a fund of Treasuries that pays out in late 2032, and that covers the top. At the 2031 meetings she signs
for $115,000.

Today was the paragraph she says to those funders. Our notes had two orders for it, the range first or the one figure
first. Before anyone read, I wrote down my guess: at least one reader given the range first would plan on $95,000. We
also wrote down that if the two orders came out even, the range would stay first.

**Tuesday 07:06.** Kai gave our notes for this part to three readers playing funders: the head of giving at a
Taiwanese company, a programme officer at an American foundation and a grants officer at Taiwan's culture ministry.
All three would count on $115,000 once a signed letter arrived. Without the letter, the company reader would plan on
$95,000, and all three said hearing the range first would make them plan on $95,000 too.

Then six test readers read Laura's actual words, three with the range first and three with the figure first. All six
counted on $115,000, so my guess was wrong and the range stays first. All six pointed to the sentence where she signs
for $115,000, and that sentence was in both versions.

All six asked something else: if the top is already in bonds and signed for, why give a range, and what could make it
$95,000? Five of six also stopped at "about $615,000" for everything she's promising. Ten payments of $50,000 plus
$115,000 is exactly $615,000, and they asked what "about" covered. Before school the words got one more sentence: the
$95,000 never depended on her shares, and since January 2031 neither end can change. The total became "$615,000 in
all".

Two of the three funders and two of the six test readers asked what could make Laura pay less than she signs for. Nora
wrote down the decision: the letter fixes the sum at $115,000, and if the fund pays less, Laura makes up the
difference from the money she keeps back.

Jun worked out what the fund's statement would show the funders. A bond fund two years from paying out is worth a
bit less than it will pay, so on the day of the 2031 move it shows about $111,700, and passes $115,000 that August.
The same fund also holds money for one of the ten payments, so a statement of it would show more than the building's
share. Our notes now keep the building's part in a second account at the same broker.

I tutor on Tuesdays. Priya has a maths test on Thursday, so we did past papers. Leo forgot his book. Mum picked me
up at six and we had dal and rice with Nani.

**Tuesday 19:13.** The report runs one comparison throughout: ink for an amount Laura already holds bonds to pay,
pencil for one she doesn't yet. Tess's six research questions tonight were all about ink: the ink on voters' fingers,
pencils whose marks can't be rubbed out, printers' proofs, signatures on banknotes. Our notes had four Chinese
characters from an old calligraphy text as an optional line for the title page, written 意在筆先. The text reads 意在筆前, and
Taiwan's education ministry dictionary lists the other as a later form. A line we had for Laura buying before she
promises, "grind the ink before you write", is out too. The one English translation we found has the writer
"pretending to grind the ink". We also dropped "stet", the printers' mark for "leave it as it is", as a word for Laura
keeping her bonds.

Jun rechecked a 1994 figure with code. Our earlier working had measured that year's fall in the payments' value from
one starting point and the recovery from another. Our notes now say that in 1994 ten bonds like Laura's fell at most
about 9% below what they cost and were back within a year.

Felix sorted out the three sources nobody could find on Monday: one was found, and two were swapped for works already
on the list. 181 sources are ready for the reference list.

**Tuesday 19:20.** Before the evening's three readers started, I wrote down my guess: the promise to make up a
shortfall would draw a question, and nobody would ask about the bottom again.

They followed most sentences first time, and all three counted on $115,000. This time they asked what stands behind
that promise. Her words said no market can change either end, then two sentences later said the fund might pay out
less. The ministry reader: "I take it a fund is not the same as a Treasury, but the page does not say so." The company
reader still asked when they'd ever see only the $95,000, so the second half of my guess was wrong. All three also
read "on today's figures", next to the letter's sum, as a figure that could still move.

That evening the notes added when each figure has been in bonds, said plainly that the fund is not itself a Treasury,
and took "on today's figures" out of the part about the letter.

**Still open.** Our own score for the plan is 2.92 out of 5, and our lowest marks are for explaining what the plan
assumes. Laura's words go to readers once more before Friday. Caplet can change the report's shape until tomorrow.
