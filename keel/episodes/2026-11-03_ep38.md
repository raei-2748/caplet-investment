# FICTION. Episode 38: The pass mark goes first

**Tuesday 3 November 2026, Sydney · Point of view: Nora · Long Room: light (one room day, the morning pick) · Final IPS for Caplet's review: end of Wed 4 Nov (tomorrow)**

> **Summary.** The last day anything new can go into the IPS. Yesterday's rooms built a version on V53 that three
> outside readers preferred, all three, and found four sentences that were wrong at their source. Today's round asks a
> plainer question that is harder to fake: which words would a stranger have to guess? Nora is the judge, and before
> anyone reads anything she writes down what counts as a pass.
> **Arcs:** 2 (two captains: Nora judges the round Kai helped shape). **Values:** 2, 3.
> **Ideas:** none new (the 0 : 3 dial).

---

Nora wrote the pass mark at 6:50, before the call, on the back of a maths worksheet she was meant to have finished.

*Every question must pass. The weakest reader decides. A member is a vote, never a reading.*

She looked at it for a while. It was the kind of sentence that sounded fine at ten to seven and would have to survive
Tess at five past.

---

"Read-back first," she said when the faces came up. "Felix."

Felix had `WHARTON_WORD.md` on one side and the Rooftop's orders on the other. He read each line slowly, by its
words, not its number. "The numbers moved seven yesterday when the case lines went in," he said. "So I'm not trusting
any number anyone remembers." Five lines. Five ticks. "All Wharton's."

"After yesterday," Kai said, "I'd have bet on one."

"Don't bet against the new file," said Felix. "It's the only thing in here that got better overnight."

Nora moved on. "Amara, near. Capital-protected notes."

"P5 says banks 'spend that interest on an option, which expires worthless if the market stands still,'" Amara read.
"My mum's vaccine fridge has a sheet taped to it that says what to do if the reading's bad. It was written before
anything went wrong. That's the bit I like about the regulators' pages: they tell you what the thing *is* before they
tell you what can go wrong with it. We start at the option."

"So what's the plain word before 'option'?"

"That's the job," said Amara.

"Kai, adjacent."

Kai had a weather page up. "Seventy percent chance of rain. Everyone thinks they know what it means, and most people
are wrong about what it's seventy percent *of*. Then I read our P5 and P6. 'That is under a tenth of her money.' That
what? 'Roughly the interest it should earn.' It who? 'Half her stocks.' Half on which day?" He sat back. "I'm not
rewriting them. I'm just counting how many times a stranger has to go back a sentence."

"Jun, far."

Jun was on the train again, camera on this time, a window full of Strathfield behind him. "Medicine leaflets. In Europe
they have to pass a readers' test before they're allowed out. Nine in ten find the information, nine in ten of those
understand it, on every single question." He paused while the train braked. "We've got three readers. We can't pass
that. But we can fail it, and on the last day failing it is the useful bit."

"That's my sheet," Nora said, holding up the worksheet. "Every question must pass. The weakest reader decides."

Tess unmuted. "Then I'm hunting the other way. A 'stranger got confused' that's really us not liking a sentence. If a
GUESS doesn't name the reader who guessed, it doesn't count."

"Agreed."

"And if your weakest reader is one of us?"

"Then it's a vote, not a reading," said Nora. "It's on the sheet."

"Good," said Tess. "I was going to make you write that."

"I know. That's why it's already there."

---

After the call Nora turned the worksheet back over and finished question four. Then she wrote one more line on the
back, smaller, under the rules, for herself:

*If it fails tomorrow, that's still a result.*
