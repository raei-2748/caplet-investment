# Keel's diary: how to keep it true

> Written Sun 8 Nov 2026 for Ray's order of that evening (register I96), verbatim: "Team authenticitiy. Continue
> simulating Keel's journey and experience in this competition. According to guardrail number 2, if you find the
> experience corny or fake, find a solution around it." Guardrail 2, verbatim: "Use skills or find skills whever you
> meet a bottleneck or you feel like you are falling short." Skills used: `stop-slop` (its rules are folded into the
> test in section 6) and a web research pass on how writers make accounts of real teams read true (section 3).
> From Ep 41 on, where `CAST.md`, `WORLD.md`, `STORY_BIBLE.md` or `ARCS.md` disagree with this file about how an
> episode is written, this file wins. Nothing in those files is deleted.

## 0. What the diary is for

Two jobs. First, it lets Caplet follow what Keel did each day in a form a person reads to the end (Ray, 3 Nov: the
team can't follow Keel). Second, it is a running account of an office going through the competition, failures
included. Wharton's fourth criterion asks for "Articulation of Competition Experience" (teamwork, communication,
learning; `competition/official/2026_27/SMApply_Deliverables_Page_2026-09-27.md:55`). Keel's episodes never appear
as Caplet's experience, but both jobs fail the moment an episode contains something that didn't happen: Caplet is
then misinformed about Keel's day, and the account stops being an account.

## 1. Diagnosis: what reads corny or fake, and why

Read for this: `INDEX.md`, `../CAST.md`, Ep 0, 5, 19, 33, 38, 39, 40 in full, and the last lines of Ep 18-37.

**1. Every episode ends on a line built to be remembered.** The habit Ray flagged in the IPS drafts on 3 Nov
(`../ips_rewrite/drafts/D35/README.md`: one-line endings, two-word kickers) runs through the diary.
- Ep 38: "*If it fails tomorrow, that's still a result.*"
- Ep 39: "*If it needs a code to make sense, it's not ready.*"
- Ep 40: "Then the room went quiet again, which was still the point."
- Ep 28: "'Rules before the first tile,' she said. 'After, only arguing.' Then she took the pen."
- Ep 29: "'That sounds like nothing.' 'It's most of it.'"
- Ep 37: "He didn't take it. But he thought about it."
Cause: the writer wanted each day to mean something and put the meaning in the last line.

**2. Characters reach the day's insight out loud, often as "not X, it's Y".**
- Ep 40: "'It's neither,' he said slowly. 'It's a broken instruction.'" and "It's the first thing, wearing a different jumper."
- Ep 38: "'Then it's a vote, not a reading,' said Nora."
- Ep 19: "'You're never clumsy. That's the problem.'"
- Ep 22: "'No. You're not him. You're the one who checks.' That's the most Jun has ever said about it."
Cause: `ARCS.md` and the CAST row "Arc" require each member to move, so the episode pays the move with an epiphany.
Charles Baxter's word for these is "recently molded plastic" (source 9).

**3. Personality by tag, not by behaviour.**
- Ep 40: "Felix nodded at that, which from Felix was a ruling." Ep 39, the day before: "Tess replied with a thumbs up,
  which from Tess counted as a speech." The same formula two days running.
- Ep 0: "That's the most Felix sentence ever spoken."
- Ep 27: "Jun said 'yeah' first, which surprised everyone, Jun included."
- Ep 5: "I said it like a rule, and Felix nodded like it was one."
- Ep 19: "**Nora:** Lunch. What does it mean, in one line." (CAST: Nora says "So what?")
Cause: CAST's "Says" and "Never says" rows are used as a script, and the narrator keeps reminding us who each person is.

**4. The narrator says what people felt or what a moment meant.**
- Ep 40: "That hurt a bit. The pitch had been through six judges."
- Ep 0: "She looked tired. She also looked like she'd wanted exactly that."
- Ep 39: "She knew what every one of them meant. That was the problem."
- Ep 32: "He seemed like someone who'd been handed the interesting job."
- Ep 5: "Kai didn't reply with a cat. I noticed."
Cause: no trust that the reader will see it. Wiseman: "I don't like to be told what to think" (source 5).

**5. An AI office's vocabulary in sixteen-year-olds' mouths.**
- Ep 40, Kai: "Capital-protected notes have a floor. Portfolio insurance has a floor. It's in every textbook we read."
- Ep 38, Felix: "The numbers moved seven yesterday when the case lines went in."
- Ep 39 quotes the problem ("Rotation row eight. LS-16. Exhibit N. Deletion test on A33.") and the office went on
  writing R10, H9, I93 the same week.
Cause: the scene is a costume over a work log, and the log's words leak through.

**6. A relative on cue for every research idea.** Ep 33 has the gurdwara's two biscuit tins, Jun's coach's lap
splits and Nora's mum's hospital chart in one episode; Ep 38 has "My mum's vaccine fridge has a sheet taped to it",
Kai's weather page and Jun's medicine leaflets. Real findings came from reading; in the diary they arrive through
someone's grandmother. Cause: the 24 Oct note "find the image in Laura's own trade" hardened into a formula.

**7. Events that never happened.**
- Episodes 0-24 (27 Sep to 21 Oct) were written in one sitting on 23 Oct ("25 backfilled daily episodes", commit of
  23 Oct), before Keel existed. Their walk-outs and votes ("He actually walked to the door. He didn't open it.", Ep 0)
  never happened in any form.
- Ep 5 invents Kai's near mis-click ("nothing in this episode happened on WInS") as a twin of Caplet's real 7 Oct
  incident: the fiction borrows a real student's experience.
- Ep 0: "Last year a team at the regional final quoted a rule from a 2015 guidebook." An invented fact about the world.
- The Long Room's bunks, skylight and "room lag" dramatise how an AI does a week's work in an hour (Ep 24: "She went
  back to her bunk ... just looking at the skylight").

**8. Every scene resolves, and Keel comes out wise.** Most days close on a vote ("5-1", "6-0") and, until Ep 33, a
"Decisions" table with an owner for everything. Ep 39 turns Ray's criticism into a rule that Amara coins and the
office adopts in four lines ("Agreed. That goes on today's whole office, not just us."); Ep 40 turns his word rule
into Kai's insight and counts "Seven lines, no codes" as a win. Two days later Caplet set Keel's IPS aside as "too
unacceptably bad for us to work with" (Book 16). The diary had no way to show that, because it only knew how to show
Keel handling things well. Tracy Kidder's editor called the same trouble "the problem of goodness" (source 3).

**9. Lessons for the real team.** "What Caplet can take from today" (Ep 0-33) turns each day into a sermon for
Caplet. Lessons belong in `../EXPECTATIONS.md` and office notes, not in a diary.

### The three biggest causes
1. **The cast file writes the scenes.** Wounds, fears, catchphrases and arcs demand a payoff every day, so each
   episode supplies one: an epiphany, a kicker, a "which from Felix was a ruling" (causes 1-4).
2. **Events are made up to fit the story, and the story flatters Keel.** A backfilled history, a borrowed incident,
   a magic room and scenes that always resolve. The real record (an IPS rejected, codes nobody could follow, mistakes
   found a day later) was more interesting and never made it in (causes 7-9).
3. **The work log leaks into the mouths.** Codes, finance terms and relatives-as-analogies give six Year 11s the
   vocabulary of an AI office (causes 5-6).

## 2. Testing the idea: only real events, invented people

The idea: every event in an episode happened in the repo that day (a commit, a run-log row, a register row, a file,
a message from Caplet, a finding, a mistake caught); only the people are invented.

- **What it fixes.** Causes 7 and 8 go entirely: nothing can be borrowed or made up, and a day that didn't resolve
  can't be written as one that did. Most of cause 2 goes too: the only discoveries allowed are the ones in the record,
  and those are about the work, not about anyone's soul. It is Tracy Kidder's rule turned round. Kidder: "I don't
  invent dialogue, I don't invent the characters, I don't invent the settings" (source 1). Keel invents the
  characters and their talk, and nothing else.
- **What it costs.** A record of events is dead on the page. Joan Didion on a dutiful diary: "What is this business
  about 'shopping, typing piece, dinner with E, depressed'?" (source 8). A commit log in costume is worse than a commit
  log.
- **The answer.** Two layers, kept apart. The work layer is real, dated and sourced in a Record table. The people
  layer is invented, small and ordinary (who did it, what they said, food, the bus, a parent at dinner) and never
  carries a finding, number, decision, message, mistake or fix. The structure comes from the record (McPhee: it should
  "arise from within" the material; Wiseman: "the story is found in the editing"). The writer picks the few events
  that mattered and sets them side by side; the order carries the point.
- **Verdict.** Adopted. First tested on Ep 41 (Fri 6 to Sun 8 Nov). Its writer ran the test in section 6 (no fresh
  reader was available in that run; the first scheduled run should re-test it with one). The test cut: a one-line
  paragraph saying the office log had no rows on Friday or Saturday, placed after Caplet's message (true, but the
  loop had been paused since 5 Nov, so the placement implied a false cause); a dinner line built to land ("I said
  they handed it in"); a three-line setup and punchline between Kai and Jun; a "That is X." sentence; and the code
  "V62", now "version 62". (Its commit message and RUNLOG row said "cut after a fresh reader's test"; this paragraph
  is the accurate account.) **Re-test, Sun 8 Nov, first scheduled run (Ep 42):** a fresh reader failed Ep 41 on
  questions 2 (Friday ended on a line that set up Saturday's message) and 11 (a line put a Caplet student in a scene);
  both were cut and four smaller lines flattened. A second fresh read failed it again on questions 2 (the new last line
  of Friday did the same job) and 6 (book numbers a reader can't follow); those were cut, and one of the two lines
  quoted from Caplet's 11:06 message (praise, quoted the morning after the IPS was set aside) was dropped under
  question 9. A third read found three small hits (two entry endings that landed a beat, "the curve" unexplained);
  all three were flattened, not read a fourth time. Ep 42 failed its first read on questions 2 and 6 and passed the
  second with one small hit, then cut. Ep 43 (Mon 9 Nov) failed three fresh reads: 16 lines (entry
  endings, finance and office words), then five (four words a Year 11 wouldn't say, one run of short sentences), then
  one ("exhibits"); each was cut or put in plain words, and it was not read a fourth time.

## 3. Sources, and what each teaches (all accessed Sun 8 Nov 2026)

1. **Tracy Kidder, interview with Win McCormack** (Tin House #17, 2003), reposted by the McCormack Writing Center,
   https://mccormackwritingcenter.org/an-interview-with-tracy-kidder. *The Soul of a New Machine* follows a real team
   of engineers at Data General building a minicomputer. "I don't invent dialogue, I don't invent the characters, I don't invent the
   settings"; "I try not to tell a story in which people say anything that they didn't in fact say"; and on theme:
   "I didn't want to have to say that. I think the message or theme or whatever is stronger if it's implicit."
   *Teaches:* fix one layer as true and never cross it; leave the theme unsaid.
2. **Kidder and Richard Todd, *Good Prose*, twenty lines collected by Paige Williams**, Nieman Storyboard, 21 Feb
   2014, https://niemanstoryboard.org/2014/02/21/20-writing-and-editing-tips-from-tracy-kidder-richard-todd. "It is
   easy to try to purchase charm at the expense of honesty, but the ultimate charm lies in getting the face more
   right than pretty." "The honest nonfiction storyteller is a restrained illusionist." "It is always a good idea to
   construct a detailed timeline." "Minor characters encountered in the flow of a story can function as lenses on the
   main characters." *Teaches:* timeline first, from the log; charm never at the cost of the record; a parent or a
   shop customer can show a member better than a speech can.
3. **Kidder in conversation with Darcy Frey, part 2**, Nieman Storyboard, 22 Oct 2010,
   https://niemanstoryboard.org/2010/10/22/tracy-kidder-narrative-nonfiction-darcy-frey-harvard-writers-at-work-part-2.

   "believability and palatability": "we all tend to push away evidence of a virtuousness that exceeds our own."

10. **Writing teenagers.** Deborah Halverson, *Writing Young Adult Fiction For Dummies* cheat sheet,

## 4. The rules (from Ep 41)

1. **Nothing happens that didn't happen.** Every event (found, made, sent, received, broken, fixed, decided,
   counted) is in that day's record: `git log`, `../pm/RUNLOG.md`, `../INSTRUCTIONS.md`, `../pm/CHANGES.md`, or a file
   they name. The Record table at the end gives each one a source. If you can't source it, cut it.
2. **Only the people are invented.** Who did it, what they said, where they were, food, buses, families, school.
   The people layer never adds a finding, a number, a decision, a message, a mistake or a fix.
3. **Who does what goes by desk.** Nora: plans, dates, requests in. Felix: checks against sources, rules, personal
   data. Jun: numbers, curves, scripts. Kai: reviews, the case against. Amara: drafting, the client, voice. Tess:
   filing, counts, the archive. One member, one real action; no member does what the office didn't do.
4. **Real times.** Entries carry the Sydney time from the commit or run-log row. The Long Room's stretch goes in the
   header field and nowhere else: no bunks, clocks or room lag in the prose.
5. **No meaning on the page.** No epiphanies, morals, "what this taught us", "which from X meant Y", or narrator
   telling us what someone felt. The POV member may say once, plainly, what she did with a feeling (Didion). The order
   of events carries the point (Wiseman).
6. **No kicker.** The last line is the next ordinary thing, or what is still not done.
7. **Leave it open.** If the record shows something unfinished or still wrong, the episode says so. Corrections show
   as a single ~~strike~~ with the fix beside it, the way a lab notebook does, and only for corrections the record made.
8. **Keel's failures get the same space as its fixes.** Keel is never the hero of Caplet's story and never wronged by
   it. Caplet's words are quoted verbatim and short, or not at all; no Caplet student is described, imagined or given
   a feeling. Caplet's real events stay Caplet's: Keel never gets a twin of them.
9. **Talk like sixteen.** Short, loose sentences, mostly about the task or about themselves. No codes (H9, R10, I93,
   LS-14): say what the thing is. No finance terms a Year 11 wouldn't use out loud. No catchphrases from CAST. No
   relative brought in to explain finance unless that analogy is itself a real research finding of the day.
10. **No lessons for Caplet inside an episode.** The "What Caplet can take" and "Decisions" sections are retired;
    the Record table replaces them.

**In five lines:** (1) Every event is real and sourced; only the people are invented. (2) Who does what goes by desk,
at the real time. (3) No epiphanies, morals, narrated feelings or kicker endings; the order of events carries the
point. (4) Leave open what the record leaves open, and give Keel's failures the space of its fixes. (5) Teenagers talk
plainly, without codes or finance jargon; Caplet appears only in short verbatim quotes.

## 5. The episode form

File `YYYY-MM-DD_epNN.md` (the last day covered). The logbook builder (`../scripts/logbook/build_logbook.py`) parses
the first three blocks, so keep them exactly in this shape:

```
# FICTION. Episode NN: <plain title, a fact from the day, not a slogan>

**<Weekday d Month 2026>, Sydney · Point of view: <member> · Long Room: <none | light | standard | deep, and what for> · <next real deadline>**

> **Summary.** Four or five plain lines: what happened, in the order it happened. No adjectives about Keel.
> **Arcs:** none on purpose (`AUTHENTICITY.md`). **Record:** <n> events, each sourced at the end.

---

<Entries. A journal or scenes, timed from the log. 600-1,000 words; up to 1,400 for an episode covering several days.>

---

(Keel is a simulated team, AI-written. <One sentence: which layer is invented, which is real.>)

## Record
| Sydney time | What happened | Source |
```

Point of view rotates as before. A journal (Tess's, or the POV member's) suits the timed form best. Titles name a
fact ("Filed on Sunday"), never a lesson.

## 6. The corniness test

A fresh reader (a person, or one new agent given only this section and the episode, no repo) answers each question
with the line it points to. Then the writer cuts.

| # | Question | If yes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is there a line you could put on a poster, a mug or a slide? | Cut it or flatten it into an ordinary sentence |
| 2 | Does the last line of any entry or scene land a point? | Replace with the next ordinary thing, or end one sentence earlier |
| 3 | Does anyone realise something about themselves, the team or life? | Cut the realisation; keep the action before it |
| 4 | Does the narrator say what someone felt, or what a moment meant? | Keep only what a camera or the log would show |
| 5 | Is there a "which from X meant Y", a catchphrase, or a reminder of who someone is? | Cut |
| 6 | Would a sixteen-year-old say this sentence out loud to friends? Codes, finance terms, speeches? | Translate into what the thing is, or cut |
| 7 | Can you find every event in the Record table? (The writer checks this one.) | Cut the event |
| 8 | Does a scene end with everyone agreeing, where the record shows nothing settled? | Leave it open |
| 9 | Does Keel come out looking wise, or wronged? | State the failure as plainly as the fix |
| 10 | Is a family member used to explain an idea? | Cut, unless the analogy is the day's real finding |
| 11 | Is a real Caplet student described, paraphrased into a feeling, or shown in a scene? | Cut; quote verbatim or leave out |
| 12 | Em dashes; "not X, it's Y"; three short sentences in a row; a run of "That is X."; adverbs doing the work? | Rewrite |

**Pass:** no hits on questions 1-7 or 11, and at most two small ones elsewhere. Fail: cut, then test again.

## 7. The scheduled run (paste as the daily task; under 250 words)

> Write today's Keel episode under `keel/episodes/AUTHENTICITY.md`. Run late in the Sydney evening.
> 1. Read `keel/episodes/AUTHENTICITY.md`, `keel/CAST.md` (names, desks and homes only) and the last row of
>    `keel/episodes/INDEX.md`.
> 2. Gather today's record: `TZ=Australia/Sydney git log --since=midnight --date=format-local:'%a %d %H:%M'
>    --format='%ad %h %s%n%b'`, today's rows in `keel/pm/RUNLOG.md`, new rows in `keel/INSTRUCTIONS.md` and
>    `keel/pm/CHANGES.md`, and the files they name. List each event: Sydney time, what happened, source. If nothing
>    happened, add an INDEX row saying so and stop.
> 3. Pick the three to six events that mattered. Give each real action to the member whose desk owns it. Invent only
>    people: talk, food, travel, family, school. Never invent a finding, number, message, decision, mistake or fix.
> 4. Write 600-1,000 words in the file format of section 5: header, summary, the `> **Arcs:**` line, timed entries,
>    the label "(Keel is a simulated team, AI-written.)", and a Record table sourcing every event. Quote Caplet only
>    verbatim and briefly; never portray a Caplet student. No em dashes, codes, lessons or kicker endings.
> 5. Give one fresh reader agent only section 6 and the episode. Cut what fails; test again if more than two lines
>    failed.
> 6. Add the INDEX row, commit with the day's Keel work, and never commit personal data.
