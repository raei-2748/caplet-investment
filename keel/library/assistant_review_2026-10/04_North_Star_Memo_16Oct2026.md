## 2. The system: one idea, four jobs

**The core idea (the team words it):** Laura commits only money she already owns.

The word matters. The case calls the payments an "operating *commitment*" and uses "promise" in a negative sense
("Rather than promising one exact amount, she wants to communicate a credible range"). But the fixed WInS notes say
"her promised minimum" (VT) and "a minimum figure in 2031" (IBTM). Pick one stance and use it everywhere (§8, item 4).

How the one idea produces the whole plan:

| Commitment | Who relies on it | How certain it must be | What backs it | Name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ten $50k payments, 2033-42 | the residency and its artists | "a high degree of certainty" (case p.3) | Treasuries bought with the 2027 deposit, each maturing before its payment, held to maturity | roots |
| Bottom of the 2031 range | co-sponsors | credible, because overpromising costs her (case p.3) | Treasuries bought with the 2028 deposit, returning about $150k by the end of 2032 | roots (the floor) |
| Top of the range | co-sponsors | stated, not committed | half the stock fund's value in 2031; the 2033 gift is capped there | branch |
| Flexibility | Laura | whatever is left | the other half of the fund, plus the 2027 leftover | branch |

Each deliverable proves a different part of it:

| Deliverable | Its job | What it shows about the one idea |
|---|---|---|
| **TNA** (23 Oct) | The past: we acted on it | One trade per layer: IBTR (payments, *tested*), IBTM (floor, *refined*), VT (branch). This is already the suggested trio. |
| **IPS** (6 Nov) | The rules: we will always do it | Three rules plus one stated trade-off (§3) |
| **Final Report** (4 Dec) | The proof: it works, and what it gives Laura | Numbers, the range and its confidence, the co-sponsor draft, the WInS implementation review, what we learned |
| **Semifinal video** | The picture: six people explain it | One diagram; each student owns one part (all members must present, per the FAQ) |

## 3. Simple surface, deep underneath

| Layer | Reader | Time | What it holds |

**Rule:** something moves up a layer only if the reader needs it to agree with the decision.

### The three surface items
- **One fact (MODEL).** On the October curves the ten payments cost about $293k (6 Oct) to $295k (13 Oct): almost exactly
  her first $300,000 deposit. Readers are surprised, then see it was there all along. That is the "aha". It also
  answers "why buy in 2027?": that is when the price fits inside the first deposit. (Room left on 13 Oct: about 0.16
  percentage points of yield. Re-price before quoting, per the 15 Oct brief.)
- **One rule.** Commit only money she already owns.
- **One picture.** Roots and branch: the roots are what she owns; the branch can grow, but nothing is committed against it.

**A second "aha", for the floor (MODEL, illustrative; re-price).** The floor is simply her second deposit coming back.
At yields near 5%, roughly $118-122k of Treasuries bought in January 2028 returns about $150k by the end of 2032. The
other ~$30k is, in effect, the interest those Treasuries will earn, and it goes into the stock fund at the start. Keeping
the principal safe while putting the interest to work up front is how a principal-protected product works. Here it is
built from two allowed ingredients, because WInS bans options. Professionals will recognise the structure; a student
will get "you get your $150k back, and the interest gets a chance to grow".

### A rule budget for the IPS
IPS V5 asks the reader to hold about nine mechanisms: buying the latest payments first, the rate fallback, floor sizing,
the rounded-down quote, the half-the-fund top, the cap, a three-step shortfall order, income reinvestment, and
substituting an earlier Treasury. A suggested budget (content only; the team writes the words):
- **Rule 1.** Buy the payments first and hold them to maturity; the reserve shrinks as each payment is made.
- **Rule 2.** Commit to co-sponsors only what is owned: the bottom of the range is the bought floor, the top is the floor
  plus half the fund, and the gift is capped at the top.
- **Rule 3.** No trading before 2033 unless a holding disappears or Laura's goals change.
- **The trade-off.** Less stock means a smaller middle-case gift, in exchange for a much better bad case.

Move down to the Final Report: the latest-first buying order, the exact rounding, steps 2-3 of the shortfall order,
reinvestment details and the iBond/T-bond split.

### Where V5 buries its best idea
- The most original part, the range rule, sits in the **last** paragraph.
- The pitch opens with a claim nobody has verified ("co-sponsors who fund the building rarely pay to run it"; Tab 15
  already flags it). Open with a fact from the case or from our own numbers instead.
- The name is used but never explained.

### The test
Give the pitch to three people who have never seen our work, one of whom has done a university finance subject. After 60
seconds, can each say the idea back in one sentence? If not, it isn't simple yet. This beats any AI judge, because after
800 pages the team can't un-know what it knows (the "curse of knowledge").

## 4. Creativity: what is actually original

The name is good packaging. The creativity has to sit in a mechanism. Candidates, most original first:

1. **A range built to hold, not a range forecast.** Most teams will run a simulation and quote something like "an 85%
   chance the gift lands between A and B". Our gift rule (floor plus half the fund, capped at the announced top) means the
   gift lands inside the range unless the Treasuries fail to pay. That changes the confidence question from "how sure are
   we about markets?" to "how sure are we about US Treasuries?". The forecast moves inside the range: *where* she lands
   (median, bad case), which the Final Report shows. Laura holds a Wharton statistics degree (case p.1). Separating "will
   it land in the range?" from "where in the range?" is exactly the distinction a statistician respects.
   *Caveat:* IBTM's payout is not fixed, which is why the floor is quoted rounded down. Say "built to land inside", never
   "cannot miss".
2. **Buy it in 2027, not in 2033.** The case says the reserve is set aside in 2033. We buy it in 2027, because that is
   when its price fits inside the first deposit, and waiting would bet the payments on six years of markets. In the
   27 Sep model, the growth-first plan missed a payment in 3.2% of paths, 13.7% if the 2028 deposit were only $75k, and
   40.8% if it never came (MODEL, before the 6 Oct WInS-only rule; re-run before quoting). The insight here is timing.
3. **The payments don't depend on her future income.** The 2028 deposit comes from "publishing advances, speaking
   engagements, licensing, and other entrepreneurial ventures" (case p.2): income likely to shrink in the same bad years
   stocks fall (INFERENCE). The payments are bought with the 2027 deposit alone, so a missing 2028 deposit only reduces
   the facility gift. This is the human-capital idea, applied to this client.
4. **The floor works like seed money.** The case says a meaningful personal contribution "may signal that the residency is
   financially viable" (p.3). A floor already held in Treasuries is a signal co-sponsors can check rather than take on
   faith. Field evidence on seed money: raising announced seed money from 10% to 67% of a campaign goal raised giving
   almost sixfold (List & Lucking-Reiley 2002, VERIFIED). No source measures a bought floor specifically; the team's
   strategy notes already say that part is our own reasoning.
5. **The name ties to her own book.** Her graphic memoir is *Messy Roots* (case p.1); Root-and-Branch gives her residency
   tidy roots. Explain the link once, literally (an open item in COMPETITION.md §9). Two things to know: "root and
   branch" is a mainly British idiom meaning "thoroughly, completely" (Merriam-Webster, Oxford Learner's), so Australian
   and UK readers may hear that first. That is harmless and arguably helpful. Also settle one name: COMPETITION.md
   still says "Roots First".

**Where each one goes:** #1 in the IPS, because it *is* the policy. #2 and #3 as the "why" behind Rule 1. #4 and #5 in the
Final Report (introduction and co-sponsor draft). Don't put all five on the surface.

**Creative presentation (Final Report, video):** one recurring picture (a 2027-2042 timeline with the three layers),
used in the Final Report, the co-sponsor page and the video. The co-sponsor draft can present the floor as something
checkable, for example the Treasury holding that backs it: proof, not a promise.

## 6. The growth story: curate, don't decorate

### The strongest arc: two years, mostly the same students
On the two roster exports, four of this year's six names were also on last year's team. Last year: work out the return
the client "needs" (11-12% a year), chase it with 12 themed stocks and two themed ETFs, lose $5,400 on one rushed trade, and trade assets
outside the rules. This year: work out what the commitment *costs*, buy it, and spend the skill on what is left. That
meant 11 trades, each checked against this year's rules.

That is a real change in thinking, from "what return do we need?" to "what does the commitment cost?". Professionals
recognise it as the shift pension funds made from asset-only to liability-first investing. It is honest, it can be
checked, and it *is* the strategy.

Last year's report already *said* "clarity beats complexity", while presenting renamed sleeves, a three-stage process and
12 stocks. Stating a lesson is not showing it. This year, show it by being simple.

- Don't script dialogue. Say who argued what and what was decided.
- Be open about AI: what it did, what we checked, and one time it was wrong. Wharton requires the citation anyway, and
  owning it turns a risk into evidence of judgment.

## 8. Risks to this North Star (devil's advocate)

1. **Simple can read as thin.** The Final Report has to carry visible depth (tools, numbers, the WInS review) so the IPS
   can stay simple. "Portfolio Analysis" in the rubric asks for "effective use of investment concepts and tools".
2. **A ladder is not unique.** Many strong teams will buy bonds for the payments. Lead with what is ours (§4), not with
   "we built a ladder".
3. **Absolute words.** "Cannot miss" and "never below" overclaim, because IBTM's payout is not fixed. Use "built to" and
   the rounding.
4. **The word "promise".** The fixed VT note says "promised minimum"; the case uses "promise" negatively; the 14 Oct
   review advised removing "promise" from the IPS. Decide: either define it once ("the only thing promised is what is
   already owned") or keep it out of the IPS and explain "promised minimum" in the VT reflection. Don't leave it
   unaddressed.
5. **Depth the team can't explain.** The repo goes far deeper than any student can defend, and at the semis every member
   presents and takes questions. Give each student one concept (§5) and one question (§7), and run a teach-back test
   before 6 Nov.
6. **Voice.** V5 is AI-written (team comment, 13 Oct). Student-written text will be plainer, and that is fine. Don't
   "humanise" AI text; write fresh from an outline.
7. **Too much coherence.** Copying the IPS into the Final Report wastes it. Each deliverable has its own job.
8. **Hype.** Sales energy that reads as hype contradicts the plan's message. Aim for calm confidence: state the cost, and
   answer the best objection.
