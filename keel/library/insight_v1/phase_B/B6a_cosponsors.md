# B6a Co-sponsors and Philanthropy: the co-funder's due-diligence view

Agent: B6a, insight_v1 run, Phase B (question generation). Written 2026-09-27. Lens: "Co-sponsors & Philanthropy".
Starting angle (the other B6 agent starts elsewhere): **the co-funder's side of the table**. What does a foundation,
capital-campaign funder, public culture agency or accountant actually *do* with a founder's pledge: what counts, what
is discounted, what is written into their own grant conditions, and on what calendar? Seed questions extended: 6, 8, 9,
15 (v4 Part 6).

This file is AI-generated research for Team Caplet, for brainstorming only. It holds **no submission-ready prose**.
Where it says what a sentence or paragraph "must contain", that is a checklist; the six students write every word
(Wharton AI policy, R-W26/R-W49). No securities are recommended here. Laura's public professional record only; nothing
here suggests contacting her.

Status labels (brief section 3). Short forms: **VP** = VERIFIED-PRIMARY (read on the page by me on 2026-09-27; URL in
section 5), **VRF** = VERIFIED-REPO-FILE, **SNIP** = SNIPPET-UNVERIFIED, **ASM** = ASSUMPTION, **INT** = INTERPRETATION
(my reading, not a fact). R-, F-, SH-, BS- ids point to the Phase A files.

Terms used below (defined once):
- **Pledge / promise to give**: a promise to give money later. **Unconditional** = nothing has to happen first.
  **Conditional** = the money is owed only if a stated hurdle is cleared.
- **Lead gift / leadership gift / seed money**: the big early gifts a campaign secures before it asks the public.
- **Challenge gift**: in the economics papers used here, an *unconditional* lead gift announced to other donors.
- **Matching gift**: "I give $1 for each $1 others give, up to a cap" (a conditional promise).
- **Capital campaign**: fundraising for a building. **Quiet phase**: the period before the public announcement, when
  lead gifts are collected.
- **Gift chart**: a funder's table of how many gifts of each size a campaign expects.
- **Present value (PV)**: what money due in the future is worth today, after discounting at an interest rate.
- **Backstop**: a named reserve source that covers an expected-but-uncertain source of money if it fails.

1. **Professional funders count only the unconditional part of a pledge (Q1).** Kresge's capital-campaign guide counts
   "Written pledges or cash" (VP). The U.S. nonprofit accounting standard treats a statement that "specified an
   intention to give" as *not* a promise to give (FASB ASU 2018-08, example 958-605-55-51, VP). In the seed-money
   papers the effect is produced by a "challenge gift", defined as "an unconditional commitment by a donor" (Rondeau
   & List 2008, VP). **INT:** only the bought floor of Laura's 2031 range is a lead gift in any funder's books. The
   rest of the range is information, not money. That sets how the two ends of the range should be labelled.
2. **Co-sponsors may write her low end into their own conditions (Q2).** Grant agreements often say "we pay if the
   project raises X". A matching grant is "a common form of a conditional promise to give" (ASU 2018-08,
   958-605-55-17, VP). Kresge wants other sources "committed, imminent or backstopped" (VP). **INT:** if a co-sponsor
   writes "conditional on the founder contributing at least US$X", the bottom of Laura's range becomes someone
   else's contract trigger. A second, outside reason (beside R-C66) why the low end must be *bought*, not a model
   percentile.
3. **The experiments say: announce a firm lead gift; do not turn her upside into a match (Q3).** Huck & Rasul (2011,
   VP abstract): charities "maximize donations given by simply announcing the presence of a lead gift". Linear
   matching "partially crowd[s] out" donors' own gifts. Rondeau & List (2008, VP): "challenge gifts positively
   influence contributions in the field, but matching gifts do not". Karlan & List (2007, VP): a match raises
   response, but 3:1 and 2:1 add nothing over 1:1. A match would also tie Laura's 2033 amount to other donors'
   behaviour, which no team can model with a stated confidence.
4. **Capital funders test operating sustainability after the building is done (Q5, seed 6).** Kresge's first seven
   questions to any capital applicant include "Do you have a plan for sustaining support gained during the campaign
   after it concludes?" (VP). Andreoni (1998, VP abstract) models seed money as solving a *fixed-cost* problem. Laura's
   ten pre-funded operating years answer the building funder's worry before it is asked. This is new primary evidence
   for BS-17, and it explains the case's asymmetry (R-AN6) from the funder's side.
5. **With an 80% lock, the 2031 range is almost a point (Q7).** ASM illustration (my calculation, section 3): at the
   median 2031 sleeve, locking 80% in a 2-year Treasury leaves 2033 capacity at about **US$199k-218k (p5-p95)**, of

   comes close to the "one exact amount" the case steers away from (R-C67). The lock share (brief section 9) is

6. **The case itself stops the 2031 floor from being paid in early (Q10).** "She will neither add to nor withdraw from

## What this teaches

A promise is worth what the listener can count, not what the speaker hopes. Funders, accountants and the economists
who run fundraising experiments agree on one simple rule: only an unconditional commitment moves other people's money.
Everything else is information. That is why Laura's 2031 range should have a bought bottom, a clearly labelled top,
and exact words about which is which. Two further lessons:
- Know your listener's calendar and contracts. A co-sponsor may write your number into its own grant conditions and
  budget one year at a time.
- Evidence must fit the audience. A mail experiment with small donors does not prove what a foundation will do, but
  that foundation's own rulebook does.
