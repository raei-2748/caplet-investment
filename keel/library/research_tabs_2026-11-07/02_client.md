## 3. The insight our IPS rests on: her career is a stock she can't sell

Caplet's IPS says her book advances and speaking fees fall in a recession, so a share-heavy portfolio would double her risk. Here is the evidence behind that sentence.

### The theory
- Economists call a person's future earnings "human capital" and treat it as part of their wealth. Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992, Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control) showed that earnings shape the right portfolio ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=420291)).
- If earnings move with the stock market, the person should hold fewer shares, because their job is already a share-like holding. Viceira (1999 NBER paper w7409; Journal of Finance 2001) found that a link between income shocks and market returns "reduces the investor's willingness to hold the risky asset" ([NBER w7409](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7409/w7409.pdf)).
- Ibbotson, Milevsky, Chen and Zhu, Lifetime Financial Advice (CFA Institute Research Foundation, 2007), make this the practitioner's rule. They cite Heaton and Lucas (2000): wealthy households with "high and variable business income invest less in the stock market" ([CFA Institute PDF](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/-/media/documents/book/rf-publication/2007/rf-v2007-n1-4580-pdf.pdf)). Laura's creative business is exactly that kind of income.
- Benzoni, Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2007, Journal of Finance) show that over long horizons wages and share prices move together, which makes human capital more share-like than it looks year to year ([Chicago Fed working paper 2007-11](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/working-papers/2007/wp-11)).

### The evidence on author income
- The Authors Guild's 2023 survey: full-time authors earned a median $10,000 from books in 2022 and $20,000 in total. Graphic novelists did better: a median $15,000 from books and $25,000 in total. Most authors earn about half of their writing income from things other than book sales, such as speaking and teaching ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/key-takeaways-from-2023-author-income-survey/)). Laura is a bestseller, far above the median, but the shape is the same: lumpy advances plus event income.
- Our reading, not a measured fact: speaking and events are the most cyclical part, since event budgets are cut early in a downturn, and in 2020 in-person events stopped altogether.
- Book sales themselves are steadier than people think: US book sales fell about 1.5% in 2008 ([New York Times, May 2009](https://www.nytimes.com/2009/05/29/business/media/29books.html)). So the honest version of our claim is that her **new advances, licensing deals and speaking fees** are what shrink in a recession, not her backlist.

### Why it matters three times over
1. **Her 2028 deposit.** It comes from the most cyclical part of her income. That is why Caplet's IPS buys all ten payments from the 2027 money, starting with 2042, so a short 2028 deposit hurts the facility contribution, never a payment.
2. **Her co-sponsors.** Charitable giving falls in recessions too. US giving fell 2.4% in 2008 and 3.2% in 2009 (Giving USA 2010, as recorded in our Final Report plan; check the original before quoting). In a bad year she has less to give and so do the people she is asking.
3. **The bond market.** In a slump, yields usually fall, which makes the Treasuries dearer to buy. Our models found the three risks arrive together (see Numbers and Models, "risks that arrive together").

## 5. The risks to Laura, and how Caplet's IPS handles each

- **Yields fall before January 2027.** The ten Treasuries cost more. On the 5 Nov close they cost $302,163 at Treasury curve prices and $303,269 with the exact instruments we hold, both a little over $300,000 (MODEL; see Numbers and Models). The IPS answer: part of the 2028 deposit completes the set first.
- **The 2028 deposit comes in short.** Every dollar short comes off the facility contribution, not a payment.
- **Reinvestment.** The bonds' interest is reinvested. If rates fall for good, the set delivers less. The IPS covers a shortfall from her retained shares.
- **Inflation.** The payments are fixed in dollars, so inflation can't break them, but it shrinks what they buy. The IPS states 2.5% inflation cuts the floor's real value by about 12% by 2033.
- **Taiwan costs and the Taiwan dollar.** See section 6.
- **A US government default.** The one risk the plan can't hedge. The IPS names it.
- **Her credibility.** The range's bottom is money already owned in Treasuries, so a share crash can't push her below a number she has announced.

## 6. Taiwan

- **Building costs are rising fast right now.** Taiwan's construction cost index rose 7.18% in the year to October 2026 ([MacroMicro, DGBAS data](https://en.macromicro.me/charts/56409/taiwan-construction-cost-index)). The building-construction index stood at 116.86 in September 2026 against 100 in 2021 ([CEIC](https://www.ceicdata.com/en/taiwan/construction-cost-index-2021100/construction-cost-index-building-construction-bc)), about 2.8% a year over five years. Our earlier checks found Taiwan CPI at +2.04% a year in August 2026.
- **The currency.** Her portfolio is in US dollars; the building will be paid in New Taiwan dollars. USD/TWD was 31.82 on 18 Sep 2026, and its daily moves imply a yearly swing of about 4-6% (Keel's September check). A stronger Taiwan dollar makes the building dearer in US dollars. The IPS keeps half the share fund unpromised partly for this.
- **What we model and what we don't.** At 3.5% a year Taiwan building inflation, the 2042 payment buys about 60% of what $50,000 buys today (Keel model suite, M4). That affects how far her money goes, not whether a payment is made. The case keeps legal and visa questions out of scope. For context only: Taiwan's Employment Gold Card has a "culture and arts" route for foreign professionals ([Taiwan Gold Card](https://goldcard.nat.gov.tw/en/qualification/field-of-culture-and-arts/)).
- **Overruns are normal.** A study of US performing-arts buildings, Set in Stone (Woronkowicz and others, University of Chicago, 2012), found projects ended well over their first budgets (our notes say about 92%; check before quoting). That supports keeping money unpromised rather than giving everything in 2033.
