Ideas and Sources: the investing ideas behind our strategy
Written by Claude (Keel) on 7 Nov 2026, condensed from the Final Report plan (/mnt/project-files/final_report/ERUDITION_PLAN.md, section 3). Caplet's IPS uses many of these ideas without naming them; the Final Report can name and cite them. Saying what we took, what we refused and why is the strongest proof that we read widely and thought for ourselves.
1. Strategies we used, adapted or refused
Cash-flow matching (liability-driven investing). Start from what must be paid and buy assets that pay it on the day. Took: the whole Treasury ladder, one holding per payment. Source: Leibowitz (1986).
Immunisation. Match a liability's sensitivity to interest rates instead of its dates. Took: the insight that price risk and reinvestment risk pull against each other. Refused: bond funds that never mature (that rival missed a payment in 11-13% of model paths). Sources: Redington (1952); Fisher and Weil (1971).
Contingent immunisation. Keep a safety floor and let the surplus take risk. Took: floor plus surplus, the shape of the 2028 deposit. Refused: the trigger that switches to safety after losses; our split is fixed in advance, so nobody has to act in a crash. Source: Leibowitz and Weinberger (1982).
Capital-protected notes. A bond that returns your money plus an option on shares. Took: the idea that the floor's expected interest is the risk budget, so the share fund is roughly the floor's interest taken up front. Refused: the option itself; derivatives are banned and an option can expire worthless, while shares don't. Source: Leland and Rubinstein (1981).
Constant-proportion portfolio insurance (CPPI). Refused: it trades constantly and can fall through the floor in a crash. We chose buy-and-hold.
The endowment model. Spend a fixed percentage and hold shares forever. Refused for Laura: a percentage rule never runs dry, but a fixed-dollar payment can. Source: Swensen (2009).
Safe withdrawal rates and sequence risk. Withdrawals in a crash lock in losses. Took: no share ever pays a payment. Source: Bengen (1994).
Target-date glide path, three-year bucket, 60/40 balanced. Refused: each broke one of Laura's two promises in 22-36% of model paths.
Wait, then buy the bonds. Its median gift is the biggest, but $300,000 bought all ten payments in only one month in four since 1871. Source: Bodie (1995) on why shares are not safer in the long run.
Human capital. A career is an asset; if it behaves like shares, hold fewer shares. Took: the core of Caplet's IPS. Sources: Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992); Viceira (2001); Ibbotson, Milevsky, Chen and Zhu (2007); Benzoni, Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2007).
Goals-based investing. Separate pots for separate goals: payments, floor, shares. Refused: trusting the labels; we tested the pots together by deleting the shares in the model. Sources: Shefrin and Statman (2000); Das and others (2010).
Safety-first. Minimise the chance of falling below what you need. Took: our definition of "high certainty" as how often a payment falls short, and where it breaks. Source: Roy (1952).
Signalling and credible ranges. A promise is believed when it costs something, and an honest forecast comes as a range. Took: a bottom already owned in Treasuries. Sources: Spence (1973); Crawford and Sobel (1982).
Rules over discretion. A rule you can't break later is believed now. Took: no overrides in a downturn. Source: Kydland and Prescott (1977).
Bond escrows ("defeasance"). Public bond escrows are sized to pay "without reinvestment" and hand back any excess. Took: price the reinvestment gap as if nothing more were reinvested. Source: municipal escrow agreements and the GFOA's guidance.
Global diversification, low costs, trading less. Took: one world index fund and eleven trades. Refused: stock picking, sector funds and home bias. Sources: French and Poterba (1991); Sharpe (1991); Barber and Odean (2000).
Capital market assumptions. Took: a stated share return with no payment relying on it. Sources: J.P. Morgan 2027 Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions; Dimson, Marsh and Staunton (2002).
Model risk. Odds can be wrong and crashes cluster. Took: four model worlds including every month since 1871, and no conclusion resting on one engine. Sources: Knight (1921); Mandelbrot (1963); the Federal Reserve's model-risk guidance SR 11-7.
2. Ideas that look relevant and aren't
Annuities: with no mortality pool to share, an annuity for Laura is just a ladder plus a fee.
Insurance ruin models: they need random claims; Laura's payments are known to the dollar.
Debt service reserve accounts: they protect against revenue shortfalls, and her payments have no revenue behind them.
Rolldown strategies: held to maturity, rolldown is already in the purchase yield.
Black-Litterman: it blends views across many assets; we hold one share fund.
TIPS: individual TIPS are banned this year (ETFs on WInS are allowed), and the payments are fixed in dollars anyway, so TIPS would hedge an inflation risk the payments don't carry.
Foreign shares in VT as a Taiwan-dollar hedge: worth a few hundred dollars once sized, and never why we chose VT.
A Taiwan share fund (EWT, raised by the team on 5 Oct): one country's shares, heavily weighted to a few chipmakers. Our reasoning: any Taiwan-dollar hedge it gives is small next to its share risk.
3. Combinations that are ours
A protected note without the option: floor plus shares, funded by the floor's own interest, timed around Laura's income risk.
Owned before it is said: the bottom of the 2031 range is already in Treasuries before Laura names it. Fundraisers do the same thing when they secure most of a campaign's goal before announcing it (see Co-sponsor Brief).
Laura's own money absorbs a shortfall first: her spare shares take a hit before the gift does, and the gift before any payment, so the ten payments sit at the top of the queue.
One storm, three holes: a slump makes bonds dearer, widens the reinvestment gap and shrinks her 2028 income at the same time, so the plan is built so that only the gift feels it.
