Deep research: analysis methods and charts
Key findings
Central banks are moving away from fan charts on their own. Bernanke's April 2024 review of the Bank of England said its fan charts "have weak conceptual foundations … and receive little attention from the public. They should be eliminated." He recommended regularly publishing alternative scenarios to "de-emphasise the central forecast". [Secondary: MNI, quoting the review. CNBC and City AM also covered it.] On 16 May 2025 (published 19 May) Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli said scenarios "can also speak to a wider audience than fan charts and similar tools, which can be quite specialist and technical". She said the Bank would "explicitly focus less on a central forecast". She also said fan charts "have their advocates and are a powerful visual representation of uncertainty", but they have limits in showing how and why outcomes differ. [Secondary: Mondo Visione transcript of the speech] I did not reach the BoE's own page; its URL returned 404.
Pension actuaries use four named ways to test risk. ASOP 51 (effective 1 Nov 2018) lists four:
scenario tests
stress tests
sensitivity tests (change one assumption)
stochastic modelling (thousands of random futures)
It does not require numerical tests. [Secondary: Milliman; WA Office of the State Actuary. The ASB's own text was not read.]
Pension plans must now publish what their promises cost at low-default-risk yields. Revised ASOP 4 requires public pension funding valuations to disclose a "Low-Default-Risk Obligation Measure" (LDROM). Per the ASB, this applies to actuarial reports issued on or after 15 Feb 2023, with a further condition on the measurement date. The LDROM values benefits using yields on low-default-risk bonds. It shows what the liability would be if the plan held only such bonds. [Verified: ASB page snippet; Segal, July 2022] I did not recheck at source that the standard names US Treasury yields as an example. [Unconfirmed] The standards board says the LDROM is not meant to be the "right" liability measure. [Verified: NIRS fact sheet quoting the ASB]
Pension funds measure risk against the promise, not on its own. "Surplus" is assets minus liabilities, and "surplus risk" is how much that gap swings. [Secondary: Cambridge Associates, Capital Group] Kroon, Wouters and de Carvalho argue that the funding ratio (assets divided by liabilities) is a better yardstick than surplus. [Snippet] My attempt to read SSGA's page on the interest-rate "hedge ratio" returned no article text.
Defeasance escrows are checked by an independent arithmetic test. A "Verification Agent" checks the mathematical accuracy of the escrow's cash flows that will repay the refunded bonds. It also checks the yields and the escrow investments. [Verified: NABL glossary. NABL does not say the agent is a CPA, so that word is removed.] State filings suggest these reports say they make no claim that the assumptions will hold. [Snippet only, unconfirmed: Wisconsin DOA filings]
Kitces: talk to clients about dollars, not odds. "Probability of adjustment" improves on "probability of failure". But it "can still greatly underestimate" how often spending is actually cut, especially under dynamic spending rules, because retirees cannot know which iteration they are on. The article concludes that "outcomes, not probabilities, are what matter to clients". It shows advice given as dollar guardrails rather than a probability. [Verified: Tharp, Kitces.com, 2022]
The textbook Monte Carlo is overconfident; history-based versions do better. Kitces.com (April 2023) backtested a simplified persona: a 60/40 portfolio over 20 years, with forecasts dated across 2002 to 2022.
Traditional Monte Carlo was markedly overconfident above 50%, "particularly between 70% and 97%".
Of 100 clients who targeted 95%, about 20 would have been overspending, not the expected 5.
The Historical model had "very tight calibration, especially from 65% to 100%". It still showed some overconfidence above 50%, but much less.
The Historical and Regime-Based models had Brier scores about 25% lower than Traditional Monte Carlo.
[Verified: Fitzpatrick and Tharp, Kitces.com, April 2023. The exact day was not confirmed.]
Endowments report how often spending is cut and by how much. Cambridge Associates (Kennedy and Majeski, 1994) argued that risk modelling should focus on downside variability over the institution's own horizon. The right measures are "shortfall risk" (the chance spending must be cut) and "shortfall severity" (the average annual, worst one-year and worst cumulative decline in real spending). Their example covers 1946 to 1993: a fund 70% in the S&P 500 and 30% in fixed income, spending 5% of a three-year moving average. It had to cut real spending in 20 of the 47 years (43%), and by more than 5% in 8% of the years. [Verified: CA PDF]
CFP Board rules on assumptions and alternatives. On Step 2 ("Identifying Potential Goals"), a planner "must discuss with the Client, and apply, reasonable assumptions and estimates", such as inflation rates and investment returns. On Step 3 ("Analyzing Potential Alternative Courses of Action"), "Where appropriate", a planner must analyse alternatives. Documentation is a principles-based requirement: the planner must act prudently in documenting "as the facts and circumstances require". The bulletin lists items a planner "may consider documenting". [Verified: CFP Board, "Focus on Ethics", 28 Feb 2019. The earlier labels "C 2.1" and "C 3.2" do not appear on that page, and "writing these down is optional" overstated it.]
US securities rules say what a probability tool must disclose. FINRA Rule 2214 requires a firm to describe "the criteria and methodology used, including the investment analysis tool's limitations and key assumptions". It must explain that "results may vary with each use and over time". It must display that projections "are hypothetical in nature, do not reflect actual investment results and are not guarantees of future results." [Verified: FINRA rulebook]
An independent 2026 benchmark puts share returns below Caplet's figure. The FP Canada / Institute of Financial Planning guidelines (16 Apr 2026, for projections of 10 years or more) give:

Asset or measure | Return
Canadian shares | 6.3%
US shares | 6.4%
International developed shares | 6.6%
Emerging-market shares | 7.5%
Fixed income | 3.2%
Short-term | 2.4%
Inflation | 2.1%
For projections under 10 years, planners may use the actual rates on fixed-term investments held to maturity, and dividend yields on equities. [Verified: FP Canada release] These are guidelines for Canadian planners. The release does not say whether they are before or after fees, or what currency basis they use.
Goals-based methods judge a plan by its chance of reaching each goal, not by average return. Das, Ostrov, Radhakrishnan and Srivastav, "Dynamic portfolio allocation in goals-based wealth management" (Computational Management Science 17(4), pp. 613–640, 2020), maximise the chance of reaching a target wealth. They report that their method "soundly beats the performance of target date funds in attaining investors' goals". [Verified: IDEAS/RePEc abstract; the PDF itself returned 401] Chhabra, "Beyond Markowitz" (Journal of Wealth Management 7(4), Spring 2005, pp. 8–34), says risk allocation should come before asset allocation. A protected portion earns below-market returns so another portion can take "aspirational" risk. [Citation verified: PMR/SSRN listings. Content is secondary: CFA Institute blog, EDHEC.] I did not read any Brunel source.

Five analyses for the Final Report, at most one chart or table each.
"Are my ten payments really paid for?" A funding-ratio line plus a sufficiency table. [Portfolio Analysis, Investment Strategy] Goes in the operating-reserve section.
Funding ratio = money set aside for the payments ÷ cost of the ten payments at the Treasury curve (the LDROM idea, in plain words).
On the doc's 5 Nov figures, the $300,000 2027 deposit against a $302,163 curve cost is about 99.3% before the 2028 top-up. Against the exact-instrument cost of $303,269 it is about 98.9%. [Inference: my arithmetic on Numbers and Models §2. The numerator is the deposit, not the market value of bonds already held. Once the bonds are held, their value and the cost move together and the ratio should sit near 100%.]
Chart the ratio weekly through the WInS period. Assets and cost should move together, which turns the October "book moved with what it owes" point into a picture.
Add a defeasance-style table: holding, maturity, value at maturity, payment, surplus or gap. Show it under base reinvestment and under zero reinvestment.
Data: the dated Treasury curve, WInS holdings and iBond yields, on one engine.
Line to use: Caplet's plan is the rare case where the funding liability is the low-default-risk (LDROM-style) measure, because it holds the bonds.
"What does 'high degree of certainty' mean, exactly?" A table of shortfall chance and shortfall size. [Portfolio Analysis, Client Knowledge] Goes where the case asks us to define certainty (Case p.3: teams "should also define what they consider a high degree of funding certainty, explain how they evaluated that level of certainty").

State two numbers separately:
the chance the 2033 gift lands inside the range. The case asks for exactly this: "state how confident they are that her 2033 contribution will fall within that range" (Case p.3).
the chance it reaches the top (the doc's "three in four").
"If markets go badly, what changes for me?" A dollar waterfall of who absorbs the loss. [Client Knowledge, Portfolio Analysis] Goes in the facility contribution / flexibility section.
Use three stresses:
shares fall 40% in 2032;
yields fall 0.5 point before 2027;
the 2028 deposit is $75k.
For each, show in dollars:
how much of the unpromised half of VT shrinks;
how far the gift falls toward the floor;
that the payments change by $0.
This is Kitces's "outcomes, not probabilities" applied to the order of losses the doc already describes. It also answers the 2023–24 judges' point about how the client feels in a downturn. The case also asks how "favorable and unfavorable market outcomes" affect the amount (Case p.3).
"Which assumption matters most?" A tornado chart of one-at-a-time sensitivities. [Portfolio Analysis; the assumptions list on Case p.4] Goes in an assumptions box or appendix.
Vary one assumption at a time:
share return at about 6.5% (my estimate of a world blend of the FP Canada figures), 7% and 7.5%;
yields ±0.5 point;
2028 deposit at $75k, $112.5k and $150k;
reinvestment −2 points;
Taiwan cost inflation at 2.5% and 3.5%.
Measure the effect on the floor, the median gift and any payment gap.
Close it with a three-line disclosure modelled on FINRA 2214: method, limitations and key assumptions; "results may vary with each use and over time"; hypothetical and not guaranteed. This mirrors ASOP 51 sensitivity testing and the CFP Step 2 duty to discuss and apply reasonable assumptions.
