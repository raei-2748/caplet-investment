# E03 · In the manner of Matt Levine: "The bank's favourite product, home-made"

> Exercise, AI DRAFT (Claude, 27 Oct 2026). Practice, not IPS text, not for submission. Brief: explain the mechanism
> step by step with whose money goes where, before any jargon; let the plain version be the joke.
> Facts: D4's thesis (a capital-protected note minus the bank); Lehman principal-protected notes (2008).

Here is a product banks like to sell. You give the bank $100. The bank promises that in five years you get at least your $100 back, plus some share of whatever the stock market did. Heads you win, tails you get your money back. Who could object?

How the bank does it is not a secret. It takes most of your $100 – say $80, depending on rates – and buys something that will grow back to $100 in five years. Then it takes most of what's left and buys an option on the stock market. And it keeps a bit for itself, because it is a bank, and that is what the bit is for. The something-that-grows-back-to-$100 is usually the bank's own IOU, which is fine right up until it isn't. In 2008 a lot of people discovered they owned principal-protected notes issued by Lehman Brothers, whose principal turned out to be protected by Lehman Brothers.

Laura's plan does the same trick with the parts swapped. The thing that grows back to the whole is a December 2032 Treasury fund, not a bank's IOU. The option is replaced by the stocks themselves – one world index fund, bought with roughly the interest the Treasuries are expected to earn. And nobody keeps a bit. If the stock market goes to zero, which would be a strange world but not a strange sentence, the floor still pays out its matured Treasuries. If the market booms, Laura keeps less of the boom than an option would have handed her, and that is the price of not paying a bank to be clever on her behalf.

The odd thing is how little cleverness it takes. This is about the simplest structured product there is, and you can build it with two tickers and a calculator. Mostly what the bank adds is a brochure.

## Critique
- **Worked:** the reader understands a capital-protected note by the end of paragraph two without having been told
  the term. That is the cure for Genius Lab prose: mechanism first, label later (or never). "Protected by Lehman
  Brothers" is a dry joke that is also the whole risk argument.
- **Worked:** "a strange world but not a strange sentence" is a Levine-style aside that admits the extreme case is
  silly while still answering it.
- **Still plastic:** "Who could object?" is a rhetorical question answered by the rest of the piece; fine once.
  The last paragraph drifts toward smugness ("Mostly what the bank adds is a brochure"); banks also add tax wrappers
  and convenience, which a judge may know. Make it one sentence or cut it.
- **Accuracy:** "$80" is right for about 4.5-5% over five years. Never call the floor "guaranteed": it is matured
  Treasuries inside a fund (near-ironclad held to the December close; BIBLE rule 6).
