# E04 · Sell-side note (Goldman / Morgan Stanley / J.P. Morgan shape): "Maturity, underrated"

> Exercise, AI DRAFT (Claude, 27 Oct 2026). Practice, not IPS text, not for submission. Brief: the call in the first
> line, three reasons, what would change our mind, risks as scenarios with a number. Cembalest-style pun title.
> Numbers: 22 Oct close headroom (Window, 26 Oct); ~$21k reinvestment (15 Oct MODEL).

**Bottom line: own the calendar, not the curve.** For a client who owes fixed dollars on fixed dates, the right Treasury exposure is a set of maturities, one per payment, held to the end. A bond fund with a duration that never arrives anywhere is the wrong tool, however cheap. We would buy all ten of Laura's payments, with money she already has, before a single share.

**Why.** First, the debt has dates and most bond funds don't. A fund like TLT rolls a basket of long bonds forever, so its price on the morning a payment falls due is whatever rates say that morning. A Treasury held to maturity, or an iBonds fund held to its December close, walks toward its final value as the date approaches. For ten known dates, convergence beats correlation.

Second, the deposit that would fund the risk is the deposit most exposed to it. Laura's 2028 money comes from book advances and speaking fees, which tend to shrink in the same slumps that drag yields down and make bonds dearer. So the money she already holds buys the payments, and the money she is still earning pays for the hopes.

Third, inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more. Laura's payments are fixed in dollars, so nominal Treasuries match them exactly, and an inflation-linked bond would insure her against a risk she does not carry (and is banned in this competition anyway).

**What would change our mind.** A payment schedule tied to costs in Taiwan dollars. The case fixes each payment at $50,000, so it isn't.

**Risks to the view.** Rates fall before January: the ladder costs more, and on the 22 October close it fit under $300,000 by about $1,100, so any gap becomes the 2028 deposit's first call. Rates fall and stay down: coupons are reinvested at less than we assumed, and a permanent two-point fall costs about $21,000 over ten years. Stocks have a great decade: the plan looks timid. That last one is a cost we chose.
