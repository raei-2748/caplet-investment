# E02 · In the manner of Warren Buffett: "The envelope and the cut-up cheque"

> Exercise, AI DRAFT (Claude, 27 Oct 2026). Practice, not IPS text, not for submission. Brief: confess one real
> mistake early and specifically, explain a mechanism with a homely image, shrink the big number to a human one.
> Facts: the STRIPS-based plan (insight_v1, 27-28 Sep) was superseded by Wharton's 6 Oct email (WInS-listed
> securities only); the ~$21k reinvestment figure is the 15 Oct MODEL estimate.

We made our best mistake early. In late September we built Laura's plan out of Treasury STRIPS – bonds with the coupons torn off, so that each one is a single sum on a single date. For a client who owes ten single sums on ten single dates, they were close to perfect: ten payments, ten sealed envelopes, nothing to reinvest and nothing left to chance. Then, on 6 October, Wharton told every team that Laura's plan could use only what WInS lists. WInS does not list STRIPS. A perfect plan you can't buy is a daydream with a spreadsheet attached.

So we rebuilt it with ordinary Treasuries and iBonds funds, and swapped the sealed envelopes for something messier. An ordinary Treasury is the same postdated cheque, cut into pieces and posted to you twice a year as coupons, with the biggest piece arriving last. Somebody has to keep the small pieces until January, and whatever they earn while they wait is a guess we made in October. If rates fall and stay down, the pieces earn less than we assumed and a cheque arrives a little light. On our 15 October numbers, a two-point fall that never reverses costs about $21,000 across the ten years – a little under half of one cheque.

We would rather have had the envelopes. But a little under half a cheque is a risk you can see, measure and set money aside for, and we know exactly where it came from, because we made it ourselves the day we gave the envelopes up.
