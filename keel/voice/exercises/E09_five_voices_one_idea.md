# E09 · One idea, five voices, then ours: the 2031 range

> Exercise, AI DRAFT (Claude, 27 Oct 2026). Practice, not IPS text, not for submission. The idea is D4's range rule:
> the bottom is the December 2032 Treasury fund's projected payout, rounded down; the top adds half of Laura's stocks,
> valued on the day she announces it. Each voice gets about 70 words. Then the convergence.

**Marks.** Elroy Dimson's definition is that risk means more things can happen than will happen. A single number for 2031 pretends that only one thing will. So Laura names a range. The bottom is money she already owns – a Treasury fund that matures at the end of 2032. The top adds half of whatever her stocks are worth that morning. We would rather give a co-sponsor the truth in two numbers than a hope in one.

**Levine.** Here is how you promise money you don't have yet: you don't. You promise the money you do have – a Treasury fund that turns into cash at the end of 2032 – and then you say "plus some more", and you define "some more" so precisely that nobody can accuse you of inventing it: half the stocks, priced on the day. It is a promise engineered to be unbreakable, which is a strange thing to engineer and a good one.

**Buffett.** In 2031 Laura will be asked how much she can give. We'd like her to answer like a farmer, not a forecaster: here is the field I own, and here is roughly what it should grow. The field is a Treasury fund that matures at the end of 2032. The crop is half her stocks, counted that morning. A farmer who promises the field never disappoints anybody. One who promises the harvest occasionally has to explain the weather.

**Ahaan.** A range is a peculiar thing to promise. Too narrow, and it is a number in disguise – one bad year from becoming a broken promise. Too wide, and it is not a promise at all, merely a shrug with a dollar sign. Ours is anchored at the bottom by money Laura already owns, and at the top by half of what her stocks are worth on the day she speaks. The bottom is a fact. The top is a hope – but a hope with a formula, which is about as honest as hope gets.

**Sell-side.** We set the 2031 range by rule, not forecast. Bottom: the projected payout of the December 2032 Treasury fund, rounded down. Top: the bottom plus 50% of the equity sleeve, marked on announcement day. Key risk: a two-year equity drawdown after 2031 pulls the delivered gift toward the bottom. The bottom itself carries no equity risk.

**Ours (the convergence).** A single number for 2031 would be a forecast dressed as a promise, and forecasts have a habit of being wrong in public. So Laura names a range, and both ends are rules rather than guesses. The bottom is money she already owns – a Treasury fund that matures at the end of 2032, its payout rounded down. The top adds half of whatever her stocks are worth on the morning she announces it. The bottom is a fact. The top is a hope with a formula attached, which is about as honest as hope gets.

## Critique
- **What each voice gave the convergence:** Ahaan's turn ("a hope with a formula ... about as honest as hope gets")
  and his rhythm; Marks's principle (one number pretends only one future), made concrete instead of quoted; the
  sell-side's precision (rounded down; valued on the day); Levine's plainness (no "sleeve", no "marked"). Buffett's
  farmer is the best image, but a second image would fight Caplet XR, so it goes to the Final Report or nowhere.
- **Why this is the method:** writing the same idea five ways shows which sentences are *ours* (the ones that
  survive the cross-examination) and which were only a writer's habits.
- **Still plastic:** "a forecast dressed as a promise" is one of those "X dressed as Y" constructions that every AI
  loves. It's true here; use it once in the whole Final Report, no more.
- **Accuracy:** "the bottom carries no equity risk" holds only if the coverage rule (E08, L-10) never takes from the
  bottom; under D1a the floor is the *last* source for a coupon gap, so say "almost none" or name the rule.
