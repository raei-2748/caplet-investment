# E01 · In the manner of Howard Marks: "Contract and mood"

> Exercise, AI DRAFT (Claude, 27 Oct 2026). Practice on Caplet's case, not IPS text, not for submission. Brief: a
> client memo built on one principle, an adage used and then turned, a self-interrogation, a plain closing rule.

There are two ways to be paid by a market. You can be owed the money, or you can hope for it.

A bond is the first kind. Somebody has signed a promise to hand you a stated sum on a stated day, and the only question left is whether they will keep it – which, when the somebody is the United States Treasury, is about as settled as questions in finance get. A share is the second kind. It pays whatever the business earns plus whatever the next buyer is in the mood to offer, and the mood is the part that moves.

Neither is better. They answer different questions, and a surprising number of famous blow-ups come from asking one of them the other's question. Silicon Valley Bank held ten-year contracts against deposits that could walk out on any afternoon, and in March 2023 they did.

Laura has both kinds of question. Ten of them are bills – $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars, which nobody else is allowed to help pay. Those are contract questions, so we answer them with contracts: a Treasury, or a Treasury fund that turns itself into cash, maturing before each January. The other question is how much she can give the facility, which has no due date and no fixed size. That is a mood question, and we let the mood answer it, through one fund that owns roughly 10,000 companies.

What could go wrong? We count three things.

If yields fall before the bonds are bought, the contracts cost more, and the gap comes out of the 2028 deposit before anything else is bought with it. A contract fixed in dollars says nothing about what those dollars will buy; but Laura's bills are fixed in dollars too, so the mismatch never reaches a cheque. And in a boom, a Treasury is the dullest thing she could own. We accept that. Nobody has ever missed a payment because a bond was dull.

There is an old line that the worst loans are made in the best of times. Its mirror is that the worst bond ladders are taken apart in the best of times – after five good years for stocks, when the Treasuries look like money left on the table. So the rule for that day is written now, while nobody is tempted: the contracts are not for sale.

## Critique (what worked, what is still plastic)
- **Worked:** one principle (contract vs mood) carries the whole memo, and every holding is explained by it, so the
  reader learns what the plan *believes* before what it owns. SVB is a real case doing real work (a date mismatch),
  not a name-drop. The adage is turned, Marks-style, into a rule for a future day.
- **Worked:** "Nobody has ever missed a payment because a bond was dull" is the one kicker, mid-memo, and it's true.
- **Still plastic:** "Neither is better" is an AI reflex (false balance). Marks would have said which one he loves.
  The three risks are tidy in a way that reads assembled; Marks lets one risk get a whole paragraph.
- **Judge test:** the opening pair of sentences is the strongest thing in this study. Candidate for the Final Report's
  strategy section, re-checked by the Courtroom first.
