# What the reading adds up to (synthesis 1, after 100 pieces, 27 Oct 2026)

AI-generated (Claude Code) voice study, not for submission. Built from `LOG.md` entries 001-100. Each point gives the
entries it rests on, so it can be checked. Ray's brief: read for **voice, tone, rhythm and structure**, insights
second; and (27 Oct) write for **Shark Tank investors, not examiners**. The emotion moves are now section 7 of
`../06_voice_guide.md`.

## 1. Where the voice lives (it is not in the sentences)
1. **Opinions, repeated with patience.** Marks has written the same four or five beliefs for 35 years (028-039,
   055-062) and Buffett opens every letter with the dullest sentence in finance (072-076). Their voices are their
   positions. Caplet's seven beliefs (guide section 1) are the right start; they must show up in every paragraph.
2. **Self-mockery buys bluntness.** Marks's wife and his repetitiveness (061), Buffett's "monumentally stupid
   decision" (072), Asness's rejected paper (046), Keynes's dentists (077), Graham's "lump of rock" (022). Every writer
   who is rude about other people's ideas is rude about himself first.
3. **Brackets that talk back.** Asness ("uh ok, boomers"), Damodaran ("an academic code word for really small"),
   Cembalest ("warning: tech jargon overload ahead"), Armstrong ("ha!"). The bracket is where a person interrupts
   their own formal sentence. In the IPS: one bracket to translate a technical word into five plain ones.
4. **Two registers on one page.** Cembalest's body is sober and his titles and footnotes are jokes (091-094); Asness's
   footnotes argue with his main text (047). The Final Report can do this; the IPS can't (no footnotes), so its one
   bracket does the job.

## 3. Endings
- **The best ends land on a thing, an echo or a test:** ankle weights coming off (027), "This is water." (078), "What
  can't you say? And why?" (025), a T-bone with hash browns (074), the order "good until canceled!" (097).
- **The worst ends summarise what the writer learned:** two of three Hopkins essays that *won* end on "challenges as
  opportunities for growth" (089, 090). Plastic survives even in winning essays, and always at the end.
- Rule for Caplet: the last sentence is a thing or a decision, never a lesson.

## 4. How the best writers handle the other side
State it at its strongest, in its own words, then answer: Asness (045, 048), Damodaran (096), John Locke winners
(080-084), Marks (038). Two refinements worth stealing:
- **"What I am not claiming"** before the reader can object (083).
- **Quote the opponent's case as a numbered list, then answer each step** (048).

## 5. Feeling (Ray's Shark Tank point): see guide section 7
Six moves: put the reader somewhere and never name the feeling; a promise with a name on it; accept a cost in public;
a number that measures a loss; dread, then relief; the reluctant narrator.

## 6. What not to copy
- **Pitch-deck checklists** (065): advice about telling stories that tells none.
- **John Locke formality** (080-087): fair, signposted, citation-heavy. Fairness yes; "Firstly ... Secondly" no.
- **Housel's exact lines** (050-054): too well known; a judge who reads him will hear the borrowing. Shapes are free.

## 7. Concrete things for Caplet's IPS and Final Report (to try in the Writing Room, not yet adopted)
1. **The ten cheques.** The payments are promises with names on them (F2, from King 070). "None of them bounces."
2. **"We buy to keep."** Buffett's line (073) is Laura's ladder exactly: no exit strategy, because the exit is the date.
3. **The bank would sell her the same promise with a fee.** Asness on buffer funds (048): protection paid for with
   capped upside, option costs and fees. Caplet's version builds it at home and keeps the upside.
4. **Liquid to whom?** Marks (061): an asset is not liquid or illiquid in itself. Laura's rungs are illiquid only to
   someone who wants the money before its date, and she doesn't.
5. **Two feelings, two clocks.** Housel's shape (054): scared for the near years, brave for the far ones. Use the
   shape, never his line.
6. **The downgrade that didn't move yields** (095): a cleaner way to say why "US default" is the residual risk and why
   it barely matters to a ladder held to the date.

## Synthesis 2 (after 186 pieces, 27-28 Oct night)
New since synthesis 1 (`LOG.md` 166-186): two inaugurals (Lincoln, Kennedy), Bagehot ch. III-IV, four 2020 John Locke
first prizes, five Damodaran posts from 2025, three 2028 Hopkins essays, Stripe, Cembalest,
O'Leary and a Shark Tank teardown. Fourteen further pieces (Zuckerberg's S-1 letter among them) were re-reads of earlier entries (logged separately, not
counted); where a re-read sharpened a point, its original number is cited.

1. **Where the bad news sits.** Lincoln puts it in the middle, never first or last (166); Kennedy turns it into a
   privilege ("a long twilight struggle", 169); Zuckerberg warns investors in one plain sentence and then explains
   (071, re-read). Synthesis 1 said "bad news first and smallest"; the better rule is **bad news early, small and in the
   middle, with the hope last**. The IPS's limit sentence belongs in its middle third.
2. **What AI can't copy is the working.** Damodaran was impersonated by an AI scam that copied his face and phrasing
   but not his reasoning or his long-held views (184). Voice is a consistent philosophy with its working shown, not a
   style. This is Book 9's "logic" route arrived at from the other side: show the causal chain, not the conclusion.
3. **Endings.** Two of the Hopkins winners end on "challenges as opportunities for growth" or close to it (089,
   090): plastic survives in winning work, and always at the end. The best endings in this batch are short and
   concrete: "This is the task of ethics." (173), "I am sorry!" (174), and the self-harm essay's "If it does not, it
   can be restrained." (084). Caplet's IPS should end on a test someone else could run, or on one thing.
4. **Openings.** Two new shapes worth a draft each: the **withheld reveal** ("Picture a country...", named a
   paragraph later, 170) and the **verbless list** ("Unicorns, rainbows, pink, and glitter.", 181). Both make a
   reader lean in before they know the subject.
5. **Emotion moves, ranked.** F3 (cost in public) and F6 (self-mockery) are the commonest; F2 and F4 the rarest and
   the most remembered. Ranking and use are in guide section 8.
6. **Pitches.** O'Leary: attention in the first seconds, the core in about 90 seconds (177). A pitch can win the room
   and still be wrong (178). Caplet's 50 words must be true in 2042, not just in the room.
7. **Counter-examples kept.** The John Locke winners that open on a dictionary definition (085) or end on a moral
   (172) won on argument. Fairness and structure: yes. Their openings: no.

**Investing finds (in the idea bank as FRB-17 to FRB-21):** risk-free is a horizon, not an asset (130, re-read);
dated cheques can't wait on stocks, since a 1929 buyer was back to even on the price index only in 1954 (097,
re-read); cash for a known need is a shock absorber, not a pogo stick (186); the market's price of a US default was
about 0.27% after the 2025 downgrade (183); private assets look calm only because appraisals lag (096, re-read).

## Synthesis 3 (after 312 pieces, 28 Oct, before dawn)
New since synthesis 2 (`LOG.md` 187-312): writing about writing (Orwell, Didion, Graham: about 60 essays in all), 30 Buffett letters in
all (1977 to Abel's first in 2026), Marks's memos old and new, Haldane on prices, patience, tails and networks,
Keynes ch. 12, Housel (about 25 posts in all), Bullock, the Hopkins and Tufts winners, White's pig.

1. **The thesis is the first sentence, and it sounds like trivia.** "Japan has 140 businesses that are at least 500
   years old." (258) "Every forecast takes a number from today and multiplies it by a story about tomorrow." (262)
   "There are 13 divorces among the 10 richest men in the world." (297) A strange, exact fact makes the reader ask
   why it is there, and the rest of the piece answers. Synthesis 2's withheld reveal and verbless list are two
   versions of this; the plainest version is a number. Caplet's IPS could open on one exact, odd number Laura owns.
2. **Mistakes are believed when they are measured.** Buffett gives every error a price: USAir at "25 cents on the
   dollar" (283), a "D" for 1999 (285), mills shrinking from $53 million to $22 million (278), "I allowed General Re
   to take on business without a safeguard I knew was important" (228). Orwell confesses vanity before purpose
   (247); Didion admits stealing her title in sentence one (248). F3 and F6 are the same move at different sizes:
   own something, in numbers. In the Final Report, Caplet's real mistakes (the cancelled orders and the notes on
   them, the mis-click that led to one trader) are worth more than any success, if they carry a number.
3. **Argue with yourself on the page.** The best writers in this stretch give the strongest case against themselves:
   Buffett against bonds while holding a "boatload" of Treasury bills (253, 255, 256), Haldane's "ability to hold,
   not just the intention" (233), Graham's "But I could be wrong." (303). This is Book 9's "critique and fuse" as a
   voice, not a section: one sentence that concedes the strongest objection, then the answer.
4. **Short closes are earned, not written.** "Come by rail." (252) "See randomness." (272) "Everything is sales."
   (264) "I plan to keep at it." (230) "Don't give up." (221) Each works because everything before it points there.
   Of eight Hopkins winners, seven end on the lesson in its own words; the Tufts essay that ends "I stole his hiding
   place." (275) ends on a thing. So: end on a thing, a decision, or three words the whole piece has earned. A short
   close with nothing behind it is a slogan.
5. **Comedy first, grief after.** White makes his own self-importance the joke for pages, so "the loss of pig" lands
   (312). Graham's "Having Kids" undercuts its own warmth (222). Feeling gets past a reader's guard only after a
   laugh or a doubt. There is no room for this in 500 words; there is in the Final Report's experience section.
6. **Judges skim.** "how little most people judging you care about judging you accurately" (270); "I bet you
   didn't read those numbers." (242). The strength has to be visible in the first two lines of each part, and each
   paragraph should hold one number a judge could repeat out loud.
7. **Plain is a choice, not a lack.** "Good prose is like a window pane." (247) "The picture dictates the
   arrangement." (248) "If you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking." (267) The investor
   writers who last (Buffett, Marks, Housel) sound the same for decades because the voice is their opinions, said
   plainly. Synthesis 2's point stands: what AI can't copy is the working.

**Investing finds (idea bank FRB-22 to FRB-40).** The strongest, fused:
- *A fact, and a fact times a story* (FRB-32, Housel): the cheques rest on a fact, the gift on a fact times a story.
  The cleanest framing of Caplet XR's split so far, and an IPS candidate.
- *Hold-ability, not intention* (FRB-26 Haldane, FRB-34 Keynes's "fetish of liquidity", FRB-14 Marks): three
  writers 74 years apart say the same thing; one IPS clause.
- *Multiplied by a single zero* (FRB-28) and *default paid* (FRB-30, FRB-40): ten cheques are a product, and the
  plan is tested by assuming nothing goes right after January 2027.
- *No bell curve under the cheques* (FRB-37, Haldane on fat tails and monocultures).
- *Buffett's 10/90, set by dates instead of taste* (FRB-38) and *Buffett against bonds* (FRB-33): a famous plan and a
  famous objection, both critiqued and fused, for the Final Report.

## Synthesis 4 (after 501 pieces, 28 Oct, midday)
New since synthesis 3 (`LOG.md` 313-501): counter-examples on purpose (generic outlooks, a BlackRock note, a dad
blog), Grantham and Montier, Cembalest, the rest of Buffett's letters, more Housel and Graham, Marks's memos as PDFs, commencement speeches (Saunders, Rowling, Lewis, McRaven, Taubes, Taleb), Ishiguro's Nobel lecture,
Feynman, Bezos and Dimon, Damodaran on what "risk-free" means, Klarman, Sleep and Terry Smith second-hand, about
thirty speeches and transcripts from the Reserve Bank of Australia (Hauser, Hunter, Bullock, Kent, Dwyer), six more
Haldane speeches (thirteen in all), Montaigne, Hazlitt, Lamb, Woolf, Orwell, White, Hitchens, and the Hopkins essays for 2022-25.

1. **Commentary says "may"; a plan says what it costs.** The generic outlooks (314-316, 403-404) fail the same three
   ways: every verb is a maybe, no number could prove them wrong, and the close sells something. Grantham's best
   piece (340) was written in the week of the 2009 low and is a plan, not a forecast. Hitchens and Buffett say what
   a choice cost them. The test for any sentence in the IPS: could a judge check it in 2033? If not, it's commentary.
2. **Doubt the forecast in one sentence, then say the plan flat.** This is the clearest new pattern, and it answers
   Book 5's "no hedging" better than any banned-word list. The RBA's people say outright that their forecasts will be
   wrong ("If we can be confident about anything, it's that our forecasts will be wrong at least in some way", 447;
   "But let me be clear, we could be wrong.", 456; "The one thing I can guarantee is that they won't exactly come
   through", 454). Marks: "I know my views on the market's direction aren't worth betting on," then, flat: "I'd
   rather bet on risk control than risk bearing" (458). Bullock goes one step further: "if we are wrong, what
   direction might it be" (453). Humility goes in one place, about what nobody can know; everything about what we
   own and do is said without a single maybe. That is the opposite of AI hedging, which spreads a little doubt over
   every sentence. Caplet XR fits it exactly: the range's top is a forecast and says so once; the floor and the
   cheques are owned and are said plainly.
3. **One frame per piece, used fully, then dropped.** Hauser builds whole speeches on Scrooge (429), the Melbourne Cup
   (437) and the War College's discarded playbook (436); Haldane on elephant seals (481) and a footrace (480);
   Cembalest makes the energy transition a teenager (421). None of them uses the frame in every paragraph; they set
   it up, pay it off, and get on with the numbers. The jokes live in titles and first lines ("Chill, dude!", 416);
   the sums are left alone. That is Book 2's subtle motif in practice: Caplet XR can open and close a piece and
   appear once in between.
4. **Off-script is more human, by one small detail.** The same central bankers are grey in prepared speeches (439,
   448, 489) and vivid in transcripts: a guitar learned in lockdown, a cold caught from a granddaughter, milk, raves
   (451-457, 484-490). The register barely changes; what changes is one specific personal thing. For the Final
   Report's experience section, that is the whole trick: one true, small detail per scene (from Caplet's real
   events only), not a warmer tone.
5. **Translate yourself.** "Put in rather plainer English, I am gloomier." (Haldane, 459) "So communications is
   everything – or almost everything." (Hauser, 432) "A bit tight" for the labour market (488). The writer corrects
   or shrinks his own sentence in public, and the reader trusts the plain version more for having seen the fancy one.
   In 500 words there is room for this once at most; the Final Report can use it more.
6. **Comedy first, then the turn, in one line.** Synthesis 3's point 5 holds and gets sharper. Lamb spends the essay
   on warm memories and turns in one sentence ("We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams.", 475); Woolf's moth
   is comic until it isn't (476); "Lessons Learned" goes from a "dramatic 11-year-old" to a family's sacrifice (472);
   the commencement speakers mock themselves before they advise (394, 395, 398). The turn is short. A long turn
   becomes a sermon.
7. **Strong first lines, weak last lines; the fix is to speak to someone.** Across 23 Hopkins winners from 2022-25,
   nearly every first line works ("I cannot dance.", "My eyes widen.", "I looked up and flinched slightly.") and
   most last lines go generic ("…a more harmonious world"). The closes that sound like a person address someone:
   "Your move." (466), "Enjoy!" (497), "Pat" (501), "Therefore farewell." (Montaigne, 463). Synthesis 3 said end on a
   thing, a decision, or three earned words; add: or hand the reader the next move.

**Investing finds (idea bank FRB-41 to FRB-65).** The strongest, fused:
- *Risk-free is a match, and a price fall isn't a loss if you hold* (FRB-55, FRB-62, Damodaran): a Treasury is safe
  for the date it pays on, so the ladder buys each cheque's own bond and never needs to sell. One IPS clause.
- *A range with a hit rate, and the direction we could be wrong* (FRB-56, FRB-60, Hunter, Hauser, Bullock): the
  bottom of the 2031 range is owned, only the top is modelled, and we name the error that would hurt. The strongest
  candidate yet for the range sentence.
- *Robust, not optimal; risk control over risk bearing; do nothing on purpose* (FRB-59, FRB-63, FRB-58, FRB-44):
  Hauser, Marks, Klarman and Terry Smith arrive at the same discipline from four directions. Final Report.
- *Say what we promise and what we don't* (FRB-51, Marks) and *more things can happen than will happen* (FRB-53).

**What 501 pieces taught that 186 did not.** Synthesis 2 found where the voice lives (in the working, not the
sentences). Synthesis 3 found that mistakes are believed when they carry a number. Synthesis 4 adds where the doubt
goes: in one sentence, about the future, never spread over the plan.

## Synthesis 5 (after 765 pieces, 28 Oct, late evening)
New since synthesis 4 (`LOG.md` 502-765): Watsa and the Amazon letters (Bezos 2003-19, Jassy 2022-25), Weschler and
Combs, about eighty more Reserve Bank of Australia speeches from 2018 to 2023 (Lowe, Debelle, Ellis, Bullock, Kent,
Jones), read in order so the forecast misses show; Emerson, Thoreau, Twain; thirty-odd great speeches from one
collection (Bogle, Adichie, Bezos, Cleese, Deresiewicz, Feynman, Gaiman, Hamming, Lewis, Munger three times, Valvano,
Minkkinen, Brown); and, read whole from an essay archive, Lamb, Hazlitt, Addison, Johnson, Seneca, Kenko, Swift,
Huxley, Repplier, Meynell, Alexander Smith, and the comic essayists Milne, Jerome, Belloc and Morley.

1. **Short first lines, shorter last lines.** The first lines that land are four to eight words, plain, and true:
   "I forget the rest." (729), "I have no ear." (708), "Bruce is a cricket." (756), "I'm a storyteller." (645),
   "I was shipwrecked before I got aboard." (693). Lowe opened the worst week of 2020 with "Good afternoon." (582).
   The best closes are shorter still and do exactly what they say: "She is dead." (765), "You lay it down." (735),
   "Pshaw!" (748), "Make good art." (651). Synthesis 4 found weak last lines; the fix here is to cut the close until
   it is an act, not a lesson. For Caplet: the pitch's first sentence under ten words, the IPS's last sentence a
   thing the plan does.
2. **Start with something small in the room, or with the reader's objection.** The old essayists begin with one
   object (Huxley's chalk, 738; Hazlitt's spider, 687; Milne's celery, 757; Seneca at Scipio's villa, 697) and earn
   the big claim later. The speakers begin by saying what the audience is thinking: "My title must seem like a
   contradiction." (649); "no doubt many of you are wondering why the speaker is so old." (657). A judge reading a
   growth-competition entry that is mostly bonds has an objection ready. Say it first, in their words, then answer.
3. **Admit it plainly, in the past tense, with no "if".** Read in order, the RBA's speeches are one story: patient in
   2018-22 ("the next move in interest rates to be up, not down", 628; "prepared to be patient", 573), the forecast
   breaks, "soul-searching" (572), and then Lowe's farewell: "With the benefit of hindsight, my view is that we did do
   too much." (595). The bond-buying review said the same lesson in conditionals ("if more weight had been given…
   might have been different", 574) and nobody remembers it. Synthesis 3 said a mistake is believed when it has a
   number; this adds the grammar: first person, past tense, no conditional.
4. **One word or object comes back at the end.** Bogle's "Enough" is the punchline of his first story and the last
   word of the speech (644); Gaiman's title closes his speech (651); Milne's celery opens and closes the essay (757);
   Brown's marble jar (667). The return is the motif done right: set up once, used once, and paid off in the last
   line, which is synthesis 4's "one frame" with an ending. Caplet XR's ten cheques give a natural return: the plan
   can end on the tenth.
5. **Joke against your own side; invert to be serious.** The funniest lines in eighty central-bank speeches are
   against the speaker's own institution: "There are so many black swans identified each day that we may as well all
   be living in Perth." (623); the missing $100 notes, "I don't have my share" (601). Munger's misery speech (656) and
   Roberts's "I Wish You Bad Luck" (661) are built inside out: list how to fail, and the advice is obvious. Humour
   aimed at yourself buys trust; humour aimed at the reader costs it. Inversion is also a risk method (FRB-80).
6. **Say the numbers flat.** Swift's "A Modest Proposal" (739) is the strongest proof that calm arithmetic is the most
   forceful voice there is. Debelle opens on "The inflation rate is currently just over 2 per cent (Graph 1)." (630);
   Twain ends a funeral joke on the bill (723). A number needs no adjective; decoration makes it look unsure.
7. **The rules for a short form were written three hundred years ago.** Addison: "give the Virtue of a full Draught in
   a few Drops" (698) and "There is always an Obscurity in Confusion" (713). Belloc: "To begin at the beginning is,
   next to ending at the end, the whole art of writing" (733). Seneca: "Our words should aim not to please, but to
   help." (716). Two warnings sit beside them: Hazlitt's man with one idea who "brings in his mistress at every turn"
   (682), which is the motif overused; and Belloc's ailing Muse, where "single lines, tags, fine phrases … are no sign
   whatever of returning health" (765), which is the best one-line description of AI prose in the log.

**Investing finds (idea bank FRB-66 to FRB-85).** The strongest, fused:
- *Never forced to sell* (FRB-70, FRB-75, FRB-62): in March 2020 Treasuries fell alongside shares because holders had
  to raise cash (Kent, 594; Debelle, 614). Each cheque is a bond that matures in its year, so Caplet XR never sells.
- *Invert: how would we miss a cheque?* (FRB-80, Munger): a short checklist of the ways to fail, each one closed, and
  the reinvestment gap named as the one still open.
- *Enough, then play* (FRB-81, FRB-83, FRB-85, Bogle, Hazlitt, Milne): buy enough for the ten cheques and stop; it is
  a spending plan, not a heap; and the shares are the game Laura can afford to be bad at (the Archive's deletion
  test found no cheque worse off with the shares wiped).
- *Name the miss and the price* (FRB-73, FRB-78, Lowe, Debelle): say once, plainly, what the ladder costs and where
  our own forecast was wrong.

**What 765 pieces taught that 501 did not.** Synthesis 4 said where the doubt goes. Synthesis 5 is about the edges of
a piece: the first line, the last line, and the one thing that connects them. The middle of a good piece can be
plain; the edges can't.

## Synthesis 6 (after 1,000 pieces, 29 Oct)
New since synthesis 5 (`LOG.md` 766-1003; 833, 837 and 838 were re-reads, so three pieces were added to reach 1,000): Bacon, Goldsmith, Steele, Chesterton, Stevenson and Leigh Hunt; the rest
of the essay archive's De Quincey, Tomlinson, Wollstonecraft, Cowper and Vernon Lee; Montaigne read whole (all 50
of the archive's essays, three of them first read earlier in other editions); Benson, Howells, Temple, Colton, Cowley, Martin, Jacks, Eliot,
Gilman, Wharton, Bly, Bird, Fuller and Zitkala-Sa; and about seventy more Reserve Bank of Australia speeches from 2015,
2016 and 2017, the years Stevens handed over to Lowe and both explained a world of very low yields.

1. **The title or the first words carry the argument.** "Buffers and Options" (912) and "An Accounting" (911) are
   whole speeches in two or three words. The shortest first lines in the log came in this stretch and each does a
   job: "Why not?" (895) answers an objection before it is spoken; "What gave me the idea?" (929) and "What is to be
   thought of sudden death?" (976) ask and then answer at once; "February dusk on the Seine." (893) puts the reader
   on a riverbank in five words. Synthesis 1 marked the question opener "good once"; these show why it works: the
   answer comes in the next sentence, not the next page. The IPS form has no title, so the pitch's first words are
   the title.
2. **Say the objection first, in the reader's words.** Howells starts with "But" (851). Gilman gives the obvious
   answer, "namely, that women are fools" (894), only to knock it down. Montaigne quotes a saying and then tests it
   (847: "'Tis a saying, "That death discharges us of all our obligations.""). This sharpens synthesis 5's point 2:
   a judge reading a growth competition entry built mostly on bonds has an objection ready, and the IPS can say it
   in the first sentence.
3. **A loss is felt through one object or one flat sum, then one short line.** Tomlinson's dead town comes alive
   through a corn-chandler's ledger in the rubble (936). Bly gives the factory wages flat (50 cents per hundred
   boxes; $1.50 a week after two unpaid weeks) and then says only "Cheap labor, isn't it?" (986). Montaigne's
   frugal Romans are a stolen set of farm tools and a gown that never "cost above ten crowns" (840). This is F4 at
   its strongest in the whole log, and it is synthesis 5's "numbers flat" with a kicker: the sum, then four words.
4. **Mark your own homework in public.** Lowe re-checks a speech he gave six years earlier (918); Stevens re-checks
   his 2009 speech (953) and calls a miss "far from the biggest forecast error I've seen over my three decades in
   this game" (961); Kent sets the Bank's forecast beside the outcome, half of it missed (919); Ellis: "The facts had
   changed, and we had changed our minds." (865). Synthesis 3 said a mistake is believed when it has a number, and
   synthesis 5 added plain past tense; this adds the setting: the writer's own earlier words, quoted back. The Final
   Report can do exactly this with the IPS (FRB-110).
5. **The short form of a plan: three rules or one chain.** Ellis ends on three rules a teenager could repeat: go in
   with your eyes open, don't get ahead of yourself, have something to catch you if you fall (913). Lowe puts a
   whole causal chain in one sentence of "which means" links (868). Both fit the IPS's budget of three rules and one
   trade-off, and Book 9's call for visible logic.
6. **Stop; don't sum up.** Montaigne ends on a Spartan teacher biting a thumb, with no moral (834), on a comic twist
   (997), on "Therefore farewell." (463); he even lets his own evidence beat his thesis and stops there (835). The
   RBA's speakers end on a name (916: "Glenn Stevens.") and on "Over to you." (958). Synthesis 5's "an act, not a
   lesson" holds across a thousand pieces.
7. **Warnings from this stretch.** De Quincey's late prose (978, 980) is grand, musical and no use for an IPS;
   Colton's triplets, "a drum, or a despot: a trumpet, or a tyrant: a fife, or a fool" (853), are a pattern the reader
   can hear coming; Jacks argues against abstraction in abstractions (885); Benson's "Ideas" sorts without ever
   saying (850). These are the AI-prose faults (rhythm on display, abstraction, sorting) in human hands.

### Looking back over 1,000 pieces
Six moves held from synthesis 1 to synthesis 6, each confirmed by writers centuries and fields apart:
- **The voice is the working and the opinions, not the sentences** (syntheses 1-2): Marks and Buffett for decades
  (028-039, 072-076), Damodaran's impostor who copied everything but the reasoning (184), Montaigne's "I do not paint
  its being, I paint its passage" (839).
- **A mistake is believed when it is measured and admitted in the past tense** (syntheses 3, 5, 6): Buffett's USAir
  at "25 cents on the dollar" (283), Lowe's "we did do too much" (595), Stevens's forecast error (961), Ellis (865).
- **Doubt once, about the future; say the plan flat** (synthesis 4): "Uncertainty is one of the few certainties in
  monetary policy decision-making." then the modal forecast (806); "The simple answer is 'not very'." (970).
- **The edges carry the piece** (syntheses 1, 3, 5, 6): short first lines, shorter last lines, and a close that acts
  ("This is water.", 078; "Make good art.", 651; "Over to you.", 958).
- **One frame or object, set up once and paid off once** (syntheses 4-5): Bogle's "Enough" (644), Lee's playhouse at
  both ends of "Limbo" (898), Kent's botany at both ends of a speech on monetary policy (968).
- **Numbers said flat** (syntheses 3, 5, 6): Swift (739), Buffett's mills shrinking from $53 million to $22 million
  (278), Bly (986).

The warnings held too: endings that state the lesson (089, 090 and most of the Hopkins winners), formality that
signposts ("Firstly ... Secondly", 080-087), advice about stories that tells none (065), the motif dragged into every
paragraph (Hazlitt's man with one idea, 682), "single lines, tags, fine phrases" (Belloc, 765), and grand music (De
Quincey, 978). One early call changed: synthesis 1's "bad news first" became "early, small and in the middle"
(synthesis 2, Lincoln 166).

**Investing finds from this stretch (idea bank FRB-87 to FRB-116).** Thirty rows, among them: plan on the likely path
and own the floor (FRB-87, Debelle); mandate constraints (FRB-88); a thousand ways to miss, one way to hit (FRB-91,
Montaigne); judge the plan at the tenth cheque (FRB-92, Solon); the facts changed, so we changed our minds (FRB-94,
Ellis); plan for the thin buffer, not the average (FRB-99, Kent); three rules a judge can repeat (FRB-107, Ellis);
never sell when the tide goes out (FRB-109, Debelle); mark our own homework (FRB-110); finance depends on trust
(FRB-114, Stevens). The three that matter most for the IPS:
- **FRB-112, 2015 was the expensive year and 2026 is the cheap one.** In 2015 savers "have to accept a lot more risk
  to generate the expected flow of future income they want" (Stevens, 963). Laura meets the reverse: at today's
  yields her ten cheques cost less than her first deposit, so she buys them outright and takes no risk for them.
  This is the "why now" behind Caplet XR, and it is the plan's best candidate for a judge's "aha". It needs the
  current ladder cost from the number sheet before it is used.
- **FRB-106, stronger buffers give more options** (Lowe, 912). The headroom above the ladder is not slack; it is
  the 2031 range's upside and the room for an overrun after 2033, and its price is the return the protected money
  gives up. It ties the range, the leftover money and the cost of protection into one idea.
- **FRB-93, promise only what you can perform** (Montaigne, 847). The bottom of the 2031 range is what Laura already
  owns; only the top is a forecast. With FRB-114 (trust) it is the rule for the co-sponsor sentence.

**What 1,000 pieces taught that 765 did not.** Synthesis 5 was about the edges of a piece. Synthesis 6 is about what
the edges are for: the first words say the argument or answer the objection, and the last words stop. In between, a
loss is shown through one object or one flat sum, and a past forecast is quoted back and checked.
