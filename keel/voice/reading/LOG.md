# Reading log: how good writers write (started 27 Oct 2026)

AI-generated (Claude Code) voice study, not for submission. Ray, 27 Oct: read 500 to 1,000 pieces, and read them for
**voice, tone, rhythm and structure**, not for content ("you know enough knowledge ... you express it poorly").
Insights come second.

**How each piece is read.** This environment's network blocks direct downloads, so every piece goes through a web
reader that returns short quotes and an analysis, not the full text. Each entry is labelled with its depth:
- `reader`: read through the web reader (first, best and last lines, rhythm, stance, structure). Quotes are short and
  come through the reader; check them against the source before reusing one.
- `full`: the whole text was read (Ahaan's essays only, so far).
- `partial` / `second-hand`: only part of the piece, or another site quoting it.

**Pace.** The web reader allows about 100 fetches an hour across this whole account, so the log grows in batches,
about 60-90 pieces an hour at best. Collections (memo anthologies, letter compilations) give several pieces per fetch.

Each entry: **Open / Best / Close** (quoted lines), **Craft** (rhythm, stance, shape), **Steal** (one move worth
copying), **Insight** (only if there is one). What the entries add up to goes in `SYNTHESIS.md` after every 100.

Count so far: **414**

## Round 2 (from 27 Oct evening)

### 020 · Paul Graham, "How to Make Wealth" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/wealth.html
- Open: "If you wanted to get rich, how would you do it?" · Close: "Let the nerds keep their lunch money, and you rule the world."
- Craft: medium sentences cut by short punches; bold section heads, one idea each; "you" throughout; dry and rude in places. Opponent: the "Pie Fallacy".
- Steal: a back-of-envelope sum built from named multipliers, then admit it is rough while defending its shape.
- Insight: you get rich through your own work only where the work is measured and has leverage.

### 021 · Paul Graham, "How to Disagree" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/disagree.html
- Open: "The web is turning writing into a conversation." · Best: "Better that than grave and wrong."
- Craft: thesis, then a graded ladder (DH0 to DH6) with an example on every rung. Calm "I".
- Steal: a named ladder lets the reader place their own objection on it.
- Insight: a ranking caps how persuasive an argument can be; it doesn't make it right.

### 022 · Paul Graham, "The Age of the Essay" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/essay.html
- Open: "Remember the essays you had to write in high school?" · Best: "At sixteen I was about as observant as a lump of rock." · Close: "And that's certainly not something I realized when I started writing this."
- Craft: one-word fragments ("Oy.") next to long stacked sentences; one-word bold heads (Trying, The River, Surprise). Opponent: the school essay that defends a fixed position.
- Steal: the ending admits the essay changed the writer's mind while writing it.
- Insight: an essay is a trial, written to find out; write what surprised you.

### 023 · Paul Graham, "What You'll Wish You'd Known" · speech-essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/hs.html
- Open: "When I said I was speaking at a high school, my friends were curious." · Best: "Rebellion is almost as stupid as obedience."
- Craft: written for teenagers without talking down; picks a worn slogan and swaps in a sharper one.
- Steal: name the cliché the reader expects, then beat it.
- Insight: stay upwind; choose the path that keeps the most options open.

### 024 · Paul Graham, "Taste for Makers" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/taste.html
- Open: "I was talking recently to a friend who teaches at MIT." · Close: "The recipe for great work is: very exacting taste, plus the ability to gratify it."
- Craft: anecdote, then the abstract question made practical; a list of bold claims, each backed by examples from several fields; one-word paragraphs ("Smack!").
- Steal: ending on a recipe with exactly two ingredients.
- Insight: if taste can improve, "taste is subjective" can't be wholly true.

### 025 · Paul Graham, "What You Can't Say" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/say.html
- Open: "Have you ever seen an old photo of yourself and been embarrassed at the way you looked?" · Best: "Argue with idiots, and you become an idiot." · Close: "What can't you say? And why?"
- Craft: universal memory as the hook, then turned on the reader; the close echoes the title as two short questions.
- Steal: circle back: the last line answers or re-asks the first.

### 026 · Paul Graham, "The Top Idea in Your Mind" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/top.html
- Open: "I realized recently that what one thinks about in the shower in the morning is more important than I'd thought." · Best: "My wife thinks I'm more forgiving than she is, but my motives are purely selfish."
- Craft: anecdote, principle, two examples, self-test; the confession is funny because it is against interest.
- Steal: end with a test the reader can run tonight.

### 027 · Paul Graham, "Stuff" · essay · reader
http://www.paulgraham.com/stuff.html
- Open: "I have too much stuff." · Close: "Imagine walking around for years with five pound ankle weights, then suddenly having them removed."
- Craft: a four-word opening confession; long paragraphs with one-line punches ("And pow, more stuff."); ends on a physical image, not a moral.
- Steal: close on a body sensation the reader can feel instead of a summary.
- Insight: an asset is worth what you get from using it; unused things are worth little, whatever they cost.

## Strand: pitches, founder letters, speeches (Ray, 27 Oct: "judges are Shark Tank investors, not examiners")
These are read for one thing: **how they move a reader who holds the money**. Template: Open / the line where the
feeling lands (Turn) / Close / which emotion and how / what makes the listener believe / Steal.

**First read of the strand (for the voice guide's emotion section):** nobody here *names* an emotion. They put the
reader somewhere (a doctor's office, a stadium in Houston, a cheque in your hand) and let the feeling arrive. The
conviction comes from a refusal or a cost the speaker accepts in public (Google won't guide earnings; Chouinard gives
the company away; Kennedy picks the hard thing).

**For Caplet, #070 is the find of the day.** King's whole speech runs on a promissory note: a cheque that came back
marked "insufficient funds". Laura is writing ten cheques to artists she has not met, dated 2033 to 2042. Caplet's job
is that none of them bounce. The bad-cheque image is too famous to borrow, but the *move* (a financial promise as a
moral one, the reader holding the paper) is exactly the emotional line the IPS lacks: the payments are not
"liabilities", they are promises with someone's name on them.
