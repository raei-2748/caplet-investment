## 1. What "best" means here

Not the cleverest report: the one a reader holding Wharton's five criteria, after forty reports that day, ranks first
and still remembers the next morning. Wharton's own bar: "The strongest reports will connect the team's established
strategy, portfolio decisions, analysis, assumptions, and understanding of Laura's goals into a cohesive and
well-supported recommendation." The first reader is a Wharton student with a stack (about 76 reports each in 2024-25);
the later readers know bonds and liabilities (Aberdeen fixed-income managers, an insurer's investment chief). Every
lever below serves one of those two readers.

## 3. The self-reinforcing loop (how every run makes the next one better)

The idea comes from fields that improve under pressure: after-action reviews (the US Army's "what was supposed to
happen, what happened, why, what next"), Toyota's andon and kaizen (stop at the weakest point, fix the method, not only
the output), and forecasting tournaments (Tetlock: score every judgement, keep the record, update on it). Four parts:

1. **A scorecard every run re-scores** (`SCORECARD.md`). Rows are Wharton's required parts plus the minimum
   requirements; columns are five tests drawn from the criteria and the readers: evidence, bond-desk precision,
   clarity on one reading, coherence with what was submitted, and distinctiveness. Each cell is scored 1-5 with the
   reason and "what would make it a 5". A score rises only when evidence lands, never when wording improves.
2. **Weakest link first.** Each run takes the two or three lowest cells as its claims to keep, kill or change, and
   sends its research workers there. Breadth research waits for a cell that needs it.
3. **Independent judges, not Keel's own opinion.** At least twice a week, three judges score the current skeleton,
   numbers and best evidence blind: different methods, different priors and different models (EXPECTATIONS 4.3), each
   marking only against Wharton's instructions. Where they disagree is the next target; their mean never replaces the
   reasons.
4. **Lessons carried forward** (`LESSONS.md`). Each run records what raised a score, what did not, and what to do
   differently (a method note, not a finding). The next run reads it first. Methods that keep failing are dropped.

Each run's RUNLOG row records the scorecard's lowest cell and its mean, so the trend is visible on the portal.
