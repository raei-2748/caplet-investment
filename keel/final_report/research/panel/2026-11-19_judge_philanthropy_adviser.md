## 4. As the co-sponsor in 2031

**What I would write into the board paper.**
- Founder's facility contribution: **US$115,000, committed, unconditional, in a written pledge**, held for her in a US Treasury fund that pays out in December 2032. I would add the NT$ figure at the 2031 rate (about NT$3.77m at the 6 Nov 2026 rate, for scale).
- Founder's operating commitment: **US$50,000 a year, 2033-2042 (US$500,000), already held in Treasuries**. That is ten years of her share of the running costs, owned before we are asked for anything.
- I would book the $115,000 as committed, not received. The $95,000 "held since January 2028" goes in a footnote at most, because it changes nothing in our decision. The fact that the operating money is already owned is what gets the proposal onto the agenda. In twenty years on committees I have rarely seen a founder show that.

**What I would still ask Laura.**
1. What is the facility's total budget, who sets it, and by when? What has been pledged so far, and what is the close date? (The plan has the residency setting the total before the 2031 meetings. I need to see it.)
2. What is the residency's whole yearly operating budget? Her $50,000 is a base. Who funds the rest each year, and for how long?
3. Who receives the money and holds the site? What is the legal body, who governs it, and who is on its board?
4. If the total is not reached by December 2032, where does her "$115,000 comes first either way" go? Does it wait for a smaller building, or go back to her?
5. Will she sign a pledge agreement naming amount, purpose and date, and show a broker statement on request?
6. If the Taiwan dollar strengthens, is her pledge fixed in US dollars, so that it shrinks in NT$? Will she meet the difference from her flexibility, as the IPS says?
7. If the building slips past January 2033, where does the money sit, and for how long?
8. What recognition does she expect, and what happens to operations after 2042?

**What would make me say no.**
- No total or no close date. An open-ended capital ask is one my board will not join.
- A building whose cost makes $115,000 a token. If her figure is a small fraction of the total, the residency is not yet viable, however well her money is held.
- No legal body or site control by the time pledges close.
- An operating budget that, beyond her $50,000, still depends on yearly public grants. That is the very fragility the report itself cites in Taiwan's residencies.

## 5. The three changes that would most raise the score

1. **3a: lead with the reserve's size and price the standard's choice.** The plan line reads: "the operating reserve's size on 1 January 2033, printed once, here [Figure 1's note item 4]: about $445,700 at today's rates". It also reads: "Open for the lead: whether 3a says what the extra Treasuries cost the building figure (the menu stays in the store, NUMBERS 4)". Wharton and the case ask for the reserve's "size and initial asset composition" in so many words. A judge working from that checklist should find the size in 3a's first sentence, not in a figure note. And the zero-interest standard is the report's most consequential choice. Say what it costs the facility: about $15,000 off each end of the range against a reserve sized for rates 3 points lower (INTENT item 3), and more against none. Then explain why it is still right. To stay within three figures, say the price in words ("about a quarter of the 2028 deposit") and keep $46,600 and the size as the other two. The aim is that the judge sees a choice among priced alternatives, not an assertion.
2. **3c: one outside device, one turn, and the real uncertainty in prose.** The plan lines are "Neyman's point ... except one built of Treasuries held for her", "The conceit turn where CONCEIT V2.2 places it (trim, bleed and safe area; dot gain; non-photo blue ...)", "the Antideficiency rule ... glossed at first use", and "The real uncertainty, in one clause with no figures". Keep one outside name: the finance chiefs' ranges, the fact a funder nods at. Move Neyman to References. Use "two inkings, two dates", which needs no gloss, in place of the print-shop terms, and cut the Antideficiency gloss. Spend the freed words on two things. First, the case's own ask, as one sentence of Table 2's spread: one future in twenty states $80,000-$95,000, one in twenty $115,000-$140,000. Second, one plain line: "She will give the top; the bottom tells co-sponsors how much of it has been held since 2028." Swap the 0.5% margin out of the prose for the spread; it is already in Table 2's note. This is the part judges weigh most, and the one every panel has scored clarity 2.
3. **3f: reach the final recommendation, and take the literature out.** The plan lines are "Three moments ... (1) the knife edge ... (2) Laura's taste for risk ... (3) 'a copout'", the copout's evidence clause "led by Uhlmann and Cohen (2005)", and the moment-2 alternatives to "Kahneman and Lovallo (Q13-009)". Put no outside citations in the reflection; a bar set before the numbers speaks for itself. Then make the closing paragraph tie each moment to the Final Report recommendation it fed: moment 2 to the Treasury-held reserve and its standard, moment 3 to VT as the top and her flexibility, moment 1 to the 2028 deposit's first job, which is the reserve's price. If the students themselves made a November call (how strict the reserve should be, or which figure co-sponsors count), add one sentence on it in their words, and only if it is their real experience.

Runners-up: fix Figure 1's $444,080 and $445,700 trap. Rewrite Table 3's cells as short sentences. In 3b, say once that the extra Treasuries also come back to her over 2033-2042, so "preserving flexibility" is not judged on $28,700 alone.

## 6. The single biggest risk

The report will be right but hard to follow. It is to be written in one day from a plan whose guards outnumber its sentences. Within about 2,400 words of prose it asks a first-round judge to hold about eighteen named pots and terms ("the top-up", "the extra Treasuries", "the 0.5% margin", "the room under the top", "her flexibility plus anything above the top", "if cash waiting earns nothing", "today's figures" against "today's market rates"), around twelve conceit turns and twenty or more outside sources. Every panel has scored the content about 4 and the clarity 2-3 in 3a, 3c and 3e. Nothing in the plan yet forces that gap shut, and the page budget has no headroom (Section 3 is fitted to about eight pages exactly). A tired judge who cannot say back the reserve, the figure and the confidence after one read will score Presentation and Analysis down, however sound the work beneath.

## 7. The 'aha' moments I would remember the next day, and the pencil-and-ink image

What I would remember:
- "Diversify Laura, not the portfolio", with her own line: "I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves."
- "$302,287 of them pays $500,000", from a team that wrote that Treasuries "won't create wealth".
- "The price of certainty is closer to a deposit than a fee": about $46,600 comes back if rates hold.
- "Erase every pencil line ... and the page still reads", closing on "No fall in share prices can."
- "A comfort letter describes today; what she owns pays in 2033." For my profession, this is the line: a pledge is owed, and hers is held.
- "Only a deposit more than about three-quarters smaller leaves the payments short". The order of purchase is the diversification.
- "The top states $115,000 for any share return from 4% to 10% a year." Honestly, this cuts both ways: it also shows the shares hardly move the promise, so the report should say plainly that the shares are there for her flexibility.
- 3f: the first plan "would have passed the usual reading of 'high'", yet was "dropped before a single trade" for how a writer is paid, and the check showed "about 1 in 31 against none".
- Laura's own words: "my own $115,000 comes first either way."

**Pencil and ink: it helps in three places and gets in the way in one, and for a reader like me it hides a conflict.**
- It helps in 2.0, where Table 1 sorts into ink (the Treasuries) and pencil (VT). It helps in 3e's erase line and in 3f's "the first plan's payments were in pencil". It is rooted in Laura's craft, since comics are pencilled and then inked, which is client knowledge rather than decoration.
- It gets in the way in 3c, where trim, bleed, safe area, dot gain and non-photo blue each need a gloss. It also gets in the way wherever turns stack, from 2.3 through 3b.
- The hidden conflict: in a development office "inked" means signed, a pledge. That is exactly the "owed" which 3d's comfort-letter aha separates from "owned". The plan knows readers hear "inked" as signed (WORDS: "everyday English reads 'inked' as signed"). I would turn that into the point at first use: "In fundraising, 'inked' means signed. In this report it means paid for." Then the image carries the pledge-versus-owned distinction instead of blurring it.
- My advice is three turns, not twelve: the 2.0 definition, 3e's erase line and 3f's pencil line. Keep the image out of Laura's words to co-sponsors, as the plan already does.
