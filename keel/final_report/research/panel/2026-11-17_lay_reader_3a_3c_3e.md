## My answers, written without looking back

a) What is promised to whom, and what could stop each payment?

Laura promises her residency in Taiwan ten payments of $50,000, one at the start of each year 2033 to 2042, for its
running costs. The plan buys ten Treasuries with her first $300,000 in January 2027, each paying out just before its
year's payment. They cost a little more than $300,000, so the first part of the 2028 deposit (about $38,000) finishes
the job: a small "top-up" to complete the ten, and then "extra Treasuries" on top, so that all ten are paid even if
the money sitting between a bond paying out and the payment date earns no interest at all. Together that is the
"operating reserve". What could stop a payment: only two things. The 2028 deposit not arriving (then the ten are
short about $3,000 at today's rates, or about $57,000 if waiting cash earns nothing), or the US government not paying
its Treasuries (a default). A market fall cannot. Smaller risks (a fund closing early or merging, a debt-limit
standoff, a trading halt) change a date or a fund, never the plan.

b) What does Laura tell co-sponsors in 2031, which figure do they count, and why can she say it with confidence?

She tells them a range, about $95,000 to $115,000 on today's figures. They count the top: $115,000 is her facility
contribution, and she never gives more than the top she stated (anything above it stays as her own flexibility). The
bottom is the part she has held since January 2028, from the Treasury fund bought then (the "floor"). The rest of the
top comes from moving half her world share fund into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. She can be certain of the
range she states, because by the time she states it both ends are money she already owns in Treasuries, counted after
their fees and after a 0.5% margin held back for other costs, and rounded down. The only way she would fall short of
the top is if those costs went past the margin and waiting cash earned nothing, and then only by about $120 for each
extra 0.1%. The figures will move between now and 2031 (lower if interest rates fall before 2028, higher if shares
rise before 2031), but whatever range she states then is already owned.

c) What does the plan assume, and what is its honest limit?

It follows the case's five areas. (1) Shares return 7.5% a year (J.P. Morgan's estimate, on the high side of the
published forecasts), with about 18% volatility; but anything from 4% to 10% barely moves the top she states, which is
set by what shares actually do before 2031. (2) The 2028 deposit arrives in full at the start of 2028 (her income is
paid on contract dates, and a downturn delays it). (3) No outside money for the payments or the bottom; co-sponsors pay
in Taiwan dollars, and public ones can be held up by budget votes. (4) Inflation changes what the payments and the
contribution buy, never whether they are paid; Taiwan building costs and the Taiwan dollar are tested against her
flexibility. (5) Her flexibility is half the shares, not sized to any cost. The team also chooses some margins: waiting
cash earns nothing; the 0.5% margin. Honest limits: a US default (named once); in the worst two-year spans, Taiwan
building costs and the Taiwan dollar together rose by more than her whole flexibility; and a share slump before 2031
followed by a cost burst before 2033 could catch her flexibility. The plan answers for the money, not for the building
or the people.

d) What would you watch between now and 2033, and what would make the team change the plan?

Table 3's seven signals: the 2028 deposit (confirmed by 1 Dec 2027; nothing by 31 March 2028 counts as missed);
Treasury payments (more than five business days late counts as a default); the debt-limit "X-date" near a date when
cash is due; notices from the Treasury funds (early end, merger, fee rise: swap the fund); trading days in 2027, 2028
and 2031 (if there is a halt or prices are off, wait); interest rates and share prices (watch only: they move the
bottom until 2028 and the top until the 2031 move, never the payments); and her flexibility's uses (the Taiwan dollar
within about 9-10% of where it was, building costs rising 1.5% to 5% a year). Only two things change the plan: a missed
2028 deposit and a Treasury default. A market fall is not one of them.

e) Ink and pencil, in one sentence.

Ink is a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on its date; pencil is any figure no Treasury
she owns will pay yet (her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the share fund, interest on waiting cash,
any model result), and she only says a figure out loud once it is in ink.
