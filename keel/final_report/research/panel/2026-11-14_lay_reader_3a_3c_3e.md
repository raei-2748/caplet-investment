## Answers, as first written (from memory, no looking back)

**a) What is promised to whom, and what could stop each payment?**
Laura promises the residency ten payments of $50,000, one each January from 2033 to 2042, toward its running
costs. The team's promise to her is that those payments won't depend on shares or on anyone else's money. Each
payment has its own US Treasury (a bond or a bond fund) bought in January 2027 with the first $300,000, ending
just before its payment. The ten cost a bit more than $300,000, so the first $2,400 or so of the 2028 deposit
finishes them, and then about $36,000 more buys "extra Treasuries" in case money that sits waiting earns no
interest; about $38,000 of the 2028 deposit in all. Most of that comes back to her, about $45,500 by 2042, if rates
hold. What could stop a payment: only the US government not paying (a default). Before January 2028 a missed
2028 deposit would also leave the first payment a few thousand dollars short. A market crash cannot stop them.
The second promise is to co-sponsors in 2031: a facility contribution inside a stated range (see b).

**b) What does Laura tell the co-sponsors in 2031, and how sure is it?**
A range, about $95,000 to $115,000 at today's rates. The bottom is what the Treasury fund bought in January 2028
(ending late 2032) will pay; the top adds half the shares, which move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings.
Each end is worked out from what those Treasuries will pay, trimmed by 0.5% and rounded down to $5,000. How sure:
the gift lands inside the range unless the US defaults; it reaches the top unless the funds' running costs eat
more than that 0.5%, and then it is short by a few hundred dollars. She means to give the top; the bottom is the
number co-sponsors can count on. The real uncertainty is before 2031: which range she will be able to state
(lower if rates fall before the 2028 money is invested, higher if shares rise).

**c) Name the assumptions the plan rests on.**
The US government pays its debts (stated first, as the condition, not as one assumption among many). Shares grow
7.5% a year (J.P. Morgan's forecast), though the stated top stays the same anywhere from 4% to 10%; only her spare
money changes. Treasury rates as the market expected in early November 2026. The 2028 deposit arrives in full at
the start of 2028. No outside money for the payments or the bottom; co-sponsors give year by year in Taiwan
dollars. Inflation: the payments are fixed dollars, so inflation changes what they buy, not whether they are paid;
Taiwan building costs (about 1.5% to 5% a year) and the Taiwan dollar are tested against her spare money. Her spare
money is half the shares, not sized to a cost. Two safety margins, not forecasts: waiting cash earns nothing, and
fund costs take at most 0.5%.

**d) What does Table 3 tell Laura to do, and when would she change the plan?**
It is a watch list of seven things in three levels. Two can change the plan: (1) the $150,000 2028 deposit: she
confirms it by 1 December 2027; if nothing has come by 31 March 2028, she states no range until it comes, and the
first payment would be about $3,000 short; nothing is sold. (2) a Treasury payment more than five business days
late, which counts as a default: the payments and the range are restated on what was actually paid. Three "look
closer" rows change a date or a fund, never the plan: a debt-limit deadline near a cash date (don't sell ahead of
it); an iBond fund closing early or merging (swap it for a Treasury ending just before the payment); a trading halt
(move the trade date). Two "watch" rows never change the plan: market prices and rates (they move the bottom until
January 2028, the top until 2031, then only her spare money, never the payments), and the uses of her spare money,
building costs and the Taiwan dollar (10% stronger costs about $11,500 more on $115,000), which draw on the spare
money, never the range. So: change the plan only for a missed deposit or a default.

**e) When is a figure "inked"? The rule as I'd tell a friend.**
A number is in ink once the money that will pay it already sits in US Treasuries she owns, which will pay it out
in time, so only a US default could stop it. Anything not yet owned is pencil: her future income, the 2028 deposit
before it lands, the share fund before half of it moves, interest not yet earned, any model's forecast. She only
says a number out loud once it is in ink.
