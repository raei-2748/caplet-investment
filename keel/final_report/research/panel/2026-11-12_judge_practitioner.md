# Panel 2026-11-12: practitioner judge

## 1. Who I am and how I read
Wealth manager who writes IPSs for authors and artists with lumpy income. Ten minutes, top-down: INTENT once, SKELETON for what a client would ask.
Marked on Wharton's guide only. Judged as if written exactly to this plan.

## 2. Would I hand it to Laura?
Yes, with changes. The idea is right for her: own the promised money before promising it, and the career is the share-like risk she cannot sell. The one reason for the changes: the plan is dense with defined terms, caveats and exceptions, and a client or her board will not get the one-page answer (what is paid, what I can tell co-sponsors, what could go wrong) without work.

## 3. Criteria
- Investment Strategy: 5. One rule runs through IPS, notes and the plan; the trade-off is stated openly (lower certain bottom, half the shares promised to no one).
- Client Knowledge: 4. Payments, reserve, 2031 meetings, currency, overruns and a co-sponsor falling short are all there. Her income pattern is used well. Liquidity and her own cash needs are thin.
- Portfolio Analysis: 4. Targets set before trading, matched, and the overrun against the deposit admitted; history tests and a recession replay. The 2.5 scorecard is possibly too clever for the length.
- Articulation of Competition Experience: 3. Events are chosen by a blind re-rank not yet done; the plan describes the method, not the people. Cannot be marked on a plan.
- Creativity and Presentation: 4. A funder-first passage and a one-glance reserve exhibit are strong; the vocabulary load and the word budget are the risk.

## 4. Parts (score / clarity)
- 2.0 Lead: 4 / 3. Right place for the idea, but it packs four notes plus a cost into 100 words.
- 2.1 What the book is: 3 / 3. The scale-model framing is honest, but a client asks why the book is not simply the real plan.
- 2.2 Construction: 4 / 4. Forced versus chosen instruments is clear and fair.
- 2.3 Diversification: 4 / 3. The reframe (diversify Laura, not the portfolio) is persuasive; academic anchors are more than a client needs.
- 2.4: merged into 2.1, not scored.
- 2.5 How well it was done: 4 / 3. Strong evidence, but 180 words of professional measures may be too much for the audience.
- 3.0 Recommendation in a paragraph: 5 / 4. The "only the facility is shared" proposition is the best thing in the plan.
- 3a Commitment and reserve: 5 / 3. Paid even if interest earns nothing, with the price named, is right; the top-up and what comes back are easy to blur.
- 3b Facility and flexibility: 4 / 2. Two kinds of money that might return, an order of use and a release rule in 260 words; this is the muddiest part.
- 3c Range and confidence: 4 / 2. A written rule is good practice, but the rounding, cutting, caps and two-part confidence read as an engineer's spec.
- 3d Co-sponsor words: 4 / 4. Order is right (what is owned, the one number to count, the ask, what is never asked). Cannot judge the language itself.
- 3e Assumptions and monitoring: 4 / 3. Two defined exits (default, missed deposit) is the right discipline; eight rows is acceptable.
- 3f Team Reflection: 3 / 3. Sound design (events end on a changed or kept recommendation), unproven until written.
- Exhibit 1 holdings table: 5 / 4. Meets Wharton to the cent, shows the overrun row and the cash.
- Exhibit 2 reserve running down: 4 / 4. One holding per payment, readable in seconds; the zero-interest columns need their question stated.
- Exhibit 3 plan under several markets: 4 / 3. Five worlds against four outputs is what a board wants; two or three of the outputs overlap in meaning.
- Exhibit 4 monitoring: 4 / 3. Fits the two-reason rule; the definitions are only as clear as their wording.

## 5. What a client would ask that the plan does not answer
1. What happens to my own cash if I need money outside the portfolio before 2033? The plan assumes no flows, but liquidity is named only for the facility.
2. In plain dollars, what do I walk into the 2031 meetings saying, and what if the number is lower than I hoped? The "lower than all-Treasury at the bottom" cost is a trade-off but not a dollar comparison a client sees.
3. Why not skip shares entirely, given the plan's own view that career risk is share-like? The case for any shares is thinner than the case against.
4. What if I am late with the 2028 deposit, not missing it? The line is 31 March 2028; I do not see a grace case for a delay of weeks.
5. What does a Treasury ETF "net asset value, not par" mean for a payment in dollars? It is named but its practical size is not.

Spends words a client does not care about: the Bodie-Merton-Samuelson and Leibowitz anchors, MSRB refunding-escrow terminology, the scale-model accounting of fixed fees and lot sizes, the stock-and-rate correlation detail, the instrument-duration measures in 2.5.

## 6. Where others will disagree with me
- A finance professor will mark the economics higher than I do (the career-as-share argument, the matching logic) and mark down the plan's tendency to restate the IPS and its reliance on a house model with independence assumptions. They will also care less about 3b and 3c clarity.
- A first-round volunteer reader will score clarity lower than I do in 3a-3c (too many defined terms) and will not credit the professional measures in 2.5. They will like 3d and the exhibits, and may reward Section 3f more if the events are vivid; I cannot.
