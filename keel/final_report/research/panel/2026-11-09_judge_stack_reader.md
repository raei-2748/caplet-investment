## 2. Would the first page make me remember it?

As planned, no. The first thing I see is a title page, then Section 2 opening on "what the book is" and a holdings table. After 40 reports, ten of them will have a Treasury ladder and a world fund and a table. The plan's strongest memorable hooks are placed late: the model that sank the team's own first plan (3f), and the governing thought (INTENT, 3.0 on page 5).

What would make me remember it: on the first page of Section 2, before the table, four sentences that a student can say aloud. One, the idea (money Laura has promised is owned in Treasuries before she promises it). Two, the one number (a range she can say in 2031 and why it holds). Three, the team's own admission (our first plan missed a payment in 40.8% of the bad cases, so we dropped it on day one of trading). Four, the candid flaw (the ten cost $301,544 at the freeze, $1,544 more than the deposit, and here is why that is fine). A headline that admits a flaw and a number that dares to be small is what stands out among 40 confident reports.

## 3. The five weakest points, most serious first

1. **The first page that carries the argument is on page 5.** Fix: open Section 2 with a four-sentence lead (idea, range, own-plan story, candid flaw), above the holdings table. Do not add a new section, only a lead paragraph. Answer open question 1: yes, move the governing thought to the head of Section 2 and keep 3.0 as the recommendation's one-paragraph recap.
2. **The Team Reflection is 300 to 400 words.** Fix: cut 2.4 (merge into 2.1) and trim 3e prose to the table, and give the reflection 600 to 700 words, about two double-spaced pages, with room for the four events below and a closing paragraph saying which one recommendation changed because of which event. Articulation is worth a fifth of my score.
3. **The confidence statement can be read two ways and may overclaim.** "Inside the range unless the Treasury defaults" sits beside "top $111,000 to $171,000" before the move. Fix: one headline sentence, one confidence number with what it assumes (deposits arrive, iBond fund pays net asset value near its yield, the move happens), then a short list of what lowers it. Say outright that the floor is not a guarantee of par.
4. **Repetition risk with the IPS and Trading Notes is built into the governing thought.** The IPS says shares and income are the same bet and the Notes say duration-matching and a floor. 2.2 and 3a lean on both. Fix: rule that each paragraph must contain something not in either (a measure, a test, a decision, a number). The new material in the plan is the cost of the plan at the freeze, the long-run ladder cost history, the margin, and the post-submission test of the career idea (2009 supports it, 2020 does not). That test is currently only in the reader arc, not in any SKELETON part. Put it in 3e or the reflection; it is the most original analysis in the record.
5. **Inflation, Taiwan and "so what for Laura" are thin.** The case lists inflation on portfolio and facility costs, and the fixed $50,000 payments buy less each year. The plan gives them a clause. Fix: in 3b, one paragraph converting the $110,000 to $135,000 into 2033 terms at the team's inflation assumption and in Taiwan dollars at today's rate (Taiwan construction costs rose 7.29% in a year, 3.27% a year over five). One sentence on what that means: the gift is a down payment, not a building.

Also: 3d is only 200 words and unscripted, and 2.3 does not answer the diversification criterion head on. Treat these as sixth and seventh.

## 5. What would make this the best of about 2,300 entries (eight lines)

1. Open Section 2 with a lead that states the idea, the 2031 range, the honest cost ($301,544 vs $300,000) and the team's own abandoned plan, so page 2 is memorable.
2. Spend words on evaluating, not restating: every paragraph holds a measure, a test or a decision that is in neither earlier document.
3. One confidence number, defined in plain words, with the three things that would lower it.
4. A recommended 2033 gift as well as the 2031 range, with the Taiwan dollar and inflation shown once, in a single paragraph.
5. A co-sponsor passage in Laura's voice that a real funder could read in thirty seconds and believe.
6. A reflection of about two pages with four dated, named events, each ending on "so the recommendation changed here".
7. One or two exhibits that each answer one question (the range forming; the reserve running down), readable at a glance.
8. Plain words throughout; no label a stranger cannot decode, and the technical measure in brackets after the plain one.

## 6. Where other reasonable judges would disagree with me

- **Opening with the candid flaw.** Some judges prefer a confident open and would score a stated $1,544 overrun as a weakness in implementation. I think candour wins, but the score on 2.5 could move a point either way.
- **A larger reflection.** Analytical judges may prefer the 300 to 400 words and more numbers. If a judge reads Portfolio Analysis as the core, extra reflection space costs analysis. I would still pay it.
- **Diversification.** A finance-trained judge may accept "diversified by date and by risk source" and mark 2.3 a 4; a rubric-literal judge will mark the 90% single-issuer holding down.
- **The governing thought as repetition.** A judge who weighs consistency across the IPS and report most will love the unchanged thesis. I would mark the same idea restated as weak; they would mark it as the point.
- **Confidence "unless the Treasury defaults".** Some will read this as admirably clear. I read it as slightly too tidy for a floor held in an iBond fund.
- **Events from outside the six students' own record.** If a judge cannot tell, they will not mind. A judge who has seen other entries from this sort of team may feel an event is rehearsed. I would rather the four events be ones the students can speak about in a finalist interview.
