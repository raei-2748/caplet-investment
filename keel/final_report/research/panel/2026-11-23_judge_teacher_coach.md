## 1. First read (about twenty minutes)

What I remember: one idea, and it is a good one. "From January 2028, every dollar Laura has promised is paid by Treasuries held for her before she promises it; only money promised to no one takes market risk." The opening test is memorable and checkable: if every share were worth nothing, all ten payments are still paid and the building still gets $95,000. I would repeat that to a colleague tomorrow. I also remember the price of the plan (about $39,000 of the 2028 deposit, "about a quarter"), the "ink and pencil" idea (a figure is in ink once Treasuries held for her will pay it), that the range is $95,000 to $115,000, that Laura states one figure ($115,000) in a signed pledge letter, and that the team dropped its first plan, about three-quarters shares, after Darren's "copout" argument. The Lehman/Spelman story ($10 million became $3 million) is the one outside story I would keep.

Where I got lost, in the plan's own words:
- 3c: "uncertain from today, held once the shares are moved", with "a top of at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures, $115,000 in about 57". Two layers, two dates, two sets of odds. I had to reread to see that the range is certain after 2031 and uncertain before.
- 3a: "paid even if cash waiting earns nothing", then "the top-up ($2,388)" and "the extra Treasuries ($36,774)", then "the first $39,162". Three names for money in one pot. The idea is clear, but the vocabulary is heavy for a teacher who is not a bond person.
- "forward rates", "implied by today's prices", "net asset value", "0.5% margin", "the room under the top", "a Treasury fund that holds Treasuries maturing in 2032". Each is defined in the notes, but the density per page will be high.
- 3e: "a rise of about 25% in the cost of her share, if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed". Too many conditions in one sentence for me.
- The conceit is "defined at first use", but it has been given turns in every part ("two inkings, two dates", "ink only goes one way", "rub out every pencil line"). I would find it clever in 2.0 and a little strained by 3e.
- The plan lists about ten "ahas" at about one a page. That is a lot for a twenty-minute read; some will blur together.

## 3. Wharton's five criteria

- **Investment Strategy: 5.** One clear thesis, fully consistent with the frozen IPS and Trading Notes (Treasuries for promised money, shares for the rest), disciplined across 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2033.
- **Client Knowledge and Objectives: 4.** Every item in the case is addressed (ten payments, reserve, facility, flexibility, co-sponsor wording, the 2028 deposit being "later and smaller"); the case's Laura-as-person is used lightly and "Taiwan" mostly as a cost and currency risk.
- **Portfolio Analysis: 5.** Quantitative and qualitative work are both there (replays since 1962, simulations, stress cases, named limits), and WInS gains are kept out as Wharton asks; the risk is that so much analysis crowds the page budget.
- **Articulation of Competition Experience: 3.5 (round up to 4 only if the writing shows real disagreement).** Three moments with before/trigger/after are the right design, but they sit last in a long report and the plan has only one figure and a few lines for each.
- **Creativity and Presentation: 4.** The pencil-and-ink conceit, the deletion test and the signed-letter idea are original and memorable; the risks are that the conceit is overused across parts and that the report is figure-dense for a fast reader.

## 4. Three weakest points, with fixes

1. **3c's two layers are the hardest read in the report.** From today's view the top is "at least $100,000 in 91 of 100, $115,000 in 57", but once shares move it is certain; a first-time reader must hold two dates and two sets of odds. Fix (inside the IPS): open 3c with one plain sentence, "By 2031 the top will be known; today it is a forecast", then give the two numbers once, in a three-line mini table (today / after the move), and drop everything else from that paragraph.
2. **Vocabulary density in 3a, 3b and 3e (top-up, extra Treasuries, margin, room under the top, net asset value, forward rates).** A non-specialist judge will mark clarity down even though the ideas are right. Fix: use one name for the money in each place (the first $39,162 of the 2028 deposit), cut "top-up" and "margin" from prose and keep them in the table notes, define "a Treasury fund" once and then say "the 2032 fund", and give every part at most three figures as the plan intends.
3. **The Reflection may carry too little of the team's own voice and conflict after a very technical report.** The conceit is also heard in every part. Fix: let 3f be the only place with Darren, Eric and Ray's actual words and arguments, with one real disagreement told as it happened, and thin the pencil-and-ink turns to Section 2, 3c and 3e so the metaphor stays fresh to the end.
