# Reader 1: a co-sponsor (head of giving, family-owned company in Taiwan)

## 1. What you would book

**US$115,000, "committed"**, but only once I have the signed pledge letter and have seen the statement at the 2031 meeting. It is not "secured" or "received" until the cash arrives in January 2033. I book signed pledges as committed and nothing else, and these notes give me a signed letter that names the amount, the purpose and the date. That is enough.

Two qualifications my board will insist on:
- **Currency.** It is a US-dollar gift and our budget is in NT$. In our plan I would carry it at a haircut to that day's rate (about 10% under), because a stronger NT$ between 2031 and 2033 makes her gift smaller in our money. The notes say she means to keep the NT$ value whole "from her flexibility" but will never put that in the letter. An intention I am never told is no use to me.
- **Before the letter.** If I heard only the words (the range and the figure) without the signed letter, I would plan on **$95,000 as "expected"** and treat the other $20,000 as upside. Funders book the low end of any range by reflex.

## 2. Belief

Yes, mostly. These are the most believable words a founder could give me, for one reason: the money already exists and I can look at it. The strongest point in the notes is not the building figure. It is that ten years of running money, $50,000 a year from 2033 to 2042, is "bought from January 2027 and complete since January 2028." Venues I have funded have struggled with running costs, not with the building. A founder who has already paid for the running years is someone I take seriously.

**What would make me believe it more:**
- Seeing the statement myself, and being told in advance how to read it (see section 4).
- One sentence saying that her gift does not depend on ours ("no matching condition; she pays first"). The notes have this. Keep it.
- A line saying that if anything ever left the fund short of $115,000, she would make up the difference from her own money. That is stronger than saying nothing.

**What would make me suspicious:**
- A founder saying "certain" about money two years away. The notes handle this well by putting the holding first and the word after it ("held for me in US Treasuries since ...").
- Finding out for myself that the money sits in a fund, not in bonds held directly, after she had said "US Treasuries" with nothing more. My finance colleague will look up the fund named in the letter. If her words said "US Treasuries" and the letter says an ETF, I would feel I had been given the polished version.
- A statement showing less than $115,000 when nobody has explained why (section 4).

## 3. What is missing

1. **What happens if the project changes.** If the residency opens late, past January 2033, does the money wait or get paid anyway? Who receives it if the Taiwan entity does not yet exist? If it never goes ahead, is the pledge withdrawn? If she dies before January 2033, does the pledge bind her estate? My board will ask these questions first, and the notes rule out "conditions" altogether without separating market conditions (which I do not need) from life and project conditions (which I do).
2. **The whole picture her figure sits in.** What the facility costs, how much has already been raised, what share $115,000 is, and what exactly she is asking us for. Without the total, $115,000 tells me she is committed but not whether the building is viable. A theatre refit we funded cost far more than this.
3. **How to read the statement, and the NT$ terms.** What the statement will show in 2031, what the fund pays in December 2032, and why the two differ. Also which day's exchange rate the NT$ figure uses (2031's, not 6 November 2026's) and who bears the currency swing.

## 4. What you would mishear

- **"Her two figures with their dates"** / **"The figure to plan on and the range the case asks for."** If she gives me $95,000 and $115,000 and then signs for $115,000, I will ask what the $95,000 is for. However it is worded ("the part held since January 2028"), a lower number next to a higher one tells a funder that the lower one is the safe one. I would put $95,000 in the board paper and $115,000 in a footnote.
- **"Her contribution is the lower of what the fund pays and the stated top."** If any version of "the lower of" reaches her words, I would read it as "up to $115,000", meaning the figure can shrink. That contradicts "certain".
- **"its payout is not fixed to the dollar"** next to "guaranteed" and "certain." If her letter names the fund and my finance colleague reads that the payout is not fixed, the strong words lose credibility. She should say "a fund that holds Treasuries maturing in 2032" herself, before we find it.
- **"co-sponsors see a statement at the 2031 meetings."** A statement in 2031 shows what the fund is worth that day. For a bond fund that pays out in December 2032, I would expect that to be below what it pays at the end, possibly below $115,000. If I see, say, $110,000 on a statement for a $115,000 pledge and no one has told me why, my board has its surprise and her credibility takes the cost. This needs one plain sentence next to the statement.
- **"about $615,000."** Adding ten years of running costs to a building gift reads like a headline built to look large. Give the two numbers separately. The running-cost commitment is impressive on its own and does not need inflating.
- **"Before January 2031 she can tell the residency's own planners 'at least $95,000'."** The arts world in Taiwan is small and planners talk to sponsors. If I first hear "at least $95,000" from staff and then "$115,000" from her, I will wonder which one is real.
- **"Lehman Brothers' own $10 million pledge to Spelman."** This is fine in the team's part for judges. If it reached her words, a failed bank in a fundraising pitch would read as bad luck invited in.

## 5. Odds and conditions

**Odds: keeping them out is right.** My board does not want a probability from a founder. "91 in 100" would be heard as "a 9% chance of a surprise", even though the notes say that once the money is moved no odds are left. If nothing is uncertain, say it plainly and show the holding.

**Conditions: half right.** I do not need fund costs, broker failure or a US default in front of me. One line saying where the money is held and that she covers any shortfall herself would do. But the notes treat "no condition in her words" as a blanket rule, and that drops the conditions I actually care about: delay, cancellation and her death. A pledge letter with no terms for those does not look clean to me. It looks unfinished, and my board would send it back.

## 6. Order

**Figure first, then the range.** I book the figure she signs for, so I want to read it first, with its date and form: "$115,000, in a signed letter, paid January 2033." If the range comes first, I fix on $95,000 and the signed figure sounds like an upsell. If the range has to appear, put it after the figure and describe it as two parts that add up ($95,000 held since January 2028, plus about $20,000 added in January 2031). Do not present it as a spread of possible outcomes.

## 7. Scores

- **Clear what she will give: 4.** $115,000, in writing, paid January 2033 is clear. Putting the range beside it muddies which number I am meant to plan on.
- **Credible: 4.** Money already held, running costs already bought and statements I can check make this better than any founder pledge I usually see. It loses a point for the gap between "Treasuries" and "a fund", and for a statement that may show less than the pledge with no explanation.
- **Would survive my board: 3.** They would accept her gift as committed. Then they would ask what happens if the project is delayed or cancelled, or if she dies, what the whole budget is, and who bears the currency swing. The notes answer none of these, so I would come back from the first meeting with homework.

## 8. One change

Replace the blanket rule "No condition in her words" with one that keeps market conditions out but has the letter state, in one plain sentence each, **what happens to the $115,000 if the residency opens after January 2033, if it does not go ahead, and if she dies before paying (the pledge binds her estate).** A family board that hates surprises cares about those three events, not about bond markets.
