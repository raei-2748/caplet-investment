# Reader 2: foundation programme officer

## 1. What you would book

**$115,000, committed, payable January 2033**, once I hold the signed pledge letter (amount, purpose, payment date, no matching condition). Until the letter arrives it is **expected**, not committed. I would never call it **secured**. Nothing is in escrow and no one holds a lien. The Treasuries sit in her own account and she could sell them tomorrow. In the docket memo I would count it at face value, because the payment date is fixed and close. In the evidence line I would note that $95,000 of it has been held since January 2028 and all of it since January 2031, "per statement".

The $500,000 of running money (2033-2042) goes in the memo as **founder-funded, expected**. It moves to committed only if it is also in writing, either in the same letter or in a second one. The notes put only the $115,000 in the letter. If the decade is the viability signal the notes say it is (section 4), it should be signed too.

I would not plan on $95,000, provided her words lead with $115,000 and present the $95,000 as one part of it. If she says "between $95,000 and $115,000", my finance office plans on $95,000, and so do I.

## 2. Belief

Yes, more than for most founders. The founders who over-promise to us are the ones whose money arrives later, from future income, a sale or a liquidity event. Here the money is held before she signs, the ten years of running costs are already bought, she pays without a match, and if every share went to zero the building would still get $95,000. That is a better pledge than most of what reaches my docket.

**What would make me believe it more:**
- A statement my finance office can read itself, with the facility lot shown apart from the 2033 running-cost lot.
- The letter binding her estate.
- A named payee.
- A line showing that the Treasuries pay more than she has pledged.

**What would make me suspicious:**
- The word "certain" from the founder before I have seen a document.
- A range and "certain" in the same breath.
- A total given as "about".
- Any figure that differs between the first and second meetings.
- Learning from a fund document, rather than from her, that the payout "is not fixed to the dollar".

## 3. What is missing

1. **What keeps the pledge standing if her life or the project changes.** I need to know who receives the money: a registered body in Taiwan, or a US fiscal sponsor. As a US private foundation I need to know that before I can fund a foreign grantee at all. I also need to know whether the pledge binds her estate, whether it is irrevocable, and what happens to the $115,000 if the opening slips past January 2033 or the residency does not open. The notes cover market risk in detail and are silent on the risks that actually break individual pledges: death, incapacity, divorce, delay and a change of plan.
2. **The budget her figure sits in.** I need the facility's cost in NT$ and US$, the share that $115,000 covers, the gap co-sponsors are asked to fill, and who carries the currency difference between a US$ pledge and an NT$ building. Item 7, "what she asks of them", cannot be judged without it. My board sizes our grant against the gap, not against her pledge.
3. **A due-diligence sheet behind the pitch.** One page: the holding by lot, what the facility lot pays in December 2032 next to its market value today, and the four ways a stated figure could still fall short, with who covers each. I write a risk section in every docket memo, and I would rather copy hers than build it from a fund prospectus.

## 4. What you would mishear

- **"Her two figures with their dates"** next to **"Held, so certain; no odds."** If everything is held by the 2031 meetings, a range reads as doubt and "certain" reads as salesmanship. Together they contradict each other. Say the $95,000 as history ("held since January 2028") and the $115,000 as the pledge, never as two ends of an outcome.
- **"co-sponsors see a statement at the 2031 meetings."** A bond fund two years from maturity shows a market value below what it will pay out. If the statement says something like $109,000 and the letter says $115,000, my finance office will flag it as under-covered. Add a second problem: one fund holds two lots ("One fund, two lots"), so a single IBTM line covers both the 2033 running payment and the building. Unless the statement splits the lots and shows the December 2032 payout, it will cost her credibility on the very document meant to earn it.
- **"pays about 15 December 2032 whatever it then has; its payout is not fixed to the dollar."** Keeping this out of her words is fine. But my finance office will read it in the fund's own documents. If she said "certain" and never showed that the pledge sits below what the fund holds, I will wonder what else was left out.
- **"about $615,000."** Ten payments of $50,000 plus $115,000 is exactly $615,000. "About" on a commitment total makes me ask which part is soft.
- **"she pays first."** If construction needs co-sponsor money in 2031-2032 and her $115,000 arrives in January 2033, she pays last in time. I would read "first" as a date claim and then check it. Say "without waiting for anyone to match it."
- **"Held for me in US Treasuries."** This is accurate, and I read it as accurate: the money is hers, not set aside for the residency. That is fine as long as nothing near it implies otherwise.

## 5. Odds and conditions

Odds out of her words: **right.** By 2031 there are none to give. A founder's probability is heard as a sales figure, and my board would ask who computed it. The 91 and 57 in 100 belong to the years before the move and to the judges.

Conditions kept out of everything she hands me: **wrong for my role.** I do not want conditions in the pitch paragraph, and the figure itself should carry none. I do want them in an annex, framed as cover rather than as trims ("pledged below what the Treasuries pay", not "could be cut by 0.5%"). If I find a condition myself, that does more damage than if she had told me. And the conditions my board actually asks about are not market ones: estate, delay and the project not opening. Those belong in the letter.

## 6. Order

**The figure first, then where it came from.** My memo has one line for "founder commitment", and the first number I hear is the one I write down. Range first anchors the board on $95,000 and reads as a margin for error, which is how we read every range we see. If the range must appear, tell it as a history: "In 2028 I could promise $95,000. Since January 2031 I can promise $115,000, and that is what I have signed."

## 7. Scores

- **Clarity of what she will give: 4.** $115,000 in January 2033, signed, comes through. It loses a point for the risk that the range, if said as a range, sets my planning figure at $95,000.
- **Credibility: 4.** The money is held before she signs, she pays without a match, and the decade is already bought. It loses a point because the holding is revocable and in her own account, and the planned statement will show less than the pledge unless it is built for a funder to read.
- **Would survive my board: 3.** The pledge itself survives. The docket memo does not, yet: no payee, no estate clause, no budget or gap, and nothing on a late or failed opening.

## 8. One change

Write the pledge letter's terms into section 5, not just "amount, purpose, payment date". The letter should also name the payee, bind her estate, and say what happens to the $115,000 if the residency opens after January 2033 or not at all. It should come with a statement that shows the facility lot separately, with its December 2032 payout above $115,000.
