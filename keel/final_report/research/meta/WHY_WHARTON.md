## 0. The five findings most likely to change how the Final Report is written

1. **The first reader has a stack.** In 2024-25, "24 Wharton students reviewed competitor reports" while "1,818
   teams completed the competition" [Source W3]. [Arithmetic] If every report was read once and shared evenly among
   them, that is about 76 reports each; read twice, about 150. Aberdeen's professionals read at scale too: in
   2022-23 they reviewed "more than 1,400 investment strategy proposals" (abrdn release, Jan 2023, quoted in B7a
   section 3.2), and in 2018 one Aberdeen manager "assembled a group of 13 other Aberdeen professionals" to read 160 reports for one region (B7a). 2025-26 had about 2,300
   reports, judged "with the help of our Wharton School student evaluators, as well as a professional review panel
   of asset managers from Aberdeen Investments" [W12]; how the reading is split is not published. Wharton's own older guidebook told teams: "Remember your reader:
   Don't lose the judges in a sea of technical jargon and rigid analysis, but rather weave in storytelling and
   colorful insight about how your strategy came together. You want your report to stand out from the hundreds we
   are reading." [Source W8, an older season's page that is still live]. So the report must work at speed for a
   rubric-holder, and the first page carries most of the weight [Inference].
2. **After the first screen, every reader who matters knows bonds and liabilities.** Aberdeen's finale judges in
   2024, 2025 and 2026 were a North American fixed-income manager and a U.S. credit specialist (R1 section 4.4, and
   [Source W15, W16]). The competition's academic director, Prof. Michael Roberts, teaches Wharton's executive course
   "Fixed Income and Credit Market Investing" [Source W22] and researches "the effect of interest rates on bank
   lending" [Source W6]. A 2026 semifinal judge, Andrea Vittorelli, sat on the 2022 finale panel as "global chairman
   of J.P. Morgan's Insurance Investment Group" [Source W17], and insurers are the professional liability-matchers.
   Two 2025-26 judges run or ran family offices [Source W13, W14]. A Treasury ladder will be read by people who
   will notice at once whether "immune to markets" and "without doubt" are qualified correctly (iBond ETFs pay net
   asset value, not par; coupons must be reinvested). Precision on bond mechanics is not a detail for this panel
   [Inference].
3. **The case was built so that the ladder is nearly given and the judgement calls are the test.** [Arithmetic] The
   ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042), valued at 1 Jan 2027 at one flat yield with annual compounding, cost $300,000 at
   about 5.09%; at 5% they cost $302,509 and at 4.5% $317,478. Repo curve-based figures are $292k-$303k (CLAUDE.md).
   The two deposits total $450,000 against $500,000 of payments, so cash alone fails before any facility gift. The
   case then asks teams to "define" a high degree of certainty, to say "how they evaluated" it, to give "a credible
   range" with a stated confidence, and warns both that overpromising "could damage her credibility" and that "a
   meaningful personal contribution may signal that the residency is financially viable" [Source W1]. [Inference]
   Many teams will reach a ladder. The ranking will come from the definition of certainty, the size and shape of the
   range, the honesty of the confidence statement, and the co-sponsor text.
4. **Wharton now polices authenticity hard, and says where it most wants the students' own voice.** The rules ban
   paid advisers and "non-Wharton extracurricular course[s] ... that claim to teach the Investment Competition" and
   reserve the right to use "third-party private investigatory firms and/or forensic analysts" [Source W9]. The
   ethics page names what teams must not do, including "invent teamwork and experiential stories" [Source W9, W10].
   Wharton's general AI guidance says: "Don't use AI for personal reflection or opinion-based tasks. We are
   interested in hearing your opinions, your stories and your thoughts." [Source W10]. A third-party site markets the
   competition as a "secret weapon" for Ivy League admission and sells course notes [Secondary W27]. [Inference]
   Wharton's biggest brand risk is a coached or fabricated winner, so a report whose experience section is specific,
   dated and plainly the team's own is safer and more credible, and generic reflection is the cheapest way to look
   like everyone else.
5. **What Wharton says it wants is learning, shown as client-first thinking, in plain words.** The FAQ calls
   "learning the nuances of investing" "our ultimate goal with this game" and says winners are selected "on the
   strength and articulation of their overall investment strategies and competition experiences" [Source W5]. The
   academic director's whole philosophy is "There's one finance. It rests on two intuitive principles: costs and
   benefits, and risk." [Source W21]. Wharton's older guidebook: "Evaluators don't want to read pages of defined
   terms as proof that you've learned something" and "Too many charts, graphs, photos, images and over-the-top
   formatting will not dazzle your readers, but instead confuse and overwhelm them." [Source W8]. [Inference] Each
   decision in the report can be shown as one line of costs, benefits and risk, in the reader's language, with the
   learning shown by what the team did differently, not by vocabulary.

## 1. Origin and stated purpose

### What the sources say
- **Start date and size.** Wharton's 2021-22 annual report timeline: "2011 - The launch of Knowledge@Wharton High
  School (KWHS) online business journal for high school students." "2012 - The KWHS High School Investment
  Competition launches with three Philadelphia teams and 15 students." [Source W2]. A 2018 Wharton story credits
  Scott Stimpfel, then Knowledge@Wharton's director of educational initiatives, as "instrumental in starting the KWHS
  Investment Competition in 2012" [Source W18].
- **The sponsor was there from the first year.** Aberdeen's May 2012 press release: Aberdeen "has partnered with the
  Wharton School's Knowledge@Wharton to run an investment competition for Philadelphia high school students"; top
  teams would "present their investment philosophies to the Aberdeen Investment Challenge Board at Aberdeen's
  Philadelphia headquarters" [Source W23]. A search excerpt of the same release ends "...to think about careers in
  investment management." [Snippet W23; the full sentence was not visible, so the quote is UNVERIFIED as a whole].
- **Financial literacy was the stated mission.** 2012: "The mission of KWHS is to promote financial literacy and a
  deeper understanding of how business works" [Source W19]. 2014, Aberdeen's CEO Gary Marshall: "Aberdeen is proud to
  support talented students and encourage financial education in high school curriculums ... The students not only
  learn how to wisely invest and increase returns, they also gain invaluable professional and relevant experience
  that can be applied to their financial and career decisions." [Source W24]. 2016, Aberdeen: the initiatives "aim to
  help students grasp basic financial literacy concepts, raise awareness of the need for improved financial literacy
  among youth and provide real-world investing experience through an international investment competition"
  [Source W25]. 2018: "The importance of financial knowledge is a key message of the KWHS Investment Competition"
  [Source W18].
- **Wharton's present-day words.** "Since the Wharton Global Youth Program launched its annual investment competition
  in 2012, its mission has persisted: empowering high school students with the knowledge and skills to be
  financially savvy and professionally exceptional." [Source W20]. The FAQ says advisers help students "achieve our
  ultimate goal with this game, which is learning the nuances of investing" [Source W5]. Mission of the Wharton
  Global Youth Program (2024-25 annual report): it "mobilizes the extensive opportunities of the Wharton academic
  community to educate and inspire pre-collegiate students to explore business practices, analyze the world's
  complex challenges, and take the first steps in becoming leaders who will transform the global economy."
  [Source W3]. Eli Lesser (2019): "The Global Youth Program is our platform for talking to high school students about
  the world of business and encouraging them to express opinions and ideas ... it is a way for the Wharton School to
  engage with students and their teachers in classrooms around the world." [Source W26]. Serguei Netessine (Senior
  Vice Dean, 2024): "Our investment competition is a cornerstone of the Wharton Global Youth Program ... It is amazing
  to see 11 different countries represented among the 2023-2024 Top 50 teams, reflecting Wharton's global reach"
  [Secondary W28, a local paper reprinting Wharton's announcement].
- **The competition page's own pitch to students** ("Why Join the Competition?") includes: "You learn about risk,
  diversification, company and industry analysis, and more investing basics", "You develop teamwork, leadership and
  communication skills", "You enhance your college and scholarship applications by building your résumé" and "You
  learn finance skills that will last a lifetime." [Source W6].
- **The academic director's mission.** Prof. Michael Roberts (2022): "We want to level the playing field for
  everyone by replacing ignorance and fear with knowledge and confidence." "There's one finance. It rests on two
  intuitive principles: costs and benefits, and risk. Every financial decision revolves around identifying the costs
  and benefits, and the corresponding risk. That's it." "They realize that the jargon and acronyms are just
  obfuscating the elegant simplicity of finance." [Source W21]. He is named on the competition page as "Academic
  Director" [Source W6].
- **Growth.** Three teams in 2012 [W2]; about 7,000 students and 1,300 final reports in 2021-22 [W2, R1]; 1,818
  completed teams, 10,118 students, 64 countries in 2024-25 [W3]; about 2,300 final reports from 79 countries in
  2025-26 [W12].
- **The prize is more Wharton.** Champions receive scholarships to Wharton Global Youth online programmes [Source
  W15]; the 2024-25 annual report counts "239 Wharton MBAs and undergrads hired to support Wharton Global Youth
  programs and competitions" and lists paid summer, online and college-credit programmes beside the free competition
  [Source W3].

### What I infer
Three purposes sit on top of each other, and what gets rewarded is what serves all three at once [Inference]:
1. **Education.** Financial literacy for teenagers: understanding risk, time, and a client's goals, with
   "knowledge and confidence" in place of "ignorance and fear". A report that teaches its reader something true and
   simple about money fits this purpose exactly.
2. **Wharton's outreach and brand.** A free, global, school-based programme that brings Wharton into classrooms in
   79 countries, uses Wharton alumni as clients and judges (alumni engagement), and leads naturally to Wharton's
   paid youth programmes. Wharton wants finalists it is proud to put on stage and quote in its newsroom: thoughtful,
   honest, human, international.
3. **The sponsor's interest.** Aberdeen has supplied readers since 2012 and wants young people who think like good
   asset managers: in Lesser's 2023 words on Aberdeen's release, "client-focused objectives, a long-term-investing
   mindset, thoughtful analysis, and strategic decision-making" (B7a section 3.2).

The overlap of the three is a team that shows it **learned to think like a careful, client-first investment team and
can explain it simply**. That is close to the FAQ's own sentence: "successful teams communicate their reasoning,
assumptions, tradeoffs, and competition experience clearly and authentically" [Source W5].

## 2. Who reads, and how they read

### The written screen (decides the Top 50)
- 2024-25: "24 Wharton students reviewed competitor reports" [Source W3]. 2025-26: "with the help of our Wharton
  School student evaluators, as well as a professional review panel of asset managers from Aberdeen Investments in
  Philadelphia" [Source W12]. In 2022-23 Aberdeen's evaluators reviewed "more than 1,400 investment strategy
  proposals" (abrdn release, 30 Jan 2023, in B7a section 3.2, marked verified there). [Inference] Many first readers
  are near-peers (undergraduates and MBAs), the rest working asset managers; both likely score against the five
  criteria, and they read dozens of reports each between early December and the Top 50 announcement (27 Jan in 2026
  [W12]).
- What an Aberdeen judge praised in written reports (2024): "It was positive to see the effort taken by the students
  to not only understand the client's investment goals, but also take into account the client's personal values,
  and use these insights to build some really well thought out portfolios." (Fionna Ross, senior investment
  specialist, abrdn) [Secondary W28]. Eli Lesser in the same release: "While it is an investment competition, we
  value seeing how both students and educators are empowered through financial education." [Secondary W28].
- Wharton's older guidebook page for the final report (2020-21 season, still online) gives the clearest statement of
  how Wharton's readers read [Source W8]:
  - "We encourage creativity and critical thinking. How can you communicate a compelling story about your teamwork,
    strategy development, analysis and portfolio development and any other critical experiences you have had during
    the 10 weeks of the competition? What will make your final strategy stand out from all the others?"
  - "We encourage a narrative format, rather than strictly a list of bullet points accompanied by charts and graphs."
  - "Overly technical strategies will not necessarily impress us. We want both qualitative and quantitative analysis."
  - "Remember your reader: Don't lose the judges in a sea of technical jargon and rigid analysis, but rather weave in
    storytelling and colorful insight about how your strategy came together. You want your report to stand out from
    the hundreds we are reading."
  - "Too many charts, graphs, photos, images and over-the-top formatting will not dazzle your readers, but instead
    confuse and overwhelm them. Make sure your report is readable."
  - "Evaluators don't want to read pages of defined terms as proof that you've learned something" (the page then adds
    "show, don't tell").
  This is an older season's guidance; this year's format and length are set by the instructions due Tue 10 Nov 01:00
  Sydney. But the reader's preferences it describes match every later judge quote (R1 section 0) [Inference].

## 3. How the design changed, and what each change tells us

| Change | Evidence | What it signals [Inference] |
|---|---|---|
| Returns stopped counting | 2015-16 guidebook: ROI "important" but "by no means the main determining factor" (T14); now "WInS is a tool for implementing your investment strategy. It is not the competition scorecard" (Guide p.3, B7a) and standings have "little to do with the final outcome" [W5] | Wharton removed the one thing a lucky team could win on; what is left is thinking and explaining |
| Stock-picking gave way to goals-based planning | Clients: Jordan 2021-22 ("long-term and a small amount of mid-term profitability" [W29]); Ash 2023-24 ("build her wealth from an initial $100,000 investment", new ETF requirement [W30]); Ayoola 2024-25 (housing units at "about $10,000 USD per housing unit", a youth centre in 2040 "estimated to cost at least $50,000" [W31]); Barwin 2025-26 ($500,000 toward "$1.5 million by 2036" plus foundation income, per Westminster's account [Secondary, T12]; "think beyond financial returns" [W13]); Laura 2026-27 (fixed liabilities, "high degree of certainty", co-sponsors, a range with confidence) | Each year moved further from "which stocks" toward "fund a real project for a real person, with other people involved". | The client is always a Wharton alum, recently a young one with a social purpose | "Since 2012, the Wharton Global High School Investment Competition has featured Wharton alumni as the competition client" [W4]; clients WG'08, W'19, W'13, WG'22, WG'23, W'18 [W4] | Wharton uses the case to show teenagers what Wharton graduates do: build things for communities. | The criteria moved from persuasion to credibility | Old: "visually pleasing presentation of data", "would win him/her over as a client"; new: "earn her confidence", "reasonable assumptions and projections", "under varying market outcomes", "clearly and credibly to prospective co-sponsors" (T12) | Charm and charts are out; trustworthy forecasts and honest uncertainty are in |

A specific, plainly true experience section protects the team; vague teamwork claims look like everyone else at best and like a breach at worst |
| AI became regulated, not banned | "You may use generative AI programs ... to help generate ideas and to brainstorm"; "You may not submit any work generated by an AI program as your own"; "how you use it must be recorded in your Works Cited pages" [W10]. Its checklist: "Did I verify the information ... Can I trace all of my citations ... Did I double check the numbers and information are most up-to-date?" [W10] | Wharton cares most about the students' own opinions and stories, and about traceable, current facts. Every number with a source and a date answers the checklist; the reflection and the opinions are where the team's own voice matters most |
| Scale exploded | 3 teams (2012) to about 2,300 reports (2025-26) [W2, W12] | First-read efficiency matters more every year: clear structure, the thesis up front, the criteria findable |
| The academic director is a finance professor who teaches fixed income | Roberts named on the competition page [W6]; executive course on fixed income [W22] | The case's bond-heavy design and its stress on "reasonable assumptions" fit his field; precision about rates and reinvestment is expected |

## 6. What this means for Caplet's Final Report: tests every piece of work must pass

Each test is a yes/no question. A section, chart or paragraph that fails one is cut or rewritten. These tests add to
the seven-point daily test in `keel/final_report/WHARTON_NORTH_STAR.md` section 7, which stays first; they come from
why Wharton runs the competition and who reads it, not from the criteria text.

1. **The stack test.** Could a Wharton student who has read 40 reports today state Caplet's central idea, the size of
   the operating reserve and the 2031 range after reading only the first page? (W3, W8)
2. **The criteria test.** Can a reader holding the five criteria find where each one is answered without searching,
   using the case's own nouns (operating reserve, facility contribution, financial flexibility, co-sponsors, a high
   degree of funding certainty)? (W5 "all five components"; case)
3. **The bond-desk test.** Would an Aberdeen fixed-income manager accept every statement about Treasuries and iBond
   ETFs as precise: net asset value not par, reinvestment of interest, the gap between each maturity and each 1
   January, yields with their date and source? (W15, W16, W22)
4. **The costs-benefits-risk test.** Does each decision in the plan come with one plain line on what it costs, what
   it gains and what risk is left? (W21; Guide p.5 "tradeoffs")
5. **The credibility test.** Is every promise to co-sponsors backed by money Laura already owns, with the confidence
   stated in numbers and words and what would move the figure? (case p.3; criteria; see META_ANGLES angle 6)
6. **The consistency test.** Does anything contradict the frozen IPS or the TNA, or quietly redesign the plan after
   seeing results? Where the IPS words were loose ("immune to markets", "without doubt"), does the report explain
   them honestly rather than repeat or hide them? (case p.4; Guide p.5)
7. **The human test.** Would Laura, a numerate storyteller who once ran a case competition, feel understood, and
   could she say the 2031 sentence to a co-sponsor in her own voice? (W15b, W13, W34)
8. **The own-voice test.** Is the experience section made of real, dated events and the team's own opinions, with no
   teamwork claim the team could not back with a specific moment? (W9, W10; CLAUDE.md "real facts and real team
   events")
9. **The show-don't-tell test.** Is learning shown by a decision the team made differently, not by defined terms or
   a list of tools? (W8)
10. **The one-chart test.** Does every chart answer one question the case asks, and could the report lose any chart
    without losing an argument? If so, that chart goes. (W8 "Too many charts")
11. **The traceability test.** Can every number be traced to a named source or model with a date, as Wharton's own AI
    checklist asks? (W10)
12. **The showcase test.** Would Wharton be glad to quote one sentence of it in its newsroom story about the
    finalists? (W3, W13, W15b: what Wharton chooses to publish is teamwork, client understanding and learning)
