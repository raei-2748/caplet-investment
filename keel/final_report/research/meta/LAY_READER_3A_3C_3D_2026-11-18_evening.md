## 3a: the operating reserve

**1. The answer, as I'd tell a friend.** The "operating reserve" is the pot of US government bonds (Treasuries) set
aside for the ten $50,000 payments. The first deposit buys bonds for all ten in 2027. Then about $39,000 of the 2028
deposit goes in as well: a small top-up of $2,388, because the ten cost slightly more than $300,000, and about $37,000
of extra Treasuries. The extras are there because each bond pays out a bit before the 1 January it's meant for, and
that money sits waiting. The plan is built so the payments still work even if the waiting money earns nothing at all.
The extras aren't really a cost: they're more like a deposit. If interest rates stay roughly where they are, she gets
it back with interest by 2042 (about $46,600, counted in 2033 money). Looking back over history, a plan that counted on
interest it hadn't locked in would have come up short in most start months since 1981.
**Headline number: about $39,000 of the 2028 deposit.** Clear, and it's the first thing the row asks for.

**2. What stopped me.**
- "the ten would assume waiting money earns the forward rates": I guessed "forward rates" means the interest rates the
  market expects later on. I couldn't see why that is worse than "today's rates" without someone telling me.
- "if cash waiting earns nothing, none beyond the 0.5% margin for fund costs": none of what? I read it three times. In
  the end I took it to mean that in the bad case the extras get spent on the payments and only the small 0.5% comes back.
- "short more often than not for start dates since 1981, far less often before": short of what, and starting what?
  My guess: if you had started this plan in some past month and counted on reinvesting the interest, the payments would
  have come up short in most months since 1981. Nothing says why 1981 is the dividing line. I guessed it was because
  rates have been falling since then.
- "each fund counted at what its bonds pay less its fee, its net asset value, not a fixed sum": I took it to mean a
  fund isn't one bond, so what it pays at the end can wobble a little after costs.
- "Damodaran's two conditions ... a state lottery's reserve bonds ... 26 U.S.C. 130(d)(2) ... Siegel's critique ...

## 3c: the 2031 range

**1. The answer, as I'd tell a friend.** In 2031 Laura tells the other funders a range for her building contribution:
about $95,000 to $115,000 if today's numbers hold. The $115,000 top is what she's actually giving, and the number they
should count. The $95,000 bottom is the part she has held since January 2028. She doesn't state anything until she
already owns Treasuries that will pay it, so whatever she states in 2031 is certain, not a forecast. A tiny 0.5% is
held back for the funds' running costs. Only if those costs ran past that would she give less than the top. What's
uncertain today is which range she'll end up able to state. It comes out lower if interest rates fall before the 2028
money is invested, and higher if shares rise before 2031.
**Headline number: about $95,000 to $115,000, top $115,000.**

**2. What stopped me.**
- "the plain figure is carried inside it as the top": which plain figure? I guessed it's the single number she'd
  otherwise give ($115,000), which sits at the top of the range.
- "Neyman's point, that a range once worked out holds or it does not and nobody knows which, except one built of
  Treasuries she owns": it took a while. I think it means a forecast's range is either right or wrong and you can't tell
  which, while hers is a list of what she owns, so it can't be wrong. Nice once I had it, but who is Neyman?
- "lower if Treasury interest rates fall before the 2028 deposit is invested": why would lower rates mean less? My
  guess: when rates are low, the same $150,000 buys less future money. That backwards relationship needs one plain
  clause, or a reader like me will assume the opposite.
- "the funds' fee from each trade day and the short-term Treasury ETF's while payouts wait": I could tell there were
  two fees, but I didn't follow "from each trade day".
- "the Antideficiency rule": no idea. I guessed it's a US government rule against promising money you haven't been

**3. Questions I'd still have.**
