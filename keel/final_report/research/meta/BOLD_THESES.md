## 1. Certainty you can check beats certainty you have to believe

**(1) Claim.** "Laura asked for a high degree of certainty, so we made the payments pass a test with no forecast in it,
cash waiting earning nothing, because a '95% chance' is only as good as the interest rate someone assumed."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** In finance, certainty is usually a probability: a simulation's "95% success" is how planners,
pensions and most written strategies speak. A zero-interest test looks needlessly expensive and unrealistic, and it
seems to throw away the simulation the team itself ran.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Deduction from the frozen text] IPS l.11: barring a US default, no payment "will depend on a share price, or on
   anybody else's money". Interest earned on cash after it arrives is set by future markets: a dependence on a price
   nobody has paid yet. A test true to l.11 therefore holds that rate at its bounded worst, zero, and lets every bond's
   own interest count (WORDS row 24: "if cash waiting earns nothing").
2. [Counterexample to the probability standard] US tax law's test for a charitable trust paying a fixed sum fails it if
   the chance of running out is "greater than 5 percent", worked out at the one rate in force when the trust is set up
   (Rev. Rul. 77-374; Rev. Proc. 2016-42; H3-066). When rates fell, trusts that had passed it failed it (Parks,
   Finestone & Leahy 2015; H3-067). A 5% probability at one assumed rate is a forecast in disguise.
3. [The same counterexample inside the case] Ten Treasuries sized only at the rates the bond market expects today, the
   reinvestment-rate risk TNA note 1 named, fall short in 55.6% of starting months since 1962; 1 in 20 short by
   $34,555, worst $45,266 from July 2007 (NUMBERS 2, design A).
4. [Limiting case] Set waiting cash to zero: the plan as recommended is $0 short in all 585 starting months and in all
   seven of the insurance regulator's rate scenarios (NUMBERS 2). The price, from January 2028: the first $38,282 of the
   2028 deposit, about a quarter at today's rates (NUMBERS 4).
5. [Analogy; disanalogy stated] Two fields already use bounding tests. Nuclear engineering's design basis names a
   function, a controlling parameter and a bound, analysed "in a conservative manner" (10 CFR 50.2; IAEA SSR-2/1 5.26;
   Q8a). Municipal bond lawyers call a refunding escrow "full cash" or "gross" when principal and interest pay every
   amount due with nothing reinvested (MSRB; Q31). Disanalogies: nuclear plants add probability studies on top; escrows
   hold instruments the competition bans and carry legal standing Laura's plan does not; and the test starts only in
   January 2028 (the ten alone are $54,595 short at zero in 2027, NUMBERS 8h).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Rev. Rul. 77-374 and Rev. Proc. 2016-42; Parks et al. (2015); 10 CFR 50.2; IAEA
SSR-2/1; MSRB on refunding escrows. **Connection:** US tax law and municipal bond practice answer the same question
("will this money last?") in opposite ways. The tax rule uses a probability at one assumed rate and broke exactly when
rates fell, which is the risk Caplet's own Trading Notes named. Laura's l.11 sides with the bond lawyers. *A judge could
look up:* Parks, Finestone & Leahy (2015), the "probability of exhaustion" test.

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 2 (design table), 4 ($38,282; its 1-in-20 range $33,884 to $54,679), 8h (reserve $444,545
against $399,089, Figure 1's note only), 8k ($45,456 comes back if rates hold). Store H3-066, H3-067. Cards Q8a (claims 1
and 2), Q20, Q31, Q4c (counts mislead across rate eras).

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** The bound costs real money, and in most futures it is never used. The 585 starting
months overlap, and most of them come from one era of rates (Q4c). It is not a guarantee: a missed deposit, custody, fund
frictions and a payment delay all sit outside it (Q8a claim 2). *The report admits it* in one place: the exception goes
in the same sentence as the standard, and the price goes in the next (WORDS row 24).

**(7) Lands.** 3a, as the backbone of the commitment paragraph; the MSRB name appears once, for bond readers. The
standard also goes in 2.0's lead. The tax-trust counterexample gets one sentence at most.

**Flags.** [W] Never "sufficient without reinvestment", "fail-safe", "guaranteed", "risk-free", "our definition of high
certainty" or "new standard" (l.11 is the definition). Never "the ten as bought would have fallen short": frame design A
as "sized at one assumed rate". Keep the design-basis idea but not the label (Q8a). [H5] Escrows' instruments appear
only as the disanalogy, never as something Laura could hold.

## 2. Promised money is leverage: a "moderate" 60/40 is the aggressive choice for Laura

**(1) Claim.** "The ten payments come first, so every share Laura owns sits on top of money she has already promised. A
'moderate' 60/40 would make her building money riskier than owning the stock market outright."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** 60/40 is the textbook balanced portfolio. Six years to 2033, and fifteen to 2042, look like
a horizon for more shares, not fewer. The world share fund (VT) is about $29,829 of a $450,000 plan (NUMBERS 4), which
looks timid and leaves growth on the table.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Analogy from corporate finance; disanalogy below] A firm with fixed debt payments gives its owners a riskier claim
   than its assets: levered beta = asset beta x (1 + debt/equity) (Hamada; Modigliani-Miller, via Damodaran; H7-028).
   Map the case onto it: the ten payments are the debt (fixed, dated, paid first); her facility contribution and her
   flexibility are the equity (whatever is left).
2. [Deduction] The ten payments do not move with share prices. So every dollar of shares carries its whole swing onto
   the money left over, and the risk of what is left equals shares divided by what is left. In January 2028, $111,718
   is left after the ten, the top-up and the extra Treasuries; the plan puts $29,829 of it in VT (NUMBERS 4). A 60/40 of
   the whole would put shares worth more than everything left over behind the leftover, and some shares behind the
   payments themselves. (Store arithmetic, to re-derive before any figure is quoted: about 0.23 against about 1.8;
   H7-028 [V].)
3. [Limiting case] With no shares, what is left is the floor's payout, $103,178 (NUMBERS 4). With shares equal to what
   is left above the floor (the plan, Perold & Sharpe's buy-and-hold with a multiplier of one; Q3a card 1), the
   contribution can fall to the floor but not below it. With more shares than that, it can fall below the floor, and
   past a point the payments are exposed too. The plan sits exactly where a total loss of shares still leaves the
   floor whole.
4. [Evidence from the benchmark on the other side, not a recommendation] More shares in the 2028 split (options C, D
   and E) add a median $4,000, $8,000 or $12,000 to all Laura has. The owned bottom falls from $95,000 to $65,000,
   $30,000 or nothing, and the contribution falls under $95,000 in 26, 62 or 79 of 100 simulated futures, against 3 for
   the plan (NUMBERS 8n).
5. [Restatement in the field's own terms] Surplus optimisation measures risk on assets minus what is owed (Sharpe &
   Tint 1990; H3-024). Seen that way, the plan's few shares are the moderate choice and the 60/40 is the geared one.

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Modigliani-Miller (1958); Hamada (1972) via Damodaran; Sharpe & Tint (1990);
Perold & Sharpe (1988/1995). **Connection:** the leverage formula of corporate finance, applied to a donor's own balance
sheet. Laura's ten payments are her "bonds outstanding" and her building money is her "equity", so "moderate" is a word
relative to what stands in front of the money. *A judge could look up:* the Hamada equation.

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 4 ($111,718; $29,829; $103,178), 8n (all three tables), 8h ($399,089 owed in 2033). Store
H7-028, H3-024, H3-016. Cards Q3a (card 1, rows 1 and 33), Q33.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** Hamada treats the debt as fixed and riskless. Laura's "debt" is her own spending, and
nobody can make her bankrupt, so the gearing acts on the contribution, not on her solvency. An unmatched 60/40 also
carries rate risk on the payments, so the store's 1.8 understates its risk (H7-028's own critique). The argument is
against an alternative; it is not proof that the plan's split is best. More shares do raise the median a little, and US
stocks since 1871 grew faster than the 7.5% assumed, which favours them (NUMBERS 8n, limits). *The report admits it:*
"more shares would add a median $4,000 to $12,000 and give up the owned bottom."

**(7) Lands.** 3b, where the floor and VT split is defended against the growthier alternative (the blind panel's open
question, NUMBERS 8n's preamble). One clause in 2.3. One paragraph at most; the formula itself stays out of the prose.

**Flags.** [H3] Say "shares only from what is left over" or "buy-and-hold" (Perold & Sharpe); never call any option CPPI.
[H1] Never "optimal". [V] Store figures (0.23, 1.8, $46k on $196k) are from an older register. 8n's options C to E are
benchmarks: the IPS (l.15, "most of the 2028 deposit") rules them out.

## 3. Name a figure only once the money behind it is a bond

**(1) Claim.** "Laura should never name a figure to co-sponsors while the money behind it is still in shares. Once a
number is said out loud, the shares behind it have to act like a bond anyway, so turning them into one before the
meeting costs a little in the middle and removes the chance of walking in short."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** Waiting earns more: shares held to 2031 have the higher expected return. Selling half on a
fixed date "whatever happened" looks like market timing, or like selling at the bottom. And buying the floor in 2028,
three years before the meetings, looks premature.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Analogy from dividend policy; disanalogy stated] Lintner (1956): firms raise dividends only to levels they can keep,
   because cuts are punished hard. Damodaran: firms that lock in fixed dividends "are in effect turning their equity into
   quasi bonds" (H7-060). Disanalogy: firms announce every year and have earnings; Laura states once and has holdings.
2. [Dominance] Once a figure is stated, meeting it earns trust and missing it costs a lot. For a promise, beating it
   earns nothing more (Gneezy & Epley 2014: a broken promise rated 8.32, a kept one 9.88, an exceeded one 9.77;
   Q25-001). So, for the promise, the shares behind a stated figure carry the stock's downside and none of the upside a
   co-sponsor will credit. A bond paying that figure does at least as well in every outcome that matters to the
   promise. Hence IPS l.15: "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries, so whatever
   figure you choose is money you already own."
3. [Counterfactual: the strongest rival, priced] Buying the floor in 2031 instead of 2028 raises the middle by about
   $7,400. But in about one simulated future in six (15.8%), and one starting month in eleven since 1871 (9.1%), Laura
   would walk into the 2031 meetings with less than the $95,000 she could have stated in 2028; from July 1929, about
   $23,000. The plan trails the rival in 59.8% of simulated futures and 68.4% of starting months: that is the price, stated
   (NUMBERS 8c).
4. [Evidence from threshold games; disanalogy stated] When the amount needed is known, groups coordinated and avoided
   catastrophe in 18 of 20; when it was uncertain, coordination collapsed (Barrett & Dannenberg 2012; Q11a-025). A bottom
   fixed in January 2028 is a known figure for co-sponsors three years ahead. Disanalogy: a lab climate game, not
   fundraising, though a facility budget is a threshold of the same kind.
5. [Rule from the source that defined the strategy family] Resets belong in the strategy's definition, written before
   trading (Perold & Sharpe; Q3a row 4). The 2031 step is one dated move, not a rule that trades on prices.
   Damodaran's fix for sticky dividends, a committed part plus a part set by what is left (H7-061), is the range rule's
   shape: a committed bottom and a top set by what was moved.

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Lintner (1956); Damodaran on dividend policy; Gneezy & Epley (2014); Barrett &
Dannenberg (2012, PNAS); Perold & Sharpe (1988/1995). **Connection:** corporate dividend policy and threshold games
together explain the timing of a donor's promise: commit, then speak. Nobody would put a firm's dividend board next to a
children's author's co-sponsor meeting. *A judge could look up:* Lintner (1956), or Barrett & Dannenberg (2012).

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 8c (the whole table), 4 (the range rule; the bottom fixed January 2028). Store H7-060, H7-061,
Q25-001, Q11a-025. Card Q3a rows 1, 4 and 34; card Q19 claim 5 (stating before the move lowers confidence [V: older
basis]).

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** The cost is real: the plan ends up behind the rival in most futures. The dated move
also sells half after a fall, fixing a lower top (Q3a row 4: a cost of the frozen design, not a defect). Lintner reaches
the store through a secondary source, and Barrett and Dannenberg is a lab study. *The report admits it:* "a bottom
named three years early costs some of the middle in most futures."

**(7) Lands.** 3c (when each end is fixed and why) and 3b (why the floor is bought in 2028). The 8c comparison can be a
row of Table 2. One paragraph.

**Flags.** [H3] Never "portfolio insurance", "CPPI" or "locks in gains". Say: a floor bought once in Treasuries, shares
that are only the remainder, one dated move (Q3a row 34). [W] Say "the shares moved into Treasuries before the 2031
meetings", never "the move" alone or "moved half".

## 4. Confidence belongs to the rule; the uncertainty is which range

**(1) Claim.** "We can say how sure Laura is that her contribution will land inside the range she states: certain,
barring a US default or a fund not working as its prospectus says, because both ends are money she already owns. What
nobody can know today is which range she will state, and that is where the percentages belong."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** Total confidence sounds like overclaiming, and trained readers distrust it. Wharton asks for
a confidence level, so a judge expects a figure like 90%. A range is usually a forecast, and forecasts are never
certain.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Deduction from the rule] Each end is what the fund's holdings pay with no more interest, less 0.5%, rounded down to
   $5,000. The contribution is the lower of what the funds pay and the top she states (NUMBERS 0, 4). So the
   contribution is at least the bottom (the floor pays at least that, net of costs up to 0.5%) and at most the top (by
   the rule). It lands inside by construction.
2. [Check] It is inside the stated range in 100% of 200,000 simulated futures, every starting month since 1871 and
   every starting month since 1962 (NUMBERS 8l, 8o; card Q19 claim 2).
3. [Separating two questions] "Will she give what she states?" Yes, barring the named exceptions. "What will she
   state?" A spread: the top, 1 in 20 low / median / 1 in 20 high, is $95,000 / $115,000 / $140,000; the bottom is
   $85,000 / $95,000 / $115,000 (NUMBERS 8o). Company guidance mixes the two questions. Laura can separate them because
   she speaks after she owns.
4. [Counterexample: ranges as forecasts] Company results land at or above the top of guidance 57.6% of the time, and
   investors price the top (Ciconte et al.; Q35). A forecast range is judged by its track record, but Laura states once
   and has none, so her range has to be checkable instead: each end named by what pays it (Q35 claim 1: readers discount
   optimism they cannot check).
5. [Theory] When interests differ, only coarse interval messages are credible (Crawford & Sobel 1982; H3-046). Laura's
   interval is not cheap talk, because it is a statement of holdings.

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Crawford & Sobel (1982); Ciconte et al.; Tang et al. (2015); Sah et al. on
calibration (Q35); Teigen et al. and Løhre et al. on how intervals are read (Q19). **Connection:** forecasting research
shows ranges are read as guesses; Laura's range is a different object, a receipt for owned money, so the confidence
attaches to the rule and the uncertainty to the date the rule is applied. *A judge could look up:* Crawford & Sobel's
"Strategic information transmission".

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 0, 4, 8l, 8o (range and contribution rows); the largest fund cost covered at zero interest is
0.50% (NUMBERS 8l). Card Q19 (claims 2 and 5, and the three things that lower confidence); Q35 claims 1-3; store H3-046.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** Confident advisers lose more credibility when shown wrong than unconfident ones (Sah et
al., Q35), so if a fund fails, "certain" costs more than a hedged word would have. The 0.5% cut covers fund costs only up
to 0.5%. "100% of simulated futures" cannot see what the model leaves out (a fund closing early, custody). Three things
lower it: stating before the move; a payments shortfall paid from facility money; a US default or a fund failing to pay
its net asset value (Q19 claim 5). *The report admits it* by naming the exceptions in the confidence sentence itself and
showing the spread of where the range will sit.

**(7) Lands.** 3c (the range and its confidence), 3d ("bottom committed; top owned and available"), Table 2's note. Two
or three sentences; it answers one of Wharton's named items directly.

**Flags.** [W] Never "guaranteed" and never a "definition of high certainty" of the report's own; say "simulated futures",
never "paths". Check the confidence wording against CANON A29-A32 before drafting.

## 5. Nobody can run on the plan, so the two reasons are the complete list

**(1) Claim.** "Every 'safe' portfolio that famously broke (a bank, a county, Britain's pension funds) had someone who
could demand cash before its bonds paid. Laura's plan has no one like that, so the only things that could break it are
the two it does not own: the 2028 deposit and the Treasury's own payments."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** SVB failed in 2023 holding Treasuries, so "it's all in Treasuries" sounds naive. Long bonds
fell sharply in 2022. And "only two reasons to change" (IPS l.17) reads like a confident slogan, an incomplete list.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Counterexamples] Orange County 1994, the UK gilt crisis of 2022 and SVB in 2023: each held "safe" assets and broke
   on a flow it did not own and had not named: repo lenders, collateral calls, deposits payable on request (Q3b;
   Q3b-034).
2. [Theory] Banks borrow short and lend long, so they are sound only while people believe it (Diamond-Dybvig, via
   Shiller ECON 252; H7-019). In Laura's plan no money is due before money arrives: every holding pays 0.5 to 10.5
   months before its payment date (NUMBERS 8h; Q8a claim 2). There is nothing to run on.
3. [Deduction] A price fall before maturity matters only if something can force a sale. Nothing here can: there is no
   borrowing (WInS bans margin), nothing callable, and no rule that sells on prices. Q3a row 33's four checks (who
   pays; owned outright; nothing forces a sale; the date matches the need) are all passed. "A fall in the market is
   not one of them" (IPS l.17) is the ability to hold, not a promise to try.
4. [Deduction: the complement] What the plan owns cannot fail it short of a US default. What it does not own is money
   coming in (the 2028 deposit) and the Treasury's payments. Those are exactly l.17's two reasons, so the list is
   complete by construction (Q3b-034).
5. [Limiting case in time] After January 2028 the deposit is in, so only one reason can still happen (Q7-031).
6. [Analogy; disanalogy stated] Defence in depth: "The existence of multiple levels of defence is not a basis for
   continued operation in the absence of one level" (IAEA SSR-2/1 Rev.1 4.10; Q8a-030). The two reasons are the two
   events that remove a level, so they are the right triggers for change. Disanalogy: a reactor has many independent
   layers; Laura's plan has few.

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Diamond & Dybvig (1983); the Q3b cases; IAEA SSR-2/1. **Connection:** the IPS's two
reasons, which read like a slogan, are derived: they are the complement of what the plan owns, and the bank-run model is
why a market fall cannot be a third. *A judge could look up:* Diamond-Dybvig.

**(5) Evidence.** Card Q3b (rows 3, 11 and 20; Q3b-034), Q3a row 33, Q7-031, Q8a-030; store H7-019. NUMBERS 8h (payment
dates), 5 (the payments' standard survives a 74.5% smaller deposit; a missed deposit leaves 2033 $3,016 short).

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** An iBond fund pays what its bonds are worth when it ends, not a fixed amount; its costs
are not capped, and its board can merge it or move its end (Q3a rows 25-29; Q36; CANON D5). That is the one soft spot.
Smaller failures sit outside the two reasons: custody, and a Treasury payment more than five business days late (Q8a).
Statements will still show price falls before maturity, and a co-sponsor reading one in 2030 could worry. *The report
admits it* in Table 3 (row 4 watches an early end, a merger, and an end moved past a payment) and in one 3e clause.

**(7) Lands.** 2.3 (the diversification and risk reframe), 3a (the reserve), Table 3. One short paragraph in 2.3; a
clause in 3a.

**Flags.** [W] Never "risk-free" or "fail-safe". Use "a Treasury default" for the two reasons and "a US default" for the
standard. Do not imply SVB's bonds were bad: its funding was the fault.

## 6. The share forecast barely matters; the Treasuries do the work

**(1) Claim.** "Change our assumption for shares from 4% a year to 10% and the top of Laura's range does not move: $115,000
every time. So a judge does not have to trust our forecast; the plan's numbers come from Treasury prices."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** A plan's results normally hinge on its return assumption. A judge expects a sensitivity table
with big swings and may suspect an error. The Trading Notes themselves cite J.P. Morgan's 7.5%.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Sensitivity] VT growth at 4%, 6%, 7.5%, 9% and 10% a year gives a top before rounding of $116,580 to $119,851. The
   stated top and the contribution are $115,000 in every column; only her flexibility plus anything above the top moves,
   from $24,648 to $33,873 (NUMBERS 4).
2. [Limiting case: the deletion test] With VT at zero, the ten payments are unchanged, the floor still pays $103,178, and
   the range is one figure, $95,000 (NUMBERS 4).
3. [Deduction] Why: the top is set in 2031 by what the shares moved into Treasuries are worth that day, which is the
   market's path (luck), and is then held in Treasuries. The forecast changes only the expected value of something the
   rule turns into Treasuries before anyone counts on it. Luck at a fixed 7.5% moves the moved shares far more than the
   choice of forecast does (Q6 rows 27-28 [V: older basis]; the current spread is NUMBERS 8o's stated top, $95,000 to
   $140,000 between the 1-in-20 ends).
4. [Counterweight, said in the same breath] The plan is not a protected note. VT ($29,829) is more than the floor's
   expected interest ($21,289), so shares at zero return $103,178 on the $111,718 left after the ten are complete
   (NUMBERS 4). It takes real market risk, but only on money not yet promised.

## 7. Her flexibility is worth something only on bad news; half is a choice, not an optimum

**(1) Claim.** "Keeping half the shares promised to no one is not indecision. A figure Laura states can always be raised
later but never taken back, and all three things the IPS keeps it for (overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor
falling short) are bad news that can only arrive after she speaks."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** Holding back looks like a lack of commitment and can signal doubt to other funders (Sahlman:
staging can signal doubt; Q5c row 8). A judge will ask "why half, and not 30% or 70%?" and will expect a model to have
chosen it. Money "for overruns" sounds like the contingency fund the case says not to size (l.106-107).

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Deduction from the case] The stated figure cannot be taken back (case l.114), and news on all three uses arrives
   after 2031 (case l.113).
2. [Theory] Arrow & Fisher (1974): when an act cannot be undone and the future will teach something, hold back:
   "underinvestment can be remedied before the second period, whereas mistaken overinvestment cannot" (Q5c-004).
   Bernanke (1983): only bad news matters to the value of waiting (Q5c card 1).
3. [Theorem; disanalogy stated] Amador, Werning & Angeletos (2006): when you value both commitment and news, the best
   simple rule is a minimum with "complete flexibility otherwise" (Q5c-011). The plan's shape is an owned bottom with
   freedom above it. Disanalogy: their commitment guards against one's own temptation, not a promise to partners.
4. [Practice in three fields] Lead donors sometimes come back, unannounced, when a campaign falls short or costs rise,
   and "you should not count on this in your planning" (Klein 2001; Q5c-015). NASA holds a reserve above the project,
   released only by the owner (Q5c card 4). Farmers hedge only 20-31% of an expected crop, never pre-selling more than
   a bad year leaves them (H3-086).
5. [Dominance fails, so it is a choice] Each $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,266 of what she keeps, and her
   total barely moves: a median $372 either way in simulated futures, at most $1,094 (NUMBERS 8o). Moving 70% raises the
   top to $125,000 but leaves her flexibility enough for a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in 50.0% of futures, against 93.2%.
   Moving 30% is not worse, just a smaller promise, with a range $5,000 wide or less in 13.9% of futures.
6. [Reductio on "optimal"] If one split were optimal, the totals would differ between splits. They agree within about
   $1,100, so the split decides who carries certainty, not how much money there is. Half is Caplet's choice in the IPS's
   order of cause (l.15); $115,000 follows from it.

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Arrow & Fisher (1974); Bernanke (1983); Amador, Werning & Angeletos (2006); Klein
(2001) and Kihlstedt; NASA NPR 7120.5E; Zulauf (farmdoc). **Connection:** environmental economics' irreversibility result,
fundraising practice, NASA's reserve held above the project and farmers' partial hedge all give one shape: promise what
you own, keep the reserve one level above the promise, and let no one count on it. *A judge could look up:* Arrow and
Fisher's "quasi-option value".

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 8o (the trade at the centre; "the total barely moves"), 8f ($15,423 median in years shares fell
against $23,315 in years they rose), 8k ($21,347; $29,823 with anything above the top), 8j. Cards Q5c (cards 1-5), Q5e
(the uses take turns), Q32. Store H3-086. ARGUMENT_LOG Sat 14 Nov.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** Her flexibility sits in world shares, so it is smaller in the years shares fall (8f).
Held reserves are drawn often and are sometimes too small (GAO: 23 of 53 major NASA projects overran by 15% or more; Q5c
card 5). The theory says keep some, not how much. One pot serves three uses, which NASA's own rules forbid for
commitments (A31; Q24). *The report admits it* in 3e: her flexibility may not cover a large overrun, and co-sponsors
should not plan on it.

**(7) Lands.** 3b (main); 3e (the limit); 3d (named as hers, never offered). One paragraph in 3b; Arrow and Fisher's
sentence used once.

**Flags.** [H1] Never "optimal", "the model chose half" or "a different split would leave her worse off"; never "a
natural hedge"; never "smallest when most needed" as a rule. [W] Say "her flexibility", never "half kept", "contingency
fund" or "buffer". Overrun allowances are percentages only, never Laura's building cost (case l.164; A31).

## 8. Residencies run out of running money, not building money

**(1) Claim.** "Residencies rarely close because the building cost too much; they get into trouble when yearly running
costs outrun yearly income. So ten years of running money owned before the doors open is the rarest thing Laura brings,
rarer than the building money."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** The case, and the co-sponsor meetings, are about the facility, so the operating payments look
like the settled, boring half. Overruns make the building look like the risk.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Counterexample: the best-known closure] Black Mountain College (1933-1957) had no endowment, raised its running
   money year by year, built simply with its own hands, and ended in debts and unpaid salaries, not because of its
   building (Q9 rows 1-3).
2. [Evidence across groups] New buildings rarely closed the groups that built them; they added running costs that some
   could not carry (Set in Stone; Q9 rows 4-7). Reserves, margins and dependence on grants predict closure (Q9 rows
   14-15). The typical US arts group holds about 1.5 months of reserves (Q9 row 13).
3. [Counterexample to "endowments solve it"] The residencies that lasted a century (Yaddo, MacDowell) used perpetual
   endowments, mostly in shares; a market fall nearly ended Yaddo before it opened, and MacDowell's endowment fell 15% in
   2022 (Q9 verdict 2).
4. [Evidence from Taiwan] Public residencies run on yearly government money: Treasure Hill's 2026 plan is 90.2%
   government (Q9c). The 2026 national budget passed on 14 Aug 2026, 351 days after it went to the legislature, and
   agencies spent at the previous year's level until then (Q10d row 26). Money voted one year at a time cannot promise
   ten.
5. [Deduction] Laura's ten operating payments are owned in Treasuries before they fall due (IPS l.11), so the facility is
   the only thing co-sponsors are asked for (SKELETON 3.0).
6. [Analogy; disanalogy stated] An endowment paying $50,000 a year forever under a 5% rule needs about $1,000,000; Laura's
   operating reserve is $444,545 on 1 Jan 2033 and runs to zero in 2042 (Q2c; NUMBERS 8h). That is Rosenwald's sunset:
   spend in a generation rather than hold forever (H4-045). Disanalogy: Rosenwald spent down to multiply through
   matching; Laura spends down because the case ends the payments in 2042. Precedent: Fogo Island Arts, where a founder
   who made money elsewhere put her own capital in first and governments joined (Q11b row 14).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Set in Stone (University of Chicago, 2012); the Black Mountain record; Yaddo and
MacDowell histories; Taiwan's Budget Act practice; Rosenwald on perpetuity. **Connection:** arts-closure research plus
public finance's one-year budget rule show that the plan's least glamorous part, ten Treasuries, is its strongest
fundraising asset. *A judge could look up:* the Set in Stone study, or Rosenwald's "Real endowments are not money, but
ideas".

**(5) Evidence.** Cards Q9 (verdicts 1-3), Q9b, Q9c, Q10d (row 26), Q11b (row 14), Q2c (row 35: after 2042). NUMBERS 8h.
Store H4-045.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** Closures have many causes, and the stories are of survivors and famous failures. The
failure that ended most residencies Q9c found was losing the site, which the plan does not touch. After 2042 the
payments end and the residency needs other money (Q2c row 35). *The report admits it:* "ten years, not forever; the
site and governance are outside this plan."

**(7) Lands.** 3.0 (the proposition no other founder can make), 3a (why the reserve matters), 3d (what co-sponsors are
asked for). One or two sentences in 3.0 and 3d; evidence in a clause.

**Flags.** [H2] The Taiwan facts are about timing (a late budget; spending at last year's level), and the cuts Q10d
records fell mostly on public media and TAICCA. Never say Taiwan's government cut arts money in a recession. [W] Never
"anchor" or "match" for Taiwan funders; no facility cost (case l.164). The 11 Nov funder panel was a simulated reader:
it can guide the notes but is never cited as evidence.

## 9. The textbook says a young earner should own more shares; Laura is the exception, and the case says where it bites

**(1) Claim.** "Finance textbooks tell a young earner to own more shares because her future pay works like a bond. Laura is
the exception, and the case's own rules decide exactly where her career risk lands: on the 2028 deposit, in a year when
the plan owns no shares at all."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** The textbook default (Bodie, Merton & Samuelson; Campbell & Viceira; target-date funds) says
the opposite. Ordinary occupations show almost no link with the stock market. The pitch line ("Laura's career is already
a giant stock she can't sell") can read as a slogan.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Name the default] Steady income is a bond you already own, so hold more shares; income that moves with shares is
   shares you already own, so hold fewer (Campbell, Cocco, Gomes & Maenhout 2001). Correlation reverses the default
   (Viceira 2001) (Q1 card 2).
2. [Why Laura is the exception] She has no paycheck and no employer to absorb shocks (Fagereng, Guiso & Pistaferri
   2018), and her income is business income, whose owners hold fewer shares (Heaton & Lucas 2000) (Q1 card 2).
3. [Evidence on the link: weak, late, real] Pay correlates 0.18 with the same year's share return and 0.37 with the
   previous year's (Q1b row 1). A correlation of 0.2 "is enough to wipe out half the benefits of trading risky financial
   assets" (Q1b card 4). Her market does not follow shares one for one: children's books slumped in 2023-24 during a
   bull market (Q12 row 17), and in downturns sales held up at the till while many authors' incomes did not (Q12b
   verdict 1).
4. [Limiting case: the case switches off the textbook's channel] Bodie, Merton & Samuelson's young worker can work more
   after a bad year. The case allows no money in or out before 2033 except the two deposits (case p.2), so her career
   reaches the plan only through the $150,000 deposit in January 2028 (Q1 card 8).
5. [Deduction: timing] With the lag, the deposit responds most to the 2026 share market and partly to 2027's, and in
   2027 the plan holds only Treasuries (Q1b-026). A share fall and a smaller deposit cannot compound inside the payments.
6. [Size] The payments' standard holds unless the deposit is more than 74.5% smaller at today's yields; a missed deposit
   leaves 2033 $3,016 short, and it is one of l.17's two reasons (NUMBERS 5). A smaller deposit lowers the floor, and so
   the bottom; it does not touch the payments (Q12b verdict 5a).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Bodie, Merton & Samuelson (1992); Campbell et al. (2001); Viceira (2001); Heaton &
Lucas (2000); Fagereng et al. (2018); Davis & Willen; Huggett & Kaplan. **Connection:** the case's "no other flows" clause
is the switch that turns the human-capital result into one dated exposure, the 2028 deposit, and the plan's
all-Treasury 2027 is exactly the holding that does not fall with it. That is the pitch's line made precise. *A judge
could look up:* Bodie, Merton & Samuelson, "Labor supply flexibility and portfolio choice".

**(5) Evidence.** Cards Q1 (cards 2 and 8), Q1b (row 1, card 4, Q1b-026), Q12 (row 17), Q12b (verdicts 1, 2 and 5).
NUMBERS 5. CANON D8.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** The link is weak and unstable (2009 fits; 2020 does not). A recession need not cut
yields, and the case gives no sign the deposit is at risk. The honest reason for Treasuries behind the payments is l.11's
certainty; the career explains why no shares stand behind any promise, not the whole ladder. *The report admits it:*
"her career and the stock market are linked loosely and late; a smaller deposit lowers the bottom, not the payments."

**(7) Lands.** 2.3 (the lead: the portfolio diversifies Laura); 3e (assumption: deposits on time; what to watch: school
budgets and speaking income from 2031). One paragraph in 2.3.

**Flags.** [H1] The pitch's "hedge" is frozen and stays the pitch's word. The report never calls the plan or her
flexibility "a natural hedge". [H2] On co-sponsor budgets: corporate giving fell in the same year; foundations cut late
and small; there is no Taiwan recession test (Q12b verdict 5b). Never say Taiwan's government cut arts money in a
recession.

## 10. "Safe" depends on what is owed: one issuer on purpose, cash is the risky asset

**(1) Claim.** "For money that must be paid on fixed dates, cash is the risky choice and a Treasury maturing on the date is
the safe one. So putting every promised dollar with one borrower, the US Treasury, is the point of the plan, not a
failure to diversify."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** Diversification is the first rule, and an evaluation habit. 89.55% of the portfolio at cost in
one issuer's obligations (NUMBERS 8g) looks reckless. Long Treasuries fell hard in 2022, and cash feels safest.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Definition shift] Measure risk on assets minus what is owed, not on assets alone (Sharpe & Tint 1990; H3-024).
2. [Theory; disanalogy stated] For a long-horizon investor, cash is risky because its rate rolls over, and the matched
   long bond is the riskless asset (Campbell & Viceira 2002; H3-016). Disanalogy: their riskless bond is
   inflation-indexed because they model real spending. Laura's payments are fixed in dollars (the case), so a Treasury
   fixed in dollars is the match.
3. [Counterexample inside the case] The reinvestment test hits money waiting in cash, not the bonds (NUMBERS 2). The
   holdings average 7.76 years and $234 per basis point, against the payments' 9.94 years and $300 (NUMBERS 1). They
   are matched payment by payment, with each bond's interest arriving early and waiting; that is what the extra
   Treasuries pay for.
4. [Deduction: issuers] Other issuers would add default risk against fixed dollar payments. The ten dates spread the
   holdings across time, not across borrowers (Q17-040).
5. [Re-scoping] The portfolio diversifies Laura, whose career is a concentrated, share-like risk. VT, thousands of
   companies, is diversified exactly where diversification helps: money not yet promised (SKELETON 2.3).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Sharpe & Tint (1990); Campbell & Viceira (2002); term premium (H3-015: she is paid a
little for holding what she would hold anyway). **Connection:** "diversified" and "safe" are both relative to what is owed;
the plan is concentrated where concentration is safe and diversified where it is not. *A judge could look up:* Campbell
and Viceira, *Strategic Asset Allocation*, on the riskless asset for a long-horizon investor.

**(5) Evidence.** NUMBERS 1, 2, 8g. Store H3-016, H3-024, H3-015, Q17-040. Card Q1c.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** One issuer makes a US default the plan's one named exception. iBond funds pay net asset
value, not par (CANON D5). A judge scoring "diversification" literally may mark it down. *The report admits it* by
naming the US default in the standard's own sentence.

**(7) Lands.** 2.3 (lead) and 2.2. Two sentences.

**Flags.** [H5] Never suggest TIPS: the reason is that the payments are fixed in dollars, not the ban. The Trading Notes'
"nonexistent inflation risk" is a loose phrase (CANON C): inflation is real for the facility (NUMBERS 8d), not for the
payments' dollar amount. [W] Never "not duration-matched": say "matched payment by payment" (CANON C12: the notes'
"duration-matching" means cash-flow matched). Never "rungs"; "dedicated portfolio" once only, in 2.5.

## 11. Co-sponsors count the bottom; she should say she intends the top

**(1) Claim.** "Co-sponsors will plan on the bottom of Laura's range, so the bottom has to be money she already owns. But
she should also say plainly that she intends to give the top, because after 2031 the top is owned money too, not a margin
of error."

**(2) Why a judge resists.** "Under-promise and over-deliver" is common wisdom. A wide range reads as unsure. Fundraisers
say aim high, and anchoring research says the top anchors expectations.

**(3) Chain.**
1. [Evidence] Analysts put 73% to 77% of the weight on the bottom of a range, more when ranges are wider (Tang et al.
   2015; Q35 row 18). Investors price the top of company guidance, because results land at or above it 57.6% of the
   time (Ciconte et al.). Laura states once, so co-sponsors bring one of the two conventions (Q35 claim 2).
2. [Deduction] Whichever convention a co-sponsor brings, it is right after the shares are moved into Treasuries: the
   bottom is committed, and the top is owned and available (Q35 claim 2; NUMBERS 8l).
3. [Evidence on promises] Exceeding a promise earns no more credit than keeping it (Gneezy & Epley 2014; Q25-001), and
   nothing can land above Laura's top anyway, because anything above it joins her flexibility (NUMBERS 4). So
   "over-deliver" has nothing to offer here.
4. [What the width is] Laura's width is about 0.19 of its midpoint ($20,000 on $105,000), against a corporate median of
   about 0.053 (Q35 claim 3), which pushes professionals further toward the bottom. So say what the width is: the bottom
   is the floor; the top adds the shares moved into Treasuries (CANON D1).
5. [Counting rules] Funders' standards exclude conditional pledges until the conditions are met (CASE; Q11b-032). That is
   why no match or challenge is offered on the top (Q19 claim 3: killed).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Tang et al. (2015); Ciconte et al.; Gneezy & Epley (2014); CASE reporting
standards; Mandel & Irwin on numbers over words (Q4c). No founder found states a range with a likelihood (Q18).
**Connection:** earnings-guidance research, promise psychology and charity counting rules together say Laura's range is
a statement of holdings, which is why "bottom committed, top intended" is honest, not hedged. *A judge could look up:*
Gneezy & Epley, "Worth keeping but not exceeding".

**(5) Evidence.** Cards Q35 (claims 1-3, row 18), Q25 (Q25-001), Q11b (Q11b-032), Q18, Q19 (claim 3), Q4c. NUMBERS 4, 8l.

**(6) Where it could be wrong.** Markets do reward beats (Bartov et al.; Q35 claim 1), so "exceeding buys nothing" holds
for promises, not market forecasts. The studies are of analysts and lab subjects, not Taiwan co-sponsors. *The report
admits it* by keeping the claim to promises and naming the two exceptions beside "she intends the top".

**(7) Lands.** 3d (the order of the co-sponsor passage) and 3c. A clause each; the passage's wording is Caplet's.

**Flags.** [W] Never "cap", "capped", "excess" or "overshoot"; never "backstop" for co-sponsors; never "anchor" or "match"
for Taiwan funders. Never cite Libby, Tan & Hunton (2006) or Tan, Libby & Hunton (2002): both are retracted (Q35).

**(4) Literature and the connection.** Long-run US interest-rate history since 1871 (NUMBERS 8e's source). **Connection:**

## 15. Our WInS portfolio is a scale model: measure what scales, calculate what does not

**(1) Claim.** "Our WInS portfolio is a two-thirds scale model of Laura's plan, like a ship model in a towing tank. It tests

**(2) Why a judge resists.** Judges expect the simulator's results discussed, and a $300,000 book holding a 2032 floor and

**(3) Chain.**

2. [Deduction] The rule (no promised money in shares) survives the scaling; the deposit's timing and the 2031 step do not,
   so they are calculated (NUMBERS 4).
3. [Evidence, measured against targets set before trading] All ten Treasury holdings at exactly their target units; VT
   sized from the cash left and bought last; bonds within the 25-basis-point band; $200 of fees, 6.7 basis points (NUMBERS
   8m; Q29).
4. [Reductio] If simulator gains were evidence, six weeks of VT would "prove" a plan built for 2033-2042. The case and
