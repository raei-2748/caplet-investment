## Part 1. The strategists: what each method was invented to solve, and whether Keel has the same problem

### 1.1 Richard Rumelt: the kernel, and the four marks of bad strategy
- **Method.** Good strategy has a kernel of three parts. "A diagnosis: an explanation of the nature of the challenge.
  A good diagnosis simplifies the often overwhelming complexity of reality by identifying certain aspects of the
  situation as being the critical ones." "A guiding policy: an overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the
  obstacles identified in the diagnosis." "Coherent actions: steps that are coordinated with one another to support
  the accomplishment of the guiding policy." Bad strategy has four hallmarks: fluff, failure to face the challenge,
  mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives.
- **Invented for.** Company and government "strategies" that were slogans, wish lists and goals in disguise.
- **Same problem for Keel?** Yes. The plan is a good timetable of activities (research, analysis, voice), but it does
  not yet say what the report's central difficulty is. Fourteen questions are a list, not a diagnosis.
- **Take.** The kernel in Part 2a. Test every planning page against the four hallmarks.
- **Source.** Rumelt, "The perils of bad strategy", McKinsey Quarterly, June 2011,
  https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy

### 1.2 Charlie Munger: inversion and checklists
- **Method.** Turn the question round: to learn how to get X, study how to get non-X. "The great algebraist, Jacobi
  ... 'Invert, always invert.' ... many hard problems are best solved only when they are addressed backward." He
  "sought good judgment mostly by collecting instances of bad judgment, then pondering ways to avoid such outcomes".
  "Use of the scientific method and effective checklists minimizes errors and omissions."
- **Invented for.** Investing and life decisions, where the costly mistakes are the avoidable ones.
- **Same problem?** Yes. The twelve lessons of the IPS (EXPECTATIONS 4.9) are already Keel's collection of bad
  judgment. Inversion turns them into guards.
- **Take.** Part 2b. The twelve lessons become killer items on the Fri 27 checklist.
- **Source.** Harvard School commencement, 13 Jun 1986, https://fs.blog/great-talks/guarantee-life-misery-charlie-munger/
  and https://www.stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-one ; "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment",
  https://fs.blog/great-talks/psychology-human-misjudgment

### 1.3 Amazon: the six-page memo and the PR/FAQ
- **Method.** Narrative memos replace slides. Bezos: "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with
  colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh
  mind. They simply can't be done in a day or two." "A great memo probably should take a week or more." Memos carry no
  author's name: "the memo is from the whole team." The PR/FAQ: write the press release before building anything, as a
  forcing function; under a page of release, up to five pages of FAQ. The internal FAQ asks "What are the top three
  reasons this product will not succeed?"
- **Invented for.** The memo: meetings where slides hid thin thinking. The PR/FAQ: teams building products customers
  did not want.
- **Same problem?** Partly. Keel writes no report prose before 27 Nov, so the PR/FAQ is used only as planning
  bullets about what a judge should be able to say (Part 2c). The memo's "set aside, fresh mind" step clashes with a
  single 24-hour session. The fix is fresh readers inside the session, and Caplet's own week after it.
- **Take.** The "top three reasons it fails" question on Tue 10; fresh readers each round; the Sat 28 handover.
- **Source.** 2017 letter to shareholders, https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders
  (SEC copy https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312518121161/d456916dex991.htm); PR/FAQ,
  https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process

### 1.4 John Boyd: the OODA loop
- **Method.** Observe, orient, decide, act. "Orientation ... is the most important part of the OODA loop since it
  shapes the way we observe, the way we decide, the way we act." Win by operating "at a faster tempo or rhythm than our
  adversaries". Fast loops at lower levels must work "within the slower rhythm but larger pattern at higher levels so
  that overall system does not lose its cohesion".
- **Invented for.** Air-to-air combat, then manoeuvre warfare (origin in air combat from secondary sources).
- **Same problem?** Mostly no. The judges are not an enemy, and there is nothing to out-turn. Two pieces carry over.
  First, re-orient when the world changes (Wharton's instructions on Tue 10). Second, in the 24-hour session, fast
  rounds must stay inside one slow pattern (the governing thought), or the draft loses cohesion.
- **Take.** The re-plan day; the governing thought fixed before Fri 27.
- **Source.** Boyd, A Discourse on Winning and Losing (Air University Press, 2018),
  https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Portals/10/AUPress/Books/B_0151_Boyd_Discourse_Winning_Losing.pdf ; Patterns of
  Conflict, https://ooda.de/media/john_boyd_-_patterns_of_conflict.pdf

### 1.5 Annie Duke: resulting, kill criteria, monkeys and pedestals
- **Method.** "Resulting" is judging a decision by how it turned out ("Poker players have a word for this:
  'resulting.'"). "The best quitting criteria combine two things: a state and a date." Monkeys and pedestals (from
  Astro Teller at X): if the job is to train a monkey to recite Shakespeare on a pedestal, do not build the pedestal
  first, because the pedestal is easy and shows progress while the monkey is the real risk.
- **Invented for.** Poker, and business decisions where people keep going too long.
- **Same problem?** Yes. Keel's pedestals are pleasant research days. Its monkeys are reinvestment, and stating the
  bottom of the range so it is true for an iBond ETF. Reviewers also "result": a draft that got approved is not a good
  draft for that reason.
- **Take.** Change 3; the stop rule on Fri 27; each research claim gets a state and a date for when it is dropped.
- **Source.** "Mental Models to Help You Cut Your Losses", Behavioral Scientist, 7 Nov 2022,
  https://behavioralscientist.org/annie-duke-quit-mental-models-to-help-you-cut-your-losses ; Thinking in Bets (2018),
  page UNVERIFIED.

### 1.6 Gary Klein: the pre-mortem
- **Method.** Imagine the project "has failed spectacularly", and have each person write the reasons independently,
  before anyone speaks. Prospective hindsight "increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes
  by 30%" (Klein, citing Mitchell, Russo and Pennington 1989; that paper not read: UNVERIFIED).
- **Invented for.** Project teams where nobody voices doubts once a plan is agreed.
- **Same problem?** Yes. Mock judges approved nearly everything in the IPS (lesson 6).
- **Take.** Part 2b now; a second pre-mortem, on the session itself, on Thu 26.
- **Source.** Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", HBR, Sept 2007, https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem

### 1.7 Philip Tetlock: the forecaster's commandments
- **Method.** Triage (work where effort pays); break problems into parts; balance the outside view ("How often do
  things of this sort happen in situations of this sort?") with the inside view; update neither too little nor too
  much; look for clashing causes; use fine degrees of doubt; balance under- and overconfidence; look for the errors
  behind mistakes; bring out the best in others; practise; and do not treat the commandments as commandments.
- **Invented for.** Forecasting world events in tournaments, where accuracy can be scored.
- **Same problem?** Partly. Yes for the range and the 7.5% figure (outside view, fine degrees of doubt). For planning,
  triage is the useful one: spend time where effort changes the answer.
- **Take.** Triage the fourteen questions on Tue 10; the outside view for Q6 (the shares) and Q10 (Taiwan costs).
- **Source.** https://goodjudgment.com/philip-tetlocks-10-commandments-of-superforecasting

### 1.8 Mission command and commander's intent
- **Method.** The commander states the purpose and end state, so that subordinates can "act to achieve the
  commander's desired results without further orders, even when the operation does not unfold as planned". Intent is
  read two levels up. Orders give "the results to be attained, not how they are to achieve them". Moltke: "no plan of
  operations extends with any degree of certainty beyond the first encounter with the main enemy force." Slim, on
  intent: "It should, therefore, be worded by the commander, himself."
- **Invented for.** The Prussian army after its defeat at Jena (1806): detailed orders failed when messengers were slow
  and battles moved. Codified in the 1888 drill regulation (per ADP 6-0's history).
- **Same problem?** Yes. Many Keel agents work for weeks without asking Caplet. They need the purpose, not a script.
  Slim's point maps onto Caplet: the intent should use the words of Caplet's own FINAL IPS, not Keel's.
- **Take.** The one-page intent of change 1, written from the FINAL IPS; every agent brief starts with it.
- **Source.** US Army ADP 6-0, Mission Command (31 Jul 2019), https://irp.fas.org/doddir/army/adp6_0.pdf

### 1.9 Eisenhower: "Plans are worthless, but planning is everything"
- **What he said.** "I tell this story to illustrate the truth of the statement I heard long ago in the Army: Plans
  are worthless, but planning is everything ... the very definition of 'emergency' is that it is unexpected, therefore
  it is not going to happen the way you are planning ... But if you haven't been planning you can't start to work,
  intelligently at least. That is the reason it is so important to plan, to keep yourselves steeped in the character of
  the problem that you may one day be called upon to solve." He did not coin it; he called it something he had heard.
- **Invented for.** National emergency planning (speech of 14 Nov 1957).
- **Same problem?** Yes. The twenty days of research are the "steeping". The timetable itself is expendable: Wharton's
  instructions and Fri 27 will not go as planned.
- **Take.** Re-plan days are part of the plan; research is judged by the understanding it builds, not by the boxes
  it ticks.
- **Source.** Remarks at the National Defense Executive Reserve Conference, 14 Nov 1957,
  https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-national-defense-executive-reserve-conference ; also
  https://www.eisenhowerlibrary.gov/eisenhowers/quotes and https://quoteinvestigator.com/2017/11/18/planning

### 1.10 Andy Grove: the cheapest stage, the planning gap, the fire department
- **Method.** "Detect and fix any problem in a production process at the lowest-value stage possible." Planning has
  three steps: what will be demanded, what we have now, and how to close the gap. "Today's gap represents a failure of
  planning sometime in the past"; so ask "What do I have to do today to solve ... tomorrow's problem?" Pair indicators,
  so pushing one does not wreck another. A strategic inflection point is "a time in the life of a business when its
  fundamentals are about to change"; "plan the way a fire department plans": you cannot predict the fire, but you can be
  ready for fires.
- **Invented for.** Running Intel's factories and managers' output.
- **Same problem?** Yes for the cheapest stage. A fault fixed in the notes costs minutes; inside the 24-hour session it
  costs an hour. Paired indicators matter too: rounds judged only by "fewer complaints" reward blandness, so pair that
  with "a fresh reader can still say the plan".
- **Take.** Changes 4, 5 and 6 move fixes earlier; paired measures in Part 2d.
- **Source.** Grove, High Output Management (1983; 2nd ed. 1995), read in a full-text copy, pages UNVERIFIED; Only the
  Paranoid Survive, excerpt, https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/72469/only-the-paranoid-survive-by-andrew-grove/9780385483827/excerpt

### 1.11 Ray Dalio: the five steps and believability
- **Method.** "Pain + Reflection = Progress." Five steps: clear goals, find the problems, diagnose them, design a fix,
  do it. "Focus on the 'what is' before deciding 'what to do about it.'" Use believability weighting "as a tool rather
  than a substitute for decision making by Responsible Parties". "Find the most believable people possible who disagree
  with you."
- **Invented for.** Decisions in a hedge fund, where each person's track record can be measured.
- **Same problem?** Partly. Keel's reviewers have no track record, so believability has to be earned in the Tue 24
  rehearsal (who caught the planted faults). The writer stays the responsible party.
- **Take.** Change 7; the writer decides on Fri 27.
- **Source.** https://www.principles.com

### 1.12 Less obvious: Pixar's Braintrust (film)
- **Method.** Experienced directors watch a film's reels and give candid notes, but "there are no mandatory notes, and
  the brain trust has no authority", which "liberates the director to seek help and fully consider the advice". Catmull:
  "early on, all of our movies suck", and the work is to go "from suck to not-suck". "People who take on complicated
  creative projects become lost at some point in the process."
- **Invented for.** Films going wrong in the middle of a long production while the director was too close to see it.
- **Same problem?** Yes. In twelve rounds the writer will get lost. One difference: Pixar's reviewers were proven
  film-makers. Keel's reviewers are not, which is why the rehearsal matters.
- **Take.** Round 1 is a whole rough draft, expected to be poor; reviewers advise, the writer decides.
- **Source.** Catmull, "How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity", HBR, Sept 2008 (copy at
  https://stem.elearning.unipd.it/pluginfile.php/197858/mod_folder/content/0/Catmull%20(2008)%20how%20Pixar%20fosters%20collective%20creativity.pdf);
  Creativity, Inc. excerpt, https://www.fastcompany.com/3027135/inside-the-pixar-braintrust

### 1.13 Less obvious: John McPhee (long-form editing and writing)
- **Method.** Settle the structure before writing, and let it come from the material: structures "should not be
  imposed upon the material. They should arise from within it." "Readers are not supposed to notice the structure. It
  is meant to be about as visible as someone's bones." Write the lead before sorting the pile of notes. "The way to do a
  piece of writing is three or four times over, never once." Research is done before writing begins. (Quotes read
  through secondary sources; exact wording against The New Yorker UNVERIFIED.)
- **Invented for.** Long nonfiction built from very large piles of notes.
- **Same problem?** Yes, closely. 1,317 ideas is a pile of notes, and Caplet's rule (no prose before 27 Nov) already
  matches "research first". The gap: McPhee settles the structure and lead before drafting; the plan leaves both to the
  week of 22 Nov or to the session itself.
- **Take.** Change 6.
- **Source.** McPhee, "Structure", The New Yorker, 14 Jan 2013, https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/01/14/structure ;
  "Draft No. 4", 29 Apr 2013, https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/04/29/draft-no-4 (both secondary).

### 1.14 Less obvious: architects and megaprojects (Gehry; Flyvbjerg and Gardner)
- **Method.** "Think slow, act fast": plan slowly and cheaply (models, storyboards, simulations), then build quickly,
  because delivery is when things go wrong. Gehry's office iterates on models before building. Pixar storyboards a film
  many times before animating (about eight, per a secondary summary). Reference class forecasting: judge a project by
  how similar projects actually went. "Think from right to left": start from the goal.
- **Invented for.** Buildings, bridges and IT systems that run over time and budget.
- **Same problem?** Yes. Fri 27 is the "act fast" phase; everything before it is "think slow". What the plan lacks is a
  cheap model of the build: a rehearsal. Keel's own reference class is the IPS, where writing began early, was then only
  tweaked, and panels approved too easily.
- **Take.** Changes 6 and 7.
- **Source.** Gardner interview, https://www.sap.com/blogs/how-do-projects-go-wrong ; MindTools interview,
  https://www.mindtools.com/blog/plan-with-purpose-my-expert-interview-with-dan-gardner ; reference class forecasting,
  https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068 ; Gehry,
  https://www.physicianleaders.org/articles/how-frank-gehry-delivers-on-time-and-on-budget (secondary); storyboards,
  https://lethain.com/notes-on-how-big-things-get-done (secondary).

### 1.15 Less obvious: investigative newspapers (Story-Based Inquiry)
- **Method.** Start with the story as a hypothesis to prove or kill: "We use stories as the cement which holds
  together every step of the investigative process ... hypothesis-based inquiry, because we begin by formulating the

## Part 2. Applying them to the Final Report

### 2a. A Rumelt kernel for the Final Report (20 days, Sun 8 to Fri 27 Nov)

**Diagnosis.**
- The strategy is frozen ("Your team may not revise its investment strategy after the submission deadline"). The report
  cannot win with a new idea. It wins by showing "the reasoning, assumptions, and tradeoffs" behind a strategy already
  written, and how well it was carried out (WHARTON_WORD sections 2 and 3).
- The hard part is not finding material (the store holds 1,317 ideas). It is three narrower things:
  1. choosing the few ideas that prove one argument, and leaving the rest in the store;
  2. facing the two honest weak points in the frozen texts without adding a rule: coupon reinvestment, and an iBond ETF
     that pays its net asset value, not par (CASE_MAP C1, C2);
  3. saying it so a judge follows on one reading, in a voice Caplet owns, in one 24-hour writing window.
- These are the IPS's own failures in new clothes: a fragmented argument, sentences people could not follow, writing
  Caplet could not own (EXPECTATIONS 4.9, lessons 2, 3, 11 and 12).

**Guiding policy.** One argument, the one already in Caplet's IPS, proved with numbers and history, faced at its weak
points, and laid out so Caplet can own it. In CASE_MAP's terms (a summary, not wording): the payments rest on bonds
she owns, not on hopes; the co-sponsors hear only what will be owned by 2031; half the shares are kept for what nobody
can foresee. Research time goes only where it tests or illustrates that argument, or answers a case requirement (A1-A13).

### 2d. The review loop for Fri 27 Nov (one 24-hour session)

**Frozen before hour 0 (Thu 26):** the intent page, Wharton's instructions, the canon file, the number register, the
argument tree and section notes, the ranked store, Caplet's own moments, the checklist, the regression log. No new
research during the session.

**Hours 0 to 4, round 1.** One writer reads in, then writes a whole rough draft from the notes, start to finish, fast.
Expect it to be poor and to be largely thrown away (Pixar; Brooks).

**Hours 4 to 20, rounds 2 to 12 (about 85 minutes each).** Every round, in order:
1. **Freeze** version N.
2. **Fresh reader.** Someone who has seen no earlier version reads once and writes, in their own words: the plan in
   one sentence; the range and how sure; what the team learned; the place they got lost. Compare with the intent page.
3. **Independent reviews in parallel.** No reviewer sees another's notes (MAP). The Bible first, then Wharton's five
   criteria (one reviewer each), then one reader for brilliance. Each scores and names at most three problems, ranked.
4. **Red team.** One reviewer argues the draft fails and gives the top three reasons a judge would mark it down.
5. **Killer items (DO-CONFIRM, five to nine).** Numbers match the register; nothing contradicts the canon file; only
   real events; no banned patterns (`keel/ips_rewrite/drafts/D35/README.md` list); within Wharton's limits; every earlier
   fix still holds (regression log).
6. **Consolidate.** One list, ranked by how many independent reviewers raised each point and which criterion it hits.
   The writer decides what to take; reviewers have no authority (Braintrust). One logged line per decision.
7. **Rewrite.** Whole paragraphs or sections; cut whole ideas, never squeeze sentences (Book 10).
8. **Blind compare.** A reviewer compares N with N+1 without knowing which is newer. If N+1 loses, revert and try a
   different fix.

**Phases.** Rounds 2 to 5: argument and structure (move, merge, cut sections; no sentence polishing). Rounds 6 to 9:
evidence, numbers, the co-sponsor text, Articulation. Rounds 10 to 12: sentences, read aloud, voice against the FINAL
IPS. Fixing structure in round 11 is the expensive stage Grove warns against.

**Paired measures.** "Fewer problems raised" counts only if the fresh reader still recovers the plan, the range and the
lesson correctly. Fewer complaints with a worse read-back means the draft got blander, not better.

**Stop rule (a state and a time).** Go to round 13 when two rounds in a row produce a version the blind compare cannot
tell apart from the last, and the fresh readers recover all three points; or at hour 20, whichever comes first.

**Hours 20 to 22, round 13.** Final checks only: the whole checklist, every number, every frozen claim, Wharton's
instructions read once more against the draft.

**Hours 22 to 24.** Margin. If unused: build the Sat 28 handover pack (claims-and-sources sheet, cut list, open risks,
a one-line log per round in After Action Review form: what we meant to fix, what changed, why, what next).

**Fresh eyes.** Rotate reviewers so each sees at most every other version; at least one reader each round is new to
the report.

## 3. Classify, then borrow

Ray's rule: "whatever problem you are tackling, there will always be a similar solution someone has attempted", and the
solution is "not necessarily finance related". The method:
1. State the problem without finance words.
2. Name its core type (a strategy problem, a communication problem, a coordination problem, and so on).
3. Ask who meets that type every day and has a tested fix.
4. Read the fix at its source.
5. Adopt the smallest piece that fits.
6. Run it through the plan's five questions: what it is; did it work; how our case is like it; how it is not; what we
   take.

Keel's ten hardest problems for the Final Report:

**1. A judge must follow the argument on one reading, though Keel knows far more than any judge.**
- Type: communication under the curse of knowledge.
- Best outside field: consulting reports and essay writing.
- Prior solution: one governing thought at the top of a pyramid, with a situation-complication-question opening
  (Minto); "explain to a friend what you just wrote. Then replace the draft with what you said" (Graham); the Curse of
  Knowledge as the named enemy (Heath).
- Source: barbaraminto.com/concept; paulgraham.com/talk.html; heathbrothers.com.
- Adopt: a fresh reader explains each draft back; mismatches go to the top of the fix list.

**2. Sounding human, and like Caplet.**
- Type: voice and authorship.
- Best outside field: long-form nonfiction editing.
- Prior solution: structure that comes from the material, lead first, three or four drafts (McPhee); read aloud and
  fix what you would not say (Graham).
- Source: McPhee, The New Yorker 2013 (secondary); Graham 2015.
- Adopt: the FINAL IPS is the voice sample; rounds 10 to 12 are read aloud; existing voice studies stay qualitative.

**3. Stating a dollar range and how sure, so co-sponsors hear it right.**
- Type: risk communication.
- Best outside field: intelligence analysis and science communication.
- Prior solution: ranges, not points (Kent); numbers over vague words, because numbers cost little trust (van der Bles
  2019, 2020).
- Source: CIA, "Words of Estimative Probability"; Royal Society Open Science 2019; PNAS 2020.
- Adopt: confidence as a number and a frequency; no bare "likely"; one reviewer reads as a co-sponsor.

**4. Twelve rounds in 24 hours without drift, or fixed faults coming back.**
- Type: iterative quality control.
- Best outside field: software engineering and film.
- Prior solution: regression testing; "plan to throw one away" (Brooks); reel-by-reel reviews with no authority over
  the director (Pixar).
- Source: The Mythical Man-Month; Catmull, HBR 2008.
- Adopt: a regression log checked every round; round 1 written to be thrown away; blind compare and revert.

**5. Staying consistent with frozen documents (the IPS and the Trading Notes).**
- Type: continuity and configuration management.
- Best outside field: film continuity and the military.
- Prior solution: the script supervisor's continuity record, which every scene is checked against (UNVERIFIED, not
  read this session); intent nested two levels up (ADP 6-0).
- Source: ADP 6-0 (read); continuity practice (UNVERIFIED).
- Adopt: a canon file of every frozen claim, checked line by line as a killer item.

**6. Many agents working for twenty days without the report falling apart.**
- Type: coordination, and conceptual integrity.
- Best outside field: software architecture and military command.
- Prior solution: one mind owns the design; a surgical team supports one writer; adding people late makes it later
  (Brooks); purpose given, method left free (mission command).
- Source: Brooks; ADP 6-0.
- Adopt: one writer; others research, check numbers and review; the intent page heads every brief; no new agents
  added after Thu 26.

**7. Choosing from 1,317 ideas.**
- Type: selection under abundance (triage).
- Best outside field: investigative journalism, software architecture and forecasting.
- Prior solution: a hypothesis to prove or kill, plus a masterfile (Hunter); "good features and ideas that do not
  integrate ... are best left out" (Brooks); triage (Tetlock).
- Source: UNESCO Story-Based Inquiry; Brooks; Good Judgment.
- Adopt: every idea is tied to a branch of the argument tree; no branch, no place in the report, but it stays in the
  store, so nothing is lost.

**8. A review panel that disagrees and catches faults, rather than approving everything.**
- Type: group judgment and bias.
- Best outside field: decision science and defence red teaming.
- Prior solution: assessments set in advance, made independently, overall judgment delayed (MAP); a rotating devil's
  advocate and leaders absent from some sessions (Red Teaming Handbook); believability earned by track record (Dalio).
  Seeding known faults to measure how many a reviewer catches is a software-testing practice (error seeding, attributed
  to Mills; UNVERIFIED).
- Source: MIT Sloan Management Review 2019; UK MoD 2021; principles.com.
- Adopt: independent blind scoring before any discussion; planted faults in the Tue 24 rehearsal; reviewers weighted by
  what they caught.

**9. Writing Articulation from real events, with nothing invented.**
- Type: reflective learning.
- Best outside field: military and wildfire After Action Reviews.
- Prior solution: four questions (what was planned, what happened, why, what next), with half the time on what to do
  next.
- Source: US Army TC 25-20; NWCG.
- Adopt: the questions go to Caplet in the Sun 15 digest about a few real moments (for example, the 7-8 Oct trades, the
  6 Nov IPS); only Caplet's answers are used; Keel's fiction never appears as Caplet's experience.

**10. Planning a one-shot creative build against a fixed deadline (the Fri 27 session).**
- Type: project planning under a hard deadline.
- Best outside field: megaprojects, architecture and polar exploration.
- Prior solution: think slow, act fast, with cheap models and many storyboard passes before the build (Flyvbjerg and
  Gardner; Gehry; Pixar); bullets before cannonballs, productive paranoia, wide margins, a steady daily march (Collins
  on Amundsen).
- Source: How Big Things Get Done (interviews and secondary notes); jimcollins.com.
- Adopt: the Tue 24 rehearsal is the bullet; about four hours of margin in the session; a fixed, capped daily output
  until then.
