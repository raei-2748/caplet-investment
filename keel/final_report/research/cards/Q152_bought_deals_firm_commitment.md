- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A firm commitment made before the money is owned is how underwriters lose money."
  2. "Laura's rule (state only what is owned) is a bought deal with no window."
  3. "In BP's 1987 offer, the Bank of England's 70p floor bailed out the underwriters."
  4. "Laura is the one promising and the co-sponsors are like buyers: she refuses to be a firm underwriter of money she does not own yet" (and the reverse reading: she IS a bought deal once the Treasuries are owned).
- **Problem class:** a fixed sum promised to others against an asset whose value moves until it is sold: who carries the gap between promising and having, for how long, and at what price. **Field that solved it first:** equity underwriting (the City's fixed-price offers with sub-underwriting, Wall Street's firm commitment and best-efforts contracts, Canada's bought deal from 1983), priced as a put option written by the underwriter (Smith 1977, as used by Marsh and by Breedon and Twinn at the Bank of England).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. True in a crash, wrong as a rule.**
  - **Losses happen in the window** between fixing the price and selling the shares: BP 1987 (two weeks), HBOS 2008 (a 57-day cash call with 8.29% take-up), Vivendi 2002 and ENEL 2003 (overnight deals).
  - **On average underwriters are paid more than the risk costs.** UK sub-underwriters earned an abnormal 0.74% of the issue over 1986-93 even counting the 1987 crash, and 1.24% without it (Marsh, via Breedon and Twinn). The 2007-09 crisis "avoided major losses ... unlike those taken in the 1987 market crash" (Rights Issue Fees Inquiry 2010).
  - **In a bought deal the bank does own the shares.** What it lacks is the buyers' cash. So "before the money is owned" is loose.
  - **Better wording:** a fixed sum promised before the shares are sold is a put option written to the person promised. Banks write it for a fee, over hundreds of deals, with hundreds of sub-underwriters. Laura would write it once, unpaid, alone.
  - **Judge's note:** the aha is not "underwriters lose money" (mostly they don't). It is that underwriters are paid to carry exactly the risk Laura's rule refuses, and can carry it only because they have many deals and a fee. A Goldman banker on BP: "You can only do that if it is a risk you can deal with."
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. The word "bought deal" fits; "no window" does not.**
  - **What makes a deal "bought":** "the lack of a 'market out' clause in the underwriting agreement, meaning that the bank assumes unconditional price risk" (Bortolotti, Megginson and Smart 2008). A US firm commitment keeps its market-out: SEC Rule 10b-9(b) speaks of "a firm commitment ... (subject only to customary conditions precedent, including 'market outs')". A 2025 agreement lets the banks walk away on "any change in financial markets" that makes it "impracticable or inadvisable to proceed".
  - **Laura after the move has no market-out:** "After the move nothing changes either end" (NUMBERS l.535). So "bought deal" is the exact word, and "firm commitment" is the wrong one for a finance judge.
  - **But every bought deal has a window,** overnight to about 20 days in Canada. Laura's has one too (January 2031 to December 2032). It holds no price risk, because what she owns is what she promised: dollars on a date. What remains is fund mechanics. The 0.5% margin pays costs, not the interest lost if the late-2032 fund ended early: $4,589 at worst with waiting cash at zero, nothing short at today's forwards (NUMBERS 3).
  - **Better:** "she sells first and promises second", or "a bought deal whose stock is the promise itself".
  - **Judge's note:** she gives co-sponsors a bought deal's certainty without ever standing where the banker stands.
- **Claim 3: KILL.**
  - **The floor was struck 50p below what the underwriters paid:** 70p against the 120p first instalment. Lamont: "The difference ... is 50p per BP share." An MP in the debate: even selling every share at 70p, underwriters "would collectively suffer a loss of more than £1 billion".
  - **Its purpose was an orderly market, for one to two months.** The scheme closed on 6 January 1988 having bought 38,576,002 shares, about 1.8% of the 2,126 million it could have had to buy (Keel arithmetic).
  - **The banks still lost.** Goldman calls BP "the largest underwriting loss in the firm's history". The four US firms put their loss at up to $200 million after tax, a third of the estimate before the floor.
  - **Judge's note:** BP's floor was someone else's promise, struck below the price and good for two months. Laura's bottom is her own holding, at the stated figure, good to late 2032.
- **Claim 4: KEEP the first reading; CHANGE the reverse one.**
  - **If she stated a top before the move,** she would stand where BP's US underwriters stood: a fixed sum promised, shares in hand, no sub-underwriters, no fee. NUMBERS l.552 sizes it: a top stated 1, 3, 6 or 12 months before the move is short in 2.9%, 6.2%, 8.6% or 10.6% of house paths (1-in-20 gap $1,770 to $5,307). The City charges for exactly this: 0.125% more for every week past thirty days.
  - **Disanalogies.** Co-sponsors pay her no spread. She gives money; nobody resells anything. Nobody can hold her to it as the Treasury held the underwriters; only her credibility can (case l.114).
  - **The reverse reading.** After the move she is not the banker but the issuer who has already been paid. Securities law's nearest shape is the min-max offering: the minimum is sold and held in escrow before anything counts (Rules 15c2-4 and 10b-9). That is her bottom, held since January 2028. The top is fixed when the move closes.
  - **Taiwan.** 證券交易法 arts 71-72 name the two promises (包銷: the underwriter must buy what is unsold; 代銷: it may hand it back). Art. 81 caps a firm promise at a multiple of the underwriter's net current assets. Laura's multiple is one, a little under after the margin and rounding.
  - **Judge's note:** Taiwanese law already lets a bank promise more than it holds; Laura's rule is the strictest version of a test Taiwan's own regulator applies.

## Five questions for the strong rows

1. **BP 1987 (rows 20-28, 32).**
   - *What it is.* The world's largest share sale priced two weeks before trading, with the banks bound to buy whatever the public did not.
   - *Did it work?* For the seller, yes: Lawson held the banks to the price and the taxpayer got £7,017m. For the promisers, no: the public took about 3%, the banks about 94%, and Goldman took its largest underwriting loss.
   - *How Laura's case is like it.* A fixed figure stated before the money is in, against shares that can fall a fifth in a fortnight, with no sub-underwriters to share it.
   - *How it is not.* Laura states nothing until the move is done; she is paid no fee and no one can sue her for a building gift.
   - *What we take.* The order: own first, state second. And Gnodde's line on why: only a risk you can deal with.
2. **Bought deal against firm commitment (rows 1-4).**
   - *What it is.* Two contracts that both "buy" the issue; only the bought deal gives up the market-out.
   - *Did it work?* Bought deals became Canada's standard; the market reads them as a quality signal (Pandes).
   - *Like.* After the move Laura's stated range has no market-out.
   - *Not like.* A bank resells over days and carries the price; Laura holds the very dollars she promised.
   - *What we take.* The word "bought deal" if a finance term is used; never "firm commitment".
3. **Underwriting as a put option priced by the week (rows 9-11).**
   - *What it is.* The promiser writes a put on the shares to the promisee; the City added 0.125% per week past thirty days.
   - *Did it work?* On average yes: excess returns even with 1987 in the sample.
   - *Like.* An early top would be the same put, and NUMBERS l.552's rising shortfall by month is the same shape as the City's weekly fee.
   - *Not like.* Banks spread the risk over hundreds of deals and are paid; Laura has one deal and no fee.
   - *What we take.* "A top stated early is a put written for nothing."
4. **The min-max escrow and Taiwan's art. 81 (rows 36-39).**
   - *What it is.* Laws that count a promise only when real money stands behind it, and cap a firm promise by what the promiser holds.
   - *Did it work?* The SEC rules have stood since 1962; Taiwan's cap is current law.
   - *Like.* Her bottom is the escrowed minimum; her multiple of owned money is one.
   - *Not like.* No law binds Laura; she chose the rule.
   - *What we take.* In 3d, a Taiwanese reader already has 包銷 and 代銷; in 3c, one clause: the strictest form of a test Taiwan's own regulator applies.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 20, 23 and 32, 3c.** BP 1987 in two sentences: priced two weeks early, the public took about 3% and the promisers the rest, and the banker's lesson, "You can only do that if it is a risk you can deal with." It gives 3c's rule a real event that a finance judge knows and few teams will have read at source.
2. **Rows 9-10 with NUMBERS l.552, 3c.** A top stated before the move is a put written to co-sponsors for nothing; the City charged by the week for that window, and the plan's own figures rise the same way (2.9% short for a month early, 10.6% for a year). This turns "why nothing is stated before the move" into finance's own logic.
3. **Rows 1-3, 3c and WORDS.** "Bought deal" means no market-out; a US "firm commitment" keeps one. If the report uses a finance word it must be "bought deal", and the honest gloss is "nothing in markets can change it", never "no window" (row 40).
4. **Rows 38-39, 3d.** Taiwan's own law has the two promises in four characters (包銷, 代銷) and caps a firm promise at a multiple of what the promiser holds. Laura's multiple is one. It is client knowledge specific to Taiwan, and it gives the co-sponsors' finance officers words they already use.
5. **Row 27 with row 28, 3c or store.** The 70p floor did not bail the banks out: it sat 50p under what they paid and took only about 1.8% of the shares. It is an aha for a judge who half-remembers the story, and it sharpens what Laura's bottom is: her own holding at the stated figure, not someone else's floor for two months.
