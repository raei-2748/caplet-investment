- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** recognising a promise to give, when the paper is signed now and the cash arrives later. Accounting standard-setters solved it first (FASB, 1993; GASB, 1998) by splitting two questions a funder runs together: whether a promise exists (a documentation test, at signing) and whether it will be collected (a measurement and an allowance, afterwards). Gift offices and building committees added the third tool: cash or assets behind the promise before money is spent.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Keep, with two words changed.** The standards confirm the split at source. FASB books a promise when there is "sufficient evidence in the form of verifiable documentation that a promise was made and received" (FAS 116 para 6, now ASC 958-605-25-8) and looks at the giver's means only when it measures the receivable ("the creditworthiness of other parties", ASC 958-605-30-4). So a signature makes her figure countable and the Treasuries make it payable. Changed: "owned" becomes "held for her" (WORDS: what she owns is the Treasuries and the fund shares, never the figure), and the letter's verb is "I promise to give", because FASB avoids even the word "pledge" (ASC 958-605-55-18). Rows 1-6, 34.
   - Judge's note: a finance judge assumes the books test whether a giver can pay. They test the paper. FASB's one dissenter in 1993 said the line between a promise and an intention is really "the percentage of the communications that ultimately results in future cash receipts" (row 6). Laura's letter is where the paper and the cash meet.
2. **Keep, with the year depending on whose books.** Under US GAAP her letter as described (one amount, the facility, January 2033, words of promise, no condition, no right to withdraw, a clause returning unspent money) is an unconditional promise. It is booked in 2031, when received, at present value and as donor-restricted until January 2033 (ASC 958-605-45-5; row 11). A US public body books it in 2031 or 2033 depending on one sentence, whether the letter fixes when the money may be used (GASB 33 Examples 21 and 21a; row 13). A Taiwanese foundation books it when the cash arrives (Q147 row 30). A campaign counts it as committed when signed (Q200 row 1). So 3d says "committed in writing in 2031, paid in January 2033" and never claims the figure sits on anyone's books in 2031. Rows 11-15.
3. **Keep the parallel; change its frame.** The pledge was the bank's own. "Lehman Brothers Holdings announced ... that it has pledged $10-million", "a five-year, $10-million partnership", "the largest corporate donation in its history", and Spelman would "receive just $3-million" (rows 16-18). The bank's separate foundation "was not part of the bankruptcy proceedings" (row 17). So it was not "a pledge from a fortune". It was a promise to be paid from five more years of a company's business. That is the shape of a writer promising from future advances, which is IPS l.9's own reason for the plan (row 20). Spelman's public filings show the year's fall in pledges receivable ($21.1m to $18.9m) but never name Lehman, so the report keeps to the Chronicle's $3 million of $10 million (row 19). Nothing in card Q200 or H7-070 is wrong; ARGUMENT_TREE row 17's label "a pledge from a fortune" is imprecise for Lehman.
   - Judge's note: in the same collapse, "over 135,000 customer accounts containing an estimated $140 billion of property" were moved to other brokers (SIPC, 2009), while Spelman's promise from the parent stopped at $3 million (row 21). Held and owed, in one bankruptcy.
4. **Change.** In Lehman's case the money was expected later. In the Madoff cases it looked held: Picower reported "a $952 million market value of its investment portfolio on its 2007 tax return" (row 23), and Madoff's statements claimed "U.S. Treasury securities", but "BLMIS never purchased or sold securities" (row 25). No failed pledge read was backed by securities held at a custodian for the purpose. What survived was paid (Picower's $50 million to MIT, "a gift it has fully paid") or truly funded (Shapiro, Wachovia) (row 26). Say: "in every failed pledge read, the money was expected later or existed only on a statement; hers is in Treasuries held for her before she signs" (row 27). The disanalogy for 3e: "held" means held at a real custodian, which a statement alone never proves.
5. **Kill.** The IPS's two reasons are reasons to change the plan, not the only ways a promise fails (card Q109). By 2031 the missed-deposit reason is spent (Q90 row 36). A Treasury default remains. So do an early end of the late-2032 fund while cash earns nothing (up to $4,589 short on the top, NUMBERS 3), her death before January 2033, a dispute, and a delay at the broker (row 28). Her death is the one a gift office asks about first. Offices write such pledges off unless an estate pays (row 30). Gift agreements settle it in writing ("binding on the donor and enforceable against the donor's estate", UVA; row 31). So the letter gains a seventh part, an estate line. It costs nothing, since the Treasuries sit in her estate either way (row 29).

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 6, 16-18, 27 and 35, for 3d (the team's sentence before her words).** Proposed wording, for the writers to put in Caplet's voice: "Treasuries held for her are why her $115,000 will be paid; a signed letter promising it, with the amount, the purpose and the date, is why a funder can count it. Lehman Brothers promised Spelman College $10 million in 2007, a promise by the bank itself to be paid over five years; when the bank failed, Spelman received $3 million." Banned beside it: "secured", "guaranteed" (for the letter or the fund's payout), "a figure owned", "intends" or "expects", "lead gift". The one aha: the books test the paper, and what makes a pledge good is whether its money exists.
2. **Row 20, for 3d.** The Spelman pledge rested on five more years of a company's business, the shape of a writer's pledge from future advances. That is IPS l.9's reason for the whole plan. So the one parallel ties back to the IPS's first paragraph without a second parallel.
3. **Rows 10 and 14, for 3d's lead-in.** The letter as described is an unconditional promise under US rules, booked when received, donor-restricted until January 2033. Under other books it counts when the cash arrives. So the honest phrase is "committed in writing in 2031, paid in January 2033".
4. **Rows 29-31, for 3d's list of the letter's parts.** A seventh part: "this promise binds my estate". Gift offices write off a dead donor's pledge unless an estate pays. The line costs Laura nothing, because the Treasuries sit in her estate either way.
5. **Rows 25 and 27, for 3e (one limit).** In 2008 the failed promises either waited on future income (Lehman) or rested on statements that named Treasuries no one had bought (Madoff). "Held for her" means held at a broker and a custodian. That is the custody line 3e already carries (store Q65-032), now with its reason.
