- **Problem class and the field that solved it first.**
  - **The problem:** what makes a written obligation good: the paper, a signature, or the law and the issuer behind it? And how does someone who must rely on it see the backing?
  - **Currency law solved it first.**
    - The US Treasury moved from hand-signed demand notes (1861-62) to engraved facsimile signatures and a printed Treasury seal (1862 onward).
    - Statute then named the obligor: 12 U.S.C. 411, from the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.
    - Taiwan's Central Bank Act requires the bank to hold full reserves and publish them (arts. 16-17).
  - **Securities law followed.** The UCC's Article 8 separates a transfer by signature (indorsement of a certificate) from a transfer by notification (an entitlement order to an intermediary). Revised Article 8 dates from 1994 (card Q230).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with one change.**
  - **The signatures are printed copies, and have been since 1862.**
    - The last US government notes signed by hand were the 1861-62 Demand Notes. Even those were signed by "designated Treasury employees", not by the officials named on them (BEP, 2009; SPMC P4).
    - Facsimiles followed, and the Treasury seal took over the job of making a note money.
    - A 1928 Treasury memo calls the Register's signature "not required by law".
    - Printed signatures outlive their signers. In March 2026 the Treasury was still printing notes with the signatures of two officials who had left office (Reuters). Every note since 1914 is still legal tender.
  - **The promise is the law's.**
    - Federal Reserve notes "shall be obligations of the United States" (12 U.S.C. 411).
    - They are legal tender (31 U.S.C. 5103).
    - They are collateralised one-for-one (12 U.S.C. 412; card Q58).
  - **The change:** since Series 1963 a note carries no promise sentence at all, only "This note is legal tender for all debts, public and private" (ANS). So the bill holds two signatures and no promise. The promise is only in the statute.
- **Claim 2: KEEP for the Treasuries, CHANGE in one word for the funds.**
  - **The Treasuries.** None has a certificate (card Q230). Even when Treasury paper existed, its signature was a printed facsimile:
    - Series E bonds bore "a facsimile of the signature of the Secretary of the Treasury" (31 CFR 316.2);
    - a 2023 Treasury directive lets paper bonds carry "facsimile signatures of former Treasury Secretaries" (TD 16-54).
  - **The UCC draws the line itself:**
    - an "indorsement" is "a signature" on a certificate;
    - an "entitlement order" is "a notification communicated to a securities intermediary" (8-102(a)(11) and (a)(8)).
    - So paper moves by signature, and Laura's holding moves by notification. Ink means owned, and the law agrees.
  - **What makes the payments certain is law, never a hand:**
    - 31 U.S.C. 3123(a), the pledge (card Q72);
    - 3123(b), "The Secretary of the Treasury shall pay interest due or accrued on the public debt", which is new to the store;
    - the 14th Amendment and Perry (card Q194).
  - **The one-word change:** her iBond fund shares sit at the depository behind one global certificate (card Q230), so "no certificate anywhere in the plan" is false. Say "the Treasuries have no certificate". Whether that global certificate is signed is a Gap.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE, keeping the core.**
  - **What is false:** "the one place a real signature enters". Laura signs account papers, the January 2028 orders and the written instruction that lets the broker send co-sponsors a statement (C11; FINRA 2231.02). In the UCC's terms those are instructions and notifications to an intermediary, not promises to anyone. Say "**the one promise she signs**".
  - **The core holds: the letter adds a promise to money already held.** The order is held, then signed, then paid:
    - the bottom has been held since January 2028;
    - the top has been held since the 1 January 2031 move;
    - she signs at the 2031 meetings;
    - the money is paid in January 2033.
  - **The letter also adds what the holding cannot give:**
    - a fixed sum, with any shortfall in what the fund pays made up from her flexibility (C3, C14);
    - a named receiver;
    - "this promise binds my estate" (C10).
  - **The guard holds.** None of this is said with conceit or printing words in 3d. "Signed" appears once, in the lead-in (C5), and never beside "ink".
- **Claim 4 (Keel's own): KEEP, with two limits.**
  - **The NT$ note names its issuer, not a signer.** The current NT$1000's feature list has "the Chinese words 'the Republic of China' and the 'Central Bank'" raised in intaglio, and no signature. Its promise is CBC Act art. 13, which makes it legal tender "within the territory of the Republic of China (Taiwan)". That is why Laura's US-dollar letter needs a named day's rate (C6).
  - **The CBC shows its backing in C11's form.** Every quarter it publishes the amount issued and the reserves held against it, by line:
    - gold in grams;
    - US dollars converted at a named day's rate (31.874 on 30 Sep 2026);
    - checked under its Board of Supervisors.
  - **From 1961 to 2000 the name on Taiwan's notes was not the obligor.** The notes said "Bank of Taiwan", but their liabilities were the CBC's (art. 14). This matches "held for her", never "in her name".
  - **Limit 1:** the CBC's backing is exactly 100%, while Laura states less than she holds.
  - **Limit 2:** backing is not a peg, and the CBC does not say what its US-dollar reserves are held in.

**Judge's note (aha test 10 candidate, with a warning).** A dollar bill carries two printed signatures and has promised nothing on its face since 1963. The Treasuries behind Laura's ten payments carry no signature at all, yet each pays a dated sum, because a statute says it shall (31 U.S.C. 3123). The candidate is at most one clause behind "guaranteed" in 3c, never in 3d. Voice round 16 retired "a signature is the other kind of ink" (0 of 3 readers followed it), and this line also makes readers think about signatures, so it is tested cold before any use.

**Second judge's note (for a Taiwan reader).** Taiwan's own central bank publishes its US-dollar reserves every quarter, converted into NT$ at a named day's rate and checked before release. That is the form C6 and C11 ask of Laura's letter and statement. A Ministry of Culture grants officer has seen it on the bank's own notice board. The note is for the store and the lead-in's reasoning only, never in her words.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 13: the UCC's own line between signed and owned.** An indorsement is "a signature" on a certificate. An entitlement order is "a notification" to an intermediary. This is the strongest reason in law for WORDS' rule that ink means owned, never signed. It sits behind "held for her" in 2.0 and 2.2.
2. **Row 18, with rows 1 and 17: the dollar bill.** It has two printed signatures and has promised nothing on its face since 1963. The Treasuries behind the ten payments carry no signature and each pays a dated sum by statute. It is an aha test 10 candidate for 3c only, tested cold first because of round 16.
3. **Rows 29-30: the Taiwan central bank's quarterly notice.** It states a US-dollar holding in NT$ at a named day's rate, line by line, checked before release. That is C11's statement and C6's rate sentence in Taiwan's own official practice. For example, $115,000 was NT$3.67m at end-September's rate and NT$3.77m on 6 November. It goes into the lead-in's reasoning, never her words.
4. **Rows 3-4: the chain 3123(a), 5103 and 411.** The ten payments are a dollar debt owed, with "shall", by the government that issues dollars. That makes them certain in dollars, which is what the case asks. What dollars buy is not promised. This is one sentence in 3e, beside TNA l.7.
5. **Rows 14-15: claim 3, corrected.** The letter is "the one promise she signs", not "the one signature". The order is held, then signed, then paid. The letter adds a fixed sum, a named receiver and an estate clause to money already held, and never brings money into being.

- **Series 2025.** Its printing status after June 2026, when Reuters said the first $100s would be printed, was not checked. The row is refused anyway.
