- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "In April-May 1979 the US Treasury paid some bills up to three weeks late because of a back-office failure; it paid holders interest for the delay after new legislation, and the scare cost the US 60 basis points for good."
  2. "If the debt limit ever bound, the authorities planned to pay bondholders first; if they could not, a maturing Treasury would roll one business day at a time, stay tradable on Fedwire, and earn no interest for the wait."
  3. "An iBond Treasury fund would simply pass a late payment through at its December end: its documents say what happens, and its cash managers stay clear of a named X-date."
  4. "'More than five business days late' cleanly separates a 1979-style glitch and a day-by-day delay from a default."
  5. "A late Treasury payment costs Laura money she cannot recover."
- **Problem class:** classifying a late payment as an operational slip or a default when the debt itself has no grace period and no default clause. **Field that solved it first:** credit-contract drafting (bond indentures' grace periods), then the credit derivatives market, which wrote a deemed three-business-day grace period into its standard definitions "to avoid technical triggers" (ISDA), and the rating agencies' default definitions (S&P's five business days; Moody's none). The payments side (the New York Fed's Treasury Market Practices Group, SIFMA) solved the operational half: how a late Treasury stays tradable day by day.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE (three parts wrong, one right).**
  - **Right: it was a back-office failure,** and only for paper checks to individual investors: bills maturing Thu 26 Apr, Thu 3 May and Thu 10 May 1979; book-entry holders, over 90% of marketable Treasuries, were paid on time (CRS R44704, read in full).
  - **"Up to three weeks" is the outer bound, not the typical delay.** Check processing was normal by Mon 14 May: a 26 April bill paid that day was 12 business days late (Keel arithmetic; no holiday in between). The average looks far shorter: the forgone interest CRS reports, about $125,000 on $122 million at bill yields "over 9%", is about four calendar days of interest (Keel arithmetic on two CRS figures; the $125,000 comes from a self-published 2012 book CRS cites, so it is weak).
  - **No new law, and no statutory interest.** Treasury "has no authority to reimburse investors for the time-value of delayed payments" (CRS). A class action was filed on 11 May 1979; the government got a stay to make a settlement offer, which "reportedly more than 80%" accepted; the bill to let Treasury pay interest for the delays, H.R. 6054 (6 Dec 1979), naming "Treasury bills that matured on April 26, May 3, and May 10, 1979", was referred to committee and went no further; the suit was dismissed with prejudice on 12 May 1980 (congress.gov; CRS). Marron's (2011) "new legislation" and CBS's (2011) "paid in full with back interest" are not supported by the record read.
  - **The 60 basis points did not come from the delay.** Nobody outside Treasury knew of it on 26 April: Treasury's press release came on 8 May and the first newspaper report on 9 May, nine business days later, with "no large movements in Treasury yields" on either day (CRS R44704). On 26 April a CPI estimate showed inflation at a 13% annual rate and the Fed announced a large, unexpected rise in the money supply; the FOMC's 27 April 1979 conference call raised the funds rate (CRS R45011).
  - **Treasury's own record of the year is silent.** The 1979 Annual Report's Bureau of the Public Debt section (pp. 160-161, the pages CRS cites for the cause) says the year's automation of individual book-entry accounts "resulted in providing faster service and more timely issuance of discount payments to the public"; a search of the full text for "delay", "late", "backlog" and "mailing" finds no mention of the late checks (FRASER full text, read).
  - **Judge's note:** the one "US default" on record was paper checks to about 4,000 small savers, on average about four days late, never announced until it was over, and it touched no book-entry holder; Laura holds only book-entry Treasuries and fund shares.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with the operative words, and one addition: the plan has no end date and no day count.**
  - 2011 (Fed staff to the FOMC, 1 Aug): three principles, "principal and interest on Treasury securities would continue to be made on time", "other payments may be delayed", "any payments that were made would be settled as usual"; principal by auctions that "roll over those maturing securities"; interest by "holding back other government payments".
  - 2013 (16 Oct): if Treasury did not prioritise, "it would instruct the Reserve Banks to roll forward in one-day increments the maturity date of maturing securities. The securities would then continue to be transferable on the Fedwire® Securities system. Interest payments would also be held within the system until the Treasury authorized their release"; "Each evening, the Treasury will make a decision"; the instruction must reach the New York Fed "by 10:00 p.m. at the very latest".
  - TMPG (2021) and SIFMA (2023): the roll "would need to continue on a day-by-day basis for as long as the delay lasts"; "Decisions on payment are made by US Treasury one day at a time"; "there would not be a cross default with other Treasury securities with different maturities"; "Interest will not accrue on the delayed payment (no compounding)"; compensation "would require explicit legislation by Congress".
  - **The authorities' own line is cause, not length.** The Fed would treat delayed Treasuries like others "so long as the default reflects a political impasse and not any underlying inability of the United States to meet its obligations" (2011) and "so long as it seems certain that delayed interest and principal payments will be made in full and in relatively short order" (2013).
  - **Judge's note:** no official plan counts days; the market's own playbook assumes "Coupon and principal payments are ultimately made in full" and simply repeats its call schedule "if unresolved for multiple days".
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The fund's documents name the risk but have no clause for a holding that pays late at the end. The prospectus says Treasuries' "timely payment of interest or principal" could be prevented by "reaching the legislative 'debt ceiling'", that "By December 15, 2032, the Fund is expected to consist almost entirely of cash", and that "After appropriate provision is made for any Fund liabilities, its net assets will be distributed"; the only lever for a late holding is the board's power to "extend the termination date" (iShares prospectus, 27 Feb 2026). The second half holds: in 2023 BlackRock Cash Management "proactively exited all early June 2023 exposure", UBS's Treasury-ladder accounts were "avoiding maturities from June – September 2023", and Treasury money funds held about 80% in maturities up to 30 days (BlackRock; UBS; IMF).
- **Claim 4: KEEP as the definition, with two limits recorded (no new trigger, CANON A34).**
  - **What it filters out:** everything the record shows for book-entry Treasuries: a weekend or holiday (paid "without additional interest" the next business day, 31 CFR 356.30); a same-day hold, which "cannot be delayed longer than COB that day" without a roll (SIFMA); an hours-long Fedwire outage (Q2b-028); and a 1979-style average delay of about four days. It is longer than the credit-default-swap market's three business days, set "to avoid technical triggers that could be caused by natural disasters, human error or catastrophic events" (ISDA, 2023), and equal to S&P's test (Q8b-028).
  - **It fires before Laura's payment is touched.** For an iBond rung ending on 15 December (17 December in 2035), the fifth business day late is 22-24 December, 7-9 calendar days before the 1 January payment (Keel arithmetic, 2032-2036, Christmas the only holiday); the T-bonds have 47-320 days (Q15-030). Reason 2 is known while there is still time to re-check the cover, which is Table 3 row 2's only action ("Never: a forced sale").
  - **Limit 1: it counts days, not causes.** A deliberate debt-limit roll of one to five business days sits under the line, yet Moody's would downgrade "even in case of a brief default", Fitch would rate the sovereign RD and the affected issues D, and swaps would trigger after three business days. For Laura that matters little: the action on reason 2 is a re-check, and a short roll costs her no payment.
  - **Limit 2: Table 3 has no word for two to five business days late.** "Paid when due, or the next business day" is normal and "over five business days" is the reason; a debt-limit roll's first week sits between them. A monitoring note (log it as a delay, read daily) fills the gap without a new trigger.
  - **Judge's note:** Table 3's line is the most forgiving of the market's day counts (Moody's none, swaps three business days, S&P five; Fitch puts the issues themselves in D at once, as summarised), and that is right for this plan, because reason 2 asks for a re-check, never a sale.
- **Claim 5: CHANGE.** No interest is owed for a delay unless Congress acts ("Neither pre-judgment nor post-judgment interest is recoverable against the United States, except where the liability is imposed by statute or assumed by contract", DOJ Civil Resource Manual 212; H.R. 6054 died). But the payments' own standard already assumes waiting cash "earns nothing" (NUMBERS l.2228), so a late payment that arrives before 1 January costs the plan no dollar it had counted on; only the date matters (Keel inference).
  - **Judge's note:** the plan's zero-interest standard, set for a different reason, also prices an uncompensated Treasury delay at nothing.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 with row 1 (Table 3 row 2's note, or 3e's two-reasons line):** the one "US default" on record was paper checks to about 4,000 small savers, about four days late on average and twelve business days at worst, and no book-entry holder was touched. It turns a US default from an abstraction into a dated event and shows what the five-day line would and would not have caught.
2. **Row 41 (3e or Table 3):** the line fires 7-9 days before any iBond-funded 1 January payment, so reason 2 is a warning, not a post-mortem. It does the job Wharton asks of "what to monitor as 2033 approaches".
3. **Row 32 (3e, one clause):** Moody's none, swaps three business days, S&P five, Fitch D on the missed issue at once; Table 3 takes the most forgiving count because reason 2 asks only for a re-check. A judge who assumes "default" is one fixed line learns that the market itself draws several.
4. **Row 15 (3e):** the payments' zero-interest standard also prices an uncompensated Treasury delay at nothing; a delay is a date risk, never a dollar risk. An aha that rests on a figure already in NUMBERS.
5. **Rows 20 and 23 together (3e or Table 3's note):** a late Treasury is rolled one day at a time, stays owned and tradable on Fedwire, and no official plan counts days. It explains why Table 3 can say "Never: a forced sale" with confidence.
