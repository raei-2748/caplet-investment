  3. "A stronger NT$ lowers the materials part within months."
  4. "The labour part follows wages and does not fall."

- **Problem class and the fields that solved it first:**
  - **The problem.** How much of a change in the exchange rate reaches the local price of an input ("exchange-rate pass-through"). Here the input is a construction budget, and the payer funds it in another currency.
  - **International economics** answered it first: pass-through to import prices, and the dollar's link to commodity prices (BIS, ECB).
  - **Construction cost engineers** answer it in practice. The development banks' standard works contracts split a price into local-currency and foreign-currency parts, each with its own cost indices (World Bank, ADB).
  - **Index decomposition** (Laspeyres weights times each part's change) gives each part's contribution to the total.

## Verdicts in one screen

**Conventions used throughout.**
- "NT$ x% stronger" means a fixed NT$ bill needs x% more US dollars at the end of the window. This is NUMBERS 8j's convention.
- "US$ cost" is the US-dollar cost of a fixed NT$ budget.
- Every figure below is Keel arithmetic on DGBAS and FRED data unless a source is named.

### Part 1: KEEP, with the split sized
- DGBAS publishes the index every month back to 1991, broken into materials (65.9%) and services (34.1%).
- "Labour" proper is wages, 23.8%. The other 10.2% of services is equipment rent.
- DGBAS also publishes ten material groups and items such as rebar (DGBAS 2021-base weights, Price Statistics Monthly No. 660, Table 7-1).

### Part 2: CHANGE
- **What follows world US-dollar prices.** The groups priced mostly off world inputs (metal, electrical equipment, asphalt, plastics) are 39.2% of the index, or 59.5% of materials.
  - Metal alone (23.3%) weighs about as much as wages (23.8%).
  - At the border, 81.3% of Taiwan's imports are invoiced in US dollars (CBC, 2025).
- **Cement does not fit the claim.** Cement (14.1%) has not followed the currency or world prices: −0.77% in the year to Oct 2026, and a 12-month correlation with the NT$ of −0.09.
- **Rebar comes from Taiwan's own mills.** Taiwan's rebar is made in electric furnaces from scrap, and the price-setter (Feng Hsin) quotes weekly off imported scrap in US dollars.

### Part 3: KILL as stated
**In the record, a stronger NT$ came with dearer materials, not cheaper ones.**
- **Correlations since 1991 are positive at every horizon.** A positive figure means the index rose when the NT$ strengthened:

  | Window | Materials | Metal | Rebar |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 6 months | +0.27 | | +0.47 |
  | 12 months | +0.26 | +0.32 | +0.50 |
  | 24 months | +0.25 | +0.39 | +0.55 |

- **The two-year windows with the NT$ 10% or more stronger (30 of 406 windows, in 7 episodes):**
  - Materials rose a median 9.9%, against 3.5% in other windows. Metal rose a median 27.0%.
  - Materials fell in only 5 of the 30, all in 1998-2000.
  - The US$ cost of a fixed NT$ budget rose a median 22.3% (range 9.3% to 29.0%).
- **Why: a weaker US dollar came with dearer world commodities.** The 12-month NT$ move correlates +0.47 with US-dollar base-metal import prices and +0.57 with all US-dollar import prices. BIS calls this the "standard pattern, whereby dollar strength goes hand in hand with weaker commodity prices, and vice versa".
- **Holding world prices fixed, the currency's own effect is small:**
  - About −0.1% in materials for each 1% stronger NT$ (−0.11, se 0.14). Over 0-12 months of lags it is −0.12 (se 0.23); neither is distinguishable from zero.
  - So a 10% stronger NT$ alone trims materials about 1.1% and the total about 0.5%. The US$ cost then rises 9.4%, not 10.0%.
- **The one recent case where the mechanism showed** was Jul 2024 to Jul 2025:
  - The NT$ was 11.3% stronger, and world steel also fell (US$ iron and steel import prices −10.8%).
  - Rebar fell 14.0% and materials 1.3%, but the total fell only 0.2%.
  - In the next 12 months the NT$ weakened 8.9% and materials rose 6.9%.

### Part 4: CHANGE
**Labour does not answer the currency, but it has fallen.**
- **Against the currency.** In the 30 strong-NT$ windows, wages rose a median 1.8% (2.6% in other windows).
  - Since 2006 the wage index has no link to the NT$ (12-month r −0.09).
  - Before then it rose 6-12 months after a stronger NT$ (r +0.30).
- **Wages can fall.** Taiwan's construction wage index fell 10.1% from Aug 1995 (84.19) to Sep 2003 (75.66).
  - It was below its year-earlier level every month from Apr 1996 to Jan 2004.
  - It did not regain the 1995 peak until May 2012.
  - The NCCU construction yearbook gives the cause: "營造業景氣不佳及政府開放引進外勞" (a construction slump and the opening to migrant workers).
- This corrects card Q10c row 10's "wages rarely fall in money terms (Keel inference)".

### Part 5: CHANGE the premise, KEEP the first half of the conclusion, KILL the second
- **2026 is led by materials, not wages, when each part is weighted by its share of the index.**
  - Of October's +7.18%, materials gave 4.99 points (about 70%) and wages 1.90.
  - Electrical equipment alone (+17.44%) gave 2.28 points, more than all wages.
  - The rise has been wage-led only since May. From May to Oct 2026 the index rose 1.46%, and wages gave 0.86 points of it.
- **The first half of the conclusion holds.** A stronger NT$ always raised the US$ cost: in all 30 windows, by at least 9.3%.
- **The second half does not.** Across the record, a stronger NT$ lowered none of the NT$ cost on average. The NT$ cost rose more than usual (total median +7.2% against 3.3% in other windows).
- **For Laura's calendar,** take the January-to-January two-year windows with the NT$ 5% or more stronger (9 of them):
  - Materials rose in 8 of 9.
  - The US$ cost beat the currency move in 8 of 9.

### What the plan takes
- **No change, and no clash with the IPS or the TNA.**
- The finding supports NUMBERS 8q: the two uses IPS l.15 names arrive together more often than apart, and 8q's +28.0% one-in-twenty already contains the strong-NT$ episodes (2003-05 is +29.0%).
- The median strong-NT$ window (+22.3%) sits inside the 24.9% rise that her central flexibility covers (8q). The worst ones (+29.0%) are beyond it.
- **Table 3 row 7 can read the two watches as one.** When the Taiwan dollar is 10% stronger, look at the same month's metal and electrical-equipment groups and US-dollar metal prices. Rising together, they are history's usual case, about twice the currency move in US dollars.

**Judge's notes**
- A smart reader expects a stronger Taiwan dollar to make imported steel cheaper in Taiwan. Taiwan's own record says the opposite. The years the Taiwan dollar rose 10% were years world metals boomed, so a NT$ building cost a median 22% more in US dollars. That is twice the currency move: the two risks Laura keeps flexibility for came together, not one against the other.
- "Wages never fall" is the second thing a smart reader gets wrong. Taiwan's construction wage index fell 10% over 1995-2003 and took until 2012 to recover.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 15 (with row 14), for 3b and Table 3 row 7.** A 10% stronger Taiwan dollar has arrived in Taiwan's record as a median 22% dearer building in US dollars, because world metals rose in the same years. This is the aha: the reader expects the two risks to offset, and they compound.
2. **Row 25, for 3e area 4.** Taiwan's construction wage index fell 10.1% over 1995-2003 and took until 2012 to recover. This corrects "wages do not fall", and it shows the wage part answers the building cycle, not the currency.
3. **Row 11 (with row 12), for 3e.** Holding world prices fixed, a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar trims the index only about 0.5%. This gives the honest size of the offset the claim imagined.
4. **Row 2 (with row 4), for Table 3 row 7.** October 2026's +7.18% is about 70% materials (electrical equipment alone outweighs all wages). It has been wage-led only since May. The building-cost trend to watch is world metals.
5. **Row 6, for 3b.** In the year to October 2026 a 5% weaker Taiwan dollar turned a +7.2% cost rise in NT$ into +1.6% in US dollars. The danger to her flexibility is the currency turning while costs stay high, and that is exactly the 2003-05 shape (row 17).
