# Q214. Read at source, does 3e's one outside parallel hold: in the 2022 gilt crisis, which portfolios were forced to sell, did any pension promise break, and can anything make Laura's plan sell?

- **Date:** Sun 22 Nov 2026, Sydney (third research round). Every source below was read that day unless marked.
- **Report parts:** 3e (the one outside parallel, ARGUMENT_TREE branch 6; the closing line "no fall in share prices can change the plan"); Table 3 row 4 (a sponsor's early end of a fund) and row 5 (the trade-day limit, 0.5% of net asset value per fund leg).
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Claim tested, verbatim** (ARGUMENT_TREE branch 6, adopted in `research/ATTACKS.md` D20 from bond judge attack 5): "The 2022 gilt crisis: the portfolios that broke fastest were leveraged ones whose funding could be called; no pension promise broke. Laura borrows nothing, so nobody can make the plan sell." Three parts are tested separately: (1) "broke fastest ... leveraged ones whose funding could be called"; (2) "no pension promise broke"; (3) "nobody can make the plan sell".
- **What I read, and in which form:** read in full: the Bank of England's Financial Stability Report of December 2022, section 5; the FPC's March 2023 summary page; Pinter's Staff Working Paper No. 1,019 (2023); the PPF's written evidence LDI0031; Logan's New York Fed speech of 23 Oct 2020; the Work and Pensions Committee's report HC 826, chapters 1-4 and summary; the Minister for Pensions' letter of 29 June 2023. Read in part or in extracts at source: the FPC record of March 2023 (PDF); the Pensions Regulator's 2 Feb 2024 review for the Committee; its April 2023 guidance on leveraged LDI; its letter of 10 Oct 2022; the FSB's Holistic Review (Nov 2020); the Fed's Financial Stability Reports of May 2020 and November 2021; the BIS Quarterly Review of March 2021 (Todorov); BlackRock's October 2020 slides for the SEC. The repo's NUMBERS section 4, Table 3 row 5 and the FINAL IPS were read for the disanalogy.
- **Not repeated** (checked in the cards and the store): card Q207 (the regulator's letter of 10 Oct 2022 on "liquidity (rather than a funding problem)"; HL3123 "Individual member benefits were not at risk"; the FSR's £14bn/£23bn/£70bn, pooled "volumes far exceeding the normal daily level", the 60%/15% split, "three to four working days", 300-400bp and 250bp; the Bank's £19.3bn at "relatively distressed prices"; SVB and the BTFP; "no lender and no depositor can make her sell"); card Q2a rows 1-7 (Cunliffe's 160bp, £50bn and "worth zero"; the IMF's £70bn, 175 pooled funds and five days; Pinter, Siriwardane & Walker (2024): a 10% fire-sale discount, pooled funds sold more, "capital can be slow moving internally"; the Committee's £400bn and 85%); card Q125 rows 43-45 (leverage as the late cure for under-hedging; 300-400bp, HC 826 para 67); card Q45 (IOSCO's -0.5% worst median and "only up to two weeks", ICI's >1% on 11-12 March, TLT about 5% below, VGSH/VGIT/VGLT/VT premiums, Duffie, SR 1170, the sellers' totals $287bn/$266bn/$196bn); card Q196 row 5 (March 2020 "broke moneyness, not dated safety", Quarles's "a surprising development", the Fed's $1.6tn, +65bp on the 10-year); store H7-037; Q26-015, Q3a-027, Q65-031 (securities lending inside the funds); Q36 (in-kind ends).
- **Problem class:** forced selling and liquidity in liability-matched portfolios: a solvent holder of safe bonds made to sell by a claim on it that falls due before its bonds pay. A financial-stability question. **Field that solved it first:** financial-stability regulation and central banking (the lender and buyer of last resort; after 2022 the FPC's buffer standard for leveraged funds, "unencumbered and immediately available"). The bond desk's dedicated portfolio, held outright, is the form that never meets the problem (card Q207).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Part 1, "the portfolios that broke fastest were leveraged ones whose funding could be called": CHANGE.** "Broke fastest" is in no source. No LDI fund is recorded as failing: on the evening of 27 September the Bank was told that "multiple LDI funds were likely to fall into negative asset values and would have to begin the process of winding up the following morning", and it stepped in on 28 September (row 6). The sources' own words are "came under particular pressure" (the FSR, row 1), "pooled funds in particular became forced sellers" (Bailey, row 3), and the problem "particularly acute for 'pooled funds', in which limited liability meant investors could decide not to provide additional capital" (Breeden, row 4). The second half holds and is measured: firms with more gilt repo before the crisis sold more gilts in it, and repo explained the sales better than swaps did (Pinter, row 10). One caveat: "pooled funds above all" is true of pressure per fund, not of volume. Segregated mandates were 85-90% of the market and "also contributed to selling pressure" (row 11).
- **Part 2, "no pension promise broke": CHANGE to the narrower statement.** What holds: "No schemes became 'insolvent', and we have not seen any claims arising as a direct result of recent volatility in the gilt market" (the PPF, November 2022, row 16). The regulator did "not believe that the recent issues significantly increased the short-term risk of schemes entering the PPF" (row 18). The government said "Individual member benefits were not at risk" (Q207). What does not hold as a blanket: a minority of schemes ended worse funded, 5% of all DB schemes on the regulator's model for 2022 (row 19), through hedges cut, sales at low prices and loans from employers (row 22). Nobody can count them: not the PPF "or any other body" (rows 20-21). Exact narrower wording: **"no pension scheme became insolvent and the pensions' insurer saw no claim from the episode"**, dated "by November 2022" where space allows. Never "every member was paid" or "no one lost anything".
- **Part 3, "Laura borrows nothing, so nobody can make the plan sell": CHANGE.** "Nobody" fails twice. A fund's sponsor can end an iBond fund early and pay its value in cash on the sponsor's date (Table 3 row 4; row 7 here is the 2022 counterpart, decisions "made on their behalf"). And Laura herself can always sell (Q207). What is true is narrower and stronger: **no price can make her sell.** She borrows nothing, pledges nothing and owes no one cash on demand. The ten are paid by their own maturities and the half kept is promised to no one. Her one planned sale, the 2031 move, goes in at a limit within 0.5% of net asset value and otherwise waits day by day, "never past the 2031 meetings" (NUMBERS 4; Table 3 row 5). A date can make her trade; a price cannot. A stressed price can lower what she states, and only before she states it, because the top is stated after the move (row 30).
- **The disanalogy, said plainly.** In 2022 the gilts were fine and the borrowing on top of them was not. What ran short was time ("the speed required to satisfy collateral calls in the form required", the PPF, row 17), and every route to a loss ran through a call for cash (row 22). Laura's funds can trade below their value in a stress, as Treasury ETFs did in March 2020 until 23 March (row 29). That is a price on a day, not a call: nothing she holds asks her for money on that day, so she does not have to sell on it.
- **Companion: March 2020, the "dash for cash", but not as a second parallel.** It hit the very market and kinds of fund Laura holds. The forced sellers were again those whose funding could be called (basis traders unwound "almost US$90 billion", FSB, row 26) or who owed cash on demand (mutual funds selling to "prepare for potential redemptions", Logan, row 28). Funds holding only US government securities took inflows (row 27). The Bank itself measures 2022 against it: 140bp in four days was "more than twice as large as the largest move since 2000, which occurred during the 'dash for cash' in March 2020" (row 14). Since 3e has one outside parallel, March 2020 belongs where it already works: as the basis of Table 3 row 5's 0.5% limit, or one clause at most (row 32).
- **Verdict sentence the report may use (3e):** "In the 2022 gilt crisis the funds forced to sell were those that had borrowed against their gilts, pooled funds above all, when lenders called for cash within days; no pension scheme became insolvent, and the pensions' insurer saw no claim from it. Laura borrows nothing, so no fall in prices can make her sell; her one planned sale, the 2031 move, waits for a price near the funds' value."
- **Never in the report:** "broke fastest"; "no pension promise broke" unglossed; "every member was paid"; "nobody can make the plan sell" or "no one can force a sale" (the sponsor's early end; her own choice); "LDI funds collapsed" or "failed" (none wound up); "the Bank rescued the pensions" (it bought gilts to restore market functioning, and funds used the time to rebuild buffers); "schemes sold equities first" (the regulator's modelling assumption, row 20); "she sells only on dates she set" (a sponsor's early end is on the sponsor's date).
- **Judge's note (fixed income):** a UK bond judge will know that no LDI fund actually wound up and that the losses fell on a minority of small schemes. The sentence above is the one he cannot fault, and "a morning from winding up" (row 6) is the detail he will not expect a high-school team to have.
- **Judge's note (general):** the parallel lands in one move: pension funds that held the right bonds were still made to sell because they had borrowed against them. Laura holds the right bonds and has borrowed nothing.
