- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** how to commit to paying money out of an uncertain source without ever having to take the figure back, when a cut costs far more than a raise earns. Corporate payout practice met this problem first. NYSE boards were already paying "regular" plus "extra" dividends by the 1920s (DeAngelo, DeAngelo and Skinner's data start in 1926). Lintner (1956) wrote their rule down from interviews.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **"A stated figure is set where it can be kept, and the extra goes out as a separate, unpromised part": KEEP, with two changes.**
   - **The evidence for it:**
     - Lintner's managers "sought to avoid making changes in their dividend rates that might have to be reversed within a year or so". Firms that paid "'extra' as well as 'regular' rates" could adjust smoothly.
     - In 2002, 93.8% of 384 executives agreed "We try avoid reducing dividends per share", the strongest answer in the survey (Brav et al., 2005).
   - **Change (a): a firm's "kept" figure is a forecast, and forecasts break.**
     - BHP dropped its "progressive dividend policy" in February 2016 and cut its interim dividend 74%, from US62c to US16c.
     - In 2008, US banks' dividends were 30% larger than their earnings.
     - COVID-19 broke dividend smoothing in major markets (Cejnek et al., 2021).
     - A meta-analysis of 99 studies finds only "a mediocre degree of dividend smoothing".
     - Laura's figure is owned, so hers is the strong form of the rule, not a copy of it.
   - **Change (b): for Laura, "the extra goes out" is wrong.**
     - Anything above the top is not paid to co-sponsors; it goes to her flexibility (IPS l.15; CANON D12).
     - An unpromised part stays unpromised only if it is not shown again and again. Kodak paid a special every year from 1954 to 1986, and shareholders came to expect it.
   - **Reword:** "A firm states a regular dividend it expects to keep and leaves the rest unpromised. Laura states only what she already owns, and anything above it stays hers."
2. **"Markets punish a cut more than they reward a raise": KEEP the numbers, CHANGE how they are used.**
   - **The asymmetry:**
     - Amihud and Li (1962-2000; 14,911 raises, 1,278 cuts): +0.87% for a raise against -4.58% for a cut over two days. The cut is about 5 to 6 times the raise in every era (Keel arithmetic).
     - Michaely, Thaler and Womack: -7.0% for omissions against +3.4% for initiations.
   - **Why the use must change:**
     - The gap has shrunk (cuts -6.43%, -4.29%, -2.63% across three eras).
     - It is partly size. Michaely, Thaler and Womack: "when we control for the change in the magnitude of dividend yield ... the asymmetry shrinks or disappears".
     - A cut to a labelled extra barely registers: -0.70%, not significant (DeAngelo, DeAngelo and Skinner).
     - An expected cut was not punished: BHP's shares closed up 2.62% on the day of the 74% cut.
     - So the penalty falls on a surprise cut to the promised part. It is a fact about share prices. No study here measures funders, so never state it as a fact about co-sponsors.
3. **"The bottom is the regular, the top the special": CHANGE (the mapping is outdated).**
   - Since the funder test of 16 Nov, co-sponsors count the top. So the stated top is the "regular".
   - Anything above the top, plus the half kept, is her retained part: Lintner's "by-product" of the dividend decision, Brav's flexible repurchase.
   - It is never a "special". A special is paid out, and when paid often it comes to be expected.
4. **"Companies never cut regular dividends": KILL.**
   - Brav's executives "try" to avoid cuts.
   - In DeAngelo, DeAngelo and Skinner's sample, 13.4% of firm-years cut the regular (median cut -25%).
   - BHP cut 74% in 2016; banks cut slowly in 2008-09; COVID-19 broke smoothing in 2020.
5. **"Dividends signal private information, while Laura's figure is public arithmetic": CHANGE.**
   - Managers "reject the notion that they pay dividends as a costly signal" (Brav et al., 2005), so what firms and Laura share is commitment, not signalling.
   - **The three disanalogies that hold:**
     - **A forecast against an owned figure.**
     - **Forever against once:** firms that cut a special raised the regular in 60.0% of 3,572 such firm-years. Laura states one range for one contribution and has no next year in which to make up a shortfall.
     - **Paid out against kept.**

**Judge's notes (aha):**
- Lintner's managers in 1947-53 moved dividends in "five, ten, or twenty-five cents" and often stated "a range such as 'between a quarter and a third.'" Laura's rule rounds each end down to $5,000 and states a range: the same habit 70 years on, except hers only ever rounds down.
- A company keeps its regular dividend low because it cannot see next year. Laura can state hers in full because she waits until it is owned. Firms hold back the size; she holds back the date.
- Kodak paid an "extra" every year for 33 years, and shareholders came to count on it. That is why co-sponsors are shown the top and never what might come above it.
- Lintner's managers avoided any change "that might have to be reversed within a year or so". The plan prices that rule: a top stated 12 months before the move would be more than the move buys in 10.6% of house paths (NUMBERS 4).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2: avoid reversals, priced (3c).**
   - Lintner's 1950s rule, "avoid changes that might have to be reversed within a year or so", meets the register's price: a top stated a year early would be more than the move buys in 10.6% of house paths.
   - A practitioners' rule, measured in Laura's plan.
2. **Row 42: forecast against owned (3c).**
   - Firms hold back the size; Laura holds back the date and states the whole owned figure.
   - It shows a judge why her "certain" is not over-claiming.
3. **Row 23: Kodak's 33 years of extras became expected (3b, 3d).**
   - It is the evidence behind never showing co-sponsors what might come above the top, or the modelled payout.
4. **Rows 14, 31 and 32: the flexible part takes the hit (3b).**
   - In 2008 and 2020, buybacks fell (82% in one quarter of 2020) while dividends held.
   - Read beside NUMBERS 4's "VT worthless" row (stated range $95,000, half kept $0, every payment and the bottom unchanged), it shows her flexibility is built to do the same job.
5. **Row 5: Lintner's rounding (3c).**
   - Managers rounded to five, ten or twenty-five cents and spoke in ranges. Laura rounds each end down to $5,000.
   - A small, true, surprising parallel that makes the rule look like practice, not invention.

## Gaps

- **Lintner's interview words:** Lintner (1956) has almost no verbatim quotes from managers, and his field notes are not public. Quotes credited to "Lintner" elsewhere in the store need a source (H7-060).
- **Paywalled full texts:**
  - Fama and Babiak (1968), Leary and Michaely (2011), Fernau and Hirsch (2019) and Cejnek, Randl and Zechner (2021) were read as abstracts only.
  - Grullon, Michaely and Swaminathan (2002, "Are dividend changes a sign of firm maturity?") was not read.
- **Older event studies:** Charest (1978) and Woolridge (1983) were read only as quoted by DeAngelo, DeAngelo and Skinner.
- **Funders:** no study of how funders or co-sponsors react to a pledge that is cut or comes in below what was stated. Card Q147's gift-office counting rules are the nearest. This is the biggest hole for using the asymmetry at all.
- **Taiwan:** no event study of dividend cuts in Taiwan was found. The TSMC row is context only.
- **Devon:** later quarters, when Devon's variable dividend fell, were not read.
- **Amihud and Li:** the 2006 journal version (JFQA) was not read. The card cites the NYU working paper (version of May 2003).
- **Mazur et al. (2023):** the co-authors' names were not recorded in this run. Add them before the entry goes into References.
