## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Three levers: the date, the thing paid, the rule.**
- (a) In every case read for this card, when a fixed promise ran short, the payer saved it by moving one of three things: the date, the thing paid, or the rule.
- (b) Laura's ten payments are bought before they are stated, in Treasuries that mature on dates written in them and cannot be called early. So no payer can move the date or pay in something else, and the only lever left is the rule, which the IPS writes down as two exits (IPS l.17).
- (c) It breaks because the rule is still Laura's to change, as Athens changed its own in a panic (idea 3). The break teaches why half the shares are promised to no one (IPS l.15): a panic over the building has money of its own, so nobody needs to touch the rule that guards the payments.
- The cases: the date (Rome kept veterans "thirty or forty campaigns"; Venice's interest fell 25 years behind); the thing paid (Rome's "soaking swamps or mountainous wastes" for cash; Florence's dowries capped and paid in two instalments; consols cut to 2½%); the rule (Athens voted away its death penalty; Caesar declared the Gauls beaten).
- Decision it serves: buying the ten payments in January 2027 before any figure is stated; the two exits; the half kept back.
- Report part: 3e's lead (how a promise breaks), or one clause in 3a.
- Sources: Tacitus, Annals 1.17 and 1.78; Kirshner and Molho (1978) n.65; Plebani (2023); Mueller (KNAW); Hansard, 9 Mar 1888; Thucydides 8.15; Appian, Civil Wars 2.41.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** A finance judge knows defaults and conversions one at a time. What they have not seen is that rescues from Athens to Goschen pulled the same three levers, and that Laura's plan takes away two of them by how it is built. The synthesis is Keel's; each case was read at source.

**2. Rome named the sum before it had the money, so it moved the date.**
- (a) In 5 CE Rome voted its soldiers fixed discharge payments, a year before it set up a treasury to pay them. When the money ran short, it kept the sums and moved the date: in 15 CE Tiberius said the state could not bear the cost "unless the twentieth year" of service was the year of discharge.
- (b) Laura does it the other way round: she owns first and states second ("whatever figure you choose is money you already own", IPS l.15), and her dates cannot move because each Treasury matures on its own. Put beside the store's Lintner line: Lintner's managers hold back a dividend's size, Rome held back the payment's date, and Laura holds back the day she states a figure.
- (c) It breaks because Rome's promise had no end (every veteran, every year, for ever), so no stock of money could ever buy it and it had to rest on a tax. Laura's promise ends after ten payments, and that end is what lets money she owns buy it outright.
- Decision it serves: stating a figure only once it is owned (the 2031 move); the ten payments as a closed set.
- Report part: 3c (stating only what is owned), or 3.0.
- Sources: Cassius Dio 55.23.1 and 55.25; Tacitus, Annals 1.17 and 1.78; Res Gestae 17; Suetonius, Augustus 49.2.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** Judges know Lintner, but few know the mutiny of 14 CE. The reversal of order (sum first and money later, against money first and sum later) is the plan's own rule, seen from its failure side.

**3. A reserve kept for a danger can be argued away; a reserve kept for a date cannot.**
- (a) In 431 BCE Athens put aside a thousand talents to be spent only if an enemy fleet attacked the city, and made it a capital crime to propose any other use. In 49 BCE Caesar emptied Rome's sacred reserve, kept for a war with the Gauls, by saying he had beaten the Gauls.
- (b) Someone can always say that a danger has come or gone. Laura's reserve is kept for ten dates and ten sums, and a date cannot be declared over: it arrives and the Treasury pays (the conceit's "certainty has dates", 3.0).
- (c) It breaks where Laura's plan also rests on a rule, the IPS's two exits, and Athens shows how such a rule falls. After nineteen years the Assembly lifted the penalty "in the consternation of the moment" when Chios revolted in 412 BCE, because it had no other money to spend. Laura has other money: the half of her shares promised to no one.
- Decision it serves: writing the operating reserve as dated sums; the two exits ("A fall in the market is not one of them", IPS l.17); the half kept back.
- Report part: 3.0 (certainty has dates), 3e (the rule as a limit), 3b (why the half kept back exists).
- Sources: Thucydides 2.24 and 8.15; Appian, Civil Wars 2.41.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** Classicists read Thucydides 8.15 and finance judges do not. It is a nineteen-year test of exactly the kind of exit rule the IPS wrote, with a known way of failing.

**4. The groom wanted the dowry on his wedding day, not later.**
- (a) From 1425 Florence's dowry fund let a father pay in 100 florins and collect a fixed 250 or 500 florins when his daughter married, seven and a half or fifteen years later. In 1447 a Florentine mother wrote that she could find no suitor willing to wait for her daughter's dowry until 1448.
- (b) A promised sum is worth what it is on the day the other side needs it. That is why Laura's top is owned before the 2031 meetings, and why the late-2032 fund pays out before the facility contribution in 2033. A co-sponsor, like the groom, counts a figure by its date as well as its size.
- (c) It breaks because Florence lent the fund's deposits to Florence itself. The city diverted the fund's interest to pay mercenaries in 1452, called its debt "immovable rock and enduring certainty" in a 1470 law, and in 1477 capped dowry payments at 100,000 florins a year in two instalments. The word "certainty" did not make the payments certain; Laura's certainty rests on what she owns, which is why the report makes the stated range, never a word, the subject of "certain".
- Decision it serves: the top owned before the 2031 meetings; the late-2032 fund's date; what Laura tells co-sponsors.
- Report part: 3d (the explanation around Laura's words, never inside them), 3c.
- Sources: Kirshner and Molho (1978); Morrison, Kirshner and Molho (1977); Plebani (2023); the Strozzi letter in Bruni (2021).
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** The Strozzi letter is niche, and the groom maps onto a co-sponsor: both count a promise by the day it pays. Florence also corrects a date already in the store (H4-035).

**5. Church buildings waited for centuries while services went on.**
- (a) From the fifth century a church's income was split into four parts, one kept for the building, which was called the "fabric". Cologne held services in its finished choir from 1322 while its towers stood half-built with a crane on top, until work restarted in 1842 and ended in 1880, 632 years after it began.
- (b) Laura's plan makes the same split. The ten payments that run the residency are bought first and in full, and the building is the part allowed to wait, shrink or change inside her flexibility (IPS l.15). Exeter still keeps a separate Fabric Fund, about 750 years after its first fabric roll: the design that lasted longest in this card.
- (c) It breaks because a cathedral could hold services for centuries in its choir, but an artists' residency cannot run without rooms, so Laura's building can wait only a little. Her flexibility, not time, does the cathedral's work. Siena shows the opposite mistake: in 1339 it voted for a cathedral bigger than its money, the work stopped after the plague of 1348, and the half-built front wall, the Facciatone, still stands with nothing behind it (why Laura never states above her top: "ink only goes one way").
- Decision it serves: building money kept apart from running money; half the shares kept back; never stating above the top.
- Report part: 3b.
- Sources: Catholic Encyclopedia, "Fabrica Ecclesiae"; Exeter Cathedral (2024, 2025); Erzbistum Köln (2018); Kölner Dombauhütte; Opera della Metropolitana di Siena.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha** for a finance judge, pedigree for a medievalist. Cologne's crane is the pencil line made visible for 350 years, and Cologne's own 1842 society of citizens is a co-sponsor that still pays about 60% of the yearly budget.

**6. A figure with an "if" in its sentence is pencil, however good the arithmetic.**
- (a) In 1789 Benjamin Franklin, a printer, left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia to lend at 5% to young tradesmen. He wrote that "if this plan is executed, and succeeds as projected without interruption for one hundred years", each would hold £131,000. Boston's reached about two-thirds of that and Philadelphia's about a fifth, in part because the apprenticeships it was built to lend to faded away.
- (b) Victorian friendly societies made the same kind of figure: they charged a flat weekly sum whatever a member's age and valued what they owed on an assumed interest rate. Laura's ten payments need no assumed rate ("sufficient without reinvestment", NUMBERS 2), and she states a building figure only once the Treasuries that pay it are owned.
- (c) It breaks because the societies that ran short survived by asking members for more (a "self-rectifying power"), and Laura gave up that lever when the IPS said her payments would not depend "on anybody else's money" (IPS l.11). Because she can never ask anyone for more, she has to own the payments before she states them. Franklin's figure still did good, too (Boston's fund held almost $5 million in 1990), which is why a pencil figure is fine for money promised to no one, her flexibility.
- Decision it serves: the completion standard (no assumed rate); stating only what is owned; keeping the unpromised half in shares.
- Report part: 3a (the standard), 3e (assumptions), 3c.
- Sources: Franklin's codicil (1789); Yenawine (2010); Philanthropy Roundtable (2000); Broten (2010); Te Ara (1966); Tasmania (1886).
- Pedigree or aha: **aha** for the pairing. Franklin's codicil is known, but its "if" read as pencil, and the friendly societies' levy as the one lever Laura gave up, are new.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 1, the three levers (3e's lead, or one clause in 3a).** It turns two thousand years into one sentence a judge can repeat: every short promise moved its date, what it paid, or its rule. Laura's plan takes two of those levers away by how it is built.
2. **Idea 2, Rome named the sum before the money (3c).** It is the plan's own rule, "state only what is owned", seen from the side where it failed. Rome kept the sums and moved the date, and the Lintner line gains a third term: size, date, the day of stating.
3. **Idea 3, Athens's nineteen-year reserve (3.0, with 3e and 3b).** It is a real test of the IPS's exit rule. A guarded reserve held until a panic had nowhere else to go, which explains in one story why half of Laura's shares are promised to no one.

Runner-up for 3d: idea 4, the groom who would not wait until 1448. Keep to one outside name per part (panel 4), so each part takes one of these, never two.
