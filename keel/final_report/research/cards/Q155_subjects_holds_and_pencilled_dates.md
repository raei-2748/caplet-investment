- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** staging a commitment between first agreement and a binding deal, and deciding which figures may be counted by people who will rely on them. The shipping trade wrote the vocabulary down first ("subjects" and "lifting subjects"; Kokusai Kisen v Johnson, 1921: "the whole thing is in abeyance"); banking law and payment-system standards (UCC Article 4; CPSS-IOSCO 2012) wrote the rule for counting only what is backed; aircraft makers' order books (Boeing under ASC 606) apply it to signed contracts.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE (the main finding, and an aha for the judge).** In almost every trade read, a figure becomes firm when someone is **bound** to it, by an act that is said or signed (subjects lifted, a contract signed, contracts exchanged, a ticket issued), usually with **part** of the money down. It does not wait for the thing behind it to be held.
  - Ship sale: bound once a recap was accepted between brokers, before any signature or deposit (The Blankenstein, 1985, CA). Deposit when there is one: 10% (Saleform 2012).
  - Venues: confirmed on "a signed contract and paid deposit", typically 50% (Horowitz, The GRAND, City Springs, Kansas City); Auckland Stadiums asks 50%, 75% or 100% by how close the date is; only Charleston asks a queue-jumping challenger for 100% up front.
  - Charters: "no fixture has been concluded until all 'subjects' have been lifted" (Baltic Exchange), and lifting is a declaration.
  - England: a house sale is "legally binding" at exchange; "You now own the property" only after the money moves at completion (GOV.UK).
  - Only systems that **report or settle** figures for others tie firmness to what is held: a bank credit is final when the payee "has received usable funds" (UCC 4-201 comment); final settlement is "the irrevocable and unconditional transfer of an asset" (CPSS-IOSCO, 2012); Boeing records no firm order while financing is open "even if satisfying such conditions is highly probable", and in 2025 took $17,759 million of signed orders out of its backlog whose payment it doubted (10-K).
  - So the claim as worded is false for the trades and true for Laura. Changed wording for the store: **"In trades that live on promises, a figure is firm once someone is bound to it, usually with part of the money down. Laura's rule is stricter than any of them: a figure is ink only when the whole of it is owned in a Treasury that will pay it on its date."**
  - **Judge's note:** a finance reader expects "signed" to mean "firm". The world's biggest aircraft order book, a shipowner and the Sydney Opera House all say otherwise, and Laura's rule asks more than any of them.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with the trades' own words.**
  - Charters: "on subs" against "clean fixed" or "fully fixed".
  - Sydney Opera House: "Pencil Booking" is a defined term in its 2025 Venue Hire Policy, and is "not [a] confirmed booking".
  - Promoters: "first pencil" and "second pencil" (UK promoters' glossary; a New Zealand venue's policy).
  - A convention centre: "TENTATIVE", "FIRST OPTION", "SECOND OPTION", "CONFIRMED".
  - Aircraft: "options" against "firm orders". Banks: "provisional" against "final" settlement. Airlines: a "flight firming tool" cancels unticketed bookings.
  - Several are literally "pencil". None is literally "ink": the firm word is always "fixed", "confirmed", "firm" or "final".
- **Claim 3: KEEP, and turn it round for Laura.**
  - The side holding something for someone else carries pencil's risk: the shipowner whose ship is "on subs" (the Baltic Code calls one such subject "very onerous ... for an owner"; Nautica lost its $8 million fixture and its damages claim); the venue holding a free date; the extra who must "keep all of the dates discussed available"; the depositor whose credit is charged back.
  - **Laura's rule keeps pencil's risk on her own side.** She states nothing before the move, so co-sponsors are shown only ink, and a fall in shares before 2031 lands on her top and her flexibility, never on a figure she has stated.
  - **Judge's note:** the trades' pencil is a cost the other side carries; Laura's is a cost she carries herself.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. Not as staged images; yes as facts behind working words.**
  - As staged images ("her top is on subjects", "a first hold", "a challenge") they would fail as every second trade image has (rounds 6-8). They are also wrong for the plan: "on subjects" means either side may walk away (Kokusai Kisen; Nautica para 46), and IPS l.15 fixes the move.
  - Three facts could sit behind a working word: the Sydney Opera House's "Pencil Booking" (behind "pencil" at 2.0); Boeing's "even if ... highly probable" (behind "nothing is stated before the move" in 3c); and STEM, which the Baltic Code says "originally stood for 'Subject To Enough Merchandise'" (behind "pencil" in 3c or 3d: the shipping trade's commonest pencil condition is whether the cargo is there).
  - The startling perspective is a comparison, not a picture: **Laura's ink is a stricter standard than the firm figure in any trade read.**
- **Claim 5: CHANGE.**
  - The Junior K [1988] 2 Lloyd's Rep 583 (Steyn J: "subject to details" means no contract) and Kokusai Kisen v Johnson (1921, "subject to stem") are on when a fixture binds; read here as quoted in the 2020 Nautica v Trafigura judgment.
  - **Kill The Merak:** it is on whether a charterparty's arbitration clause was incorporated into a bill of lading (Scarman J, 28 July 1964), not on subjects.
  - Pagnan v Feed Products (1987) is on intention to be bound with terms still open: the parties are "masters of their contractual fate" (Bingham J, as quoted). So in law the parties **choose** when to be bound (ink means decided), where Laura's ink means owned (Q114's disanalogy again).
  - A law firm's note dates The Junior K to "1958"; the judgment's own citation is 1988.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 25 with row 26 (3c, and 2.0 or 3d): Boeing counts no order while financing is open "even if ... highly probable", and in 2025 struck $17,759 million of signed orders whose payment it doubted.** It is the aha for "nothing is stated before the move" (a top stated a year early would be bought in all but about 11 of 100 paths, and Laura still waits), and it shows a finance judge that signed is not firm even in a factory's order book.
2. **Row 36 (2.0, 3d): the changed claim.** In every trade read, a figure is firm once someone is bound to it, with part of the money down. Laura's ink asks for the whole figure owned. This is the startling perspective Book 19 asks for, carried by a comparison, not a staged image.
3. **Row 2 with row 3 (3c or 3d): STEM, "Subject To Enough Merchandise".** By the Baltic Code's account, the shipping trade's commonest pencil condition is whether the cargo is really there. In a 2016 case an $8 million fixture stayed pencil because the charterer had not yet contracted a cargo. It is the closest dissimilar match to "a figure stated only once the thing that pays it is owned".
4. **Row 15 (2.0 or 3d): the Sydney Opera House's defined "Pencil Booking".** It is a pencil fact from a performing-arts house in the team's own city, and even there ink is a signature and a deposit. One clause shows that Laura's rule is stricter than the Opera House's. Whether Australia earns its place is Caplet's call.
5. **Row 37 (3d): who carries pencil.** Shipowners, venues and performers carry the other side's pencil. Laura's rule keeps hers on her own side, so co-sponsors are shown only ink. It gives 3d's line before her words a reason a judge has not seen.
