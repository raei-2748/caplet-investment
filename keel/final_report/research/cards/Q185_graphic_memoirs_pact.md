## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. A memoir is signed and sworn, and only the signature allows no degrees

(a) Philippe Lejeune, whose 1975 essay is the standard theory of the memoir, says a memoir makes two promises at once: the author's name on the cover is a signature, which readers accept or refuse but "never" quibble over, and the story inside is an oath to tell the truth, which readers check by degrees.
(b) Laura's statement in 2031 makes the same two promises, the signature being that the money is her own and comes first, and the oath being that a Treasury fund already held for her will pay the figure; because she states a figure only after buying what pays it, her oath is as all-or-nothing as Lejeune's signature, since on the day she speaks the money is held or it is not.
(c) It breaks because the late-2032 fund's payout is not fixed (if the fund ended early in 2031 while waiting cash earned nothing, the top would fall short by up to $4,589; at today's forward rates nothing is short, NUMBERS section 3), so a sliver of degree stays inside each figure, which is why each end is cut by 0.5% and rounded down and why the report never says a stated figure "cannot move".

- The correction: card Q174 read Lejeune as saying a memoir's formula is "not 'I, the undersigned' but 'I swear'", as if a memoir were unsigned. The text says the referential pact's formula "would not be 'I, the undersigned' either", and the essay gives the signature its own section, titled "I, the Undersigned". A memoir is signed; the oath is a second, separate promise.
- Decision: 2.0's definition (ink means owned, never signed); "certain" takes the stated range as its subject, with no probability label (cards Q71, Q74).
- Report part: the store's reason the 2.0 guard is right. Never name a signature beside the definition (voice round 7; the rater's note on Q174-012).
- Source: Lejeune (1989), pp. 13-14, 21-24.
- Pedigree or aha: PEDIGREE for the report. It explains a guard, and to land it needs the word "signature" in the sentence, which is exactly the reading the guard keeps out.

### 2. The honest oath has two limits, and the second one is her flexibility

(a) Lejeune's "supplementary proof of honesty" has two parts, not one: the memoirist limits her oath to what she can know, and she also names the part of her life it covers, "not committing myself in any way about some other aspect" (p. 22).
(b) Laura's plan uses the second limit: she states a range for the facility and nothing about the half of her shares promised to no one, so co-sponsors hear that her flexibility exists (3d's one clause: it, not they, meets a stronger Taiwan dollar) but never its size, much as Art Spiegelman, in *Maus*, drew the camp orchestra his father did not remember half-hidden behind marching prisoners, "to insist that they were there".
(c) It breaks because a memoirist leaves out a past that is already fixed, while Laura leaves out an amount that still moves with share prices and could be small by 2033, so her silence about its size is not privacy but the accurate way to treat a pencil figure.

- Decision: half the shares promised to no one (IPS l.15); 3d's one clause on her flexibility; 3b sizes it for judges only.
- Report part: 3d, as the sharper form of Q174-013 if that stays the report's one Lejeune row. It already carries the rater's fix: she states only what she owns and keeps the rest promised to no one. The orchestra stays in the store.
- Source: Lejeune (1989), p. 22; Spiegelman (2011), pp. 30-31, as quoted; *Maus II*, p. 54, as described.

### 3. Whoever receives an ambiguous message must pick one reading

(a) In 2001 Lejeune looked back on his own theory and wrote that whoever receives an ambiguous message "cannot stay sitting between two chairs", which is why almost every novel built on the author's own life is read as a memoir anyway.
(b) A range is an ambiguous message, and Keel's funder test (four simulated funders, 16 Nov) found what Lejeune predicts: all four booked one figure, not a range, and filed it as "committed", and the framing decided which (the top when told both ends were already held, the bottom otherwise); so whatever Laura says will be booked as a commitment, which is why the top is inked before she speaks and 3d leads with that one figure.
(c) It breaks at the thing her genre is admired for: Spiegelman drew the march once with the orchestra and once without and left readers holding both, and Hillary Chute praises graphic memoirs for rejecting "the categories of nonfiction and fiction altogether", but a funder's books cannot hold two versions, so in her words to co-sponsors Laura gives up her genre's best device.

- Companion from the opposite side: when *Maus II* appeared on the *New York Times* fiction list, Spiegelman wrote that "to the extent that 'fiction' indicates a work isn't factual, I feel a bit queasy", and the Times moved it to nonfiction. A true book on the fiction shelf is the mirror image of a pencil figure filed as committed. Either way, the shelf a reader files you on decides what the reader does with you.
- Decision: the 2031 move before the meetings; 3d leads with the single figure to count; nothing pencil is said.
- Report part: the strongest candidate for the report's one Lejeune clause, ahead of Q174-013, because it predicts something the plan's own test checked: in 3c ("why nothing is stated before the move") or 3d's team lead-in. Unnamed, it is the store's reason for 3d's single figure.

### 4. A hedge on every sentence hides the truth; a smaller claim keeps it plain (the best companion)

(a) In *Fun Home*, Alison Bechdel shows herself at ten, unsure her diary was "absolutely, objectively true", adding "I think" to each sentence and then a "curvy circumflex" over words and whole entries, until "the truth is barely perceptible behind a hedge of qualifiers, encryption, and stray punctuation" (pp. 141-143, 169).
(b) Laura's plan is honest about uncertainty the other way round: it says fewer things and qualifies none of them, and the funder test showed why, since two of the four funders read the one qualifier then in her words, a margin for running costs, as a sign the $115,000 "might be trimmed"; so the margin moved to 3c for judges, and her top is now stated bare.
(c) It breaks because her doubt is real, not compulsive: fund costs beyond the fee, and an early fund end, can touch the top, so the plan prices them before she speaks (the 0.5% margin and the rounding down) and explains them to judges in 3c, which puts the qualifier where it belongs without denying it.

- Decision: 3d states the top bare; "certain" on the stated range; no "barring a US default" after "guaranteed" (Book 19 makes the same point from Caplet's side).
