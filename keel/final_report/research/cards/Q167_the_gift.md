## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Carnegie's condition, turned around and bought

- **The parallel.** Andrew Carnegie paid for a town's library only after its council promised, by resolution, to spend a tenth of his gift every year on running it. In 1916 his foundation stopped all new Ohio grants because 27 of Ohio's 82 Carnegie libraries had broken that promise. In Indiana, not one had.
- **What it shows.** Laura's plan is Carnegie's condition turned around. The running money still comes first. But Laura does not take someone else's promise of it: she buys it herself in Treasuries before the building is asked for. So the promise that broke at a third of Ohio's libraries is the part of her plan that is already paid for.
- **Where it breaks.** Carnegie wanted the town's yearly promise as proof that the town wanted a library. Laura's Treasuries prove only her own commitment, so the report should never say that co-sponsors must carry running costs. The break also shows why her version is the stronger one. A promise of future taxes rested on future town councils. Hers rests on two deposits she makes herself, in 2027 and 2028.
- **Decision it serves:** the ten payments are bought before anything else (3.0, 3a), and co-sponsors are asked only toward the building (3d).
- **Report part:** 3.0 or 3a, one sentence.
- **Sources:** Tansey (2016); National Park Service (n.d.-a, n.d.-b); Carnegie (1889).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. A finance judge may know Carnegie's condition (Q67), but not that a third of Ohio's libraries broke the promise it demanded, or that Laura's plan buys that promise outright.

### Idea 2. Franklin's projected gift, and "I'll match Ben Franklin"

- **The parallel.** In 1789 Benjamin Franklin left Boston £1,000. He wrote that if it "succeeds as projected without interruption for one hundred years", it would be £131,000 for public works. It reached only about three-fifths to two-thirds of that. This was too little to build a school and also pay its running costs, until Andrew Carnegie said "I'll match Ben Franklin". He gave $408,396 in bonds, and only their income was spent, to run the school.
- **What it shows.** Franklin's figure is a pencil figure in its purest form: a sum that still needed interest to earn it, with its condition written into the sentence. That is exactly why Laura names no top until the move, when the Treasuries that will pay it are held for her.
- **Where it breaks.** Nobody had planned around Franklin's estimate, and a Massachusetts court later called the shortfall "unimportant". Co-sponsors will plan around Laura's figure, so the case's warning about promising more than she can contribute makes her standard stricter than Franklin's. Boston also split the work the opposite way to Laura: Franklin's money paid for the building, Carnegie's bonds paid the running costs, and the city gave the land.
- **Decision it serves:** no figure is stated before the move (3c), and a forecast return is pencil (3e).
- **Report part:** 3c, one clause, or 3.0 together with idea 1 (see "Best three", item 1).
- **Sources:** Franklin (1789); Franklin Foundation v. Boston (1957); Franklin Foundation v. Attorney General (1960); City of Boston (1922).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. Every finance reader knows Franklin as the patron saint of compound interest. Few know that his own projected gift fell well short, and that a second giver had to supply the running money.

### Idea 3. Seneca's silent exception, and the IPS's two reasons

- **The parallel.** Seneca says a wise man begins every plan with "if nothing happens to prevent it". He adds that every promise carries a silent exception: "if I can, if I ought, if things stay as they are" (On Benefits 4.34, 4.39). He keeps a small promise even at a loss, but lets a large one go when things have changed (4.36).
- **What it shows.** The IPS says Seneca's silent exception out loud and cuts it down to two named exits: "a missed deposit or a Treasury default", with "A fall in the market is not one of them". After the move, her stated range needs no "if I can", because the Treasuries that pay it are already held for her.
- **Where it breaks.** Seneca's exception protects the giver's freedom to change course, and the IPS gives that freedom up for the stated range. The break teaches that this is the price of being believed. Co-sponsors expect a founder's large figure to carry Seneca's exception, and they discount it for that reason.
- **Decision it serves:** the two reasons to change the plan, and the confidence stated for the range (3c).
- **Report part:** 3c or 3d, one clause. Keep the two reasons and the ways a promise can fail as two separate lists (CANON; card Q109).
- **Sources:** Seneca, De beneficiis 4.34-4.39 (Basore's Latin; Stewart's English).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha for most judges. Lawyers know the doctrine that a promise holds only while circumstances stay the same (rebus sic stantibus), but few readers know Seneca's text.

### Idea 4. Rome's shared buildings: "the work which had to be finished first"

- **The parallel.** In AD 112 Pliny told the emperor Trajan that the galleries and colonnades private donors had promised for Nicaea's theatre were "all postponed now that the work, which had to be finished first, has come to a stop". Trajan ruled that the donors be asked to build only once the theatre stood. Roman law also bound a donor once a city had started building in view of the money he had promised. If he later became poor, it held him only to a fifth of his estate.
- **What it shows.**
  - Co-funders' promises have always waited on the part that must be finished first. Laura's owned money is that part, in money.
  - Rome made the city carry the risk that a founder's fortune fell after he promised. After the move, her stated range carries none of that risk.
- **Where it breaks.** Nicaea's theatre stalled because it was sinking into soft ground, not for lack of money.
  - So owned money removes the money reason for a stall, never the building's own. Keeping the building on track is the residency's work, and paying for overruns is the job of her flexibility.
  - Rome's rule bound the donor by law. Laura's figure binds her only through her credibility.
- **Decision it serves:** Laura's figure goes first and carries no conditions, while the co-sponsors' pledges can be conditional (3d, SD-3, SD-7). It also gives 3e one limit.
- **Report part:** 3d, one sentence, or SD-3's evidence clause.
- **Sources:** Pliny, Letters 10.39-10.40; Digest 50.12.1.4, 50.12.9.
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha, and very niche. It is the oldest record found of the exact structure of the 2031 meetings.

### Idea 5. The opening gift and the clinching gift

- **The parallel.** Mauss, following Malinowski, writes that in the Trobriand kula the first gift is the vaga, the "opening gift". It binds the receiver to a return gift, the yotile, the "clinching gift". The oldest written building campaign, in 1 Chronicles 29, opens the same way: King David gives first "of mine own proper good", and the leaders then give more than he did.
- **What it shows.**
  - The case asks Laura's figure to work as the opening gift: to "make potential co-sponsors more willing to contribute". The big capital funders are written to be clinching gifts, which pay only at the end.
  - An opening gift that might shrink opens nothing. Mauss's rule that a giver who fails to honour a gift loses face "for ever" (« à jamais ») is what card Q67's cut figures show.
- **Where it breaks.** In the kula the return gift goes back to the first giver. Co-sponsors give to the building, not to Laura, so her gift opens by example and binds no one. That is why it must be exact and owned rather than generous.
- **Decision it serves:** Laura's figure first and unconditional (3d). It gives SD-3 its oldest names.
- **Report part:** 3d or the store. The names are vivid, but the report's words stay "first" and "unconditional".
- **Sources:** Mauss (1925); 1 Chronicles 29:3-9.
- **Pedigree or aha:** a medium aha. Mauss is known to anthropologists, and the two names make SD-3 easy to see.

### Idea 6. A gift the receiver can carry

- **The parallel.**
  - Seneca mocks Alexander for giving a man a city: some friends are "not strong enough to put a city in their pockets". He asks each giver to give "neither more nor less than we are able".
  - Cicero says that giving beyond one's means wrongs the nearest claims first.
  - Maimonides ranks highest the gift after which the receiver "will not have to ask others".
- **What it shows.** A building is the modern city in the pocket: a gift whose running costs the receiver may not be able to carry. Laura pays the running money first, which makes her building gift one the residency can hold. This follows Cicero's order, because the nearest claim, the ten payments, is bought first. It also gives the residency what Maimonides ranks highest: ten years in which it need not ask anyone for running money.
- **Where it breaks.** All three writers measure a giver's means against a fortune the giver already knows. Laura's facility money is not known until the move, so for her "within her means" has to mean "within what she owns", a tighter test than any of them set. And Maimonides' highest gift lasts for good, while hers covers ten years; funding after 2042 is outside the case.
- **Decision it serves:** the order in which the plan buys things (3a, then 3b), and the half kept, defended as the middle of Seneca's two-sided rule, never as "optimal".
- **Report part:** 3a or 3b, one clause at most.
- **Sources:** Seneca, De beneficiis 2.15-2.16; Cicero, De officiis 1.42-1.44; Maimonides, Gifts to the Poor 10:7 and 7:5, Appraisals 8:13.
- **Pedigree or aha:** mostly pedigree, because these texts are well known. Only the application is new.

## Best three for the report

1. **Carnegie tried both halves of Laura's choice** (rows 1, 8 and 9; ideas 1 and 2).
   - As a library founder he took towns' promises of running money, and by 1916 a third of Ohio's had broken them.
   - As a co-funder in Boston he paid the running money himself, in bonds. That happened only because Benjamin Franklin's projected £131,000 had come up well short.
   - Why a judge says "aha": two of the most famous names in American money run the experiment for us, and the result points to Laura's choice: own the running money and state no figure that interest must still earn. It belongs in 3.0 or 3a.
2. **Nicaea, AD 112** (rows 23 and 24; idea 4).
   - Pliny wrote that every private donor's promise for the theatre was postponed because "the work, which had to be finished first, has come to a stop". Trajan ruled that the donors pay only once the theatre stood.
   - Why a judge says "aha": it is SD-3's structure 1,900 years early. It shows a judge why Laura's figure has to be the one with no conditions, and why it must be owned. It belongs in 3d as one sentence.
3. **Seneca's silent exception** (rows 11 and 12; idea 3).
   - Every promise, Seneca says, carries an unspoken "if I can, if I ought, if things stay as they are".
   - Why a judge says "aha": the IPS's two reasons are that exception said out loud and cut to two. It shows a judge why co-sponsors discount founders' figures, and why Laura's need no discount after the move. It belongs in 3c or 3d as one clause, with the two reasons and the ways a promise can fail kept as separate lists.
