- **Problem class:** matching dated payments with coupon bonds when the coupons arrive years before the payments. **Field that solved it first:** fixed-income portfolio management. Homer and Leibowitz's "interest on interest" (Salomon Brothers memo of 5 Oct 1970; *Inside the Yield Book*, 1972), then pension dedication (cash matching), which Salomon began marketing in 1979. The market's product answer came after: zero-coupon corporate bonds (1981), zero receipts on Treasuries (TIGRs and CATS, Aug 1982) and the Treasury's own STRIPS (Jan 1985). Municipal escrows' "sufficient without reinvestment" is the same answer in law (card Q54).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Reinvestment risk exists only where a coupon arrives before the payment it funds. Pension dedication mostly avoided it by structure. Where some was left, practitioners more often assumed a cautious positive rate than none.
  - **Structure first.** A cash-matched book is safe "only if all interest coupons go directly to loss payments so that there is no reinvestment exposure" (Ferguson, 1983). In a monthly pension dedication, "almost all cash flow is paid out each month", so there is "very little reinvestment risk" (Fabozzi, 2005, ch. 48). Retiree payments are front-loaded: at 13%, 90% of the value of a life annuity at 65 falls in its first ten years (Bader, 1983).
  - **Rates assumed.** Bader advised building the book on a "best estimate" rate, with an optional margin, and noted that prefunding "occasionally" pays "even if the reinvestment rate is zero". Fabozzi's worked dedication assumes 3% when the book yields 7.83% and actuarial rates were 5-8%. Leibowitz often built for "no reinvestment" (already in store, Q16-026).
  - **The bigger 1983 worry was calls.** Called corporate bonds left funds "unable to reinvest the proceeds at a high enough rate". Funds answered with noncallables or low-coupon bonds (Bader).
  - **Laura is the hard case.** Her first payment is six years after purchase and the ten are level, so every coupon of the five T-bonds waits. Interest on interest is 13.3%-15.3% of each of the last five payments (NUMBERS 8a).
  - **Judge's note:** dedication escaped reinvestment risk by spending each coupon as it arrived; Laura cannot spend one before 2033, which is why the extra Treasuries exist.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, worded as "the strictest found, and a fee below it".**
  - **The floor of practice is 0%.** Leibowitz's "no reinvestment", the escrows' and rating agencies' "without reinvestment" (Q54, Q20), and a mortgage deal's "Assumed reinvestment rate: 0%" in Fabozzi's handbook.
  - **The plan goes one step further.** It is sized so the ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, after the bill fund's 0.15% fee (NUMBERS 0, 2). That is 0% less a fee.
  - **Positive "conservative" rates were breached.** Fabozzi's 1992 example assumed "only 3%". The 6-month Treasury yield averaged under 3% in every month from Sep 2001 to Feb 2005, and in 217 of the 407 months since Nov 1992 (Keel arithmetic on FRED DGS6MO).
  - **Never say "below every assumption".** Say equal to the strictest, then a fee below it.
  - **Judge's note:** a 3% reinvestment rate was the cautious choice in 1992; nine years later bills paid less for three and a half years running.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Stripping was the market's third answer, not its only one, and it was partly a tax trade.
  - **First answer:** spend coupons on the payments due (cash matching, 1979 on).
  - **Second answer:** discount and zero-coupon corporate bonds (1981), bought at 40-100bp less yield (Silver, 1981-82).
  - **Third answer:** zero receipts on Treasuries (TIGRs and CATS, Aug 1982), which Treasury allowed only after TEFRA 1982 closed the tax gaps (Becketti, 1988; TreasuryDirect), then STRIPS (1985).
  - **Laura can use none of the three before 2033.** She has no payment to spend coupons on, and STRIPS are banned this year. So she does what an escrow does: she sizes at zero and holds extra Treasuries.
  - **The difference in cost.** A zero's buyer gave up yield for the bond's whole life. Laura's extra Treasuries cost $35,894 of the 2028 deposit, and at today's market rates the ten never need them: about $45,000 (2033 money) comes back to her (NUMBERS, the top-up reconciliation).
  - **Judge's note:** the 1982 buyer of a 14.25% Treasury "locked in" a coupon, not a compounded rate. With coupons rolled in 6-month bills he earned about 9.3% a year to 2002 (Keel arithmetic, row 42).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 42 (3a, the aha).** The 1982 Treasury that "locked in" 14.25% compounded to about 9.3% with its coupons in bills (Keel arithmetic). It shows in one example why "pay what they say" and "earn what they yield" differ, and why the extra Treasuries exist.
2. **Row 20 (3a or 3e area (1)).** A Federal Reserve bank's own 1981 sum: the same bond, the same yield, 14.25% with coupons, 16.00% as a zero, "the ultimate protection against reinvestment risk". It is an outside authority for the risk the report sizes.
3. **Row 11 with row 10 (3e area (1), claim 2).** Practice's "only 3%" reinvestment rate of 1992 was breached for 42 months from 2001. The plan's test (cash waiting earns nothing, after a fee) is the strictest found, and the report can say so without overclaiming.
4. **Rows 5 and 8 (3a, the disanalogy).** Dedication escaped reinvestment by spending each coupon as it came. Laura cannot spend one before 2033, so the textbook's comfort does not carry over. This is where a judge who knows dedication would push.
5. **Row 21 with row 38 (3a, the cost of certainty).** In the 1980s certainty cost 40-100bp of yield for life. Laura's extra Treasuries cost $35,894 of the 2028 deposit and mostly come back at today's rates. Falling rates also made dedication dearer, which is IPS l.13's completion in another form.

## Gaps

- **Leibowitz (1986), Parts I and II, full text.** It is CFA members only, and only the abstracts were read. The reinvestment rates his worked examples assumed are still unread at source.
- **Leibowitz's Salomon memos of 1970 and 1979.** They were read only as Ferguson reprints them (Appendix I, Table C). The 1972 edition of *Inside the Yield Book* was not read at source: the "compounds automatically" quote is secondary (row 2), and the 1972 book's own worked example of realised compound yield was not found.
- **Realised shortfalls of real 1980s dedications after rates fell.** No source found reports whether a named dedicated book ran short on reinvestment. Euromoney and the NBER chapter report cost and re-optimisation, not shortfalls. A next round could try pension plan 10-Ks of 1986-1995 (Chrysler, Ameritech, American Airlines) or the SOA's *Record* panels of 1984-1987.
- **The Wall Street Journal stories of 12 and 18 Aug 1982 on TIGRs and CATS.** They are cited by Garbade and were not read.
- **Whether Treasury called the 14% bonds of 2006-11** that backed the first TIGRs (row 25) was not checked.
- **The 14.25% bond of 2002 (row 42).** Its exact issue date, price and call status were not checked at source, so the Keel arithmetic assumes a purchase at par.
- **Fabozzi handbook (7th ed., 2005).** The CMO and index chapters' authors and numbers behind row 12 were not noted.
- **The 1981 ban on long zeros.** Silver says zero-coupon issues longer than ten years did not exist in 1981. That was not confirmed elsewhere.
