## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, CHANGED. "Set aside first" is the real advice, but the parallel breaks in four places (rows 1-11).**
  - **The real advice.** The Authors Guild: "Every time you get paid, you should immediately transfer 30% to 40% into your business savings account", then "pay yourself a monthly salary" from what is left. Four other creator sources give 20-40% for tax:
    - Authors Guild, on advances: 20-30%;
    - Comic Art Ed!, for cartoonists: 20-30%;
    - Steve Laube Agency: "at least 30% ... and 40% is even better";
    - The Creative Independent: "roughly a third".
  - **Why first.** "Receiving a large book advance can tempt you to splurge." Thaler: "When the check arrives in the mailbox it tends to get spent."
  - **The plan's order has the same shape.** The first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes before anything is left for the floor and shares (the $2,388 top-up, then $35,894 of extra Treasuries; NUMBERS). The IPS wrote that order down 14 months before the money arrives (Keel arithmetic, 7 Nov 2026 to Jan 2028).
  - **Where it breaks.**
    - (a) An author sets aside a percentage of each cheque; Laura sets aside fixed dollars.
    - (b) The author's set-aside is mostly for tax due within months; Laura's covers ten payments due 2033-2042.
    - (c) The author holds it as cash; Laura holds it in dated Treasuries.
    - (d) The author protects her own spending; Laura keeps a promise to others. Her own living costs sit outside the portfolio (case).
  - **The evidence-based version favours dollars.** "Simply saving a certain percentage of monthly income may leave a family with an inadequate cash buffer" (JPMorgan Chase Institute, 6 million accounts).
  - **Judge's note:** The Authors Guild tells authors to pay themselves a salary and keep a "bare minimum option ... for lean times". The residency's $50,000 is a salary with no lean-year option, so the plan buys it whole before 2033 instead of paying it from a running account (row 4).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Not "more parts" and not "paid by the page", but the money does wait on her own pages (rows 12-24).**
  - **Number of parts.**
    - Prose advances come in "two to six installments, with three or four being most common", and "The larger the advance, the more installments" (Authors Guild 2026).
    - Graphic novel accounts give two to four parts. Kurtz (2026): signing, final script, completed book, publication. Ostertag (2021): signing, "sometimes a second check halfway", the rest at hand-in. Already in store: the AOI's thirds and one cartoonist's half and half.
  - **Not paid by the page.**
    - The ASA says page rates "are not applicable to graphic novels ... a creator is paid an advance and royalties".
    - An agent says an advance "is not calculated based on a 'page rate'".
  - **What does differ.** The middle parts wait on the creator's own art milestones, and a graphic novel is slow.
    - One editor puts signing to release at "a year" on average for experienced creators. One recent signing goes on sale "September 2027". His slowest creator took "seven or eight years".
    - Kids Comics Unite: timelines are "often 1-3+ years".
  - **Say instead:** the 2028 deposit "can depend on her own delivery dates and the publisher's acceptance as well as the market". Her contracts are not in the case, so "can", never "does".
  - **What it means for the plan.** Late money is less money for the plan (Q12-031). NUMBERS 5 gives the room:
    - the payments stay full unless the deposit falls by more than 98.4% at today's yields;
    - the escrow standard holds unless it falls by more than 74.5%;
    - "most" holds unless it falls by more than 49.0%.
  - **Judge's note:** a graphic novel is not paid by the page, yet its money waits on the pages. So part of the 2028 deposit's timing is in Laura's own hands, not the market's (rows 16-17, 24).
- **Claim 3: KEEP, one level deeper. The IPS's "later and smaller" has causes that are not the economy (rows 25-36).**
  - **Ordinary years.**
    - In 2024, not a recession year, 52% of 262 UK authors surveyed had trouble with advances or royalties, "the average delay of over a year". One wrote: "made me wait two years to receive bulk of advance that used to be paid on signing".
    - The US Guild (2026): "payment timelines have gotten longer".
    - UK agents answer that late payments are "only a small minority of transactions" (CONFLICTING on how common).
  - **The agent's account.** The money passes through it under a clock: 10 business days, 21 at most (US AALA Canon 2026; UK AAA Code). When that link failed, a New York agency's bookkeeper moved more than $3.4m in 2011-2018, over $2.7m of it authors' royalties. One author's "approximately $200,000" due in fall 2017 was explained away until March 2018.
  - **Comics' own chain.**
    - Diamond held "payments to vendors" in April 2020. At its 2025 Chapter 11 it owed $40m unsecured, and publishers had been unpaid "for up to two months".
    - The small publisher AfterShock stopped paying some creators in 2022: "About six months ago, they basically just stopped paying". The company said "There are no non-payments".
  - **Lumps get spent in bad years.** Japanese households spent .437 of bonus pay against .685 of wages. But "During the recession-oil shock period 1974–76 the MPC from bonus income jumped to over 1.0".
  - **Judge's note:** in 2024, a year without a recession, half of the UK authors surveyed had a late advance or royalty, and the average delay was over a year (row 25).
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. Lumps can be chosen, if the payer is trusted (rows 37-38).**
  - In Kenya, "86% of farmers pick monthly payments over daily payments even when the price for daily payments is 15% higher".
  - But "many buyers lack the credibility to offer infrequent payments".
  - The ladder is the same bargain the other way round. Laura pays up front and is repaid in ten lumps on fixed dates by the payer markets trust most. An advance in parts is the opposite case: paid later by a publisher who can delay.
  - **Judge's note:** dairy farmers give up a higher daily price to be paid monthly, but only by a buyer they trust. Laura's ladder makes the same trade with the US Treasury as the buyer (rows 37-38).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 4 (2.3, 3a).** The residency's $50,000 is a salary with no "bare minimum option", which is why it is bought whole before 2033. It turns the Authors Guild's own advice into the reason for the ladder.
2. **Row 25 (3e area (2)).** In 2024, a non-recession year, the average delay was over a year for UK authors with problems. It gives the deposit's timing risk a second cause besides recessions, without changing the IPS.
3. **Row 16, with rows 17 and 24 (3e area (2)).** The money waits on the pages, though it is not paid by the page. This corrects claim 2 and explains why Table 3 row 1 asks for written confirmation by 1 December 2027.
4. **Row 38 (3a).** Farmers trade price for lumps only with a buyer they trust. It gives a startling outside parallel for buying ten dated Treasuries, and shows where it breaks.
5. **Row 22 (2.3, CONCEIT V2).** An advance is pencil until the book is accepted. It lets Laura's own trade carry the pencil-and-ink turn, set up where it is used.
