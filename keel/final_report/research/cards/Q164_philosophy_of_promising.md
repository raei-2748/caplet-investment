## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Nietzsche: the right to make promises is earned by becoming calculable, and Laura buys hers

- (a) Nietzsche says that before anyone can make a promise, he must first learn "to distinguish between necessitated
  and accidental phenomena", become "calculable", and so be able to "guarantee himself as a future" (Second Essay, s.1).
- (b) The IPS opens by saying Laura cannot do this with her income, because in a recession her advances "come later
  and smaller"; so the plan does Nietzsche's sorting on her money instead: what must happen (Treasuries held to their
  dates) carries every figure she states, and what may happen (shares) carries none until half of it is moved.
- (c) It breaks because Nietzsche's promiser makes himself reliable through his own will, while Laura's reliability is
  bought from the US Treasury; the break shows that the IPS's first paragraph (her career is a holding she cannot sell)
  is the reason she cannot promise from herself, and the rest of the plan is how she earns the right anyway.
- **Decision it serves:** state a figure only once it is owned; the ten payments owned before the doors open (most of
  each in January 2027, the rest in January 2028). **Part:** 3.0, or 2.0 beside the definition of ink.
- **Source:** Nietzsche (1887/1913), Second Essay, ss.1-2, Samuel's translation, read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A judge may know the phrase "an animal that can promise"; almost none will have seen that
  Nietzsche's list of what must come first (sort necessary from accidental, "see the distant as present", "fix with
  certainty what is the end, and what is the means", "calculate") reads as a description of a bond ladder bought six
  years ahead, or that the IPS's opening is the confession that makes the plan necessary.

### 2. A founder's figure is two acts in one sentence: a forecast or a report, and a promise

- (a) Searle says a promise is about "a future act A of S", the speaker's own act, and that a sincere promiser only has
  to think the act possible; Austin says a statement about something that is not there ("John's children are all bald"
  when John has no children) is "void".
- (b) So a founder who tells co-sponsors "I will put in $115,000" does two things at once: she says what she will have,
  which is true or false, and she promises what she will give, which is kept or broken; when the first part is a
  forecast, a wrong forecast becomes a broken promise, and Laura's rule makes the first part a report of Treasuries she
  already owns before she makes the promise.
- (c) It breaks because philosophy asks a promiser only to believe she can perform, so a founder whose top rests on
  shares makes a sincere and perfectly valid promise; the break shows that Laura's rule is stricter than the philosophy
  of promising (it asks for the money, not the belief), and that the case's warning ("If Laura promises more than she
  can ultimately contribute") is about exactly the promise philosophy lets through.
- **Decision it serves:** never state a figure that might have to shrink; 3c's confidence (the stated range is certain
  after the move) and 3d's one figure. **Part:** 3c (why the confidence is "certain"), 3d.
- **Source:** Searle (1965), section V, read at source; Austin (1962), Lectures II and IV, read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Finance judges talk about forecasts and commitments as different things, but rarely notice
  that one sentence to a funder is both; the split explains in one step why a share-based top is a risk to Laura's
  credibility and an owned top is not.

### 3. Hobbes: a gift promised in the future tense binds nothing; ink is the other sign that makes it bind

- (a) Hobbes says a free gift promised as "tomorrow I will give" is "a sign I have not given yet" and binds nothing,
  while "do give to be delivered tomorrow" means "my tomorrow's right given away today"; and future-tense words can pass
  a gift "if there be other signs of the will to transfer a right, besides words".
- (b) Laura's facility money is a free gift in Hobbes's sense ("not contract, but 'gift,' 'free gift,' 'grace'"), so
  her words alone would bind little; the Treasuries bought before the 2031 meetings are the other sign besides words,
  and they let her speak as close to Hobbes's present tense as a founder can: the money is set aside now, to be handed
  over in 2033.
- (c) It breaks because the Treasuries stay hers in law until she gives them (she could still sell, Q18-027), so no
  right passes in 2031; the break shows what ink does and does not do: it removes every way she could fail to give
  except her own choice, and her own choice is the one thing a founder's word can honestly promise.
- **Decision it serves:** state a figure only once it is owned; 3d's language, where her figure comes "first either
  way" (voice round 9). For the conceit: pencil is the future tense, ink the present tense of a gift. **Part:** 3d;
  2.0 or CONCEIT V2.2 as one fact behind "owned".
- **Source:** Hobbes (1651), Leviathan ch. 14, read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The covenant language of Leviathan is known; its tense test for free gifts, and its rule
  that other signs besides words can make a future-tense gift bind, are not, and they explain why a founder who owns
  the money can be believed when one who expects it cannot.

### 4. Hume and Hobbes on going first: the first mover's risk, priced by the half kept back

- (a) Hume says two farmers whose corn ripens on different days both "lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence
  and security", because whoever helps first must "remain in uncertainty"; Hobbes says "he that performeth first, has
  no assurance the other will perform after", unless "a common power" can compel the second.
- (b) In 2031 Laura is the one who goes first (in Taiwan the founder pays first, and SD-3 shows that capital funders
  are written to pay last), and no power compels the co-sponsors, whose pledges stay conditional; the IPS prices that
  risk in advance, because half the shares are kept for, among other things, "a co-sponsor falling short".
- (c) It breaks because Hume's and Hobbes's first mover hands something over and can be cheated of it, while Laura
  hands nothing over before 2033 and none of her ten payments rests on anyone else's money (IPS l.11); the break shows
  that her risk as first mover is not being robbed but being left with a building short of money, which is exactly
  what her flexibility is for.
- **Decision it serves:** half the shares promised to no one; Laura's figure first in 3d. **Part:** 3d (one clause
  behind SD-3), 3e.
- **Source:** Hume (1739-40), T 3.2.5.8, read at source; Hobbes (1651), ch. 14, read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha as a pedigree. SD-3 ("first money in a field of last money") is the store's strongest
  co-sponsor idea; this gives it two ancestors, 1651 and 1740, and shows that the first mover's danger, and the reserve
  that answers it, were described before fundraising was a profession.

### 5. Kant's liar, and the honest promise the world breaks

- (a) Kant condemns the borrower who promises to repay "although I know this will never happen", because if everyone
  did it "no one would believe that anything has been promised him" (Groundwork 4:422).
- (b) A founder who states a top resting on shares passes Kant's test, since she believes she can pay; the case's
  warning is about her, not the liar, and Kant's reasoning still explains the price of her habit: when many sincere
  figures shrink, funders learn to discount every stated figure (Carnegie Hall books about 89 cents per pledged dollar,
  Q139-001), so an owned figure is worth more because it is rare.
- (c) It breaks because Kant's test is about the will, and Laura's danger was never a bad will; the break shows that
  the classic accounts (Kant's test, Hume's "penalty of never being trusted again", Austin's insincere promise) mostly
  police promisers who could keep their word and choose not to, while Laura's rule polices the case none of them
  forbids: the honest promise that a fall in share prices later breaks.
- **Decision it serves:** never state a figure that might have to shrink. **Part:** 3c or 3d (the reason for the rule
  in one clause); 3f if the reflection needs the team's own reason for "none" (IPS l.19).
- **Source:** Kant (1785), Ak 4:422, read at source in an English translation; Hume T 3.2.5.10; Austin p. 16.
- **Pedigree or aha:** Kant's example is pedigree (every ethics course teaches it); the gap it leaves is the aha.

### 6. Seneca's saving clause, written down and closed

- (a) Seneca says the wise man begins everything with the clause "If nothing shall occur to the contrary", and that a
  promise binds only if "All conditions must be the same as they were when I made the promise".
- (b) A founder whose top rests on shares needs Seneca's clause, "if markets hold", but co-sponsors count the figure,
  not the clause (the funder test of 16 Nov: they count the top); Laura's owned figure needs no clause, so she can say
  it plainly.
- (c) It breaks because Seneca's clause is silent and kept in the promiser's mind, while the IPS writes down the only
  events that would make Caplet change the plan and closes the list at two (a missed deposit, a Treasury default); by
  the 2031 meetings both deposits are in, so one of the two can no longer happen, and the break shows that the plan
  turned a Stoic's private reservation into a short public list.
- **Decision it serves:** the two reasons to change (IPS l.17); 3d's plain figure. **Part:** 3d, 3e. Never "only two
  things can break the plan" (Q109: the reasons to change and the ways a promise fails are two lists).
- **Source:** Seneca, De Beneficiis IV (English text on Wikisource), read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The escape clause in a promise is 2,000 years old and well known to lawyers as rebus sic
  stantibus; seeing the IPS's two reasons as that clause, written out and almost used up by 2031, is new.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 2 with rows 24-26 (3c, 3d): one sentence to a funder is a forecast and a promise at once.** It explains in a
   single step why a share-based top endangers Laura's credibility (a wrong forecast becomes a broken promise) and why
   an owned top does not, and it gives 3c a reason for "certain after the move" that a judge has not read elsewhere.
2. **Idea 1 with rows 1-3 (3.0 or 2.0, and 3d): Nietzsche's right to make promises, bought rather than bred.** It
   joins the IPS's opening (a career she cannot sell) to its last line ("with these promises, ours is to take none"):
   the plan exists because Laura cannot make herself calculable, so she makes her money calculable instead.
3. **Idea 3 with rows 16-18 and 31 (3d, 2.0): Hobbes's tense test for free gifts.** It is the strikingly dissimilar
   parallel Book 19 asks for, true at source, and it makes ink a tense rather than a signature, which is the one reading
   of the conceit readers keep getting wrong (card Q42).
