- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. The quotation in ARGUMENT_TREE branch 2 ("in amounts sufficient to meet required prize payouts on dates immediately prior to" each prize) is the lottery's rule, word for word.
  2. The lottery holds "extra bonds ... against reinvestment risk", a public precedent for Laura's extra Treasuries.
  3. The lottery meets its rule with zero-coupon Treasuries (STRIPS), and that is the disanalogy: with coupon Treasuries, certainty is bought by holding extra.
  4. The multistate jackpots (Powerball, Mega Millions) are funded the same way.
  5. Illinois 2015 shows a promise breaking for lack of authority to pay, not lack of money; Laura's plan has an analogue.
- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** a public body that must pay fixed, dated sums for decades from money it holds now (deferred-payment prizes). Public finance and lottery accounting solved it in the 1980s (Illinois's 1985 law, card Q2b), by buying Treasuries that mature before each payment and, in Illinois, by putting them in a trust fund with a standing legal authority to pay. Municipal defeasance (card Q31) is the sister field.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. The quote drops five words without an ellipsis, and the rule is a policy, not a law.**
  - The words, in the policy the Lottery Commission reaffirmed on 25 Sep 2025 (s.III, p.1): "The Lottery's investment portfolio shall be structured so that investments mature in amounts sufficient to meet required prize payouts on dates immediately prior, or as near prior as practicable, to prize payment dates." ARGUMENT_TREE's version cuts "or as near prior as practicable," and stops at "prior to".
  - The rule is the Commission's own. The statute, Government Code 8880.25.5 (added in 2009), only lets the Commission "Invest funds on deposit in the State Lottery Fund" and "Purchase and sell securities". Never "California law requires".
  - A redline went to the Commission on 19 Nov 2026 (item 8(a)): it reads "mature in into amounts" and "principal primary objectives". Every other change in that redline puts the old word first, so the new text most likely reads "mature into amounts" (Keel's reading; the struck-through formatting could not be seen). The words "on dates immediately prior, or as near prior as practicable, to prize payment dates" are the same in both versions, so quote those, and cite the September 2025 policy.
  - **Judge's note:** the cut words are the ones that describe Laura. Caplet's own trade note says the Feb-2037 bond is "the latest WInS bond available beforehand" (TNA l.5): that is "as near prior as practicable". Two of her ten end 231 and 320 days early (CANON B8).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE (as a match for the extra Treasuries, kill). The lottery's "reinvestment risk" is call risk, and its reserve is filed under credit risk.**
  - The policy defines the risk it means: "credit risk and reinvestment risk associated with a credit rating downgrade or exercised call" (s.VI, p.3). If a bond is "unexpectedly called prior to maturity", the proceeds are reinvested (s.V).
  - It ties the reserve bonds to its municipal bonds: "Municipal Securities with inherent credit and reinvestment risk. The Lottery shall employ strategies to mitigate such risk, including ... purchasing bonds to hold in reserve" (s.VI).
  - The audited statements file the reserve under credit risk: "Credit risk is further mitigated by a $2 million par value agency bond reserve. The bond reserve has a fair value of $1,996,480" (ACFR FY2024-25, p.51).
  - The size: about 0.27% of the portfolio's par value ($729,728,738) and 0.38% of its fair value ($522,572,026). Laura's extra Treasuries cost $35,213 against $302,287 for the ten in 1 Jan 2027 money, about 11.6%, roughly thirty times the lottery's share on a value basis (all Keel arithmetic).
  - So the extra bonds are real and the words "reinvestment risk" are the lottery's own, but they answer a different risk: a call or downgrade on a municipal or agency bond. Laura's answer the rate at which her interest and early maturities earn while they wait. Never pair the two as the same thing. The store rows that do should be corrected: Q16-020's use, Q31-010, Q28-014 and Q150-011 (SB-8).
- **Claim 3: KEEP, now verified at source.** "Prize investments consist of zero coupon U.S. Treasury bonds, zero coupon agency bonds, and municipal bonds" (ACFR FY2024-25, Note 2, p.49). The 2025 portfolio report gives 83.0% Treasuries, 6.4% agencies and 10.6% municipal bonds, and labels the Treasury bar "STRIP". A zero pays nothing until it matures, so a lottery has no interest to reinvest. Wharton bans STRIPS for Laura (Week 4 email), so her Treasuries pay interest years early. That interest makes up 3% of the 2033 payment and about 15% of the 2041 and 2042 payments (NUMBERS 8a), and the extra Treasuries make the ten safe even if it earns nothing. This replaces card Q31 row 10's "STRIPS reading is Keel's inference".
  - **Judge's note:** a fixed-income judge will know a lottery holds zeros. The aha is the reverse: the competition's ban on zeros is why Laura needs the extra Treasuries at all, and the lottery shows what the rule costs when zeros are allowed (about 0.4% kept spare, and that against credit risk).
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. The pooled games pass the money to one state, and that state buys the bonds and sets the payment dates around them.**
  - Mega Millions: the jackpot "shall be funded by the Selling Lotteries" and is paid "by the Party Lottery that sold the winning Mega Millions Ticket". The 29 later payments are made "annually to coincide with the month of the federal auction date at which the bonds were purchased", "within seven (7) days of the anniversary of the annual auction date" (M2G2 Rules, 2025, rule 30.1).
  - Powerball: 30 payments rising 5% a year since the 7 Oct 2015 draw (4% from 2005). The winner "has no recourse on the MUSL or any other Selling Lottery". California's 2026 memo says it buys "U.S. Treasury securities" for its own SuperLotto Plus, Mega Millions and Powerball annuities.
  - The reserves these games keep are for other things: Powerball's Prize Reserve Account is "used to guarantee the payment of valid, but unanticipated, Grand Prize claims that may result from a system error or other reason". None of the lottery reserves read is held against interest rates.
  - **Judge's note:** the lottery picks the payment date to fit the bond. Laura cannot: the case fixes her dates at each January, so she must find bonds that end before them, and two end months early.
- **Claim 5: KEEP, one level deeper. Illinois kept its Treasury-funded prizes in a separate pot with a standing authority to pay. Only the pot that needed a yearly budget vote stopped.**
  - The law since 1985 (20 ILCS 1605/27): deferred prizes' money is held "separate and apart from all public money or funds of this State in a special trust fund outside the State treasury", invested only in direct US obligations. "This Act shall constitute an irrevocable appropriation of all amounts necessary for that purpose, and the irrevocable and continuing authority for and direction to the Director and the State Treasurer to make the necessary payments out of such trust fund for that purpose."
  - Cash prizes come from an account refilled "from moneys appropriated for prizes from the State Lottery Fund", and "Moneys from this account may not be used to pay amounts to deferred prize winners" (20 ILCS 1605/20.1).
  - In October 2015 the Comptroller's office said: "Without a budget, we can't, by law, make any payments on an appropriated fund without a court order or consent decree or statutory continuing appropriation" (NBC Chicago, 14 Oct 2015).
  - The trust fund paid $37.0 million of installment prizes in the year the budget was frozen (FY2016 audit, cash flow statement). Its scheduled payments fell from $370,659,003 on 30 Jun 2015 to $349,685,003 on 31 Dec 2015. No source found says an installment winner waited. That the installments kept being paid on time is Keel's inference from the statute, the Comptroller's rule and these figures. No direct statement was found.
  - **Laura's analogue.** Nothing in her ten payments waits on anyone's yearly vote. Treasury interest has a standing appropriation (store Q58-035). Each T-bond pays itself, and each fund pays out when it ends. What is left is delay with the money present: a broker's failure (cards Q72, Q78), a fund suspension (Q36), her own January instruction, and a broker's hold under FINRA Rule 2165 of up to 55 business days. That hold applies only if she is 65 or older (the case gives no age) or impaired, and only if exploitation is suspected. The principal of a Treasury rests on borrowing authority, the debt limit (Q58-035, IPS l.17's second reason). None of these is a reason to change the plan. Table 3 can watch them.
  - **Judge's note:** in Illinois's 2015 freeze, the prizes held in Treasuries in a trust fund with a standing authority to pay seem to have been paid. The ones waiting on a budget vote waited five months. The lesson is Laura's two-pot design, not "states are unreliable".
- **Verdict for 3a: CHANGE the parallel's wording, keep its place.** Candidate wording for the writers (a note, to be rewritten in Caplet's voice): "California's lottery, which pays jackpot winners over thirty years, writes the same rule into its investment policy: its investments must 'mature in amounts sufficient to meet required prize payouts on dates immediately prior, or as near prior as practicable, to prize payment dates' (California State Lottery, 2025). It keeps the rule with zero-coupon Treasuries, which pay nothing until the day they mature. Those are not open to Laura, so her Treasuries pay interest years early, and the extra Treasuries keep her rule true even if that interest earns nothing."
  - **Never:** "immediately prior to" without "or as near prior as practicable" (a misquote); "California law" or "statute" for the rule (it is the Commission's policy); "extra bonds against reinvestment risk" as the lottery's precedent for the extra Treasuries (its $2 million reserve covers calls and downgrades and is filed under credit risk); "only Treasuries" for the lottery (6.4% agencies, 10.6% municipal bonds); "exactly" (the rule itself allows "as near prior as practicable"); the policy's "prudent" (banned for the range rule; do not import it); STRIPS as anything Laura could hold; "every lottery" (Minnesota buys insurers' annuities, store Q2b-011); the lottery's "fully funded" carried over to the facility; any line that makes Illinois a third reason to change the plan.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 6-8, the reserve is not a reinvestment-rate reserve (3a; ARGUMENT_TREE branch 2).** It stops a misstatement a fixed-income judge could catch with one click: the lottery's "reinvestment risk" is a call on a municipal bond, and its $2 million is filed under credit risk.
2. **Row 10, zeros against coupons (3a, "the price, in the next breath").** The parallel stops flattering and starts explaining: the lottery buys zeros, Laura may not, and the extra Treasuries are what that ban costs.
3. **Row 4, "as near prior as practicable" (3a).** The words the current quote drops are Caplet's own rule ("the latest WInS bond available beforehand"). Quoting them whole makes the parallel exact.
4. **Rows 22-24, Illinois's two pots (3a's "why the two pots are separate", or 3e).** The prizes held in Treasuries, in a trust fund with a standing authority to pay, kept moving. The prizes waiting on a budget vote waited five months. It is the public case for the separation the plan already has.
5. **Row 18, the lottery sets its dates by the bonds (3a or 2.2).** It shows in one clause why Laura's ladder has gaps and a lottery's does not: her dates are fixed by the case.
