## Verdicts in one screen

- **(a) CHANGE.** Simon's satisficing is a real and exact description of the structure, but "aspiration level" and "stopping" are his words for a search that goes on until one option clears a bar, so the report should not say the team searched and stopped. It can say what the choice has in common with the rule: two bars named in advance (IPS l.15's three uses, and a top a co-sponsor can count) and no claim to have found a maximum.
  - **What Simon means.** In 1955 he cuts the pay-off to two values ("anything over this amount as 'satisfactory,' anything less as 'unsatisfactory'"), says the boundary is "the 'aspiration level'", and that "the first satisfactory alternative that is evaluated as such" is the one chosen (pp. 104, 110). The aspiration level moves with search: as satisfactory options turn up easily "his aspiration level rises", as they turn up with difficulty it "falls" (p. 111). In the 1956 paper the organism has "neither the senses nor the wits to discover an 'optimal' path" and needs "a path that will permit satisfaction at some specified level of all of its needs" (p. 136), with several needs met by "a primitive choice mechanism" (p. 134). In 1978 he names it: "I called this mode of selection satisficing" (Nobel lecture p. 356).
  - **Where the fit is good.** IPS l.15 gives three needs (building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short) and a top that is "money you already own". Simon's 1956 organism meets several needs at set levels without a single common measure; the plan does the same without one utility number. Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 8o: at half, her flexibility is $21,179 and covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar 1.84 times; at 70% moved it covers it 1.06 times (barely), at 30% moved 2.82 times. So half clears a bar that 70% only just clears.
  - **Where it does not fit.** Simon's search has a sequence and a moving bar. The half was fixed in the IPS, not found after trying alternatives, and the team's record shows no stated numerical bar. Any numerical aspiration level (for example "covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar more than once") would be a reading added afterwards, so it must be labelled Keel's, never "the team's aspiration level".
  - **Judge's note:** a split "down the middle" and a satisficing split look the same on the page; what tells them apart is that half sits inside a stretch where the stated top does not change. In NUMBERS 8o the $115,000 top holds from 44.5% to 57.3% of the shares moved, and half is inside it, 5.5 points above its start. Moving a little more or less would not change what co-sponsors are told.
- **(b) CHANGE (narrow it, do not use it as proof for half).** DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal's result is about dividing money across N assets: of 14 models on seven datasets, "none is consistently better than the 1-N rule", because "the gain from optimal diversification is more than offset by estimation error", and about 3,000 months of data are needed for 25 assets, 6,000 for 50 (abstract, read via IDEAS). It does not say a promised-versus-unpromised split is best at one half.
  - **Where it breaks, exactly.** (1) 1/N is a rule for weights across assets whose means and covariances must be estimated; Laura's half divides one asset's value (VT) between a promise and no promise, and no asset weight in the plan is 50-50 (the plan's weights are set by the ten payments and the floor). (2) Its test is out-of-sample Sharpe ratio and certainty-equivalent return; the half is judged by what co-sponsors can count and what is kept for three named uses, a different score. (3) The paper compares naive with sample-based optimal rules on estimates from history; the team's own search over splits (NUMBERS 8o) is also an estimate from history and simulation, so the parallel to draw is "the estimate would be shaky", not "1/N wins". (4) The abstract gives no result for two assets or for known parameters; "simple beats tuned" is a result about estimation error, not a general law (the paper itself reports a long window can flip it; only the abstract was read, so no caveat beyond the 3,000 and 6,000 months is claimed).
  - **What does carry over.** Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 8o: what Laura has beyond the ten (contribution plus flexibility plus anything above the top) differs by a median of only $365 between splits in 200,000 simulated futures; the split decides what is promised and what stays hers, not how much there is. When the total barely moves, fine-tuning the split has little to win, which is the same shape as the 1/N finding, but it is shown by the team's own table, not by the paper.
- **(c) CHANGE.** Simon does not say stopping is always rational; he says it is the rational response when searching has a cost that cannot be priced. The sentence can stay if the reflection gives the reason.
  - **What he says.** Optimising with the cost of search "equated with its marginal return" would need the decision maker "to be able to estimate the marginal costs and returns of search" (Nobel lecture p. 356); satisficing "provide[s] good enough decisions with reasonable costs of computation" (p. 350). He also says "Neither approach, in general, dominates the other" (p. 350). His 1955 case is the house seller who sets an acceptance price (p. 105, appendix p. 115) because the next offer is not known.
  - **Fit to the team.** The team could not price more search about the split: Taiwan building costs, the Taiwan dollar and co-sponsors' budgets are unknown dates in the future (NUMBERS 8o is a model, not a measurement). The reflection may say "we stopped because more modelling would have bought little", and the evidence that it would have bought little is 8o's: the total moves by about $365.
  - **Judge's note:** "good enough" in Simon's sense is a bar fixed beforehand, not tiredness after a search. The honest line is that the bars were named in IPS l.15 before the figures were run.
- **Never:** "optimal" (Kay 2020 is the pedigree for why); "the team's aspiration level" without saying it is Keel's reading; "1/N shows half is best"; "Nobel-winning proof that half is right" (his lecture says neither approach dominates).
