## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Hosts must sign promises before the award, and the promises are open-ended. Laura's figure is owned before she speaks, and it is closed.**
  - **What is required.** The national government must give "a legally binding instrument" that the country will respect the Charter (Rule 33.3, Charter in force 24 June 2026). The IOC's Executive Board sets "the guarantees and other commitments to be provided in support of a candidature" for each edition (Bye-law 1.4 to Rule 33). For 2030 the list ran to 23 guarantees. G 4.1 is the money one: a "Games delivery guarantee, in the form of financial guarantees, covering a potential economic shortfall of the OCOG", issued by "The government - national, regional, local or others". G 1.2 says the Olympic Village "must be underwritten by a guarantor to ensure that a shortfall in the construction budget would not impact its availability".
  - **"Before the award" is softer than it sounds.** Guarantees not in hand at the election may be obtained "within eighteen (18) months after the formation of the OCOG" and then count as pre-election commitments (Olympic Host Contract 2032, §27.2). For 2026 both bidders missed the 11 January 2019 deadline for government financial guarantees; the IOC gave a second date, 12 April, and said it would not be an elimination stage (Francs Jeux, 22 Mar 2019). When bidders are few, the deadline bends.
  - **A guarantee is not ownership.** The host promises to pay from taxes not yet raised, for a sum not yet known. Laura states a fixed figure that Treasuries she already holds will pay: the bottom held since January 2028, the rest since the shares moved in January 2031 (SKELETON 3d; NUMBERS 4: $95,000 to $115,000 centrally).
  - **Timing runs the other way.** Bids are priced "up to seven years before the Games" (Flyvbjerg and Stewart, 2012); Brisbane signed in July 2021 for 2032. Laura speaks in 2031, two years before 2033, after the move. Stating late is what lets her figure be owned rather than promised.
  - **Judge's note:** in the Olympic contract the one party that never pays an overrun is the one that states a fixed figure. The Charter says the IOC "shall have no financial responsibility ... other than the contribution determined in the Olympic Host Contract" (Rule 36.2). Laura's figure has the IOC's shape, not the host's. Even the IOC's figure is softer than hers: its USD 898 million broadcast contribution to Brisbane can be reduced "in case the IOC does not receive the revenues" (OHC 2032, §9(d)(ii)).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. True of the hosts; the IPS citation is wrong for the range.**
  - **Who stood behind the guarantees.** Governments, so in the end taxpayers: Montreal's debt was paid through a provincial tobacco tax until 2006 (IOC, 2024; CBC, 2006). Hannover's Expo 2000 deficit, up to DM 2.4 billion in the worst case, went two-thirds to the federal budget and one-third to Lower Saxony (Bundestag 14/6786). Rio's state declared a "state of public calamity" 49 days before the Games, and the federal government sent R$2.9 billion it did not ask back (Decree 45.692; Agência Brasil). Voters and governments said no to that risk in Denver (1972), Los Angeles (1978), Oslo (2014) and Boston (2015). France pulled its Expo 2025 bid because the plan did "not offer the necessary guarantees to rule out any financial risk for the French taxpayer" (BIE, 2018).
  - **The citation.** IPS l.11's "or on anybody else's money" covers the ten operating payments. For the facility range the right line is l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own". Say both, each for its own promise.
  - **"Someone else's money" needs care.** A host government is spending its own future tax revenue. Its own revenue can fall in the same downturn when the bill arrives, and that is what Rio's decree shows (row 31). The closer match to IPS l.9 is a promise resting on income, not on money held.
  - **Judge's note:** Rio's guarantee broke for the reason IPS l.9 gives for Laura's career. The state's own income (ICMS sales tax and oil royalties) fell in the same downturn as the bills came due.
- **Claim 3: KEEP the first half, with numbers and the counter-evidence; CHANGE the link to Laura.**
  - **Size.** Every Games 1960-2016 with data overran: 19 of 30. The mean is 172% in real terms (median 118%), "the highest overrun on record for any type of megaproject" (Flyvbjerg, Budzier and Lunn, 2021). Their Table 4 gives the comparison: roads 20%, bridges and tunnels 34%, energy 36%, rail 45%, dams 90%, IT 107%, Olympics 172%. Olympic overruns were 10 of 10 against 5-9 of 10 for the rest, and schedule overrun was 0%.
  - **Tail.** Costs fit a power law (alpha 1.2-1.7: finite mean, infinite variance). The authors put the "true mean" overrun at 172%-746%.
  - **Counter-evidence.** A 2022 re-analysis with more government data "could not replicate" the 2000-2016 results. It finds 13-178% for direct non-OCOG investments 2000-2014, and Games costs under 0.28% of GDP. So never say "the Olympics caused Greece's crisis" (Preuss et al., 2022; IOBE, 2015: Athens's state funding was about 1% of state spending 2000-2010).
  - **The link.** IPS l.15 lists "building costs that overrun" first of three uses, in the IPS's order. The order is not a ranking, and no ranking of our own is added (SKELETON 3b). The Games are never a yardstick for her building. Average sports-related cost is $12.0 billion; they are public, and they open on a date that cannot move. The case also says teams "are not expected to estimate that cost" (case l.164). The small-building class is Q5d's.
  - **What the Games do teach Laura** is the shape of the promise. Whoever signs an open-ended guarantee pays the overrun; whoever states a fixed figure does not. Her contribution "does not rise with overruns" (SKELETON 3d), and her flexibility may meet them because it is promised to no one (IPS l.15).
  - **Judge's note:** the Games overrun with "100 per cent consistency" and 0% schedule overrun for one reason. "The opening date cannot be moved", so "there can be no trade-off between schedule and cost" (Flyvbjerg et al., 2021). Laura's dates cannot move either. That is why the money for each one is fixed and held before the date, not found after it.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 5 (with 6 and 43), 3d or 3c.** In the Olympic contract the party that states a fixed figure, the IOC, never pays an overrun, and the host that signs an open guarantee pays them all. Laura's figure has the IOC's shape, and it is firmer: the IOC's can be cut if its TV money does not arrive, while hers is already held.
2. **Row 17 (with 15), 3c and 3b.** The Games show 0% schedule overrun and 10 of 10 cost overruns, because "the opening date cannot be moved". A fixed date turns every problem into money, so a plan with fixed dates must fix the money before the dates. That is the ladder's logic, told from the other side.
3. **Row 31, 3e or 2.3.** Rio's guarantee broke when the state's own income (sales tax and oil royalties) fell in the same downturn as the bills came due. This is IPS l.9's argument about a writer's income, played out on a government's books.
4. **Row 42, 3d.** Osaka's three partners each promised a third of the cost, and each bill rose 1.9 times. A promise stated as a share of the cost grows with the cost; Laura's dollar figure does not, which is why her contribution does not rise with overruns.
5. **Row 24 (with 22-23), 3d.** Montreal's own official report called the Games self-financing "if the installations are excluded". Laura's words state her own figure and where the facility's total will be set, never that the facility is paid for.
