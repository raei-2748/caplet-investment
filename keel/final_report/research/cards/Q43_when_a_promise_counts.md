**Claims tested.** Laura's rule: she states a figure to co-sponsors only once Treasuries she already owns will pay it,
barring a US default; each end is counted at what the bonds pay with no further interest, less 0.5%, rounded down to
$5,000; anything the funds pay above the stated top goes to unpromised money ("pencil and ink"; "ink only goes one

1. The rule is public budgeting's "full funding" principle.
2. The rule is accounting prudence or conservatism.
3. Some primary source says a building project or a commitment should start only with the money for it in hand.

**Problem class and the fields that solved it.** When may a promise be made, and when may an amount be counted:
commitment timing (when a promise is made) and recognition (when an amount counts). Three fields own it.
- Public budgeting law. In the US: the Antideficiency Act, 31 U.S.C. 1341, as read by GAO's Red Book, and OMB's full
  funding policy (Circular A-11 Appendix J, the Capital Programming Guide), with GAO and CBO reports on incremental
  funding. In Taiwan: the Budget Act (預算法).
- Accounting standard-setting and its economics. The IASB Conceptual Framework (2018) and FASB Concepts Statement 8 on
  prudence and neutrality; Basu (1997) and Watts (2003) on why conservatism exists.
- Builders' own capital-gift rules: university capital policies, Arts Council England, and the Charity Commission for
  England and Wales on appeals.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Full funding: CHANGE.** Laura's rule is narrower and closer to the Antideficiency Act than to full funding.
   - Full funding is about paying for a whole asset before starting it. GAO-01-432R: "full funding ensures that the
     full estimated costs of decisions are recognized at the time that the commitment is made".
   - The Act is about the act of promising itself: "The very act of obligating the United States to make a payment
     when the necessary funds are not already in the account is also a violation of 31 U.S.C. § 1341(a)" (GAO, 2006,
     ch. 6). That is Laura's "no figure stated before the move" (SKELETON 3c).
   - Full funding fits two parts of the plan:
     - The ten payments: all ten bought as one complete set. From Jan 2027 to Jan 2028 they leaned on the 2028
       deposit (IPS l.13), which OMB would count as full funding through an "advance appropriation" and GAO says
       "could be viewed as a form of incremental funding". They are inked in Jan 2028.
     - The bottom: OMB's "useful segment" must be "programmatically useful if no further investments are funded".
       That is the deletion test: VT at zero leaves a one-figure range of $95,000.
   - It breaks in four places:
     - Budget authority is permission to spend, not money held; the US can borrow, Laura cannot.
     - Full funding prices the whole building, and Laura pays only her share.
     - Her rule is self-imposed policy, like OMB's (not statute).
     - Taiwan's public co-sponsors fund continuing works one year at a time. Budget Act Art. 39: total, period and a
       yearly quota; "The budget for each year shall be prepared according to the quota of each fiscal year".
2. **Accounting prudence or conservatism: CHANGE.**
   - Fair as contracting conservatism, which Watts (2003) defines as "the asymmetrical verification requirements for
     gains and losses". His reason fits co-sponsors closely: lenders whose claim is capped "are concerned with the
     lower ends" and "obtain verifiable lower bound measures". A co-sponsor's claim on Laura is capped too:
     min(what the funds pay, the stated top).
   - Unfair as standard-setters' "prudence". IASB CF 2.17: "The exercise of prudence does not imply a need for
     asymmetry". FASB CS8 BC3.27 leaves conservatism out "because including either would be inconsistent with
     neutrality".
   - How to say it: one-sided on purpose, the way lenders write it into loan contracts. Never "prudent accounting" and
     never "a conservative estimate". The standard-setters' main objection (an understatement now inflates later
     results) does not bite, because the cap sends any excess to her flexibility, in the open.
3. **Money in hand before building: KEEP, worded narrowly.** Builders' own rules say it, read at source:
   - UNC Chapel Hill: 100% in cash before award (already in store).
   - Cornell (Nov 2025): gifts "75% in hand with written, binding commitments for the full 100%", plus a backstop for
     anything not in hand.
   - Cal Poly: pledges equal to 105% signed before construction.
   - Idaho: securities count "upon receipt" and are sold.
   - UW System: "If at any time, funds are not available in the account, then no new contracts nor contract amendments
     will be executed".
   - Arts Council England: 100% of partnership funding before start (already in store).
   - Taiwan's Budget Act asks a "major public construction" project for "an explanation of the financing" before it
     is budgeted (Art. 34).
   - No charity regulator found says "start only with money in hand". The Charity Commission asks instead that an
     appeal state in advance a "secondary purpose" for surplus or shortfall money. That is exactly Laura's
     excess-to-flexibility rule.
   - Laura's standard is stricter than any builder's. They count binding pledges plus a backstop; she counts only
     owned Treasuries, because she offers co-sponsors no backstop (CANON A30).

**Strongest parallel (exact):** "the law is violated not just when there are insufficient funds in an account when a
payment becomes due. The very act of obligating the United States to make a payment when the necessary funds are not
already in the account is also a violation of 31 U.S.C. § 1341(a)." (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2006,
*Principles of Federal Appropriations Law*, 3rd ed., Vol. II, ch. 6, GAO-06-382SP; the 1992 2nd edition has the same
sentence with "not only if" on p. 6-13.)

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. The promise itself is the breach (GAO's Red Book on 31 U.S.C. 1341; rows 9-13).**
- What is it? US federal law forbids an agency to promise money beyond what an appropriation holds, or before one is
  made, and GAO reads the offence as the promise, not only the missed payment. Promising against "anticipated
  receipts" is a breach (Customs, the Air Force). A promise made "subject to appropriation" still carries "a moral
  obligation". An open-ended promise is allowed only if "limited to a definite amount known at the time".
- Did it work? GAO's decisions still apply it (B-300480, 2003). GAO names its aim as stopping "coercive
  deficiencies": promises that force Congress to find money later.
- How is Laura's case like it? The rule "no figure stated before the move" is this test applied to a private promise.
  VT before the move and the 2028 deposit before it lands are anticipated receipts. The cap makes the promise a
  definite amount. The 0.5% cut answers "upward adjustments" (fund costs).
- How is it not? "Funds in the account" means legal permission to spend, which a borrowing government can renew;
  Laura's ink is money owned, open only to a US default and the funds' costs. No law binds Laura, so her rule binds by
  her own policy and by showing the holdings.
- What Caplet takes: for 3c, if one evidence clause is wanted, the plain version: US law treats the promise made
  before the money is there as the breach, not only the payment missed (GAO, 2006). For 3b, the "moral obligation"
  line is the public-law reason ink only goes one way: a figure once stated binds even when it is conditional.

**Card 2. Full funding and the useful segment (OMB A-11 Appendix J; GAO-01-432R; rows 1-4).**
- What is it? OMB policy: before any obligation, budget authority must cover a whole "useful segment", a part that is
  "programmatically useful if no further investments are funded". Advance appropriations count for OMB; GAO says they
  "could be viewed as a form of incremental funding".
- Did it work? GAO's evidence is from the failures: incremental projects cost more and finished later (DOE: $236
  million to $313 million, 3 years late; row 6). The policy is not statute (row 15) and gives way for defence
  procurement and high technology.
- How is Laura's case like it? Two complete segments are bought before anything rests on them. The ten payments are
  inked in Jan 2028. The bottom is inked in Jan 2028 and passes the deletion test: with VT at zero, the range is one
  figure, $95,000. The year from Jan 2027 to Jan 2028, when the ladder leaned on the 2028 deposit, is the "advance
  appropriation" case.
- How is it not? Full funding prices a whole asset. Laura funds only her share of a building whose total the case
  does not ask the team to set, so her rule cannot make the facility fully funded. Taiwan's public funders budget
  continuing works one year at a time (row 22).
- What Caplet takes: never "full funding" for the facility or for Laura's rule as a whole. If the term is used at
  all, "the ten payments were bought as one complete set" carries the idea without the jargon.

**Card 3. Lenders want a verifiable lower bound (Watts, 2003; Basu, 1997; rows 25-31).**
- What is it? Conservatism is "the asymmetrical verification requirements for gains and losses". It exists because
  lenders' payoff is capped: they get nothing extra in good outcomes, so they "are concerned with the lower ends" and
  use "verifiable lower bound measures". Parties free to choose write it into contracts (every GAAP departure in
  private debt covenants is conservative; Leftwich, 1983, via Basu).
- Did it work? It has lasted, in Watts's words, "for so long", against regulators. Basu measures it at two to six
  times more response to bad news than good. Watts predicts the FASB's attempt to ban it "for neutrality" will fail.
- How is Laura's case like it? Co-sponsors hold a capped claim: min(what the funds pay, the stated top). Above the
  top they get nothing, so the bottom and its proof are what they need (3d's "the figure to count"). Ink is a
  verification test: anyone can check a figure against owned Treasuries. The extreme form (count nothing until 2033)
  is rejected for Watts's reason, the cost of not being timely: that is why the top is stated in 2031, once owned.
- How is it not? Accounting conservatism measures what a firm has; Laura's rule decides what she promises. No court
  enforces her figure.
- What Caplet takes: if a parallel is drawn, "one-sided on purpose, the way lenders want accounts kept", with the
  reason in one line: whoever is promised a capped amount cares only that the bottom is there.

**Card 4. What standard-setters reject, and what they still support (IASB CF 2018; FASB CS8; rows 32-36).**
- What is it? Both boards keep caution but reject systematic one-sidedness in general-purpose reports. IASB 2.17:
  "does not imply a need for asymmetry". FASB BC3.27 drops conservatism as "inconsistent with neutrality", because an
  understatement now "leads to overstating financial performance in later periods".
- Did it work? The rule is in force (IASB 2018; FASB 2010, amended). Watts and Basu show practice stays one-sided
  anyway, and the IASB concedes "not all asymmetry is inconsistent with neutrality".
- How is Laura's case like it? The reversal objection does not reach her: what the rounding holds back never returns
  to the co-sponsors' figure, and its home (her flexibility) is stated. FASB's QC26 also backs the form of her
  statement: "A range of possible amounts ... also can be verified".
- How is it not? Neutrality is a duty of people who report to investors. Laura is the person making the promise, and
  her duty is to keep it.
- What Caplet takes: never "prudent" or "conservative accounting" for the range rule; a judge who knows IFRS will
  read it as a mistake. Never "a conservative estimate" for either end: the ends are owned holdings, counted with a
  stated cut, not guesses shaded down. Keep "prudent" for SKELETON 2.3's trust-law meaning (UPIA) only.

**Card 5. What building on promised money costs (GAO-01-432R DOE; GAO/AIMD-99-32; Larsen et al.; rows 6-7, 20-21).**
- What is it? Projects that received 60%, 40% and 43% of planned early funding ran late and over budget. One went
  from $236 million to $313 million (+33%) and took 3 years longer, because "certain contractor expenses and
  administrative costs continued to accrue each month".
- Did it work? This is GAO's evidence for full funding. Its 2019 inland-waterways review says full up-front funding
  "would likely reduce the overall costs" over the long term (GAO-19-20, excerpt).
- How is Laura's case like it? The facility will be paid for by several funders. One whose money is owned is not the
  late piece.
- How is it not? These are federal science and works projects, many times the size of a residency.
- What Caplet takes: for 3d's ask (capital pledged conditionally on the facility's total being reached by one close
  date, Q11c), a reason in plain words: buildings funded in pieces run late and cost more. The figure stays in the
  store unless 3d needs it.

**Card 6. Shares count for a building once sold (University of Idaho APM 40.06; Cornell; rows 37-40).**
- What is it? A university building policy counts gifts of securities "upon receipt" and sells them at once. A unit
  may keep holding them only if it "is willing to accept the financial risk or reward". Illiquid gifts count only
  "upon conversion to cash". Cornell will not let a project "parse" itself into pieces unless each piece "can exist
  indefinitely without future phase execution".
- Did it work? These are standing policies (Idaho last updated 2008, Cornell revised 2025). No outcome data was
  found.
- How is Laura's case like it? The 2031 move is the same rule: half of VT becomes Treasuries before any figure rests
  on it, and the half still in shares carries its own risk as money promised to no one (IPS l.15).
- How is it not? The university is the builder counting its own donors; Laura is a donor counting her own promise.
  Idaho's 90% is a fee, not a safety cut.
- What Caplet takes: store only. It is a strong answer if a judge asks "why sell shares before the meetings?":
  universities count donated shares for a building only once they are sold.

**Card 7. Say in advance where surplus money goes (Charity Commission; rows 43-44).**
- What is it? An appeal may carry a "secondary purpose" saying how surplus (or short) money will be used. If it
  does, trustees must follow it and need not ask donors or the regulator. If it does not, a failed appeal's starting
  point is that donors get their money back.
- Did it work? It is the regulator's current guidance (updated 3 Feb 2026), built on the Charities Act 2011.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her rule names the home of any surplus before the meetings: anything above the top
  goes to her flexibility (SKELETON 3c; WORDS). Co-sponsors are never left wondering whether a good year raises the
  gift.
- How is it not? It covers charities raising from the public; Laura is one donor and the money stays hers until paid.
- What Caplet takes: support for keeping the excess rule explicit in 3c. The regulator found nothing like "start
  only with money in hand", so claim 3 must name builders and arts funders, not "charity regulators".

**Card 8. Taiwan's own budget law (Budget Act Art. 5, 7-9, 25, 34, 39, 76; rows 22-24).**
- What is it? Continuing works are budgeted as a stated total with a yearly quota, voted each year. Fixed-amount
  commitments may run at most four years ahead and must be listed. A major public construction project needs a
  financing explanation before it is budgeted.
- Did it work? It is Taiwan's governing budget statute (amended 2021).
- How is Laura's case like it? Public co-sponsors in Taiwan think in totals voted year by year. A private figure owned
  in full five years ahead is outside what their own law lets them do.
- How is it not? The Act governs government budgets; the Ministry of Culture's residency grants follow their own
  directions (cards Q9c, Q10d).
- What Caplet takes: legal backing for SKELETON 3d's half-clause on public money paid one year at a time. Nuance:
  Taiwan law does allow fixed multi-year commitments (up to four years), so never write that its public funders
  cannot commit beyond a year.

**Card 9. The honest cost of certainty (the SST termination reserve, B-159141; GAO and CBO on "bias against capital";
rows 14, 16).**
- What is it? To obey the Act, the FAA set aside money each year against a remote risk of contract termination.
  Appropriators cut it as "unnecessarily large amounts" tied up for "the highly remote possibility". Up-front funding
  can make capital "seem prohibitively expensive".
- Did it work? Appropriators cut the reserve as money tied up for a remote risk. GAO still holds up full funding as
  best practice.
- How is Laura's case like it? Certainty costs money. Each $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,300 of what she
  keeps (NUMBERS 8o), and the extra Treasuries ($35,894) sit against a fall in yields.
- How is it not? Laura's reserve mostly comes back (about $45,500 in 2033 money if rates hold); the SST's would not
  have.
- What Caplet takes: for 3e, if a limit is named, the cost of certainty, stated with the money that comes back.

## (d) Verdict

**1. Full funding: CHANGE.**
- Keep: the ten payments as one complete set, inked Jan 2028; the bottom as a useful segment (deletion test); the
  cost of building on late money (GAO's DOE example) as store evidence for 3d's ask.
- Kill: "Laura's rule is full funding". Full funding is OMB policy for whole assets. Laura funds only her share, so
  her rule cannot fully fund the building, and Taiwan's public funders budget year by year (Art. 39).
- Change to (a note, not prose): "Laura's rule is the one US law sets for agencies: no promise before the money is in
  the account (GAO, 2006). She keeps it by her own policy, with owned Treasuries in place of an appropriation."

**2. Prudence or conservatism: CHANGE.**
- Keep: contracting conservatism (Watts, 2003): asymmetric verification, chosen by people with capped claims, who
  care about a verifiable lower bound.
- Kill: "accounting prudence". IASB 2.17 and FASB BC3.27 reject systematic asymmetry by name.
- Change to (a note): "One-sided on purpose, the way lenders want accounts kept: whoever is promised a capped amount
  needs proof of the bottom. What the rounding holds back never comes back into the co-sponsors' figure; it goes to
  her flexibility, in the open."

**3. Money in hand before building: KEEP, worded narrowly.**
- Builders' rules say it (UNC, Cornell, Cal Poly, Idaho, UW System; Arts Council England in store). Taiwan's Budget
  Act asks only that major public works explain their financing before they are budgeted (Art. 34), not that the
  money be in hand.
- No charity regulator says it; the Charity Commission asks for a stated secondary purpose.
- Every builder accepts binding pledges and a backstop; Laura counts only owned Treasuries.

**For the lead to decide:**
1. Whether 3c carries the Red Book clause as its one evidence clause, or keeps its current numbers-only form. Keel
   leans to numbers-only in 3c and the Red Book line in the store as a defence card.
2. Whether WORDS gains three bans: "full funding/fully funded" for the facility; "prudent/prudence/conservative
   accounting" for the range rule; "conservative estimate" for either end.
3. Whether 3e's limits name the cost of certainty with the SST example or without it (Keel: without; the numbers
   carry it).
