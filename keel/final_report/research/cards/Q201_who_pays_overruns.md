## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Taiwan's Ministry of Culture shares a building's cost with its owner only on the way down

- (a) **The parallel.**
  - When Taiwan's Ministry of Culture pays part of a building, its rules pay the last instalment as its own share of the cost actually settled. The total paid can never exceed the sum it approved.
  - A grantee that runs short "應自行籌措財源配合，不得要求追加補助數額" (must raise the money itself and may not ask for a larger grant).
  - A design change must be paid for "在原計畫預算下" (inside the original budget), and anything beyond that is the grantee's to raise.
  - So when a building comes in under budget, the grant shrinks in proportion. When it comes in over budget, the grant stays where it was.
- (b) **What it shows about Laura's plan.**
  - The judges are right that funders do not share an overrun in proportion to their gifts. The plan should stop pricing a building-cost rise as if they did.
  - Their remedy, "met by co-sponsors", needs one change. A co-sponsor who promised a fixed sum stands where the Ministry stands and does not top up either.
  - A larger overrun is the owner's to meet. The owner has four tools: the contingency inside the budget, a redesign that fits the budget, a later or smaller first phase, and new gifts.
  - Laura's stated figure works like the Ministry's approved sum: a ceiling no overrun can raise.
- (c) **Where it breaks.**
  - Laura is not the Ministry. She founds the residency, and in a small residency a shortfall reaches the founder first.
  - So the honest job for her flexibility is narrow: her own share of an overrun on the part she funds, about 25% if shares earn the 7.5% assumed, carried first so that she can ask others to carry theirs. It is never the building's whole overrun.
  - In Set in Stone's theatres, it was "large and characteristically committed boards" whose gifts after the project covered the gap. In this residency, Laura is that board.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15's first use, "building costs that overrun", and the "about 25%" in 3b and 3e (NUMBERS 8u row 4).
- **Report part:** 3b, in one sentence: "a rise of about 25% in the cost of the part she funds". 3e gets one clause on who meets a larger overrun.
- **Source:** 文化部 (2020), 文化部推動藝文專業場館升級計畫補助作業要點, points 8 and 9(1). 文化部 (2021), 私有老建築保存再生計畫補助作業要點, points 9 and 10(2). Woronkowicz et al. (2012), p. 20. Rows Q201-001 to -005, -011 and -025.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Read at source, the Ministry's rules hold a clause most readers have never met: the grant follows the cost down and never up.
  - This answers the "pro rata" charge exactly. Up to the budget, contingency included, every fixed gift pays in proportion. Above the budget, no one's gift grows.

### Idea 2. The same rule on both sides of the table: Laura buys first and states second, and the campaign raises first and builds second

- (a) **The parallel.**
  - The University of North Carolina will not award a gift-funded building until it holds "100% of the budgeted amount in cash". It makes an exception at 85% cash, with the rest due during construction.
  - It collects early "so that interest earned on the cash may partially shield against the inflationary costs of construction".
  - Villanova wants 50% in cash and 75% pledged before work starts. Arizona expects "full cash funding of the project account". Washington pays its arts grant only after "all other money needed to complete the project has been raised".
  - Lehman paid Spelman $3m of a $10m pledge. Spelman's head of development said: "We don't spend money unless we have it."
- (b) **What it shows about Laura's plan.**
  - These policies are Laura's own rule, owned before it is stated, written for the building's side. They are the honest answer to a lost co-sponsor.
  - The residency signs no building contract until the money is held. A funder who falls away then means a later start or a smaller first phase, not a hole that her flexibility must fill.
  - UNC's reason for collecting early is the plan's reason for buying the late-2032 fund in 2028. Interest earned while the money waits is the first defence against rising building costs.
- (c) **Where it breaks.**
  - A university can lend itself the gap. UNC charges its bridge loans interest "at the University's blended rate", and Arizona's Treasury offers "bridge funding".
  - A new residency has no balance sheet to lend from, so its rule has to be at least as strict.
  - A rule of order trades a hole for a delay. That makes the next question where her payout waits while the building is late (idea 4).
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15's third use ("a co-sponsor falling short"). Also INTENT's "buys first and states second", applied to the building.
- **Report part:** 3e (the outside-funding assumption), in one sentence. 3d may carry the order: "my figure is held before I ask; the building is contracted once yours is".
- **Source:** UNC (2021). Villanova University (2014). University of Arizona (2026). Washington State Department of Commerce (2022). Masterson (2010). Rows Q201-013 to -018.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha as a synthesis.
  - Cash-first policies are routine to a development officer. A finance judge will not expect the founder's portfolio rule and the campaign's construction rule to be the same rule, given for the same reason (interest against building inflation).

### Idea 3. A campaign leaks a little all the time and loses a funder rarely, so her flexibility is sized for the leak and for lateness, never for a lost funder

- (a) **The parallel.**
  - Many colleges expect "between 2 percent and 5 percent of all pledges" never to arrive, and Kresge saw 0.62% of its challenge pledges withdrawn.
  - When one funder does fail, it fails in a lump. Spelman lost 70% of Lehman's pledge.
  - In Taiwan, one Ministry artist-village grant runs from NT$0.7m to NT$1.8m ($21,362-$54,932, NUMBERS 8d). The average, about $37,450, is 1.8 times her flexibility.
- (b) **What it shows about Laura's plan.**
  - Her flexibility is about $21,200, and about $9,600 in a bad 1 in 20. It can close a campaign's ordinary leak:
    - about $420,000 of pledges at 5% unpaid;
    - about $1.06m at 2% (Keel arithmetic).
  - It can also bridge a pledge that arrives late. Lateness is the most common way funders fall short: in 2008 three in four of Kresge's capital grantees asked for more time.
  - Bridge money comes back when the pledge is paid. Money used to replace a lost funder is gone.
- (c) **Where it breaks.**
  - The case gives no campaign size, so the report can say what kind of shortfall the flexibility covers but not the dollar size of a campaign it covers.
  - Telling co-sponsors that she holds money for "a co-sponsor falling short" invites them to give less (store Q22-038). The bridge belongs in 3e and Table 3, never in Laura's own sentences.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15's third use, read as a co-sponsor paying a little less or later. NUMBERS 8o lists the co-sponsor use as "not sized".
- **Report part:** 3e, in one sentence. 3b gets one clause in the list of uses.
- **Source:** Masterson (2010). Store Q25-020, Q51-004 and Q139-005. NUMBERS 8d and 8o. Rows Q201-019 to -021.
- **Pedigree or aha:** half and half.
  - That pledges leak is known to every fundraiser.
  - The split into a leak she can close, a lateness she can bridge and a loss she cannot replace is the distinction the judges asked for. It is said in kind, with one scale figure.

### Idea 4. In dollars her figure can wait; in buildings it cannot

- (a) **The parallel.**
  - Taiwan's ten public arts buildings opened 3 to 9 years late (card Q145).
  - If the residency's building is late, the late-2032 fund's payout (on or about 15 December 2032) waits in the short-term Treasury ETF, which holds Treasury bills. Every waiting dollar in the plan already sits there (NUMBERS section 0).
  - Keel arithmetic on every month since January 1991 compares US dollars held in three-month bills, less a 0.15% fee, with what those dollars would build in Taiwan:

    | Wait | What half of all waits lost | What the worst 1 in 20 lost |
    |---|---|---|
    | 3 years | 1.5% (about $1,700 of $115,000) | about 21% (about $23,800) |
    | 7 years | 6.2% | 26% |

- (b) **What it shows about Laura's plan.**
  - A delay changes no figure she states and moves no payment. It changes what the dollars build.
  - In 9.2% of 3-year waits, and in about a quarter of 7-year waits, the loss was larger than her flexibility (18.4% of the top).
  - Most of that spread came from two causes: the Taiwan dollar, and the years when bills paid almost nothing.
  - At the 4.19% three-month bill rate of 6 Nov 2026, waiting gains about 1% a year after the fee, measured against the register's central 3.0% rise in building costs at a fixed exchange rate.
- (c) **Where it breaks.**
  - The residency's other funders lose to the same building costs, because their grants in Taiwan dollars are fixed too. They escape only the currency.
  - Taiwan's Ministry pays 30% at 30% progress, 40% at 70% and 30% at completion. Washington pays only as reimbursement. So her payout should be drawn the same way, against the building's milestones.
  - If she paid it early into an unbuilt project's account, the delay's risk would move from Treasury bills to the residency's bank balance.
- **Decision it serves:** where the facility money sits from December 2032 until the building draws it. This is the judge's clause for attack 24.
- **Report part:** 3e (a limit with one figure). 3d gets "drawn against the building's milestones". Table 3 gets a building-start watch.
- **Source:**
  - Card Q81's bill-rate file (FRED TB3MS) and card Q131's file of DGBAS's construction cost index with the Taiwan dollar, both read at source by those cards. The method is under row Q201-022.
  - 文化部博物館事業推展補助作業要點 (point 9). Washington State Department of Commerce (2022).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. "Her figure can wait" is true in dollars and only partly true in what the dollars build. The table puts a number on the gap for the first time, and shows that the currency causes most of the tail.

### Idea 5. Every funder names its sum in its own money, so Laura's figure is in US dollars and her flexibility keeps its value in Taiwan dollars only by her choice

- (a) **The parallel.**
  - Every capital-grant rule read today states its sum in its own currency: the Ministry in Taiwan dollars, Washington in US dollars, Arts Council England in pounds. None moves with an exchange rate.
  - Cost engineers keep currency outside a building's contingency, as a finance risk handled separately (AACE, store Q5-007 and Q10e-033).
- (b) **What it shows about Laura's plan.**
  - Her stated figure is in US dollars, because her Treasuries pay US dollars. A stronger Taiwan dollar raises the building's cost to the residency, not her obligation.
  - IPS l.15 still lists "a stronger Taiwan dollar" among the flexibility's uses, because she means to keep her own gift's value in Taiwan dollars. She does this by choice.
  - Her flexibility does that in an ordinary move. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger takes $11,500. Her flexibility alone covers that in 93 of 100 model futures, and with anything above the top in 99.74 of 100.
  - In the worst 1 in 20 two-year spans since 1991, building costs and the Taiwan dollar together rose by more than 28.0%. That rise needs $32,191, which uses up all she keeps ($28,682) and leaves $3,509 for the residency's other sources (NUMBERS 8j, 8q, 8u).
- (c) **Where it breaks.**
  - She could instead promise a figure in Taiwan dollars. The currency risk would then be hers, and the flexibility would be the cushion it needs.
  - The plan chose a US-dollar figure plus a voluntary cushion. The report should say that once, in 3d.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15's second use, and the currency of the 2031 statement.
- **Report part:** 3d (one clause naming US dollars). 3e (the ordinary case and the 1-in-20 case in one place).
- **Source:** 文化部 (2020, 2021). Washington State Department of Commerce (2022). Arts Council England (n.d.). NUMBERS 8j, 8q and 8u. Rows Q201-026 and -027.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree. Any finance judge knows a promise has a currency. Its job here is to close finance professor 10 and plain reader 15, not to surprise.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 1, pro rata down, capped up (Q201-003, -005, -025).** Taiwan's own grant rule follows a building's cost down and never up. So "overruns are shared pro rata" is true only for the contingency inside a budget.
   - **Campaign director 5 stands, and the claim changes.** The report says "a rise of about 25% in the cost of the part she funds, if shares earn the 7.5% assumed". In dollars that is about $21,200 of shares (about $9,600 in a bad 1 in 20), and with anything above the top about $28,700. A larger overrun is the residency's, met by contingency, redesign within budget, phasing and new gifts.
   - The judge's "met by co-sponsors" needs "new gifts", because fixed gifts do not grow, hers or theirs.
   - **Finance professor 10 stands, and the claim changes.** The report names US dollars as the currency of her figure in 3d, and writes "the cost of the part she funds", never "a building-cost rise of 25%".
2. **Idea 2, the same rule on both sides of the table (Q201-013, -014, -018).**
   - **Campaign director 6 stands, and the claim changes.** One sentence in 3e: her flexibility can close a small gap at the end of a campaign or bridge a gift that arrives late, and it cannot replace a funder who withdraws. The residency guards against a lost funder as UNC, Villanova and Arizona do: no building contract until the money is held.
   - UNC's own reason, interest earned early against building inflation, is the plan's reason for buying in 2028. A judge sees Laura's rule ("buys first and states second") reappear as the campaign's rule.
3. **Idea 4, in dollars it waits and in buildings it does not (Q201-022, -023, -030).**
   - **Campaign director 24 stands, minor.** Its remedy is already the plan's waiting rule. One clause: the payout is "held in Treasury bills until drawn against the building's milestones", as Taiwan pays its own grants (30/40/30).
   - 3e gets one figure: over a 3-year delay, half of all historical waits lost about 1.5% of what the money would build, and the worst 1 in 20 lost about a fifth, mostly from the currency. Print the 3-9-year fact only beside that clause.
   - **Plain reader 15 stands.** 3e gives the ordinary case and the 1-in-20 case in one place:
     - a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar, covered in 93 of 100 futures;
