**Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change)**
1. "A calibrated word from an established scale, paired with its number, is the right way to say how sure: verbal words
   alone are read inconsistently." Which word fits ("virtually certain", "almost certain", or none), and which word a
   bond reader would call an overclaim?
2. "The residual risk Laura names ('barring a US default') can be given a market-based size." Is it below 1% from the
   2031 meetings to 15 Dec 2032, and what do CDS prices hold besides default risk?
3. "Natural frequencies with their denominator ('in every one of 585 historical windows since 1962') are understood
   better than a percentage." Is the effect real for near certainty, where the count is "every one"? Does "zero out of
   585" mislead?

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** Communicating a conditional near-certainty (an outcome already owned,
with one rare residual) to lay readers who will act on it. Solved, in parts, by: intelligence estimative language (Kent,
1964; ODNI ICD 203), climate assessment (the IPCC uncertainty guidance and its tests by Budescu and others), judgment and
decision research on verbal and numeric probabilities and on natural frequencies (Mosteller and Youtz; Mandel and Irwin;
Gigerenzer and Hoffrage; McDowell and Jacobs), and sovereign credit markets (CDS-implied default probabilities, rating
agencies' default studies).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Pairing a word with its number beats a word alone (agreement 71.76 against 39.75 in Mandel and
   Irwin, N = 1,202), but the number alone does as well (74.63), and readers shade high words down: the public read
   IPCC "very likely" (90-100%) at about 62% on average and 65-75% at the median even with the number in the sentence;
   "almost certain" averages 86% across 20 studies. No scale has a word for "owned, barring one event": ICD 203 stops
   at "almost certain" (95-99%), Kent's "almost certain" is 93% give or take about 6%, and only IPCC "virtually certain"
   reaches 100% (99-100%). So: state the owned range as a fact with its named exception (Kent's type-one statement;
   IPCC para 5), give numbers with what they are out of for what is uncertain, and use no likelihood word. If one is
   wanted, only "virtually certain (99-100%)", once, never alone. "Almost certain" undersells (heard as roughly 1 chance
   in 7 to 10 of failing); "guaranteed", "risk-free", "ironclad" and an unqualified "certain" or "100%" are what a bond
   reader calls an overclaim.
2. **Claim 2: KEEP, as context with caveats, not as the plan's probability.** The Chicago Fed's CDS-implied,
   risk-adjusted one-year chance of a US credit event was about 0.3-0.4% in 2022, about 0.2% after June 2023 and below
   0.3% in mid-November 2024; about 1% to 1.2% in late 2024 and April 2025; 4% at the 2013 and 2023 peaks and 6% in
   2011. Over the roughly 1.5-2 years from the 2031 meetings to 15 Dec 2032, that is below 1% outside a debt-limit
   standoff (Keel arithmetic, about 0.4-0.8% if the quiet one-year rate held), and not inside one (4-6% for a single
   year). It is an upper bound: it carries a risk premium, euro contracts may price dollar devaluation, the market is
   tiny ($5.7bn net notional in 2023), it assumes sellers pay, and it gauges a "technical default" (a late payment,
   likely cured), not insolvency. Ratings: AA+ (S&P, Fitch) and Aa1 (Moody's), all stable; S&P's 1975-2024 study shows
   0.00% cumulative default for AA sovereigns at every horizon from 1 to 15 years. CANON A4 keeps "barring a US
   default" structural; the market size belongs in one clause, never as "the chance the plan fails".
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The natural frequency effect is real (4% against 24% correct, odds ratio 7.1, 35 articles,
   226 estimates; the co-sponsor tab's "unconfirmed" figure is now confirmed at the source), but it is an effect on
   Bayesian computing tasks, not on reading a stated certainty. "Every one of 585" misleads twice: on the engine's
   holdings it is true by construction, and the 585 windows overlap (about four independent rate eras, NUMBERS 7).
   Zero failures in four independent trials bounds the failure rate only below about 53% (Keel arithmetic). The
   structure carries the confidence, not the count. Keep counts, with their reference class and the word
   "overlapping", for the robustness test of the rule and for the uncertainty before the move ("the top is at least
   $100,000 in about 93 of 100 modelled paths", NUMBERS 4).
4. **The finding that changes 3c most (row 27).** The case asks how confident Laura is that the contribution falls
   "within that range". The contribution is the lower of what both funds pay and the stated top (NUMBERS 0, 4; CANON
   D12), so it can fall below the bottom only if both funds together pay less than the stated bottom: centrally they
   would have to pay about a fifth less than their holdings at zero interest ($118,443 against $95,000; Keel
   arithmetic). A fund paying more than 0.5% below its holdings only stops the gift reaching the top (at 0.75%, short
   in about 1 modelled path in 17, by at most $429), and the gift is still inside the range. So "in range" has one
   exception (a US default); "the top is owned" has two.

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. The case's test turns on the bottom alone (rows 10, 26, 27)**
- What is it? The case asks for confidence that the contribution falls "within that range". By Caplet's rule the
  contribution is the lower of what both funds pay and the stated top, so it can never be above the top, and it is below
  the bottom only if both funds together pay less than the stated bottom.
- Did it work? Centrally both funds at zero interest, less 0.5%, give $118,443 against a bottom of $95,000: they would
  have to pay about a fifth less than their holdings before the gift fell outside (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4). The
  worst fund-cost case tested (1%) leaves the top up to $881 short in the house model; the gift is still inside.
- How is Laura's case like it? IPCC para 11D asks a range to say what sets its outer bounds and the chance of landing
  outside; here the bounds are owned money and the outside needs a US default.
- How is it not? Two promises sit in one sentence: "inside the range" (one exception) and "the top is owned" (two
  exceptions). A reader told only the second may hear a weaker claim than the case's test needs.
- What Caplet takes: split the confidence statement into "inside the range" and "at the top", each with its own
  exceptions and sizes. Ask the numbers owner for the smallest margin across paths (Gaps).

**Card 2. Owned means stated as fact; likelihood words only where uncertainty lives (rows 2-4, 7, 8, 13, 14)**
- What is it? Kent and the IPCC both say a finding beyond reasonable doubt is stated as fact, without a qualifier.
- Did it work? The scales built for near-certainty do not reach Laura's case: ICD 203 stops at 95-99%; Kent's top
  word is 93% give or take about 6%; the public hears "almost certain" at about 86% and "certain" at 95-99.6%.
- How is Laura's case like it? After the move both ends are money she owns in Treasury funds, which is a fact about her
  account, not a forecast.
- How is it not? It is a conditional fact: true barring a US default (and, for the top, fund costs above 0.5%). The
  condition must never be dropped (CANON C13, check 5).
- What Caplet takes: no likelihood word for the owned range; the fact with its named exception; if a word is ever
  needed, "virtually certain (99-100%)" with its scale named once.

**Card 3. Numbers do the work; a word adds nothing (rows 9, 17-21)**
- What is it? In every study read (the largest about 11,000 responses in 17 languages), words alone are read toward
  50%; a number in the sentence fixes much of it; a word beside the number adds nothing measurable.
- Did it work? Agreement 39.75 (words), 71.76 (words and numbers), 74.63 (numbers alone), N = 1,202; across 17
  languages the same regression toward 50%.
- How is Laura's case like it? Co-sponsors are lay readers deciding whether to commit money on her figure.
- How is it not? The tests used middle and high words ("likely", "very likely"), not 99-100% claims; no study tested
  a conditional certainty ("certain, barring X").
- What Caplet takes: numbers, each with its role ("at least" for the bottom, "at most" for the top) and what it is out
  of; no word where a number exists.

**Card 4. The default residual has a market size, as an upper bound on a delay (rows 28-33)**
- What is it? The Chicago Fed reads a risk-adjusted chance of a US credit event from one-year CDS premiums.
- Did it work? It tracks politics: 0.2-0.4% in quiet periods, about 1% in late 2024 and April 2025, 4-6% at the
  2011, 2013 and 2023 peaks; each of those three standoffs was closed by a law (Budget Control Act 2011,
  Continuing Appropriations Act 2014, Fiscal Responsibility Act 2023; Benzoni and Wernick).
- How is Laura's case like it? Her range is owned Treasury money maturing in late 2032; "barring a US default" is the
  only credit risk (CANON C9).
- How is it not? The figure overstates real-world risk (risk premium, euro contracts, a tiny market), prices a
  late payment rather than a loss, and no quote covers 2031-32 today.
- What Caplet takes: keep "barring a US default" structural (CANON A4); in 3e or a 3c clause, size it once as "below
  1% a year outside a debt-limit standoff, up to 4-6% at the worst standoffs, on a measure that overstates it", and
  name the debt-limit calendar as the indicator to watch (Q26 T10).

**Card 5. Natural frequencies are right for "which range", wrong for "inside the range" (rows 22-26)**
- What is it? Counts out of a named total are understood better than single-event probabilities in Bayesian tasks.
- Did it work? Yes: 4% against 24% correct across 35 articles; 16% against 46% in the original study.
- How is Laura's case like it? Before the move, the uncertainty is real and statistical: which range she will be able
  to announce. Counts out of 200,000 modelled paths fit it ("the top is at least $100,000 in about 93 of 100").
- How is it not? After the move, "every one of 585" is a property of the rule, the windows overlap (about four eras),
  and zero in four bounds the failure rate only below about 53%.
- What Caplet takes: counts with their reference class for the before-the-move uncertainty and for the rule's stress
  test; the after-the-move claim rests on ownership, with the count as support, never as the odds.

**Card 6. Say what each number is out of, and what role it plays (rows 11, 21, 22)**
- What is it? A number is read wrongly when its reference class or its role is unstated: "30% of what?", and "exactly,
  at least, at most or roughly?".
- Did it work? Only New York's majority read "30% chance of rain" as intended; numeric quantifiers stay ambiguous even
  when words are dropped.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her range has two numbers with opposite roles (a floor she will at least give, a cap
  she will at most give) and several denominators (house paths, stock windows, joint windows).
- How is it not? Her readers are fewer and can ask questions in 2031; judges cannot.
- What Caplet takes: every number in 3c and in Table 2's note names its role and its denominator, and pairs the claim
  with its reciprocal (IPCC para 4).

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE.**
- Keep: a word alone is read inconsistently (Kent's board: 20:80 to 80:20; the public reads high IPCC words toward
  50% in 17 languages).
- Change: the fix is the number, not the pair. Words plus numbers beat words (71.76 against 39.75) but not numbers
  alone (74.63); Budescu found most readers still shade "very likely" (90-100%) to about 65-75% with the number in the
  sentence.
- Which word fits: none, for the owned range. ICD 203 has no band above 99%; Kent's "almost certain" is 93% give or
  take about 6%; the public hears "almost certain" at 78-90% depending on the scale. Only IPCC "virtually certain"
  (99-100%) reaches the claim, and readers shade it too (store H10-057). Kent and IPCC para 5 both say: state an
  overwhelming finding as fact.
- Bond reader's view: an overclaim is "guaranteed", "risk-free", "ironclad", or "certain"/"100%" without the exception
  (the US is AA+/Aa1 and its CDS has priced 0.2-6% a year). An underclaim that invites questions is "almost certain" or
  "highly likely".

**Claim 2: KEEP, with caveats, as context.**
- Size: one-year risk-adjusted chance about 0.2-0.4% in quiet periods (2022; after June 2023; mid-November 2024), about
  1-1.2% in late 2024 and April 2025, 4% (2013, 2023) and 6% (2011) at the peaks (rows 28, 30).
- From the 2031 meetings to 15 Dec 2032 (about 1.5-2 years): below 1% outside a debt-limit standoff (Keel arithmetic,
  0.4-0.8% if the quiet one-year rate held for two years); not below 1% if a standoff falls in the window (one year at
  4-6%). Not provable either way: no CDS quote covers that window today.
- Caveats that must travel with any figure: CDS prices hold a risk premium, possible dollar devaluation (euro
  contracts), liquidity of a tiny market ($5.7bn net notional), seller risk, and the cheapest-to-deliver discount; they
  gauge a technical default, likely cured, not insolvency.
- Ratings: AA+ (S&P 26 Jun 2026; Fitch 13 Aug 2026), Aa1 (Moody's 16 May 2025), all stable; S&P's AA cumulative default
  0.00% at 1-15 years (1975-2024).
- For Laura (Keel reasoning, UNVERIFIED as to any default's length): her range's holdings mature by 15 Dec 2032 and the
  money is needed at the start of 2033, so a short technical delay changes timing by days; it is a delay of a payment,
  not a loss of value, unless a default were not cured. Q26 T10's reading ("one business day late on a security she or
  her funds hold") remains the lead's call.
- CANON A4 holds: the phrase stays a structural definition; the market size is context, never "the chance the plan
  fails".

**Claim 3: CHANGE.**
- Keep: the effect is real at the source (McDowell and Jacobs: 4% against 24%, odds ratio 7.1; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage:
  16% against 46%). The co-sponsor tab's "unconfirmed" mark can go.
- Change: it is an effect on computing a posterior, not on reading a stated certainty; no study read tests natural
  frequencies at 100%.
- "Zero out of 585" misleads: true by construction on the engine's holdings (NUMBERS 4); overlapping windows, about
  four independent eras (NUMBERS 7); zero in four bounds failure only below about 53% (Keel arithmetic). History cannot
  sample a US default at all.
- Use counts for: the rule's stress test (holdings and costs the rule does not assume, NUMBERS 3), and the uncertainty
  before the move (NUMBERS 4: the top at least $100,000 in 92.6% of house paths, about 93 in 100; at least $110,000 in
  73.6%, about 3 in 4; at least $120,000 in 47.0%, about 1 in 2).
- Every count names its reference class (Gigerenzer et al., 2005).
