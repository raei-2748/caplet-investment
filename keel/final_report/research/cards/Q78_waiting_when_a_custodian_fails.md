- **Problem class:** getting your own property back from a keeper that has failed. This is bailment and custody, not lending.
- **Field that solved it first:** the law of things held for another person. For securities, it was carried into broker liquidation (SIPA, 1970) and the bank rules for securities held in custody (17 CFR 450.4; 12 CFR 51.8). For pooled funds, the answer was the court-supervised pro rata distribution (Reserve Primary, 2009).

## Verdicts in one screen

### Claim 1: CHANGE

Change it to: "Where customer property was kept apart, it came back whole. Most accounts came back in days to weeks by bulk transfer, a claims process took months, and a contested tail took years. Where property was pooled with the firm's or stolen, customers lost part of it and waited longer."

- **Days to weeks, by bulk transfer:**
  - John Muir moved about 8,000 of 16,000 accounts within 10 days.
  - Fitzgerald DeArman & Roberts moved about 80% of 30,000 claims within three months.
  - MF Global's 318 securities accounts moved under a court order 39 days after the trustee was appointed.
  - Refco's regulated customers moved to Man Financial within weeks.
- **Months, by the claims process:**
  - Blinder Robinson had paid about half of 61,000 claims at six months, and about one year in all.
- **Years, for the contested tail:**
  - Lehman's last securities customers were paid from 7 Jun 2013, 1,722 days after the liquidation began (Keel arithmetic). The claims left over were "among the largest" and were disputes over who counted as a "customer".
- **Losses where assets were not kept apart:**
  - Refco Capital Markets kept customer securities "commingled in a fungible pool". Its customers got 52.81% after 14 months and 99.7% only after a decade.
  - Peregrine's domestic futures customers got 72.16% after nine years.
- **What it takes from the customer:** a claim filed on time, usually within 30 or 60 days to get securities back as securities, and within six months at the latest.

### Claim 2: CHANGE

Change it to: "The holdings are not the sponsor's or the custodian's to lose. Law and contract keep them out of a failed keeper's estate. What a keeper's failure can cost is time, plus anything held as a cash deposit."

- **The law:**
  - A bank that holds government securities for customers must keep them "free of any lien, charge or claim of any third party" (17 CFR 450.4).
  - The OCC: "If a bank is bought or fails, custody assets remain the property of the account owner."
  - Cash is different. "Cash deposits are not securities, even if they are held in a custody account", and deposits are open to the bank's creditors (U.S. Bank). Rule 17f-5 makes the same carve-out for cash.
- **The record:**
  - iShares moved its Treasury funds' custody from State Street to JPMorgan on or around 6 May 2024, by a supplement. Holders did nothing.
  - No case was found of a Treasury fund losing holdings through its sponsor's or custodian's failure (bounded search: SEC releases, GAO, court dockets, closure notices).
  - The nearest operating harm is days of stale prices (BNY Mellon, Aug 2015, store Q2b-027).
- **Who keeps the holdings in 2026:**
  - JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. keeps IBTM, IBTO, IBTQ and IBTR: 53.90% of Table 1 at cost.
  - State Street keeps IBTP and VT: 16.23%.
  - The five bonds and the cash (29.44%) are with the broker.
  - All three shares are Keel arithmetic from NUMBERS 8g.

### Claim 3: CHANGE

Change it to: "The Primary Fund lost 1.26% of its assets in one issuer's paper (Lehman). Its holders got 50.8 cents after 45 days, 78.9 cents after 79 days, 98.6 cents after 501 days and 99.1 cents after six years."

- **The holdings were never what was in question.** The wait came from the fight over who bore the loss. About 30 lawsuits had been filed, and the board set aside $3.5bn.
- **What differs for a fund holding only Treasuries:**
  - There is no issuer that can fail other than the US.
  - The iBond funds are not $1.00 funds paying cash on demand. Their creations and redemptions settle in kind through the Federal Reserve System.
  - The 2008 run went *into* Treasury money funds (about $300bn).
- **But "only government paper" did not stop a freeze.** The Reserve U.S. Government Fund, which held government and agency paper, was suspended for two months. It paid $1.00 only after the Treasury agreed to be its buyer of last resort.
- **What the warning becomes for Laura:** a frozen fund pays late and pro rata. It does not lose Treasuries.

### Also asked

- **1979:** bills due 26 Apr, 3 May and 10 May were paid by check up to about 18 days late. The interest holders lost was estimated at $125,000 on about $122m, about 0.10% (Keel arithmetic).
  - The Treasury "has no authority to reimburse investors for the time-value of delayed payments".
  - More than 80% of holders reportedly took a settlement offer. A bill to compensate the rest died in committee, and the class action was "dismissed with prejudice on May 12, 1980".
  - Book-entry holders were untouched. All of Laura's holdings are book entry.

### Judge's notes

- **The variable is "kept apart", not the size or brand of the firm.**
  - Refco's regulated customers and the customers of Refco Capital Markets were in the same failed group in the same week.
  - The first group moved to a new broker within weeks with nothing lost.
  - The second group waited 14 months for 53 cents and a decade for 99.7.
- **The Primary Fund's custodian bank did not lose a dollar of the fund's assets.** What it did on 15 Sep 2008 was stop *lending*: it "suspends the fund's overdraft privileges after honoring $10 billion". In a fund the custodian is a keeper, and in a run it can also be a lender. Laura's floor fund never needs it to lend.
- **The one late Treasury payment cost check holders about a tenth of one percent, and the law gave them no way to claim it.** The payments the plan needs are all owned in book entry, which 1979 never touched.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 12 (3a or 3e): Refco's two fates.** The same group and the same week: weeks and nothing lost where property was kept apart, a decade and a haircut where it was pooled. One sentence shows a judge that "owned" is a legal fact with a measured price.

4. **Rows 27-32 (2.1 and 2.3): the keepers.** JPMorgan keeps the floor and three rungs, State Street keeps one rung and VT, and the broker keeps the bonds. A custodian changed under IBTM in May 2024 and nobody had to act.
5. **Row 19 (3e, a judge's aha): the custodian as lender.** In 2008 the Primary Fund's custodian did not lose assets. It stopped lending. A fund that pays out once, at its end, never needs the loan.
