## The four answers, read at source

1. **The rule (2.24, summer 431 BCE).**
   - A thousand talents from the money on the Acropolis were "set apart" and "not to be spent", and the war was to be fought "from the other money" (ἀλλ’ ἀπὸ τῶν ἄλλων πολεμεῖν).
   - The only exception was "defending the city in the event of the enemy bringing a fleet to make an attack by sea" (ἢν μὴ οἱ πολέμιοι νηίτῃ στρατῷ ἐπιπλέωσι τῇ πόλει καὶ δέῃ ἀμύνασθαι).
   - Anyone who "should move or put to the vote" another use faced death (θάνατον ζημίαν).
   - The best hundred triremes of each year, with their captains, were held back under the same rule: "None of these was to be used except with the money and against the same peril".
2. **Who could break it.**
   - The penalty fell on the speaker and the chairman, the one who proposes and the one who "puts to the vote", never on the voters. So only the Assembly, the body that made the rule, could lift it.
   - It did so in two steps, both in the same panic: first it "took off the penalty attaching to whoever proposed or put to the vote", then it voted to spend (8.15).
   - The same two steps already guarded Athena's other money. Under the Kallias decrees (434/3 BCE?), money above 10,000 drachmas could not be used "unless the People vote immunity (adeian) as when they vote about capital tax" (OR 144).
3. **When, and for what.**
   - In summer 412 news came that Chios, "the greatest of their number", had revolted. The Athenians feared the rest of the allies "would not consent to keep quiet".
   - "In the consternation of the moment" (ὑπὸ τῆς παρούσης ἐκπλήξεως) they lifted the penalty and voted to "man a large number of ships".
   - The ships: Strombichides sailed with 8, then Thrasycles with 12; 10 fresh ships replaced them, and 30 more were to be manned.
   - So the money paid for a fleet in Ionia, not for the city's defence. The conditions of 2.24 "were not fulfilled, so the assembly had first to vote to override those conditions" (Oxford World's Classics note). Blamire: Athens "finally turned to the protected fund of 1,000T" for naval reinforcements (p. 115).
4. **Was the money there?**
   - Yes in 412. Thucydides says Athens had "jealously avoided touching" it "throughout the whole war". Blamire does not list it among the "remaining reserves" spent on Sicily, and says it "continued to be protected by special sanctions" (p. 113 n. 95).
   - No in 411, when the reserve's own danger came. After Euboea revolted, "Neither the disaster in Sicily ... nor any other had ever so much alarmed them" (Greek: ἔκπληξις μεγίστη, the same word as 8.15). They feared the enemy would "sail against Piraeus, which they had no longer ships to defend" (8.96).
   - By mid-411 Athens's money "was apparently close to exhaustion", and in 410/9 the restored democracy "had inherited an empty treasury" (Blamire, pp. 115, 117).
   - Athens survived because the Spartans "proved the most convenient people in the world for the Athenians to be at war with" (8.96).
   - The hundred reserve ships may never have been kept for long: "possible that this was indeed decided in 431 but not adhered to for long" (OWC note). In 413 Athens had "not sufficient ships in their docks, or money in the treasury" (8.1).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. The rule was written beside the other money, and it lasted exactly as long as the other money did.**
- (a) In 431 Athens set a thousand talents aside and decided in the same sentence that the war would be paid "from the other money". For nineteen years it was: from the allies' tribute (reassessed in 425 at a target of 1,460 to 1,500 talents a year, against 600 in 431), from a tax on citizens' property (200 talents in 428) and from loans out of the gods' treasuries on the Acropolis. Athens lifted the rule in 412, after Sicily had used up "remaining reserves", when the greatest ally revolted and the others might follow.
- (b) Laura's IPS also names its other money in the same paragraph as the figures she will state: half the shares, "promised to no one ... for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short" (IPS l.15). So a building scare has money of its own, and nobody has to touch the rule that guards the payments (l.17).
- (c) It breaks because most of Athens's other money belonged to other people, so it ran out when they walked away, while her flexibility is her own. Even so, her flexibility is smallest when shares fall between the 2031 meetings and the 2033 contribution, the years co-sponsors may cut too: the house model's median is about $15,200 then, against $23,000 when shares rise, and about $12,800 after a fall of more than a fifth (NUMBERS 8f). That is Athens's order of events, measured: one loss empties the free money, then a contributor leaves.
- Decision it serves: her flexibility (IPS l.15); the two reasons (l.17); "a co-sponsor falling short" as one of the three uses.
- Report part: 3e (how a promise breaks), 3b.
- Sources: Thucydides 2.24, 8.1, 8.15 (Crawley; Jones's Greek); Blamire (2001) pp. 106-115.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** A finance judge does not know that the rule's own wording planned for other money. That rule broke at the exact event Laura's flexibility names, a contributor falling short, right after a venture had used up the free money.

**2. The reserve was spent the year before its own danger came.**
- (a) Athens kept the money for one danger, an enemy fleet attacking the city, and in 412 spent it on a different one, a revolt across the Aegean. In 411 its own danger came: Euboea revolted, and Piraeus lay open with no ships to defend it. By then Athens's money was "apparently close to exhaustion", and the city was saved only because Sparta did not attack.
- (b) Money kept for a danger goes to whichever danger comes first, because nobody knows when its own will come. Laura's reserve is kept for ten dates, and the Treasury bought for the January 2041 payment matures just before it. Any raid would show as a named year with nothing in it, and a date, unlike a danger, always comes.
- (c) It breaks because the reserve decided neither 412 nor 411 (Sparta's slowness did), so Athens shows the cost of keeping money for something that may never come. The thousand talents sat untouched for nineteen years while Athens borrowed from its own gods at interest (6% a year until 427/6, then 1.2%; Blamire p. 108). Laura's Treasuries earn interest while they wait, and what they cost her is the shares she does not buy (NUMBERS 8n).
- Decision it serves: writing the operating reserve as dated sums; buying the ten payments before anything is stated.
- Report part: 3.0 (certainty has dates), with 3e.
- Sources: Thucydides 8.15, 8.96 (Crawley; Jones's Greek for 8.96.1-2); Blamire (2001) pp. 108, 115, 117.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** Classicists know 8.96, but the pairing has not been made in a finance setting: the reserve was spent the year before its own danger arrived. It turns "certainty has dates" into a story with a date on each side.

**3. Four levers, not three, and the plan leaves all four on the building's side.**
- (a) Before Athens touched its rule, it pulled a fourth lever that card Q166 missed: more money from others. It more than doubled the tribute it asked of the allies in 425, taxed its citizens' property in 428, and in 413 swapped the tribute for a 5% levy in the allies' harbours. It pulled the rule lever only when the others' money failed.
- (b) For Laura's ten payments, the Treasuries set the dates and pay in dollars. IPS l.11 rules out "anybody else's money", and l.17 writes down the rule, so only the rule is left. The building keeps all four: co-sponsors are other people's money, the project is still developing (the case speaks of her "financial flexibility as the project develops"), and her flexibility is unpromised. So a building shortfall always has a lever to pull that does not touch the payments.
- (c) It breaks because Athens guarded its fourth lever as hard as its reserve: a property tax on citizens needed the same vote of immunity as Athena's money (Kallias decrees). Laura's co-sponsors are the building's first resort, not its last. Her stated figure is also not a lever: it is owned before it is stated, and the plan never treats it as one, so the building's levers are its budget, its timing and its other funders, never the figure she named.
- Decision it serves: IPS l.11's "anybody else's money"; l.17's two reasons; l.15's flexibility. It also fixes the rater's note on Q166-001.
- Report part: 3e's lead (with card Q166 idea 1), or one clause in 3b.

**4. The best companion: the same city, the same guard, the opposite pot.**
- (a) About eighty years later Athens guarded the opposite pot: its festival fund (the theoric fund, paid out to citizens) against the army. In 349/8, with Athens "on the point of sending your entire force to Euboea and Olynthus", Apollodorus carried a decree letting the people decide whether the surplus went to the army or to the festivals. A citizen then indicted it as illegal, and a court fined Apollodorus one talent ([Dem.] 59.4-5; Hansen 1976, pp. 237-238).
- (b) A guard on proposing protects whichever pot it is put on. Here the pot was regular payments, and the pressure came from a great uncertain cost, war, which is the direction of Laura's plan: payments guarded, the building facing the uncertain cost. Each Athenian guard held for years and fell only to a vote of the whole Assembly. In 412 the Assembly broke the first after Sicily, and "only some ten years later" than Apollodorus, Demosthenes carried a proposal that "all money be transferred to the Stratiotic Fund" (Philochorus, via Hansen).
- (c) It breaks because Athens could not make its war wait, while Laura's building has other funders and her flexibility. It also carries the judge's sharpest question, the one Apollodorus and Demosthenes put to Athens: why keep running money untouchable when the building needs it? The case answers that by putting the payments first ("support the commitment with high certainty", CASE_MAP A9), and card Q77 holds what happens if the building does not happen.
- Decision it serves: why the guard sits on the payments and the free money faces the building (IPS l.11, l.15).
- Report part: store; at most one clause in 3e (one outside name per part, panel 4, so Athens's reserve or this, never both).
- Sources: [Demosthenes] 59.4-5 (Murray); Hansen (1976) pp. 236-239.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha** for the pairing, which is niche. Demosthenes is known, but not the court that fined a man for proposing, in wartime, what the Assembly had just approved.
