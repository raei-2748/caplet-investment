## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The study never tests "money before building", so the claim cannot be kept as a finding. What the record does say is narrower, and it points the same way.
  - **The field's own advice.** The Aspen Institute's notes for artists tell them to "provide sufficient liquid assets to the foundation to sustain its functions initially without art sales" (Vincent, 2019, updated Nov 2025, p. 6). The 2010 study: "Unless blessed with a substantial financial endowment from the start, study center and exhibition foundations will periodically sell artworks or other assets ... to support operation".
  - **Buildings are where the strain shows.** House museum foundations "often are challenged to balance the scope of their missions with the financial resources provided by their donors' estate plans", and "In many cases, the result is a decision to convert to public charity status" to raise outside money (2010 study, 2.2).
  - **Cases, building first.** Donald Judd's estate "contained art, real estate, debt, and little cash" (Architect, 2013). His foundation sold 26 lots for $24,468,800 in one evening in 2006, "earmarked to set up a new endowment" for 17 buildings (artnet, 2006). Its first restoration budget of about $8 million grew to $28 million (Architect, 2013). It has filed as a public charity since FY2013, the year 101 Spring Street opened (ProPublica).
  - **The first artist's residency.** Louis Comfort Tiffany's foundation (1918) ran his Long Island estate as a residency. It "converted to making grants to individual artists after its collections and property were sold in 1946 and 1949" (2010 study, 3.1).
  - **Cases, money first.** The Joan Mitchell Foundation spent ten years on "modest resources" while the estate settled; its assets were $923,462 at the end of 2002 (990-PF). It then grew a portfolio from art sales, bought the New Orleans site in 2010 and opened the Center in 2015. It runs on its own money: in 2014 it "doesn't solicit donations, and doesn't even accept gifts" (artnet, 2014).
  - **Money first does not save a building.** The Rauschenberg Foundation received $325,978,217 from the estate in FY2012 (ProPublica), and residencies began on Captiva that year. On 27 Aug 2025 its board decided to sell the site after "recurring storm damage, broader climate risks, and rising maintenance costs". Keeping it "would require substantial additional investment and site modifications". The proceeds and the upkeep money go to artists' programmes elsewhere. It sold on 31 Mar 2026 (Business Observer).
  - **Better claim:** in the artist-endowed record, what ended were sites, not the giving. The first artist's residency (Tiffany) and one of the richest (Rauschenberg) both sold their buildings, and the money kept paying artists. Foundations that built before they had money (Judd) sold the founder's best work and went to the public for gifts.
  - **Judge's note:** the artists' own adviser tells them to fund the first years "without art sales"; Laura's ten payments are that rule written in Treasuries.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** True by dollars, false by count. "Must sell" is true for those without a financial endowment, and the field's money is getting less liquid.
  - **By dollars.** Art was 45% of all assets in 2005 ($1.1bn), 57% in 2010 and 70% in 2015 ($5.37bn of $7.66bn). Financial assets fell to 27% from 38% (2010 study; 2018 supplement). "the field's liquidity has continued to decline" (2018).
  - **By count.** In 2005, 46% of the foundations held only financial assets. A third held financial assets and art, 15% held financial assets, art and buildings, and 6% held financial assets and buildings (2010 study, 2.1). In 2015, 58% held any non-financial asset, including land or equipment (2018).
  - **Who holds art.** Operating foundations that hold art: art was 74% of their assets (41 foundations), against 49% for grantmakers (66) (2005). Living artists' foundations are "funded solely with financial assets", with few exceptions (2010 study, 2.2).
  - **One foundation's tax form.** Joan Mitchell, 2023: art and archives were $437,271,500 of $577,316,288 at market value (75.7%). Financial assets were 22.0% and the New Orleans campus 2.1% (Keel arithmetic on the 990-PF). Its own words: the model "uses funds from strategic sales of artworks ... to grow an investment portfolio and support mission-driven work".
  - **Why selling hurts.** Art can fall in the same downturn as shares. "The process of liquidating large-scale art holdings to form endowments slows significantly with a decline in the art market", which "slowed and declined substantially in the second half of 2008" (2010 study, 5.1, 2.1). "Block sales of art assets to achieve liquidity can be problematic if sales are discounted" (2.2).
  - **The disanalogy for Laura holds, and it is sharper than the brief said.** These foundations hold the founder's career as their main asset. Laura's IPS says her career is already "a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell" (IPS l.9). So her operating money is in Treasuries that pay set dollars on set dates, and nothing is sold to make a payment.
  - **Judge's note:** an artist's foundation is a career turned into a portfolio, 70% of it in the founder's own work. Laura's plan does the reverse on purpose.
- **Claim 3: KEEP as context only, with a range, never one figure.**
  - **The Joan Mitchell Center.** Its 2023 cost was $2,023,402, a quarter of the foundation's spending (990-PF Part VIII-A). It had 16 artists in a deliberately small year, 2 of them virtual (the announcement names 13 on site in the text read). That is about $126,000 per artist, more per artist on site (Keel arithmetic). It had 37 artists in 2024, 35 in 2025 and 31 in 2026; their 2024-26 costs were not read.
  - **Jentel (Wyoming).** It spent $396,454 in 2008 for "more than 60 residencies annually": at most about $6,600 each (Keel arithmetic; Aspen profile).
  - **Thread (Senegal).** It hosts two artists at a time for four weeks, August to May: at most about 20 a year (Keel arithmetic).
  - **What it means.** $50,000 a year is about 2.5% of the Joan Mitchell Center's 2023 cost, or about seven or eight Jentel-sized residencies at 2008 spending (Keel arithmetic). What a year of operations buys varies more than tenfold by model. Context for "a base", never her facility's cost.
- **Claim 4: KILL.** Large foundations usually get their main money late. Research the study cites found that "larger foundations receive their primary endowments an average of 18 years after establishment, following death of the primary founder and the surviving spouse" (2010 study, quantitative report s.6).
  - **Living founders give year by year.** Their foundations work "on this type of *pass-through* basis", spending what the founder gives each year (2010 study, 4.3). Jentel was one: 97.8%-99.5% of its revenue was the founder's gifts (Q32 row 31).
  - **Laura is the reverse.** She is a living founder whose ten years are owned before the first payment: $444,545 of Treasuries on 1 Jan 2033, against $399,089 the payments are worth at today's market rates (NUMBERS 8h, MODEL).
  - **Judge's note:** most founders' money arrives after they die and most living founders give year by year. Laura's arrives before the doors open.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 1 (with row 2), 3a.** The artists' own adviser tells them to fund a foundation's first years "without art sales"; Laura's ten operating payments are that advice written in Treasuries, dated to 2042. It turns her reserve from a finance choice into the field's own standard, kept for ten years rather than "initially".
2. **Rows 7, 9 and 44, 2.3.** Artist-endowed foundations hold 70% of their money in the founder's own work (2015), and the ones that run programmes are the most art-heavy. Laura's plan does the reverse on purpose, because her career is already the concentrated holding (IPS l.9). It gives a judge an aha: the institutions most like hers are the least diversified in philanthropy.
3. **Rows 19, 21 and 22, 3b (and 3e).** The first artist-founded residency (Tiffany, 1918) and one of the richest (Rauschenberg, 2012-2026) both sold their sites, and the money kept paying artists. The ten payments are dated dollars for operations, not a building, so a building that costs too much is a facility question, and her flexibility, not the payments, meets it.
4. **Rows 28-29, CONCEIT V2.x and 3.0.** On one 2023 tax form the same paintings are $22.8 million at book and $437.3 million at market, and holding them costs about $695,000 a year. A figure no sale has turned into money is pencil, however large; a Treasury held to its date pays what it says. Never in tables or Laura's words.
5. **Rows 11 and 10, 3.0.** Most founders' main money arrives about 18 years after the institution starts, at their death, and living founders give year by year. Laura's arrives six years before the first payment. It is the plainest way to say what "the proposition no other founder can make" means (SKELETON 3.0).
