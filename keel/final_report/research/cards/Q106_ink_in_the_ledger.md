- **Problem class and the field that solved it first.** The problem: when may a figure that is not yet certain be
  written where others will rely on it, and how is a wrong one corrected without hiding it? This is a recognition and
  record-integrity problem.
  - Italian merchants solved it first. Pacioli (1494) corrects by a counter-entry, never by taking a figure away.
    Venetian writers of the 1530s-1540s state "never ... erase".
  - Standard-setters solved it for gains not yet earned: FAS 5 (1975), carrying forward ARB 50, and IAS 37.
  - Taiwan wrote the correction rule into statute for government books (會計法) and into regulation for company books
    (商業會計處理準則).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
   - **Pacioli never says "never erase".** His rule (ch. 31, repeated in chs 32 and 36) is the storno: to "take out"
     (Florentine "stornare") a wrong entry, write the same amount on the opposite side and mark both with a cross.
     Then "it is just as if you had written nothing". The fix is an addition, and both entries stay on the page.
   - **The explicit rule is Manzoni's, not Pacioli's.** "Never cross the wrong entry out or erase it, for you cannot
     prove what was there, and hence it will be construed as deceit" is Geijsbeek's (1914) rendering of Manzoni's
     index. Manzoni wrote in Venice; Geijsbeek lists a 1534 first edition and reproduces a journal from 1540.
   - **Ownership or record?** Record. Pacioli's reasons are all about proof:
     - pages are numbered against someone who "would wish to do something crooked" (ch. 6);
     - a public officer's clerk seals the books "to make the books authentic for any case in court" (ch. 7);
     - keeping two sets of books is "perjury" (ch. 7).
   - **Where the parallel breaks:**
     - (a) A ledger's ink records the past; Laura's ink is a future payment she already owns.
     - (b) A ledger in ink can hold "bad money": Pacioli records debts not owed "from good people" (ch. 3). So ink in a
       ledger is no promise that the money will come.
     - (c) A bookkeeper corrects in ink. Laura's ink is never corrected, because each Treasury is held to its date.
   - **Where it holds better than the claim.** Pacioli's 13th inventory item is money with Venice's Camera de
     l'Imprestì ("municipal loan bank", ch. 17). The merchant lists it with:
     - the office's book;
     - the page of his account;
     - the clerk's name;
     - dates "put down precisely so that you know when everything falls due and what the per cent. is".

     The first printed book on bookkeeping already lists claims on a public office by where they are recorded and when
     they fall due.
   - **Say:** "Since the first printed bookkeeping book (Pacioli, 1494), a wrong figure in ink is not taken out; it is
     answered by a second entry, and both stay on the page."
   - **Never say:** "accounting's oldest rule is never erase" or "Pacioli forbade erasing".
   - **Judge's note:** a smart reader has it backwards. A ledger's ink does not stop mistakes; it makes every
     correction public. That is why the top waits for the move. A top stated early and cut later would be a storno in
     front of co-sponsors, with both figures remembered. A top stated 1, 3, 6 or 12 months before the move is not yet
     owned in 2.9%, 6.2%, 8.7% or 10.7% of house paths (NUMBERS 4, l.378).

2. **Claim 2: CHANGE.**
   - **The Business Entity Accounting Act (商業會計法) has no "erase" article.** What it does say:
     - numbered pages "shall not be damaged" (art. 24);
     - nothing is recorded unless a real event stands behind it (art. 33);
     - forging or altering (變造) books, or destroying pages, risks up to five years in prison or a fine of up to
       NT$600,000 (art. 71);
     - destroying pages or the audit trail draws a fine of NT$60,000-300,000 (art. 76).
   - **The strike-through rule for company books is in a regulation**, 商業會計處理準則 art. 13: 「劃紅線二道」 (two
     red lines through the error), with the correct figure written above and signed or sealed by whoever corrects it.
     Its official English (2021) gets the second sentence wrong.
   - **The literal ban is in the Accounting Act (會計法), which governs government agencies.** Art. 121 extends it to
     subsidised private bodies, as the government's accounting office decides. Art. 67: a wrong entry is ruled through
     and signed or sealed, and 「不得挖補、擦、刮或用藥水塗滅」 (no patching, rubbing, scraping or blotting out with
     chemicals).
   - **Say:** "Taiwan's Accounting Act forbids scraping or rubbing out an entry in government books: a wrong figure is
     ruled through and signed" (art. 67). For company books: "struck through with two red lines and signed".
   - **Never say:** "the Business Accounting Act forbids erasing". Never give legal advice.
   - **Strongest Taiwan find:** the Negotiable Instruments Act (票據法) art. 11(3). On a cheque or note, any item
     "except the amount" (除金額外) may be rewritten by its writer before delivery, signed at the change. On 23 Mar 2026
     the Taichung District Court voided a promissory note whose amount had been changed. In Taiwan's own law, the
     amount is the one line that can never be pencil.
   - **Judge's note:** Q83's arts-grant rule (「擦刮挖補塗改」 void) uses the same verbs as the national Accounting
     Act. No source read ties the two, so say "the same words", never "derived from".

3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.**
   - **True for bookkeepers:**
     - entries were in ink and running totals ("footings") in small pencil figures. "These footings should always be
       written in pencil" (1912; also 1911 and 1920);
     - the trial balance was in "light pencil marks, which may be erased" (1887);
     - a footing was inked only when the account was ruled and closed: "Rule and foot the account; write the footings
       in black ink" (1912).
   - **True for auditors' ticks:**
     - "a check mark in colored pencil should be made to the right of the amount" (Castenholz 1919, via Ariail &
       Hughes 2014);
     - "a tick in red pencil" (Peloubet 1949).
   - **Not a rule for working papers:**
     - "Some prominent firms prepare their working papers in pencil, some in ink. There seems to be little uniformity
       of practice" (Peloubet 1949);
     - British clerks in the 1940s marked their checks with stamps "often using different colored ink" (Matthews 2006).
   - **What did split pencil from ink:** who would read the paper and whether it would last. "Report drafts may be
     prepared in pencil, since the copy of the report itself is the permanent record. If working papers are to be
     examined by a third party, papers prepared in ink convey a greater impression of authenticity" (Peloubet).
   - **Judge's note:** in a ledger, the pencil was the total, not the entries. What happened was written in ink; what
     it added up to while the account was open was in pencil until the account was closed. For Laura:
     - the ten payments and the bottom are entries;
     - the shares' value is a running total in pencil;
     - the move is the closing that inks the top.

4. **Claim 4 ("in the red"): CHANGE.**
   - The origin story rests on secondary pages:
     - Gary Martin quotes Rollins (1907): "Formerly it was customary, and is now with some bookkeepers, to make an
       entry of a loss in red ink, from whence arose the term 'in the red'".
     - Barry Popik quotes the OED: first "in the red" 1926, first "in the black" 1928.
   - Textbooks of 1887 and 1912 also wrote gains, closings and rulings in red: "The use of red ink is largely a matter
     of custom" (1912).
   - Keep all of it out of the report. CONCEIT already forbids colour for VT, and the report makes no puns.

5. **Claim 5 ("no rule like Laura's"): KILL.**
   - **IAS 37:**
     - "An entity shall not recognise a contingent asset" (para 31).
     - It is not recognised "since this may result in the recognition of income that may never be realised. However,
       when the realisation of income is virtually certain, then the related asset is not a contingent asset and its
       recognition is appropriate" (para 33).
     - It is then recognised "in the period in which the change occurs" (para 35).
   - **US GAAP** (FAS 5 para 17, 1975; now ASC 450-30-25-1): gains "usually are not reflected in the accounts since to
     do so might be to recognize revenue prior to its realization". Any disclosure must "avoid misleading implications
     as to the likelihood of realization".
   - **KPMG (2025):** "probability is not a factor in the recognition of gain contingencies".
   - **Laura's rule is accounting's rule for gains.** A figure is stated once it is no longer contingent, not once it
     is likely.
   - **She is stricter in one place.** IAS 37 para 34 discloses a gain that is "probable"; Laura tells co-sponsors
     nothing of her pencil.
   - **Judge's note:** the conceit is not a picture laid over finance; it is how finance's own standard-setters treat a
     gain. "Probability is not a factor" answers anyone who asks why a top that is 97% likely a month before the move
     is still not stated (NUMBERS 4, l.378: 2.9% of house paths fall short at one month).
   - **Where it breaks:**
     - IAS 37 is about claims that arise from past events; its example is a lawsuit the entity is pursuing (para 32).
       It is not about a portfolio's future value.
     - Laura writes a letter, not financial statements.

     So use it as a parallel, never as "IFRS requires".

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 16, IAS 37 (2.0 definition, 3c).** Laura's rule is the global accounting standard's rule for gains: nothing is
   booked until it has stopped being contingent. The conceit becomes the judge's own professional rule.
2. **Row 19, "probability is not a factor" (3c, with 3a).** It answers the hardest question: why not state a top that
   is 97% likely a month early? It also shows that the plan books risks early and gains late, as accountants do.
3. **Row 34, comics against ledgers (3d, 3c).** The conceit's own image, white paint over a mistake, would be deceit in
   a ledger. Figures for co-sponsors live in the ledger, so the top waits for the move. This is a new turn that needs
   no trade glossary.
4. **Row 26, the total inked at closing (3c, 3d).** The move is the closing: the top was a pencil total and is now
   ink, while half the account stays open as her flexibility. It is plain bookkeeping that any finance judge knows.
5. **Row 22, Taiwan's amount line (3d).** On a Taiwanese cheque or note, everything but the amount may be rewritten,
   and a court voided a note in March 2026 because its amount had been changed. This is the residency's own law
   behind "state it once".

Runner-up: row 3 (Pacioli's 13th item: claims on a public loan office listed with where they are recorded and the
date each falls due, 530 years before Table 1's CUSIPs).
