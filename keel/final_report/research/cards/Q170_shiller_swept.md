## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Shiller wanted to sell an artist a floor under her career; Laura cannot buy one, so the plan puts the floor under her promises instead

(a) Shiller opened *The New Financial Order* with a young violinist who puts off her career because no one will share its risk, and his cure, livelihood insurance, would have given her "a floor ... guaranteed on his income and exciting prospects" above it. (b) Laura is that artist in the world as it is: no such policy was ever sold (Shiller himself found "no precedent"), so the IPS cannot put a floor under her income and puts one under what she promises: the ten payments and the bottom of her range are Treasuries, and the top is shares, the same floor-and-upside shape Shiller drew, moved from the career to the promise. (c) It breaks where his insurance would have paid her when writers' incomes fell, and the plan pays her nothing then; that break is the honest scope of the IPS's word "hedge" (CANON A2): it keeps her promises from falling with her income and does not prop up her income.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.3 and l.9 (her career as an unsellable holding, so the portfolio leans the other way) and the floor-and-top shape of the 2031 range (l.3, l.15).
- **Report part:** 2.3, one or two sentences; a clause in 3c.
- **Source:** Shiller (2003), Introduction and pp. 109-110; Abramowicz (2020) on why no market exists.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha for most judges. Human capital is pedigree (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson), but that the best-known proposal to insure careers starts from an artist, offers a floor plus upside, and was never built, is not; and the move of the floor from the career to the promise is Caplet's own.

### 2. Certainty underneath is what pays for daring on top (tenure, Charles Ives and the IPS's closing line)

(a) Shiller calls academic tenure a risk-management device: universities guarantee a young high achiever's income "to take risks" with research, and in *Finance and the Good Society* he shows Charles Ives, an insurance man whose fortune let him write music nobody yet wanted, so "He did not have to take hack composing assignments". (b) The IPS's last line is the same division of labour ("You take risks on the page ... With these promises, ours is to take none"): the ten payments are guaranteed so that the residency can commit to artists years ahead and its artists can take risks with their work, which turns the plan's caution from timidity into the thing that buys boldness. (c) It breaks on cost: Shiller says tenure needs "an extensive—and ultimately very expensive— monitoring system" against moral hazard, while Laura's base is bought in advance and may change for only two reasons, so nobody has to watch whether the money will be earned, only whether the paper pays (Table 3); the break teaches that paying in advance is what removes the monitoring.
- **Decision it serves:** the ten payments in Treasuries rather than shares (IPS l.11) and the closing line.
- **Report part:** 3a (what the commitment's certainty is for), or the opening of 3f's closing paragraph; never more than two sentences.
- **Source:** Shiller (2003), p. 110; Shiller (2012), ch. 20 (Ives and Schoenberg).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows tenure, but not as Shiller's risk-management argument, and has not seen it used to answer the "too cautious for six years" objection with the client's own trade.

### 3. Osaka's rice bills: "true rice", "rice on the books" and the empty bill (the conceit's ink, tested 270 years early)

(a) In Osaka the lords sold rice bills, and one kind was "evidence of a property right in a certain amount of rice which was stored in the warehouse" while another, the "empty" bill, was issued "without connection to the issuing warehouse's inventory"; by 1749 the bills in circulation claimed more than 110,000 bales against about 30,000 in store, "almost four times" the rice. (b) Laura's 2031 figure is the first kind of bill: before she states a dollar, Treasuries she holds will pay it (IPS l.15), with the 0.5% margin and the rounding down keeping what she states under what she holds, so her ratio runs the other way from Osaka's; a co-sponsor's pledge is the second kind, owed against next year's budget the way an empty bill was owed against next year's harvest. (c) It breaks at the fix: the Shogunate's bans on empty bills (1650s, 1660, 1761) and its rules on their form all failed, and empty bills became safe only in 1773, when the government began "guaranteeing all rice bills ... into bakufu-backed bonds"; Laura's ink is not rice in a warehouse but already the sovereign's own bond, which is why the one thing that would turn her bills empty is the IPS's second reason, a Treasury default.
- **Decision it serves:** the rule that Laura states a figure only once it is held in Treasuries (CONCEIT; IPS l.15), why her figure is Treasuries and not anyone's pledge, and the two reasons (l.17).
- **Report part:** 2.0, as the true fact behind the working word "ink" (one clause), or 3d's reserves (why her figure differs in kind from a pledge). Never in a table or in Laura's own words.
- **Source:** Schaede (1989), pp. 492-494 and 507-508; Takatsuki (2012); Nagao (1975); West (2000); Shiller 2008 L21 ch.2 and 2011 L15 for Dojima as the first futures market.
- **Pedigree or aha:** strong aha. A finance judge knows Dojima as "the first futures market" (Shiller's own lecture says so); almost none know that it ran on two kinds of bill, one backed and one empty, or that the empty ones were made good only by a sovereign guarantee, which predicts the plan's choice of issuer.

### 4. Built to be spent: why Laura's reserve is not an endowment

(a) Shiller notes that universities grow endowments "with no plan to spend them down", and cites Henry Hansmann: they "have not drawn down their endowments—not in any historically known contingency", and in 2007-09 "instead they tended to curtail activities". (b) Laura's operating reserve is the opposite kind of money: every dollar of it has a January to pay, and what is left over comes back to her by 2042, so in a crisis the residency does not cut programmes to protect a balance; the year's money is already labelled for that year. (c) It breaks on time: an endowment serves a purpose with no end date and must keep its buying power, while Laura's reserve covers ten dated, fixed-dollar years and says nothing about 2043, which the case does not ask; the break teaches that the reserve's certainty was bought by giving up forever.
- **Decision it serves:** the reserve as Treasuries matched to ten dates (IPS l.11, l.17); the house rule never to call it an endowment.
- **Report part:** 3b, one sentence of evaluation.
- **Source:** Shiller (2012), ch. 17 (Hansmann's point; the Shakers).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree-plus. Nonprofit finance specialists know Hansmann; most finance judges do not, and the contrast explains a word choice the report already makes.

### 5. Charity is capricious and insurance is sound: why the ten payments use none of anyone else's money

(a) In his last 2008 lecture Shiller quotes Gustav Schmoller on how Germany's "older charitable relief funds" were rebuilt "on the sound principle of insurance", and adds that charity "tends to be whimsical and capricious", flowing to whatever need is in the newspaper. (b) The case already keeps outside money out of the ten payments, and the IPS adds "or on anybody else's money" (l.11): Bismarck's move from gifts to money set aside in advance is the move from a co-sponsor's goodwill to Laura's own Treasuries, while co-sponsors are left the building, the visible, nameable part donors like to fund. (c) It breaks on pooling: Bismarck's insurance spread risk over millions of workers, and Laura funds one residency with no one to pool with, so she cannot insure the payments and has to buy all ten outright; the break teaches why matching in advance, not insurance, is the only sound principle open to a single giver.
- **Decision it serves:** no co-sponsor money in the ten payments (case; IPS l.11); co-sponsors on the facility (3d).
- **Report part:** 3a or 3d, one clause.
- **Source:** Shiller 2008 L24 ch.2 (Schmoller, *Charakterbilder*, 1913, p. 57, Shiller's translation).
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche pedigree. True and apt, but it supports a rule the case already sets.

### 6. Even the harshest reading of Shiller's CAPE moves Laura's top by one $5,000 step at most (pedigree, for 3e's note)

(a) Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E for US shares read 40.71 in October 2026, higher than 98.9% of 1,750 months since 1881, and the world's was 28.54, above 91% of readings since 1980. (b) Read in the harshest way, as one over CAPE with no inflation added (Keel arithmetic), world shares would earn about 3.50% a year, just above the 3.417% that NUMBERS 8v finds is enough to state a $115,000 top, and only the US reading (about 2.46%) falls below it, which states $110,000; the ten payments and the bottom do not move at any return, and with J.P. Morgan's 2.5% inflation added both readings clear the step. (c) It breaks on horizon: CAPE speaks about ten years, and the shares' job ends at the 2031 move, three years after they are bought, where card Q6 found CAPE explains about a tenth of returns; the break teaches that the plan made the 7.5% nearly beside the point, since VT is about $29,600, some 6.6% of her $450,000 (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS).
- **Decision it serves:** the 7.5% in Table 2's note; the share of the 2028 deposit put in VT.
- **Report part:** 3e or Table 2's note, a clause at most.
- **Source:** YCharts and other sites quoting Shiller's ie_data file; Siblis Research for the world CAPE; NUMBERS 8v.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree. Every judge knows CAPE; the only new thing is how little it can move.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 3, Osaka's rice bills (rows 10, 11, 13).** A bill backed by rice in the warehouse against an "empty" one, a 1749 count of nearly four bills for every bale, and a fix that worked only when the government stood behind the paper: it explains why Laura's ink must be Treasuries, and it predicts the IPS's second reason.
2. **Idea 2, tenure and Ives (rows 5-7).** It answers the obvious judge's objection ("why so cautious with six years to wait?") with Shiller's own argument: certainty underneath is what lets artists take risks on top, which is what the IPS's closing line already says.
3. **Idea 1, the violinist's floor (rows 1-3).** The best-known plan to insure careers began with an artist, offered a floor with upside, and was never built; Caplet put the same shape under her promises instead, which shows a judge the IPS's career argument is a known gap in markets, not a team's hunch.

## Gaps

- *Macro Markets* (1993) was not read: no free copy was found in the time; its estimates of how small a share of national income trades in markets would sharpen row 4.
- *Finance and the Good Society* and ch. 8 of *The New Financial Order* were read in full-text copies found online (an archive.org upload and a university server), not in the publisher's edition; page numbers for ch. 8 come from the copy's running heads.
- Shiller's own ie_data file was not opened; the CAPE and excess-CAPE figures are secondary sites' readings of it, and they disagree on the excess yield.
- Hansmann's own writing on endowments (row 18) and the Japanese text of Nagao (1975) were not read.
- No source was found on whether any exchange lists futures on a building-cost index; China's rebar futures (row 25) were not checked at source.
- The 2008 lectures were fetched whole but read in full only for L24; the rest were read by chapter or searched by keyword (see the first bullets).
