- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "1994 was the worst bond year in decades."
  2. "A fall in a bond's price changes nothing for a holder who keeps it to maturity."
  3. "Orange County failed because it held long bonds."
  4. "The WInS price moves are noise, not performance."

- **Problem class:** a loss in market value against a loss of money. A fall in price becomes a loss only through a sale, and a sale is forced only by a claim that falls due before the bond pays (a lender, a redeeming shareholder, a debt to roll). **Fields that solved it first:** bond mathematics (a bond's price is the value today of fixed payments, so it returns to face value on its date), accounting (FASB Statement 115, in force for most banks from 1 January 1994: "positive intent and ability to hold to maturity"), and the law of repurchase agreements (a repo lender may sell the collateral on default, Bankruptcy Code s.559, SEC 1995 fn).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, dated and sized. Never "the worst ever".**
  - **The yields** (H.15 via FRED; spot points checked at FRED). The 10-year rose from 5.83% (31 Dec 1993) to 8.05% (7 Nov 1994). The 30-year rose from 6.35% to 8.16% (4 Nov). The 1-year rose from 3.63% to 7.32% (13 Dec), so short rates rose most.
  - **The Fed.** It raised its target seven times, from 3.00% to 6.00%, between 4 Feb 1994 and 1 Feb 1995. The largest step was 75bp on 15 Nov 1994.
  - **Calendar returns.** On Damodaran's 10-year Treasury return series, 1994 lost 8.04%. That was the worst year from 1928 to 1994 (the previous worst was 1969, at -5.01%). It is fifth worst of 98 years now: 1999 (-8.25%), 2013 (-9.10%), 2009 (-11.12%) and 2022 (-17.83%, about 2.2 times 1994) were worse.
  - **Peak to trough.** The New York Fed puts 1994's fall on a 10-year zero at -17.5% by November 1994. That is fifth of the fifteen largest since 1961, behind the two Volcker-era falls.
  - **What others said.** The Chicago Fed reports that the press called 1994 "the worst year for the bond market since the 1920s". Excluding 1979-82, the authors say "among the worst of the postwar period". The Richmond Fed has "$600 billion in value" lost from January to September and quotes the name investors still use: "the bond bloodbath".
  - **Funds** (Keel arithmetic on Yahoo adjusted prices). Vanguard's long-term Treasury fund lost 6.99% in 1994, intermediate 4.55%, short-term 0.64%, total bond 2.60%. In 1995 they gained 30.2%, 20.5%, 12.1% and 18.3%.
  - **Judge's note:** 1994 was the worst year for ten-year Treasuries in the 66 years before it, and 2022 was more than twice as bad. What matters for Laura is not the size of the fall but that nothing she owed fell due during it.
- **Claim 2: KEEP for what is paid; CHANGE "changes nothing".**
  - **The regulators' words.** The SEC: "If you intend to hold a bond to maturity ... The bond's price may change, but you will be paid the stated interest rate, as well as the face value of the bond, upon maturity". FINRA: for a "buy and hold to maturity" investor, "fluctuating interest rate levels don't change that".
  - **A ladder like Laura's in 1994** (Keel arithmetic on H.15, method in row 11). Ten $50,000 payments due 1 Jan 2000-2009 sit six to fifteen years ahead, as hers do from 2027. They were worth $272,118 on 31 Dec 1993 and $237,959 on 4 Nov 1994: 12.6% less, and 14.7% below their 12 Jan high. They were back to $272,118 by 4 May 1995. Treasuries bought to pay them paid $500,000 on the ten dates whatever the price did in between.
  - **A real note.** The 10-year note sold on 9 Feb 1994 (5-7/8%, due 15 Feb 2004) went for 99.590. Nine months later the Treasury's own 10-year auction cleared at 7.97%, which puts it near 86.5 (Keel arithmetic). Its terms paid $100 on 15 Feb 2004.
  - **What a fall does change, part 1.** It changes what the same money could earn elsewhere. The SEC's next sentence: "On the other hand ... you might be able to sell the bond and reinvest the proceeds into another bond that pays a higher coupon rate".
  - **Part 2.** It changes the rate at which coupons are reinvested. That rate goes up, and Laura's test counts zero anyway (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Part 3.** It changes everything for a fund with no end date. Its holder waits for rates to fall back, not for a date: Vanguard's long Treasury fund took 16 months to regain its January 1994 high.
  - **Say instead:** "holds" needs the ability to hold. FAS 115 bars the label when a bond might be sold for "liquidity needs" or "funding sources and terms" (rows 17-18).
- **Claim 3: KILL. Orange County held short notes and failed on what it owed.**
  - **Its holdings were short.** It held "four-year notes and structured notes" of US agencies (SEC 1995). Card Q3b row 3 has an average of "nearly four years", and Jorion an average duration of 2.74 years. Laura's are longer.
  - **No issuer defaulted.** "Orange County's difficulties occurred without a single default by an issuer of the structured notes" (SEC 1995, fn 22).
  - **What broke was the borrowing.** Falling collateral values "required additional commitments of securities". Then "Requests for withdrawal of deposited funds, combined with the negative interest return under the reverse repurchase agreements, apparently generated a cash-flow squeeze".
  - **The sale.** First Boston sold about $2.6bn of collateral on 6 Dec 1994, and the county sold the rest by 20 Jan 1995. Participants got 77 cents on the dollar (LAO).
  - **What would have come back.** In a report paid for by Merrill Lynch, Miller shows the unsold portfolio's paper loss shrinking from $1.64bn (1 Dec 1994) to about $0.37bn (30 Jun 1995). Jorion answers that the court found the county insolvent and that a further $1.1bn loss had a 5% chance.
  - **Where they agree.** Both say the bonds came back. They disagree only on whether the county could wait.
  - **Judge's note:** per dollar of her own money, Laura's ten Treasuries (duration 7.76 years, NUMBERS 1) are about as sensitive to rates as Orange County's leveraged pool (7.4, Jorion via Q3b row 2). One failed in 1994; the other cannot fail that way, because the danger was never the bonds' length but a lender who could ask for cash before they paid. For the store and a judge's question only (Orange County stays out of the prose).
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. "Not performance" KEEP; "noise" is the wrong word, and the report never repeats it (TNA l.15).**
  - **Not performance.** Wharton never lets WInS gains be evidence (CANON A10, C3).
  - **Not noise either.** In 1994 the price moves were news. Thorbecke finds "news about inflation" and policy uncertainty behind them. The New York Fed finds 1994's yield moves "nearly entirely due to changes in the future path of policy".
  - **The accountants' dissent.** FAS 115's own dissenters said intent to hold "disregards the best available information ... namely, its observable market price".
  - **What a price fall is.** It is the new price of the same promise. On 4 Nov 1994 the replay ladder could have been sold and the same ten payments bought back for the same money, less the dealer's spread. The loss becomes permanent only when the cash has to go somewhere else (row 13).
  - **The report's evaluation:** a WInS price shows what the bonds would fetch that day. It is a true price and the wrong measure for money already promised, because it does not change what they pay.
- **Claim 5: KEEP, as a list of who was hurt and why, never as "nobody lost".**
  - **Askin's mortgage funds (Feb-Apr 1994).** Leveraged about threefold (complaint). Margin calls of more than $131m came over 28-31 Mar, $49m was met, and Chapter 11 followed on 7 Apr.
  - **A $1-on-demand "government" money fund (Mar-Sep).** See card Q196 row 12.
  - **Orange County (Dec 1994-Jan 1995).** Repo lenders sold its collateral.
  - **Mexico's dollar-indexed tesobonos (Dec 1994-Jan 1995).** $30bn fell due in 1995 against $12.5bn of reserves. They were paid in full, about two-thirds in dollars from a package of more than $50bn of other people's money.
  - **What they share.** Each had a claim that fell due before its assets paid. None held plain Treasuries, unborrowed, to their dates.
  - **Judge's note:** what 1994 shows that 2022 does not is that a slow rise in the US Treasury market itself, spread over eleven months and led by the Fed, broke borrowers as surely as a three-day spike in gilts. The plan's protection is that nothing is owed before each bond's date, not that the fall is fast or slow.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 11 (Figure 1's note or 2.3).** "In 1994, the worst year for ten-year Treasuries since at least 1928 at the time, a ladder like this lost up to about 15% of its market value in ten months and paid every dollar on its dates." It turns "market value is a by-product" into history a judge can check, dated and labelled Keel arithmetic, never a plan figure.
2. **Row 13 (2.3 or 3e, one sentence).** A price fall is the new price of the same promise. The loss is made only when cash must go elsewhere, so for promised money the only prices that matter are those on the days Laura buys. It is the plain reason under "no fall in prices can make her sell".
3. **Row 4 with row 5 (3e or Figure 1's note, if 1994 is named).** "Worst in decades" is true and dated: -8.04%, the worst year from 1928 to 1994. Never "the worst ever": 2022 was about 2.2 times larger. A judge who remembers 1994 will check the claim.
4. **Row 10 (2.2).** Why term funds and T-bonds rather than a Treasury fund with no end. In 1994 the long fund's holder waited 16 months for rates to fall back, while a held bond only waits for its date. It shows the team chose dated holdings for a reason, with CANON C2's NAV caveat in the same breath.
5. **Row 19 with row 17 (evaluating TNA l.15, 2.2 or 3e).** The accounting rule that lets a holder ignore price moves began on 1 January 1994. Its own dissenters called the price "the best available information". So the report evaluates "price noise" rather than repeating it: the price is true, and it is the wrong measure for money already promised.

For a judge's question, not prose: row 29 (Laura's rate exposure per own dollar matches Orange County's leveraged pool, and she cannot fail its way) and row 25 (in the same county, notes held to their dates were repaid in full while the pool that had to sell returned 77 cents).
