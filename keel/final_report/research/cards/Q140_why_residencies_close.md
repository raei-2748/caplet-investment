- Problem class: organisational mortality (why organisations die). The field that met it first is organisational ecology in sociology: the "liabilities of the youth and small size of organizations" (as Hager 2000 sums it up) and the "liability of adolescence" (Hager, Galaskiewicz and Larson 2004). Nonprofit accounting followed with Tuckman and Chang's (1991) four warning ratios. Stinchcombe (1965), the usual first citation, was not read at source in this run.

## Verdicts in one screen

**Claim 1. CHANGE: income that can stop predicts closure better than a thin surplus does.**
- Hager (2001) studied 7,266 US arts nonprofits. Low equity predicted exit only for museums, theatres and music. Relying on one main income source predicted it for visual arts, theatre, music and general performing arts. No measure predicted it for dance or for performing arts centres. Only for theatres did all four ratios predict exit. The 14.1% who "exited" had only stopped filing tax returns: Hager says they "might be more properly labeled missing".
- Lu, Shon and Zhang (2020) followed 272,729 US charities from 2005 to 2015, of which 9,663 (3.5%) dissolved. Having several sources of income lowered the risk of dissolving by about 40% (hazard ratio about 0.6). The operating margin had no effect at all (hazard ratio 1.0000).
- Green et al. (2021) compared UK charities that closed with ones that did not. Closed charities took 60% of their income from grants, against under 25% for survivors. Among the closed, 85% of large and 60% of small charities had six months of cover or less, against 45% of survivors. In their words: "Greater vulnerability is associated with dependence on grant funding, not overall concentration."
- The truest form of the claim: places close when money that can be taken away is taken away and there are few months of cover behind it. A thin surplus is not the warning sign.
- Judge's note: in the largest US study, a nonprofit's yearly surplus told nothing about whether it would dissolve; what mattered was whether its income could stop all at once. Laura's ten payments are owned in Treasuries before the doors open, so nobody can withdraw them.

**Claim 2. CHANGE: the first decade is the dangerous one, but nobody counts residency deaths. The nearest counts name money first, then the site, then the founder.**
- No survey read counts residency closures by cause. The Alliance of Artists Communities' 2020 survey contradicts itself (row 30). Res Artis's 9% "forced to close indefinitely" is already in the store as Q9b-017.
- The nearest count is museums. Mapping Museums (2025) found 524 UK museums that certainly closed between 2000 and 2025, "far more likely to close before they are ten years old than at any other point". In its words, "Finance was most cited as the trigger for closure", meaning "funding cuts and rising costs, but only a small number of insolvencies". Loss of premises was "a recurrent issue". Retirement and death were "the main factor for the closure of private museums".
- In the Twin Cities panel (Hager 2000), 37 of 229 nonprofits closed. Financial difficulties came first, "too small" second and the loss of a founding director third.
- In Taiwan the cause was different. Grass Mountain, the gap Q9c-023 left open, ended when the city let the burned and repaired site to a marketing company on a four-year contract (rows 23-25). That fits Q9c's thesis that losing the site ended most of the Taiwanese residencies found.
- Judge's note: the commonest death in the counted data is a funder walking away, not a building falling down or a bankruptcy. Laura's plan owns the walk-away risk out of existence for ten years.

**Claim 3. KEEP, sharpened: none of the 29 creator- and founder-run places in Q100 and Q118 held running money before opening, and the places that last placed property or an estate first.**
- Bogliasco: the family villa was given irrevocably, the foundation was formed in 1991 and the first Fellows came in 1996.
- Wurlitzer: the founder kept the place funded herself from 1954.
- Joan Mitchell: ten years on "modest resources" before the art came (store Q101-024).
- None of them owned the running years as dated bonds. Owned money did not save Rauschenberg's Captiva residency either; storms and upkeep did that (store Q101-021).
- Judge's note: Petronio bought the building first and hoped grants would run it; it closed in five years when "Grants that he was counting on didn't come through". Rauschenberg had the running money and closed over its site. Laura's plan owns the running years first and leaves the building to the facility contribution ("The building is a different matter", IPS l.15).

**Claim 4. CHANGE: counts exist only for museums (reasons ranked, not counted in the text read) and one city panel. The cases show four losses.**
- A grant ends:
  - Auchindrain: Historic Environment Scotland had paid about 90% of costs for three years, then stopped. The museum later reopened.
  - Petronio's grants did not come through.
  - BluSeed's state grant dwindled.
  - Japan cut its residency budget 60% "without prior notice".
- The founder leaves: the Twin Cities divorce case; private museums closing on retirement or death.
- The site goes: Grass Mountain re-let, Taipei Artist Village (Q9c), Rauschenberg.
- Upkeep grows: BluSeed's building costs; Wurlitzer pausing for repairs in 2026.
- The first and third are what owning the ten payments answers. The second and fourth belong to the facility contribution and the co-sponsors.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 27 (3.0, 3a).** "Finance was most cited ... but only a small number of insolvencies." Places close because money stops, not because they go bust. That is the death Laura's ten owned payments answer, and it gives the report its reason for buying the running years first.
2. **Rows 33 and 36 together (3a, 3d).** Petronio owned the building and hoped for grants; it closed in five years when "Grants that he was counting on didn't come through". Rauschenberg had the money and closed over its site. Laura's plan does the reverse of Petronio and says the building "is a different matter".
3. **Row 9 (3a).** In 272,729 US charities, the operating margin had no effect on dissolving (hazard ratio 1.0000), while income that could not all stop cut the risk by about 40%. A judge who thinks a healthy surplus is what keeps a residency alive has it backwards.
4. **Rows 12 and 14 together (3a, 3d, 3e).** Closed charities had six months of cover or less; Laura's residency starts with 120 months of its running money owned. The honest limit is Green et al.'s: "high but stable grant income" fails when it ends, so 2043 is the co-sponsors' and the residency's to plan.
5. **Rows 24 and 25 (3d, Taiwan).** These fill the gap Q9c-023 left open: Grass Mountain ended when the city let the site to a marketing company, which now keeps "some business use to pay for upkeep". In Taiwan the site, not the money, ended most of the public residencies found (Q9c), which is why it matters that the IPS keeps the building apart.
