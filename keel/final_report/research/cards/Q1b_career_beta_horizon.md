## Verdict on tonight's claim

Claim: "Over the horizon that matters for the plan (the 2028 deposit and the years to 2033), Laura's income and world
share prices tend to fall together, even though year-to-year book sales have their own cycles."

**CHANGE. Keep the core; split the horizon; drop "world" and "together".**

1. **The 2028 deposit (a one-year window): weak in the same year, stronger a year later.** In US data the same-year
   correlation of aggregate earnings growth with share returns is 0.18 (0.25 for college graduates); with the previous
   year's share return it is 0.37 to 0.39 (row 1). Household estimates of the same-year link are about zero (row 3);
   the cointegration model sets it to zero on purpose (row 5); the worker-betas paper measures share betas against the
   previous year's return by design (row 7). So the deposit is exposed mainly to the share markets of 2026 and 2027,
   years in which Caplet's plan owns no shares (ladder bought January 2027; VT bought only with the 2028 deposit,
   IPS l.13-15). "Fall together" therefore cannot mean a double loss on shares inside the portfolio. It means a smaller
   deposit arriving while falling yields make the ladder dearer (Q1 row 48; CANON D8, D9). Row 26.
2. **The years to 2033 (five to six years): the claim is strongest here, but it is not about the payments.** After
   January 2028 her income never reaches the portfolio (case p.2). It reaches the facility: the case lists "continued
   business income" among the sources for any remaining facility cost (case p.3). Over five to seven years, the one-year
   lag and cointegration (effects show over about 1/kappa = 5 to 20 years, 6.7 in the baseline, row 5) make a joint bad
   state in 2031-33 (a low VT ceiling and thinner business income at the same time) more likely than chance. That joint
   state is what HALFKEPT and MOVE2031 are built for. Row 27.
3. **At the very top of the income ladder the link is strong and fast; whether Laura is there is unknown.** Top
   earners' income growth correlates above 0.6 (in absolute value) with GDP growth, unemployment and S&P 500 returns
   (row 10); share-return betas at the 99.9th percentile are 0.28 to 0.47 against 0.03 to 0.15 at the median (row 7);
   top-1% incomes have been 2.4 times as cyclical as average since the early 1980s, and the authors' explanation is
   "superstar" scale (row 12).
4. **Her own cycle is large and sometimes runs against shares.** US independent artists, writers and performers: real
   value added -25.0% in 2020 (S&P +18.02%) and +18.4% in 2022 (S&P -18.04%) (row 22). Australian writers' mean creative
   income roughly halved between 2000-01 and 2007-08 while all artists' incomes stayed "relatively stable" (row 24).
   Q12 row 18: graphic novels about -31% over 2023-24 in a bull market.
5. **"World share prices" is not what the evidence measures.** Every labour-income study here uses US shares; VT is 62.4%
   US (row 20); and the link between human capital and listed shares may be "extremely small" (row 19).

**The refined form that survives:** a top earner's income tends to fall in the same downturns as shares, usually a year
or more after them, and it also has large swings of its own. Both point to the same design (promised money in
Treasuries, only unpromised money in shares), but for different reasons: the first because shares would add to her bad
years; the second because the ten payments need certainty whatever shrinks her income. The certainty requirement, not
the correlation, is what carries the ladder (rows 13, 15).

**Wording guidance for the report (phrases, not prose):**
- Use: "in the same downturns, usually a year or more later"; "her income also has cycles of its own"; "the 2028 deposit
  is the one place her career touches the portfolio"; "share markets" with "US evidence" named; for the top-earner
  research, a conditional ("if her earnings are near the top, studies of top earners find ...").
- Avoid: "her income and share prices fall together" or "at the same time" (the same-year link is weak: rows 1, 3, 4, 5,
  13); "her career behaves like the stock market" or "her career is a share" (an average college graduate's career is
  under 20% stock-like, row 2; the defensible form is "a large holding she cannot sell, which tends to shrink in bad
  years"); "world share prices" as a measured link (rows 19, 20); "top earner" stated as a fact about Laura (rows 7,
  10); any link between her income after January 2028 and the ten payments (case p.2); "VT hedges her career" or "the
  Treasuries hedge her income" (the plan avoids adding to her risk; it does not offset it).
- The IPS's own words "during that same period" (l.9; CANON B4) are supported if read as "in the same downturn"; read
  as "in the same months", they are not (rows 1, 21). Note it honestly; the IPS is frozen.

## (b) Cards: the six strongest ideas

### Card 1. Shares first, pay a year later
- **What is it?** In US data, the aggregate part of earnings growth correlates about 0.18 with the same year's share
  return and about 0.37 with the previous year's (Huggett & Kaplan, Table 3, 1967-2008). Summaries of the field put the
  same-year link at 0.06 to 0.1 and the lagged link at 0.32 to 0.5. The worker-betas paper measures betas against last
  year's return by design. Rows 1, 4, 7.
- **Did it work?** It fits the occupation data: writers' unemployment rose through 2009 (7.9% for the year, 10.0% in Q4)
  while the S&P rose 25.94%, and artists' unemployment after the 2001 recession peaked in 2003 (row 21).
- **How Laura's case is like it:** the deposit at the start of 2028 is funded by earnings that come before it (case
  p.2); on these numbers they respond most to the share market a year earlier.
- **How it is not:** these are employees' aggregate earnings; her income is business income, which may move faster
  (rows 3, 14); her income's timing is not known.
- **What the team takes:** the word "later" in the IPS is the version the data support. The deposit's market year is
  mostly 2026 and 2027, when the plan owns no shares, so the overlap cannot cause a double loss on shares; it can only
  shrink the deposit while the ladder gets dearer (row 26).

### Card 2. The value of a career against this year's cheques
- **What is it?** A career's value can move with shares even when this year's pay does not. The cointegration model
  sets the same-year link to zero yet finds a correlation of about 50% between returns on human capital and share
  returns for a young worker, because wages and shares cannot drift apart for ever. Its effects show over about 1/kappa
  years: 20, 6.7 or 5 for kappa of 0.05, 0.15 or 0.20 (Keel arithmetic on the paper's rule). Rows 5, 6.
- **Did it work?** It explains why the young hold few shares. But the evidence rests on pre-war data, and Huggett &
  Kaplan find a much smaller stock part: under 20% of a college graduate's career (row 2).
- **How Laura's case is like it:** early career (Keel inference: graduated 2018, case p.1); the window from 2027 to 2033
  is about one baseline time frame.
- **How it is not:** the portfolio sees her career only through one deposit, a one-year window where the model gives
  no link at all.
- **What the team takes:** the IPS's "a large holding of shares" is a claim about what her career is worth, which holds
  best over five to seven years; it is not a claim about this year's cheques. CLASH (partly) noted: the typical
  college-educated career is mostly a bond; Caplet must show why Laura is not typical (rows 3, 12, 14).

### Card 3. The top of the ladder is different
- **What is it?** Earnings risk to the cycle is U-shaped in income and jumps at the very top. Share-return betas: 0.08
  at the median, 0.11 at the 99th percentile, 0.29 at the 99.9th for women aged 26-35 (0.10, 0.21 and 0.47 for men
  36-45). Top earners' income growth correlates above 0.6 with GDP, unemployment and the S&P 500. Top-1% incomes have
  been 2.4 times as cyclical as average since the early 1980s. Rows 7, 8, 10, 12.
- **Did it work?** It is now the normal pattern: in the last two recessions the highest earners "suffered the most"; in
  2008 the top 1% fell about twice as much as the median (row 11). The authors' reason is superstar scale: the most
  talented now sell to the whole market, so they gain most in booms and lose most in busts.
- **How Laura's case is like it:** a bestselling author whose books are "read in classrooms around the world" (case
  p.1) is the type case of scale.
- **How it is not:** her income percentile is not known; the data are employees' earnings (self-employment excluded in
  the worker-betas paper) and, in two of the studies, men only. Q1 row 17: top earners in services are less cyclical
  than average.
- **What the team takes:** a conditional, not a fact: if her earnings are near the very top, the textbook "weak link"
  does not apply to her. Correction noted: Q1's 3.70 is for men aged 36-45; for women aged 26-35 the 99.9th-percentile
  GDP beta is 2.26 (row 8).

### Card 4. The broad market is a poor hedge for ordinary jobs, yet a small link still matters
- **What is it?** For ten ordinary occupations, no significant link with aggregate equity; across studies, correlations
  of -0.1 to 0.2. But "a correlation of 0.2 is enough to wipe out half the benefits of trading risky financial assets".
  Rows 13, 18.
- **Did it work?** Households behave as if correlation is the test: a one standard deviation fall in the
  income-return correlation raises the chance of holding shares by about 11 points, and people with high but
  uncorrelated income risk are about 24 points more likely to own shares (row 15).
- **How Laura's case is like it:** her money is business income, which the literature describes as positively linked
  to shares (rows 3, 14).
- **How it is not:** no study here measures a writer's or artist's income against shares, and none uses a world index;
  VT is 62.4% US, and whether careers move with even the home market is disputed (rows 19, 20).
- **What the team takes:** the honest chain for the report is: the link for most jobs is small; it is larger for
  business income, at the top, and with a lag; and even 0.2 halves the gain from shares. CLASH (partly) noted: for an
  income that swings on its own, theory and evidence say hold more shares, not fewer, so the certainty requirement
  (case p.3), not the hedge, must carry the Treasury ladder.

### Card 5. Her own weather
- **What is it?** Writers' and artists' incomes swing for reasons unrelated to shares. BEA: independent artists,
  writers and performers -25.0% in 2020 (shares +18.02%), +18.4% in 2022 (shares -18.04%). Australian writers' mean
  creative income about halved from 2000-01 to 2007-08 while the artist economy around them was stable, then rose about
  46%. "A substantial income in one year ... may be followed by a year or two of financial drought". Rows 22, 24.
- **Did it work?** It matches Q12's genre finding (graphic novels about -31% over 2023-24 in a bull market) and the
  advance-then-drought shape of author pay.
- **How Laura's case is like it:** her deposit comes from advances, speaking, licensing and ventures (case p.2), all
  lumpy.
- **How it is not:** 2020 was a lockdown; the BEA industry is mostly performers; the Australian figures are averages
  across many writers.
- **What the team takes:** say "her income also has cycles of its own" alongside the downturn link. Own-cycle risk
  alone trims shares only a little (5 points for a doubling of income risk, row 16), so it supports the design through
  the certainty requirement, not as a hedge argument.

### Card 6. Where the overlap lands in this plan: 2027, and 2031-33
- **What is it?** The plan meets Laura's career in two places. In 2027, through the deposit (one year, lagged link, no
  shares held). In 2031-33, through the facility: VT sets the top of the band, half of VT moves to Treasuries before
  2031, and the case lists "continued business income" among the sources for any remaining facility cost (case p.3).
  Rows 26, 27.
- **Did it work?** Nothing to test yet; it is a mapping of the evidence onto the case's dates.
- **How Laura's case is like it:** over five to seven years the lag and cointegration make a weak VT and a thinner
  business income more likely together than apart (rows 1, 5).
- **How it is not:** co-sponsors may cover the rest of the facility, so her business income may not be needed; whether
  Taiwan co-sponsors' budgets fall with shares is not researched.
- **What the team takes:** the claim "over the horizon to 2033 her income and shares tend to fall together" is true
  where the plan already protects her (a fixed floor, a range not a point, half of VT unpromised, the other half locked
  before 2031) and is not about the ten payments at all. This answers the judge who asks "why hedge a career you never
  pay into the portfolio after 2028?".

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **The deposit's share-market risk sits in a year when Laura owns no shares.** Earnings respond about twice as much to
   last year's share market as to this year's (0.37 against 0.18 in US data, row 1). The January 2028 deposit is earned
   in 2027, so it depends most on the 2026 market (VT +12.86% to 30 Sep) and partly on 2027's, while the plan holds
   only Treasuries. The "double hit" the textbook fears cannot happen inside the portfolio in 2027; it can only shrink
   the deposit while the ladder gets dearer (row 26).
2. **The real "fall together" is in 2031-33, and it lands on the building, not the payments.** The case names
   "continued business income" as a source for the rest of the facility (case p.3). A share slump in 2029-31 tends to
   reach her income a year or more later, in the same window that VT sets her ceiling. That is exactly where the plan
   keeps half of VT unpromised and locks the other half before 2031 (row 27).
3. **A correlation of only 0.2 halves the gain from holding shares** (Davis & Willen, row 13). And the reverse holds:
   people whose income is risky but uncorrelated hold more shares, not fewer (row 15). So the honest reason for the
   ladder is the case's demand for certainty; the career link is the reason not to add shares beyond what is unpromised.
4. **At the top, pay has become a share-like asset since the 1980s.** Top-1% incomes are 2.4 times as cyclical as
   average, and top earners' income growth correlates above 0.6 with S&P 500 returns; the reason is superstar scale
   (rows 10, 12). A bestselling author is the type case, if her earnings are near the top.
5. **The independent-artist economy moved against shares in both recent tests:** -25.0% in 2020 when shares rose 18%,
   +18.4% in 2022 when shares fell 18% (row 22). Her career is not an index fund; it is a single, hit-driven holding
   with a delayed link to bad years.
