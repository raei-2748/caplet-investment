## Verdicts in one screen

1. **D1, career as shares: the reply PARTLY WINS.** The textbook says most careers are bond-like and push a young
   earner toward MORE shares (Campbell and Viceira; Ibbotson et al.; Cocco et al.), and the measured link between pay
   and share prices is small (Davis and Willen). The same authors reverse the advice for share-like, commission-type
   careers, which is nearer Laura's (advances, fees, licences). But the honest measure is modest (Q1c, Q33), so the
   career cannot by itself carry so few shares. What carries the Treasuries is the case's certainty standard for fixed
   dollar payments (l.88-92); the career explains why no promised dollar sits in shares and why the 2028 deposit is the
   one place her income meets the plan. NUMBERS 5 shows the payments survive that risk.
2. **D2, the ladder: the reply WINS.** "Six to fifteen years is long enough for shares" (Siegel) fails the case's test
   on the critic's own figures (shares lost to bonds over about one five-year period in five since 1926) and on the
   modern literature (Samuelson; Bodie; Pástor and Stambaugh; Anarkulova et al.). "Cash matching is the dearest way"
   (Leibowitz; Fabozzi et al.; Ryan) is right about cost and wrong about the standard: the extra Treasuries buy what
   immunisation cannot, payments sufficient if interest earns nothing (NUMBERS 2), at a named price. "Nominal bonds
   are not riskless" (Campbell and Viceira) misses a nominal promise (l.90); inflation-indexed bonds are not permitted.
3. **D3, the floor and VT: the reply WINS on timing and PARTLY WINS on the instruments.** Locking the floor in 2028
   costs about $7,400 in the middle (NUMBERS 8c), and that is the price of a bottom that cannot fall before the
   meetings (case l.114). The iBond fund critique (no promised amount; cash at the end; discounts in March 2020) is
   answered for a holder to the end and bounded by the range rule's 0.5% (NUMBERS 3), but a friction above 0.5% or a
   board action (Q36) stays a residual risk. The world-fund critique (currency, cap weights) cannot reach any promise
   (deletion test, NUMBERS 4).
4. **D4, half kept and the 2031 move: the reply PARTLY WINS.** "Money is fungible" (Thaler) fails: the labels are the
   case's own and one of them is a public promise; the split barely changes the total (NUMBERS 8o). "A glide path loses
   wealth" (Estrada; Arnott) fails: the move buys an owned figure for about $900 of median wealth (Keel arithmetic on 8o).
   "A reserve in shares shrinks when needed" (Froot, Scharfstein and Stein) partly wins: her flexibility is about a
   third smaller when shares fell (NUMBERS 8f), cushioned by the excess, which is Treasury money.
5. **D5, two reasons only: the reply PARTLY WINS.** "An IPS is reviewed at least yearly" (CFA Institute) and "rules
   need escape clauses" (Lohmann) are met only if the report shows that watching is not changing (Table 3, Q26) and
   that a smaller deposit or a fund event is absorbed by the plan as written (NUMBERS 5; Q36). "Rebalance to a constant
   mix" (Merton) loses to Perold and Sharpe's own "neither strategy dominates".

**The two the report must raise and answer itself:** (1) "Six to fifteen years is long enough for shares; Treasuries
throw away the equity premium" (C4); (2) "A young person's career is bond-like, so she should hold more shares, not
fewer" (C1). Both attack the IPS's own opening (pitch l.3, l.9), both are a thoughtful judge's first intuition, and both
answers teach something (section c).

### D1. Laura's career as a holding of shares she cannot sell

**C1. "A young person's career is like a big bond, so she should hold more shares, not fewer."**
- Who and where: Campbell and Viceira (2002; April 2001 draft): "For most investors, human wealth is sufficiently stable
  in value to tilt financial portfolios towards greater holdings of risky assets." Ibbotson et al. (2007): "a typical
  young investor would be well advised to hold an all-stock investment portfolio (perhaps even with leverage)". Cocco,
  Gomes and Maenhout (2005): labour income "substitutes for riskless asset holdings".
- Strength against Laura: HIGH. It is the textbook default, and the pitch says the opposite in its first line.
- Best honest reply:
  - The same authors reverse their advice for share-like careers. Campbell and Viceira: "Only if labor income shocks
    are sufficiently positively correlated with risky asset returns will an investor with labor income hold a safer
    portfolio". Ibbotson et al.: careers "highly correlated with the stock market or the economy (e.g., stockbrokers,
    commissioned sales people)" call for "more bonds", and "For young investors with equitylike human capital, the
    financial assets should be invested predominantly in fixed-income assets." Laura's pay comes as advances, speaking
    fees and licences (case l.43-45; IPS l.9), nearer the commission case. Q1 and Q1b found authors' income fell after
    2008 (2009 advances cut), on a lag; Q1c found it did not in 2020 (CANON B4 is PARTLY).
  - Concede the size. Q1c (Huggett and Kaplan): the share-like part of a college worker's career is under a fifth. Q33
    (Heaton and Lucas): business owners still keep about 0.6 to 0.7 of their liquid assets in shares. So the career
    alone supports "fewer shares than the default", not "almost none".
  - What carries the Treasuries is the case: fixed $50,000 payments, "not adjusted for inflation", funded "with a high
    degree of certainty", with no outside money (l.88-92). The IPS states that second reason itself (l.11: "it's fixed
    and non-negotiable"). The career says why no promised dollar sits in shares; certainty says why so many dollars are
    promised. VT is $29,829 of the $450,000 deposited, 6.6% at cost (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4).
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS.** The critique is right that the career cannot carry the bond share alone; the reply
  wins once the certainty standard is put beside it. The report should show both causes and say which does the work.

**C2. "Pay and share prices barely move together, so 'counterbalance' over-claims, and ignoring income risk costs
little."**
- Who and where: Davis and Willen (2013): "Aggregate equity returns are uncorrelated with the occupation-level income
  innovations." Ibbotson et al. (2007), reporting Davis and Willen (2000): a correlation of "–0.2 to 0.1", so "the
  typical investor need not worry". Cocco et al. (2005): ignoring income risk costs "an order of magnitude smaller,
  except when we allow for a disastrous labor income shock".
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM. The data are for occupations and salaried workers; Davis and Willen themselves note
  Heaton and Lucas (2000) on the self-employed, and Q1b found the same-year link weak (0.18; 0.25 for graduates) but a
  year's lag stronger (0.37-0.39).
- Best honest reply: the plan does not rest on the size of the link. Her income meets the portfolio in one place, the
  2028 deposit (case l.43-45, l.63-65). The payments stay full at forward rates unless the deposit falls by more than
  98.4%, and stay sufficient even if cash waiting earns nothing unless it falls by more than 74.5% (56.4% after a
  1-point fall in yields) (NUMBERS 5). A missed deposit leaves the 2033 payment $3,016 short and is one of the two
  reasons (NUMBERS 5). The plan works whether the link is zero or 0.4.
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS.** It wins on the payments. Concede that "definitely not a contributor" (l.9) is a
  direction; never claim the portfolio offsets her income.

**C3. "Someone who can work more after a bad market has insurance, so she should take more investment risk."**
- Who and where: Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992): "labor supply flexibility creates a kind of insurance against
  adverse investment outcomes"; "The ability to vary labor supply ex post induces the individual to assume greater risks
  in his investment portfolio ex ante."
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM-LOW. An author-entrepreneur can plausibly take on more work.
- Best honest reply: the case shuts that door for the payments: no additions after the 2028 deposit (l.63-65) and no
  reliance on outside money (l.91-92), so extra work after a 2030 crash cannot reach the ten. It can reach the building:
  "continued business income" is a named source for the rest of the facility cost (l.86). That is the part of the plan
  that holds shares (VT sets the top; her flexibility stays in shares). BMS also show the result can reverse when the
  wage itself is very risky.
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** for the payments; the critique is already met on the facility side.

### D2. The ten payments bought in January 2027 and held to the end

**C4. "Six to fifteen years is long enough for shares; putting $300,000 into Treasuries throws away the equity
premium."**
- Who and where: Siegel (1992): stocks beat short-term bonds "88.8 per cent of the time over 30-year horizons"; since
  1871, over 20 years or more, they "underperformed short-term assets only once and have outperformed long-term bonds 95
  per cent of the time".
- Strength against Laura: HIGH as intuition, and the IPS invites it (l.9: "6 years to wait would usually own shares").
- Best honest reply:
  - The critic's own figures fail the case's test. Siegel: "Even with holding periods as short as five years, stocks
    have outperformed long and short-term bonds by a four-to-one margin since 1926 and a three-to-one margin since
    1872." That is about one five-year period in five lost (one in four since 1872) (Keel arithmetic); the first payment
    is six years away. "Usually" is not "a high degree of certainty" (l.91).
  - Samuelson (1963): a person who would refuse one favourable bet cannot rationally accept a long run of them; risk is
    cut "by subdividing risks among more people", not by adding years. Laura cannot subdivide: one person, ten fixed
    payments, no outside money.
  - Bodie (1995): insuring shares against earning less than the risk-free rate costs more the longer the horizon (about
    18% of the money at 5 years, 25% at 10, 35% at 20, at 20% volatility, in his restated table), and "For guarantors
    of money-fixed annuities" the long-horizon argument "is unambiguously wrong". The ten are a money-fixed annuity.
  - Pástor and Stambaugh (2012): "stocks are substantially more volatile over long horizons from an investor's
    perspective". Anarkulova, Cederburg and O'Doherty (2022): across 39 developed markets, "a 12% chance" of losing to
    inflation over 30 years; US history is the lucky sample.
  - The plan's own record: the team's first plan (about three-quarters shares) missed at least one payment in 3.2% of
    model runs, 13.7% with a $75,000 deposit and 40.8% with none; buying the payments first never missed
    (FACTS_PLAIN; 27 Sep model on an older basis, not in the register; CANON A3 is the team's own turn).
  - The cost, named: shares probably earn more. For the facility money, 8n shows more shares leave Laura more in all in
    about 63 of 100 futures (69 and 71 on history), by a median $4,000 to $12,000, while the contribution falls under
    $95,000 in 26 to 79 of 100 against 3, and its lowest since 1871 falls to $70,000-$10,000 against $100,000.
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** for the payments. The report concedes the premium and says what it buys instead.

**C5. "Buying each payment bond by bond is the dearest way to fund known payments, and your ladder isn't even
matched."**
- Who and where: Leibowitz (1986b): immunization "offers greater flexibility than simpler forms of dedication, such as
  cash matching". Fabozzi, Tong and Zhu (1990): immunization "often requires a lower initial cost", and symmetric cash
  matching costs less "than either". Ryan (2013): dedication is "designed to match future values, not present values",
  coupon bonds cannot match for certain "because of reinvestment risk", and "Dedication thus gave way to immunization".
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM-HIGH for a fixed-income judge. The instruments' duration is 7.76 years against the
  payments' 9.94 (NUMBERS 1); the ten alone are $54,595 short if interest earns nothing and short in 55.6% of history
  windows (NUMBERS 2). The fix costs money: $336,656 in 1 Jan 2027 money against the payments' $301,544, $35,112 or
  11.6% more (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 1-2), and it moves the central range from $130,000-$155,000 (no margin) to
  $95,000-$115,000 (NUMBERS 4, "What certainty costs").
- Best honest reply:
  - The critics are right about the ten alone, which is why the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit exists: the top-up
    ($2,388) completes the ten, and the extra Treasuries ($35,894) make them sufficient even if cash waiting earns
    nothing, after the fee: short $0, in 0 of 585 history windows (NUMBERS 2). That is a stricter standard than
    immunisation, which is sized at today's rates.
  - Immunisation's own author names its weakness: it "requires portfolio changes over time and is susceptible to
    certain sequences of market movements" (Leibowitz, 1986b). Rebalancing the bonds as rates move contradicts the hold
    rule (A8), and a cash-matched book needs no rebalancing (Q16). Symmetric matching lets later bonds pay earlier
    payments, which the plan rules out.
  - The price is the case's standard (l.91), named in the register: $33,150 of floor payout moved to the payments'
    safety against no margin (NUMBERS 4).
- Verdict: **the reply WINS, at a stated price.** The report shows the price beside the standard.

**C6. "Nominal Treasuries are not safe for a long-term investor; inflation can eat them."**
- Who and where: Campbell and Viceira (2001): "Long-term inflation-indexed bonds are most suitable for this purpose, but
  nominal bonds may also be used if inflation risk is low"; (2002 draft): "In the presence of significant inflation risk
  ... nominal bonds are risky assets for long-term investors".
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM. The case asks for "the effect of inflation on portfolio projections and facility
  costs" (l.147), so a judge will look for it.
- Best honest reply: the promise is in dollars: "each payment is a fixed $50,000 and is not adjusted for inflation"
  (l.90). Against a dollar promise, a dollar Treasury held to the end is the matching asset; Campbell and Viceira's
  riskless asset is defined for real spending, which the case does not ask the portfolio to fund. Their own draft adds
  that "nominal bonds may be adequate substitutes ... if inflation risk is modest". Inflation-indexed Treasuries are
  not permitted for either contribution (Wharton Week 4 email; SMApply FAQ). Inflation decides what the payments buy,
  not whether they are paid: at 2.4% the 2042 payment is worth $35,032 in 1 Jan 2027 dollars, about 70% of today's;
  at 3.5%, $29,845; the 10-year breakeven on 6 Nov was 2.23% (NUMBERS 8d).
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** for funding the payments. The real erosion is a limit for 3e, carried by the
  residency's budget, never by the payments.

### D3. The late-2032 Treasury fund as the floor, and VT for the rest

**C7. "Locking the floor in January 2028 throws away three years of upside; wait, or manage above a safety net."**
- Who and where: Leibowitz and Weinberger (1982): contingent immunization pursues "active bond management within a
  framework that provides a minimum return" and "enters the immunization mode only when absolutely necessary"; Part II
  (1983): it "seems to offer the best of both worlds". Perold and Sharpe (1988): constant-proportion and option-based
  rules have "better downside protection and better upside potential than buy-and-hold" when markets trend.
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM. NUMBERS 8c builds the strongest rival on Caplet's own design (floor bought in 2031):
  it raises the middle by about $7,400, and FINAL ends behind it in 59.8% of house paths and 68.4% of history windows.
- Best honest reply:
  - The case prices a broken promise above a missed upside: overpromising "could damage her credibility" (l.114). The
    rival walks into the 2031 meetings with less than $95,000 in 15.8% of house paths and 9.1% of history windows, and
    from a July 1929 start with about $23,000; FINAL's lowest is $106,683 (NUMBERS 8c).
  - The dynamic rules' authors say they "do worse in relatively trendless, volatile markets", that "neither strategy
    dominates", and that "Only buy-and-hold strategies can be followed by all investors" (Perold and Sharpe). Contingent
    immunisation needs someone to trade at the worst moment; the plan is buy-and-hold in their terms (Q3a), with one
    dated move.
  - Lohmann's trade (Q7 row 5): credibility bought with flexibility, and 8c prices it.
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** on the case's own test. The price (about $7,400 in the middle; behind in about six futures
  in ten) is stated, not hidden.

**C8. "Defined-maturity bond ETFs are not bonds: they promise no amount, hold cash at the end, and can trade far below
their value in a panic."**
- Who and where: BlackRock's iBonds case study (2025): "an exact yield cannot be guaranteed"; the last months are held
  in cash equivalents; "As monthly income distributions increase, end-date payouts tend to decrease". The prospectus:
  the funds "do not seek to return any predetermined amount" (CANON C2). ICI (2021): in March 2020 "Even government
  bond ETFs ... had discounts in excess of 100 basis points on March 11 and March 12". Haddad, Moreira and Muir (2020):
  ETF discounts to net asset value were larger "for safer bonds", a Treasury ETF among them.
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM-HIGH for a fixed-income judge. Five of the ten rungs (IBTM to IBTR) and the floor are
  iBond funds, and the IPS says "pay what they say" (l.13) and "without doubt" (l.15) (CANON C2, C5).
- Best honest reply:
  - A discount hurts only a seller. Every fund is held to its end (A8) and is paid its net asset value at termination,
    not a market price; ICI reads the 2020 discounts as price discovery. The three iBond Treasury funds whose endings
    were read at the source paid net asset value 10 to 14 days before 1 January (NUMBERS 8h).
  - The cash wait costs up to about 2% of a rung if cash earns nothing (NUMBERS 3), and the extra Treasuries' 0% test
    already counts every waiting dollar as earning nothing (NUMBERS 2).
  - The floor: the range rule states each end at what the fund's holdings pay if no more interest is earned, less 0.5%
    (4.312% under the modelled payout centrally); both ends are owned in every house path, history window and joint
    window against frictions up to 0.5% of value (NUMBERS 3).
  - What stays: a friction of 1% would leave the top not owned in 12.0% of house paths (NUMBERS 3); the fund's board may
    end, merge or extend a fund without a vote (Q36; Table 3 row 4); a fund swap is paid from the reserve, never the
    shares.
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS.** The report says "close to the value of the Treasuries they hold" (TNA l.15), never
  "guaranteed", and shows the 0.5% and Table 3's watch.

**C9. "One cap-weighted world fund is the wrong share fund for a Taiwan building: wrong currency, heaviest in whatever
is dearest."**
- Who and where: Adler and Dumas (1983), as surveyed by Coeurdacier and Rey (2013): real exchange-rate risk "leads to
  equity home bias if local equities have higher returns (than abroad) when local prices are higher". Arnott, Hsu and
  Moore (2005): cap weighting "will overweight all currently overpriced stocks"; their fundamental indexes beat cap
  weights by 215 basis points a year, 1962-2004.
- Strength against Laura: LOW-MEDIUM.
- Best honest reply:
  - The currency half fails in the literature it comes from: "it is doubtful that the hedging of real exchange rate risk
    can account empirically for the equity home bias"; Cooper and Kaplanis reject the needed correlation "for all
    countries considered" (both Coeurdacier and Rey); van Wincoop and Warnock find the implied home bias "close to
    zero". A Taiwan share tilt would barely protect Taiwan costs. The IPS meets the currency with her flexibility
    instead (l.15): it covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in 93.2% of house paths, 99.77% with anything above the top,
    and in 98.1% of the real currency's two-year moves since 1983 (NUMBERS 8j, 8o).
  - The cap-weight half is contested: Arnott et al. allow the gain may come from "hidden risk factors", and Bogle's 2006
    reply calls fundamental indexes value and small-company tilts with higher costs. The market-weighted fund is the
    one portfolio every investor can hold at once (Perold and Sharpe's point, row 23).
  - Neither reaches a promise: if VT went to zero the ten are unchanged and the range is $95,000 (deletion test); VT
    growth from 4% to 10% a year leaves the central stated top at $115,000 (NUMBERS 4).
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** for the promises. Concentration stays a risk to the top before the move and to her
  flexibility (3e).

### D4. Half the shares promised to no one; the other half moved before 2031

**C10. "Splitting VT into a promised half and an unpromised half is mental accounting; money is fungible, and half is
arbitrary."**
- Who and where: Thaler (1999): "Each of the components of mental accounting violates the economic principle of
  fungibility. As a result, mental accounting influences choice, that is, it matters."
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM. "Why half?" is a natural judge question.
- Best honest reply: the pots are the case's, not a bias: it asks for an operating reserve, a facility contribution and
  flexibility as separate amounts (l.94-107), and one of them is a public figure that must not be overpromised
  (l.114-117). A label that is a promise to outsiders is a real constraint. Das et al. (2010, held in Q33) show goal
  accounts can be efficient. And the split barely changes how much there is: moving 30% or 70% instead of half changes
  what Laura has beyond the ten by a median of $372 (house), $877 (US stocks) and $1,094 (rates and stocks together);
  each $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,266 of what she keeps (NUMBERS 8o). Half is the middle of a nearly
  one-for-one trade, Caplet's choice in the IPS's order of cause; never "optimal".
- Verdict: **the reply WINS** against fungibility; the arbitrariness is conceded and priced.

**C11. "Moving shares into bonds on a date is a target-date glide path, and studies find the reverse path does
better."**
- Who and where: Estrada (2014): lifecycle strategies make investors "aggressive with little capital and conservative
  with much more capital"; contrarian paths gave "higher upside potential, more limited downside potential, and higher
  uncertainty" across 19 countries and 110 years. Arnott (2012) found the inverse path ahead on US data since 1871 (via
  the Texas State Securities Board's account; UNVERIFIED at the source).
- Strength against Laura: LOW-MEDIUM.
- Best honest reply: the move is not de-risking for wealth. It makes the top an owned figure before Laura states it
  (IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"; case l.114); Estrada's measure is end wealth with
  no promise in the middle. It costs little: 8o's totals are linear in the share moved, so moving none instead of half
  would leave about $930 more at the median (Keel arithmetic: 2.5 times 8o's $372; a benchmark, not a change), and
  would leave a range of one figure, the bottom. Only half moves, once; nothing glides.
- Verdict: **the reply WINS.**

**C12. "A reserve held in shares is smallest exactly when it is needed."**
- Who and where: Froot, Scharfstein and Stein (1993): hedging "adds value to the extent that it helps ensure that a
  corporation has sufficient internal funds available to take advantage of attractive investment opportunities"; the
  logic is to have money when the needs arrive.
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM-HIGH. Overruns and co-sponsor cuts may come in a slump (Q4b; CANON B4: private and US
  co-sponsors, never Taiwan's government), when the shares are down.
- Best honest reply:
  - Concede the shape: her flexibility's median is $15,423 if shares fell over 2031-2033 against $23,315 if they rose;
    under $15,000 in 22.7% of house paths; lowest window $5,779 (September 1929) (NUMBERS 8f).
  - The uses take turns (Q5e): building costs and co-sponsors may move with a slump; the Taiwan dollar's two-year move is
    unlinked to shares (+0.09, NUMBERS 8j).
  - Part of what she keeps is Treasury money: the excess above the stated top. Her flexibility plus the excess has a
    median of $23,201 when shares fell, and is under $15,000 in 3.3% of house paths (NUMBERS 8f).
  - Keeping it all in Treasuries keeps less: against option A (everything to the floor), the plan gives up $15,000 of
    median contribution for $20,000 more median flexibility, at the same 1-in-20 low of all money, $159,000 (NUMBERS 8n).
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS.** The critique wins on the shape, the reply on the size. Never claim her
  flexibility rises when its uses do.

### D5. Only two reasons to change the plan

**C13. "Good practice reviews an IPS at least yearly and sets rebalancing rules; 'only two reasons' looks like no review
at all."**
- Who and where: CFA Institute (2010), individual investors: "shall review the IPS ... no less frequently than
  annually" (its example); "Define the process for rebalancing portfolios to target allocations". Institutional
  investors: "reviewing and revising policies as required".
- Strength against Laura: MEDIUM.
- Best honest reply: watching is not changing. Table 3 (Q26) gives each signal a source, a date, a threshold and a
  "carry on" level, as mining trigger plans and pension triggers do. A smaller deposit is absorbed by the order the IPS
  sets (ladder, extra Treasuries, floor, VT; NUMBERS 5: "A smaller deposit is not one of the two reasons"); a fund
  event is a swap paid from the reserve (Q36). A cash-matched book held to the end has no target weights to rebalance
  (Q16).
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS**, and only if the report shows Table 3.

**C14. "A rule with no way out is brittle; good rules keep an escape clause for extreme shocks."**
- Who and where: Lohmann (1992), held in Q7 row 5: "By retaining the option to dismiss the central banker at a cost, the
  policy-maker forces the central banker to accommodate when extreme shocks are realized."
- Strength against Laura: LOW-MEDIUM.
- Best honest reply: the two reasons are the escape clauses, for the two shocks that can break the payments. Everything
  else leaves the promises whole: the payments survive a deposit cut of up to 74.5% on the escrow standard (98.4% at
  forward rates) (NUMBERS 5); a market fall reaches only the top before the move and her flexibility (NUMBERS 4). Q7
  already records what is missing: no path back is stated, the deposit trigger is partly in Laura's hands, and no one
  outside checks the rule.
- Verdict: **the reply PARTLY WINS.**

**C15. "Theory says hold a constant mix and rebalance as prices move; a buy-and-hold plan lets the mix drift."**
- Who and where: Merton (1971, held): constant proportions for an investor with constant relative risk aversion.
  Perold and Sharpe (1988): a constant mix does better in "relatively trendless, volatile markets".
- Strength against Laura: LOW.
- Best honest reply: Perold and Sharpe: "There is no reason to believe that any particular type of dynamic strategy is
  best for everyone"; buy-and-hold gives a "minimum return proportional to the amount allocated to bills", which is
  what the case asks of the payments and the floor. Rebalancing would sell Treasuries after a fall to buy shares, so
  promised money would sit in shares (l.11). Merton's investor has no dated promises.
- Verdict: **the reply WINS.**
