- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A passage passes when most of Keel's readers get it right."
  2. "Three blind readers are enough to know whether the report's point lands."
  3. "A one-sentence meaning plus a check question shows the reader got the point."
  4. "A reader saying they understood, or liking the passage, is evidence it works."
  5. "The 2031 range and its confidence can be tested by asking readers to restate it."
- **Problem class:** measuring whether a written message was understood, from small samples of real readers, with a pass mark fixed in advance. **Field that solved it first:** readability research (Taylor's cloze procedure, 1953) and survey methodology (cognitive interviewing, from Tourangeau's 1984 four-stage model); later made formal by regulators who must pass or fail a label (ANSI Z535.3 and ISO 9186 for safety symbols; FDA label comprehension studies, 2010), by sensory science for "is A the same as B?" questions (ISO 4120 triangle test), and by central banks and EU and US securities regulators who tested summaries on the public.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Regulators write the target down before the test and pass a message only if the low end of the 95% interval clears it. The FDA's words are "the lower limit of the two-sided 95 percent ... confidence interval for the comprehension rate is above the predefined target level".
  - Typical targets: FDA labels about 90%; ANSI safety symbols 85%, with no more than 5% "critical confusions" (readings that are the opposite of the meaning); ISO symbols about 67%; cloze 60%.
  - The FDA calls them "targets, not hard pass/fail".
  - Keel arithmetic (adjusted Wald, the small-sample method Sauro and Lewis 2005 recommend):
    - 3 of 3 right has an interval of 38-100%. 18 of 18 gives 79-100%. Plain passages heard as Caplet's, 20 of 24, give 64-94%. Conceit passages, 2 of 24, give 1-27%.
    - Every reader must be right in 19 straight reads to show 80% or better; with one miss, 27 reads.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Three readers are enough to find problems, not to measure a rate or claim two things sound alike.
  - Nielsen's formula, with a typical problem seen by 31% of readers: 3 readers find about 67% of problems and 5 about 84% (Keel arithmetic).
  - Faulkner's 60-user study: random sets of 5 found anywhere from 55% to 99% of the problems.
  - Willis: rounds of 4 to 10, repeated after each fix, and "very little in the way of 'truth in numbers'".
  - "Sounds like Caplet" is a similarity claim. ISO 4120 needs about 60 assessors to claim similarity, against 24-30 to find a difference.
  - So Keel's small rounds can kill a passage but cannot crown one. Conceit at 2 of 24 against Caplet's own 18 of 18 is decisive. Plain at 20 of 24 shows no detectable gap (Keel arithmetic: one-sided Fisher p about 0.10) but cannot show the two are alike.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The fields agree on four things:
  - open answers scored by two independent scorers;
  - a separate count of critical confusions;
  - applied questions about a third party ("a co-sponsor's board must budget one figure: which?");
  - check questions worded differently from the passage.
  - The Bank of England's own test admits its questions "more closely mirrored language" in its new summaries: "A fair challenge is whether the phrasing ... biased our results."
  - Gigerenzer and Hoffrage counted an answer only if the number was right and the reader's written reasoning showed how they got it.
- **Claim 4: KILL.**
  - The same Bank of England layered summary raised self-reported understanding by 0.71 points on a 1-5 scale, more than an economics degree's 0.55 (Haldane and McMahon 2018). Measured directly, the old summary scored 2.02 of 5 and the best new one 2.85 (Bholat et al. 2018).
  - People "frequently overestimate their understanding".
  - The FTC's privacy-notice designers found focus groups "do not tell the researcher what a consumer will actually do with a notice".
  - Harvard staff choosing funds with real money read the SEC's summary prospectus faster (22.5 against 31.2 minutes) but chose no better.
- **Claim 5: KEEP AND EXTEND.** Restating is the floor. Risk researchers add three probes:
  - the reader's lowest, best and highest figure, scored against the stated range (Budescu's method);
  - "the highest and lowest values that would not surprise" the reader (Joslyn, Nemec and Savelli 2013), which shows whether the reader took the range for owned money or for a forecast;
  - one decision question.
  - Pre-list the critical confusions for Laura's range:
    - the top owned before the 2031 move;
    - the gift able to fall below the bottom, or to land above the top;
    - the range paid by co-sponsors or by the ten payments;
    - "owned" heard as "signed" or "promised".
  - Zero critical confusions allowed.
- **Judge's note (for 3.0 and 3d):** in the EU's 2020 test of 7,684 investors, "less than half of the respondents were able to identify ... the product that guaranteed a positive return or guaranteed investors their money back".
  - The feature a careful reader would think impossible to miss, the guarantee, is the one readers miss most.
  - So Laura's ten payments owned in Treasuries must be stated so plainly that a skimming judge cannot miss them. Book 19 allows the strong word: "guaranteed".
- **Judge's note (for the exhibits):** the Bank of England's partner test of 4,521 adults found the comprehension gain was "driven by the material's reading grade, rather than visuals or word count". A plainer sentence did more than a picture.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 42, the guarantee is what readers miss.** Serves 3.0 and 3d. In the EU's own investor test, fewer than half could spot the product that guaranteed their money back, so the owned ten payments must be stated so plainly that a judge skimming 40 reports cannot miss them. A judge's aha: the safest feature is the easiest to miss.
2. **Rows 22 and 38, critical confusions and "would not surprise".** Serve 3c and 3d. They test the range as co-sponsors will read it: a reader not surprised by $90,000 has taken owned money for a forecast, the single misreading that would sink Laura's 2031 meetings.
3. **Rows 4 and 6, small rounds kill but cannot crown; similarity needs about 60.** Serve the voice programme and every blind panel. They tell Keel which of its verdicts stand (the conceit's 2 of 24) and which must be worded as "no gap found" (plain 20 of 24).
4. **Row 17, check questions must not borrow the passage's words.** Serves every test. The Bank of England's own caveat shows that a test can measure word-matching instead of meaning, so a pass does not prove the reader understood.
5. **Row 15, self-report against direct test.** Serves the reflection's tests and every reader panel. The same summary looked better than an economics degree on self-report and scored under 3 of 5 when tested directly: score what readers say back, never what they say they understood.
