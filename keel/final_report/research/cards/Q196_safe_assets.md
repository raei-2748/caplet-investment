## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Safe means nobody has to find anything out, and a Treasury held to its date is the purest case

- (a) Gorton defines a safe asset as one taken at face value "without expensive and prolonged analysis", and Holmström calls the state this creates "no questions asked": the holder does not look, because nothing she could learn would change what it pays.
- (b) Laura's five T-bonds are the purest case, because everything that decides what each one pays (its face value, coupon, date and issuer) is printed on it, so nobody can know more about it than anybody else; IPS l.11 says the same in a writer's words, and Holmström's line that for debt "Only default will trigger a value assessment" is why IPS l.17 lets a Treasury default change the plan and a fall in the market not.
- (c) The parallel breaks because Gorton's safe assets are valued as money, things you can spend or sell at once, while Laura holds hers to dates: in March 2020 Treasuries became hard to sell for about ten days, yet a holder waiting for a date lost nothing, so what Laura needs is only the part of safety Gorton calls paying "at par with a very high probability", and the ease of selling, which Treasury buyers also pay for, is the part she buys and never uses.
- **Decision it serves:** the ten (IPS l.13), IPS l.11's definition of a high degree of certainty, IPS l.17's second reason.
- **Report part:** 2.0-2.1 (what a Treasury held to its date is), 3a, 3e (the one premise: the US pays).
- **Source:** rows 1-7.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the definition is pedigree to a fixed-income judge. Reading IPS l.11 as a definition of "no questions asked", and l.17's second reason as Holmström's only trigger, is new; so is the split between the money kind of safety that failed in March 2020 and the dated kind that did not.

### Idea 2. A Treasury fund is a share in a pile of Treasuries, so it leaves one question, and Table 3 row 4 is that question

- (a) Gorton notes that when cash flows are split into debt and equity, the information is split too: the debt needs no questions and the equity carries all of them; and Holmström says a money fund that "breaks the buck" suddenly puts its holders "in the position of equity holders".
- (b) An iBond fund never promised a dollar, because it pays its net asset value (TNA l.15: "a net asset value, as opposed to a promise of par"), so its holder always holds equity in a pile of Treasuries and could want to know three things a T-bond holder never needs: what its cash earns in its last months, what it costs beyond its fee, and whether its board ends it early. The plan makes the first not matter by sizing every payment as if that cash earns nothing, covers the second with the 0.5% margin, and watches the third in Table 3 row 4.
- (c) The parallel breaks on the run: a money fund owed every holder $1 on demand, so the first to leave were paid in full and holders raced for the door (the Reserve Primary Fund received about $40 billion of redemption requests in two days), while Laura's funds owe her nothing before their end, so there is no prize for leaving first; what can reach her is the board's choice, and the 1994 Community Bankers fund shows how, because its notes were guaranteed as to principal by the US government or its agencies, yet it sold them before their value recovered and paid 96.1 cents.
- **Decision it serves:** the five fund rungs, IBTM to IBTR, and the floor fund (IPS l.13, l.15); the 0.5% margin on the payments and the range; Table 3 row 4; SKELETON 3d's rule that "guaranteed" takes her Treasuries as its subject, never a fund's payout.
- **Report part:** Section 2 (the instruments), 3e, Table 3.
- **Source:** rows 8-14.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Most judges treat a defined-maturity Treasury ETF as "basically a bond". That it is equity in a pile of debt, and that the plan answers each of the three questions such equity raises (two by sizing, one by watching), explains a design choice a judge would otherwise read as fussiness: why five payments carry a margin and a watch row and five do not.

### Idea 3. Each stated figure sits where a pawnbroker would put it: far enough below what backs it that nobody needs the exact number

- (a) Holmström's pawnbroker does not price the watch exactly; she lends with "a big enough haircut" because "A safe lower bound is all that is needed", and Gorton draws the same thing as a kink: debt needs no questions while what backs it is believed "sufficiently far to the right" of the point where the debt stops being paid in full.
- (b) Laura's range rule is a pawnbroker's rule: each figure is what her Treasuries pay if cash waiting earns nothing, less the 0.5% margin, rounded down to $5,000, so the top sits about 2% in all below what backs it ($115,000 stated on $117,731) and the bottom 3.16% below, and that room under each figure is her distance from Gorton's kink. The money funds of 2008 rounded the other way: a fund showed $1.00 for any value down to $0.995, so a small loss stayed hidden until it crossed half a cent and then showed all at once, while Laura rounds down, so what stays hidden under her figure is money, not a loss.
- (c) The parallel breaks because the pawnbroker holds the watch and a repo lender holds the bond, while co-sponsors hold nothing: the Treasuries are held for Laura at her broker, so the plan gives co-sponsors the no-questions part of a secured loan without its legal part, which is exactly why all four funders in the funder test filed her figure as "committed" and none as "secured".
- **Decision it serves:** the range rule (NUMBERS 3): the 0.5% margin, the rounding down, and stating the top only on what the moved half has bought.
- **Report part:** 3c; Table 3 row 4 (the one event that can go past the room).
- **Source:** rows 15-20.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the pawnbroker is pedigree to an economist; "a money fund rounded up to its promise, so its losses stayed hidden until it broke; Laura rounds down to hers, so what hides under her figure is money" is an aha, and true to the figures. The room has a limit, which keeps it honest: an end of the late-2032 fund in January 2031 with cash earning nothing would leave the top $4,589 short (NUMBERS 3), so the figure is never called one that "cannot move".

### Idea 4. Words that leave nothing to ask, and no figure that could start the asking

- (a) Gorton and Ordoñez show how a boom builds a crash: while nobody checks, lenders accept weaker backing as if it were good, "a kind of amnesia sets in", and the longer nobody has checked, the bigger the fall when a small shock makes everybody check at once.
- (b) A figure Laura stated on shares in a rising market would be heard the same way, with no questions, and co-sponsors would plan their own budgets on it; the plan's rule that no figure is stated before the shares move into Treasuries means that only figures nobody will need to question in a crash ever reach a co-sponsor. Its rule for her words does the rest: the funder test found that naming the margin "reads as if $115,000 might be trimmed", so in her words nothing is named that could lower the figure, and one reader said that if both ends are in Treasuries when she meets them, the gap between the ends "is about her willingness to give, not about whether the money exists".
- (c) The parallel breaks because Gorton and Ordoñez's lenders skip checking to save its cost, while a co-sponsor could check Laura's holdings cheaply (Table 1 lists them), so the danger the rule guards against is not lazy co-sponsors but timing: the fall that would turn a share-based figure into a question can come in the same downturn that delays her advances and squeezes co-sponsors' budgets (IPS l.9), so the question would arrive exactly when nobody could afford the answer.
- **Decision it serves:** stating only after the 2031 move (IPS l.15: "so whatever figure you choose is money you already own"); 3d's words (one figure, the margin never named as able to lower it, the December 2032 condition said in full).
- **Report part:** 3d; 3c.
- **Source:** rows 21-24.
- **Pedigree or aha:** Gorton and Ordoñez are niche to most finance judges. The aha is that the plan's wording rule, which the funder test found by trial, is the no-questions rule that Holmström and Gorton found in money markets and in 19th-century bank panics. The guard that keeps it honest: leave out only what cannot change a co-sponsor's decision (the margin, which sits under the figure), never what can (the December 2032 condition is said).

### Idea 5. A new issuer's promises are discounted until it has a record; Laura borrows the Treasury's

- (a) Before 1863 thousands of American banks issued their own notes, and Gorton's price data show that the notes traded at par near the bank and at "fluctuating discounts further away", and that a new bank's notes were discounted more, and watched harder, until it had a record.
- (b) Gorton found that a note was worth what a debt is worth that can be collected only when the holder gets back to the bank; Laura's figure can be collected only in 2033, two years after she states it, and the residency it pays for has no record yet, so a figure resting on her career or on shares would be a new bank's note read far from home. A figure resting on Treasuries she owns needs no record of hers, which is what the 1864 Act did when it made every national bank note "express upon their face that they are secured by United States bonds".
- (c) The parallel breaks on the deposit: the 1864 bonds were handed to the Treasurer and held "exclusively for that purpose", while Laura's Treasuries are hers, held for her at a broker, so she can borrow the Treasury's reputation but not the Treasurer's custody, and the honest word for her figure is "committed", never "secured".
- **Decision it serves:** stating the figure as money already owned in a US Treasury fund (SKELETON 3d: "held for her in a US Treasury fund that pays out in December 2032"); never "secured".
- **Report part:** 3d (at most one clause; card Q58 row 37 already limits the banknote to one bridge sentence).
- **Source:** rows 25-28.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the 1864 Act is pedigree (store Q58-032). The aha is the newness discount: in the only market where we can see strangers price new issuers' promises, a new issuer paid extra until it had a record, and Laura skips that cost by never stating a promise of her own making.

### Idea 6. Pencil and ink are Holmström's two systems inside one plan

- (a) Holmström argues that share markets and money markets run on "diametrically opposite" logics: in a share market "Every piece of information about the value of a firm is relevant for pricing its shares", while a money market is built so that nobody needs to find out (his Table 1: "Two entirely different systems").
- (b) Pencil and ink are those two systems inside one plan: VT's part of the top is pencil because each day's news changes it, the Treasuries are ink because no news changes what they pay, and the 2031 move carries the promised half from the first system to the second before anyone relies on it, while the kept half stays in the first because nobody relies on it.
- (c) The parallel breaks on purpose: Holmström's money markets avoid questions so that cash can change hands overnight, while Laura's ink avoids questions so that a promise can wait six years; the no-questions logic was built for speed, and the plan uses it for patience.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.11 ("only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares"), the 2031 move (IPS l.15), the half kept.
- **Report part:** the conceit (CONCEIT version 2): 2.0's definition of ink at first use, or 3c.
- **Source:** rows 29-31.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha, and the one Book 19 asks of the conceit ("a startling new perspective"): pencil and ink are not degrees of confidence but two kinds of information. With card Q174's finding that ink can be checked (every Treasury is listed by CUSIP), it gives ink its full sense: checkable in one look, and never needing a second.

## Best three for the report

1. **Rows 8-11 and 14 (idea 2), for Section 2 and 3e with Table 3 row 4.** A Treasury fund is a share in a pile of Treasuries, so it leaves three questions a bond never does. The plan answers two by sizing (cash waiting earns nothing; the 0.5% margin) and watches the third (an early end). A judge who reads iBond ETFs as "basically bonds" sees why five payments carry a margin and a watch row and five carry neither, and that TNA l.15 already knew the difference.
2. **Rows 15-18 (idea 3), for 3c.** A money fund rounded up to its promise, so its losses stayed hidden until it broke; Laura rounds down to hers, so what hides under her figure is money. The pawnbroker's "A safe lower bound is all that is needed" gives the range rule a reason a judge can repeat, and the room's limit (an early end in 2031 with cash earning nothing) keeps the claim honest.
3. **Rows 29-31 (idea 6), for the conceit's definition in 2.0 or 3c.** Pencil and ink are Holmström's two systems inside one plan: news moves pencil, no news moves ink, and the 2031 move carries the promised half from one system to the other before anyone relies on it. It turns the conceit from a picture of confidence into a claim about information, the "startling new perspective" Book 19 asks for.
