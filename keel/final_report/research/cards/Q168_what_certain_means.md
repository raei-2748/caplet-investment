- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** what "certain" can mean for one person's dated money promises. Probability theory met it first (Jacob Bernoulli, 1713: "moral certainty" reached by repetition, with the line to be fixed by magistrates); insurance solved it in practice by grouping many cases (Knight, 1921); economics named its limit (Knight 1921 and Keynes 1921, 1937: odds that can be measured against an uncertainty that cannot; Keynes's "weight" of evidence); decision theory drew its boundary (Savage, 1954: "small worlds"; Lindley: Cromwell's rule); philosophy asked what certainty rests on (Descartes's moral and absolute certainty; Wittgenstein's hinges); and Kelly (1956) and Peters (2019) showed why one person living one path cannot borrow certainty from an average across many futures.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Keynes's list of what "we simply do not know" contains two things in Laura's plan, and the plan treats them in opposite ways.**
(a) In 1937 Keynes listed what nobody can put odds on, among them "the price of copper and the rate of interest twenty years hence", and for his example of a risk you *can* calculate he chose a government bond: "the prospect of a Victory bond being drawn" (Britain repaid its 1919 Victory Bonds a batch at a time, in a yearly drawing "determined by lot").
(b) Laura's plan buys its way out of needing any later interest rate, because each payment is bought on a day its price is known (January 2027, completed if need be in January 2028) and from January 2028 the ten are met even if cash waiting earns nothing, while it gives the building, whose cost rise to October 2026 was about 70% materials (card Q131), no forecast at all, only room: the half promised to no one.
(c) The plan does not escape interest rates, it chooses the day it meets them, which is why IPS l.13 completes the ladder from the 2028 deposit if January 2027's price is too high; and the break teaches one more thing, because Keynes's drawn bond left only *when* it repaid to chance, never *whether*, while Laura's bonds leave neither, since each date is printed on the bond.
- Decision: why the payments are bought, not forecast (IPS l.11-13); why the building gets flexibility, not a figure (IPS l.15).
- Report part: 3a (one clause), 3b, 3e.
- Source: Keynes (1937), pp. 213-214; London Gazette, 13 June 1919, p. 7550; NUMBERS 2; card Q131.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** A finance judge knows "We simply do not know"; very few remember that the same sentence names the long interest rate and copper, the two things this plan handles in opposite ways, or that Keynes's model of the calculable was a bond.

**2. One life cannot be averaged, so certainty has to come from owning, not from odds.**
(a) Probability turns into something like certainty only by repetition: Bernoulli worked out that 25,550 draws make you "morally certain" of an urn's ratio, Knight's insurers turn a loss into "a smaller fixed charge" by grouping thousands of cases, and Peters (2019) reminds economists that one person does "not live spread out across a multiverse".
(b) Each of Laura's payments falls once, on one date, in her one life, so the first plan's 96.8 in 100 (NUMBERS 8s) was a promise about copies of Laura she would never be, and its misses all fell on one day, 1 January 2033, when all ten had to be bought; Knight said it in 1921: "when an individual instance only is at issue, there is no difference for conduct between a measurable risk and an unmeasurable uncertainty".
(c) The idea breaks in two places, since Knight himself allows that a true probability holds "however unique the instance" and economists reject Peters's wider claim that ergodicity replaces expected utility (Doctor, Wakker and Wang, 2020), but the plan needs neither, only the old and uncontested point Kelly made at Bell Labs in 1956: the bet that "would maximize the expected value of his capital" leaves the gambler "broke with probability one if he continued indefinitely".
- Decision: IPS l.11's "one step further" (owning a payer for each date), and 3f's check that priced it (about 1 in 31 against none from January 2028). The first plan was dropped for IPS l.9's reason, how a writer is paid (CANON A3); this idea explains why the check's average was the wrong kind of comfort, never why the plan changed.
- Report part: 3a; 3f (the check only); 3c (the range stated from her own path).
- Source: Bernoulli (1713/2006), Part IV, ch. 2 and 5; Knight (1921), Part III, ch. VIII; Peters (2019); Kelly (1956), p. 919; NUMBERS 2, 8s.
- Pedigree or aha: Peters and Kelly are pedigree to a quantitative judge; **Knight's one-instance sentence beside Bernoulli's 25,550 draws is the aha** for a general finance judge: averaging needs many lives, and Laura has one.

**3. Savage drew a line around where rational planning works, and the plan's ink is that line.**
(a) Savage, who built the modern theory of rational choice, limited it to a "small world" whose possibilities can all be listed, called planning a whole life that way "utterly ridiculous", and gave the two sides two proverbs: "Look before you leap" inside a small world, "You can cross that bridge when you come to it" outside it.
(b) Laura's plan uses each proverb where Savage says it belongs, deciding the ten payments in full in January 2027 (ten dates, ten sums, one issuer) and leaving the building's figure until 2031 with half kept back for bridges she cannot see yet; and where Savage confessed "I am unable to formulate criteria for selecting these small worlds", the plan has one: a promise belongs in the small world when a Treasury she owns will pay it on its date, which is exactly where ink ends and pencil starts.
(c) Savage's small worlds are made small by simplifying the thinking, while Laura's is made small by buying it, so a surprise that would break a model leaves her payments alone; but the small world is not sealed, because the issuer's promise and the funds' running costs sit at its edge (row 32), and Savage's own warning, that choosing small worlds is "a matter of judgment", applies to where the team drew the line.
- Decision: the three dated steps (January 2027, January 2028, the move before the 2031 meetings; GOLDMINE 27); why nothing is stated before the move (NUMBERS 4); IPS l.15.
- Report part: 3.0, 3b, 3c; `CONCEIT.md` (a strikingly dissimilar parallel: ink as the border of a small world).
- Source: Savage (1954/1972), pp. 16-17; Gigerenzer (2021).
- Pedigree or aha: **aha.** Decision theorists know small worlds; almost nobody has seen a portfolio whose two halves are Savage's two proverbs, or a working answer to the criterion Savage said he could not give.

**4. The plan's certainty is a deduction from a few named premises, not a forecast: this is the test of "real idea or trick of words".**
(a) Keynes split how likely a conclusion is from how much evidence it rests on, its "weight", and Lindley's "Cromwell's rule" allows a probability of 1 only when what you know "logically implies" the event.
(b) Turning a question about chance into one about ownership does not abolish probability but moves it, swapping a forecast with many light premises (share prices, rates, co-sponsors' budgets, Keynes's "we simply do not know") for a short list of heavy, named premises (the US Treasury pays its notes, her own deposits arrive, fund costs stay inside the 0.5% margin, the broker holds her securities), from which each payment and both ends of the stated range follow by arithmetic that a co-sponsor can check against a statement.
(c) It stops working where ownership has no fixed sum, since VT is owned and still pencil (card Q157), so owning is necessary but not enough: the thing owned must be a fixed dollar sum due on or before the promise's date; and because the premises are facts about the world, not logic, Cromwell's rule still bars calling them probability 1, which is why "certain" takes the Treasuries or the stated range as its subject and never a dollar figure (WORDS).
- Decision: IPS l.11 as the only definition of "high" (CANON A4) and what makes it more than a slogan; 3c's basis; 3e's premises, said once.
- Report part: 3a, 3c, 3e.
- Source: Keynes (1921), ch. VI and XXVI; Lindley (2006), section 6.8; Cromwell (1650); NUMBERS 4, 8u.
- Pedigree or aha: **aha** for the judge who reads "certain" as an overclaim: it is a conditional, made short and checkable. The ingredients (weight, Cromwell's rule) are niche even to statisticians.

**5. The hinge and the door: why the one doubt is said once.**
(a) Wittgenstein wrote that our doubts "depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt", like hinges, that "If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put", and that "All testing ... takes place already within a system".
(b) Every test in the report (200,000 simulated futures, 585 replays of history, Table 2's named worlds) takes the Treasury's payment as given, so no test can test it, and the IPS's own picture of the range is a door on that hinge, the markets able to "increase or decrease the top" while "the bottom will remain constant"; so the report names the one doubt once, outside the tests, where IPS l.17 already lists it as one of its two reasons to change the plan, and never hangs it on a figure.
(c) The analogy breaks usefully, because Wittgenstein's hinges ("I have two hands") have no price while a US default does, since markets quote it (store H1-033, H1-034), which makes it a small risk in Knight's measurable sense rather than a true hinge, and that is exactly why naming it once is honest rather than evasive.
- Decision: the wording rule (Book 19: no "barring a US default" on any figure; IPS l.11 keeps its one); 3e's frame for Table 2; the shape of the range (IPS l.15).
- Report part: 3e, Table 2's note, `CONCEIT.md`.
- Source: Wittgenstein (1969), sections 105, 115, 341-343; Lindley (2006); Cromwell (1650).
- Pedigree or aha: niche and unexpected in a finance report; the **aha** is that the tests cannot test the hinge, so the report states it once as the frame.

**6. Knight's two kinds of income, with Laura on both sides of the bargain.**
(a) Knight divided all income into "two kinds of income, and two only", contractual income fixed in advance and residual income left over, and described how the "confident and venturesome" guarantee "a specified income" to the "doubtful and timid".
(b) Laura's career is pure residual income (advances and fees that come "later and smaller" in a recession, IPS l.9), so for every promise she makes to others the plan makes her a holder of contractual income, Treasuries, and keeps the residual, the half kept, for herself; Knight even imagined someone securing "the payment of his obligations by pledging his own earning power", which is the one route IPS l.9 closes, because her earning power can fall in the same downturn as shares and co-sponsors' budgets.
(c) Knight's guarantor is a businessman who can fail, while Laura's is the issuer of the currency her promises are written in, so her contract is stronger than any Knight described; and Knight's residual earner is paid for bearing uncertainty, while Laura's half kept is no reward, only room for the building's unknowns.
- Decision: IPS l.9's counterbalance; the two pots; 2.3.
- Report part: 2.3, 3b.
- Source: Knight (1921), Part III, ch. IX.
- Pedigree or aha: pedigree (Knight is known); the two-income map onto her two pots is niche and worth one clause in 2.3.

## Is ownership a real idea or a trick of words? (the test asked for)

**Verdict: a real idea, with three edges.** The charge is fair on its face: "she owns a Treasury that will pay $50,000" is itself a probability statement about the United States. It fails as a charge for three reasons.
1. **It changes what can happen, not only the label.** A payment owned in a Treasury held to its date is the same in every simulated future and every history window the register runs (NUMBERS 2; NUMBERS 4 for the stated range after the move). An average across futures and her one path agree because nothing in the payment depends on the path (Peters's condition met by construction, idea 2).
2. **It changes who can check it.** De Finetti: "PROBABILITY DOES NOT EXIST", only someone's belief. A co-sponsor can only trust Laura's belief; a holding can be shown on a custodian's statement (store Q22-020). After the move the confidence she states is the second kind (row 30).
3. **It changes the weight, not just the odds.** The first plan's 96.8 in 100 rested on a model of share returns (NUMBERS 8s); "none" rests on contracts and a few named premises (idea 4). Keynes: weight raises the worth of an action, "but we cannot measure the amount of the increase", so the report prices what can be priced (aha row 10) and names the rest in one clause.

**Where it breaks.**
- **A US default.** Not removed; reduced to one named premise shared by all ten payments and both ends (aha row 2: one issuer carries all ten), priced small by markets (store H1-033), said once (idea 5). A technical delay is the realistic form (store H2-086: bills paid late in 1979).
- **The half kept in shares.** Owned, still pencil (card Q157): ownership converts only a fixed dollar sum due by the promise's date (row 31).
- **What the dollars buy.** Ownership makes the nominal $50,000 certain as written in the case, not what it buys in Taiwan (store H5-041; NUMBERS 8d). The match holds because the promise is fixed in dollars.
- **Before the move.** The top is a forecast until the move, and the plan says so: a top named 12 months early would be more than the move buys in 10.6% of house paths (NUMBERS 4). The trick-of-words charge would stick only if the report called a fund's payout or a dollar figure "certain"; WORDS's rule (certain takes the Treasuries or the stated range as subject) is what keeps it honest.

## Best three for the report

1. **Keynes's list, idea 1 (rows 1-5), for 3a and 3b.** The sentence every economist half-remembers turns out to name the two things Laura's plan treats in opposite ways, the long interest rate (bought out) and copper (left open, met with room), and its example of the calculable is a government bond; a judge sees in one clause why the payments are bought and the building is not forecast.
2. **Savage's small world, idea 3 (rows 22-25), for 3.0 and the conceit.** The founder of rational-choice theory said his theory works only in a small world and that he could not say where one ends; Laura's plan answers him with a working border, what a Treasury she owns will pay on its date, which is exactly where ink ends and pencil starts.
3. **One life, idea 2 (rows 11, 15-16, 18), for 3a.** Bernoulli needed 25,550 draws to be "morally certain"; Knight said that for a single case a measured risk is no better guide than an unmeasured one; Laura makes each payment once, in one life, so she gets certainty by owning a payer for each date. Paired with idea 4 (row 32) in 3e, it answers the judge who thinks "certain" is a trick of words: it is a deduction from a short list of named premises.
