- **Problem class:** releasing one funder's money against another's, so that public money is never spent on a project the private side never pays for (a conditional, staged co-funding contract). **Field that solved it first:** public grant administration (challenge and matching grants; Singapore's 2013 Cultural Matching Fund copied the dollar-for-dollar model), with contract law deciding when a promise to give is owed (China's Charity Law and Civil Code).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **"They match a pledge": CHANGE.** A pledge can open the door, but only cash is matched. Hong Kong's arts council asks at application for "documentary proof (e.g. Letter of Intent/Sponsorship Letter)" that the gift is "secured and deliverable", then matches "the exact amount of cash sponsorships and donations received" and pays out within about 20 working days of the cheque arriving (rows 13-15). Hong Kong's government scheme lets a group in if it "已經或將會" (has or will) raise the money, then matches up to 200% of "實際現金收入" (actual cash income) (row 20). Singapore matches only cash already received, certified by a chartered accountant, looking back up to two years (rows 1-2). Japan's state arts fund counts only costs the group has already paid, proved by bank records, and pays after the year ends (rows 33-34). None of the four matches a promise as a promise.
   - **Judge's note:** people assume the founder's promise is what pulls in public money. In the region's biggest matching schemes the promise only gets the project through the door; the public money follows the cheque. In 2031 Laura brings to that door something none of these schemes asks for there: money she already owns.
2. **"Proof is a bank balance at application": CHANGE.** Four places, four kinds of proof, and none of them is a bank balance at the door. Hong Kong asks for a letter of intent. Singapore asks for a chartered accountant's statement of cash already received. Korea asks the company giving the money for its balance sheet, income statement and profit statement, and screens it for "재무건전성" (financial soundness) and the means for "지속적인" (continued) support (row 25). Japan asks for bank records of costs already paid. Taiwan asks for no bank certificate either (Q48).
3. **"The founder's own money can be matched": CHANGE (kill it for Hong Kong and Korea).**
   - Hong Kong: donors must not be "related to the Applicant in terms of control or management", and a group may not fund itself (row 16).
   - Korea: no match where the company founded or funded the group, or where there are family ties (row 26).
   - Singapore: gifts from board members, trustees and key staff must be declared, but they are not excluded (row 5).
   - So if Laura controls the residency, her money brings co-sponsors to the table, but in two of the three schemes it would not itself be matched.
4. **"Matching funds pay for buildings": CHANGE.**
   - Hong Kong's arts match pays for projects. It excludes "capital financing expenses" and venues the group owns (row 18).
   - Korea's arts match may not pay for buying assets or for facilities: "자산취득비, 시설비" (row 27).
   - Singapore's fund has paid for capital items, but it stopped matching endowment and reserve funds in 2018 (row 9).
   - Building money comes through other channels:
     - Hong Kong's Revitalisation Scheme pays for renovation "in part or in full". It adds only two years of start-up help, and only if the project "is projected to become self-sustainable" (rows 21-23).
     - Korea will pay 50% of a new anchor cultural building from 2027, after the budget ministry checks who will bear the running costs (row 31).
   - **Judge's note:** in Asia, public building money always asks who pays the running costs once the doors open. Hong Kong's capital grants were 27 times its start-up grants (Keel arithmetic, row 22), and its commissioner says breaking even in year three is the hardest part (row 23). Laura's plan settles that question first: the ten operating payments are owned before anyone is asked for building money.
5. **"Laura's money counts only when paid": CHANGE.** The matching formula counts money when it is received, in local currency, on the day it lands (rows 1, 6, 15, 17), so for any matcher Laura's contribution counts from late 2032 (NUMBERS 4: paid December 2032). The door test, though, is about whether the money is secured. Laura's bottom is owned from January 2028, and her top before the 2031 meetings. That passes every door test found here years before the counting starts (row 42). The years between the door and the cheque are where a pledge can fail; the plan has already removed that risk.
   - **Judge's note:** China's law draws the same line from the other side. A culture pledge becomes legally owed only once it has been announced publicly in the media (row 40). Saying a figure in public is what turns it into a debt. Laura says only figures she already owns.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 42 (3d).** The door and the cheque. Every matcher near Taiwan lets a project in on a promise but pays only against cash. Laura comes to the 2031 door with money she already owns, and the cheque in December 2032 is a formality. This gives 3d its plain reason why an owned figure is worth more than a pledge.
2. **Row 44 with rows 21-23 and 31 (3.0, 3a).** In Hong Kong building money was 27 times running money, and the hard part is breaking even in year three. Korea now checks running costs before it pays half a building. Laura's plan owns the running costs first. This is a judge's aha that turns the "ten payments first" order into the answer funders now ask for.
3. **Row 40 with row 41 (3c, 3d; fits the conceit).** In China a culture pledge becomes legally owed only once it has been announced publicly, and the law lets the donor out if her finances worsen markedly. Laura states only owned figures and needs no such exit. It shows why the plan states a figure only once it is owned.
4. **Row 43 (3d, 3b).** A matcher adds money when the founder gives more; a gap-filler gives less. Which co-sponsor Laura faces decides whether her top or her bottom matters most to them. This is new analysis for the words to co-sponsors.
5. **Row 4 with row 3 (3b).** Singapore will not match a gift with strings or a refund clause. Laura's flexibility sits outside the gift (the half kept, and the excess above the top that never leaves her), so what she pays can be unconditional. It shows the half kept is not hedging inside the gift: it is what lets the gift itself carry no conditions.

## Gaps

- **Taiwan's own Civil Code on gifts** (whether a public or notarised pledge to an arts body can be revoked before delivery, as in China's Art. 658) was not read. That is the most useful next read for 3d.
- **Singapore's capital spending:** the 2022 slides list a "Capital Expenditure" type, but their order makes the capital share of the S$229.7m unclear. No public breakdown of how much went to buildings was found.
- **Korea's 2027 capital rate:** read only in Seoul Economic Daily. The amended enforcement decree of the Subsidy Act itself (기준보조율 table) was not read at law.go.kr.
- **Hong Kong's Revitalisation Scheme guide** (para 4.1, conditions on the capital grant and whether the operator must co-fund works) was not read. Only the scheme page, a newsletter, the Audit summary and a press interview were.
- **Japan:** whether the Agency for Cultural Affairs' successor to its 2009 priority-support programme still uses the deficit-filling limit was not checked. The question's own quote attributed to an official was not used, because the government's answer does not repeat it.
- **Mainland China's state arts fund** (国家艺术基金) and any matching rule it has were not researched. Only the pledge law was.
- **No scheme found accepts or ranks a statement of securities** (such as Treasuries) as proof of private money. Row 42's claim that ownership beats a letter is Keel's reading, not a funder's rule.
- **Not used:** a Singapore FAQ PDF marked "CONFIDENTIAL" appeared in search results. It was not used; every Singapore row rests on the public terms, FAQ 2026, slides and Hansard.
