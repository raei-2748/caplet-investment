- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**

- **Problem class:** keeping a dealer-run auction and an over-the-counter bond market fair for a small buy-and-hold buyer: corners, false bids, hidden prices. **Field that solved it first:** futures regulation, whose large-position reports the 1992 Joint Report copied ("As is demonstrated by the CFTC, large position rather than large trader reporting is more effective"), and auction theory (Vickrey 1961; Friedman's uniform-price proposal of 1959, repeated in 1991).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KILL.** No official source ranks markets by honesty, and the record shows abuse more than once.
  - **What officials say.** In 1992 the agencies called it "the largest and most liquid securities market in the world" (Joint Report). In 2015 four agencies called it "the deepest and most liquid government securities market in the world" (SEC press release 2015-144).
  - **What the record shows.** Salomon's false bids, 1989-1991. Traders on J.P. Morgan's Treasuries desk spoofed the cash Treasury market from April 2015 to January 2016 (SEC 2020-233). In 2015 an SEC Commissioner called it "arguably the least transparent fixed income market in the U.S." (Aguilar).
  - **What was not proven.** A claim that ten dealers rigged auctions in 2007-2015 was dismissed, and the Second Circuit affirmed on 1 Feb 2024.
  - **Say instead:** the deepest and most liquid government bond market in the world, policed by its own seller, the Treasury, and by three regulators, and still a market where abuse has happened and been punished.
  - **Judge's note:** the market that taught the world to price safety was "arguably the least transparent fixed income market in the U.S." as late as 2015. What keeps Laura safe is that she never needs to trade with anyone after she buys.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE to "more than near-identical Treasuries".**
  - **The size.** After the 22 May 1991 auction, the two-year note traded on average 31.36 cents per $100 above comparable seasoned notes from issue to four weeks after. Other two-year notes of 1986-1991 averaged 4.23 cents. At week four the gap was 42.94 cents, a yield 25 basis points lower (Jegadeesh 1993, Table VII).
  - **Financing.** Salomon financed the notes at repo rates "generally 100-200 basis points below" the general rate (Joint Report, citing Salomon's testimony).
  - **Who paid.** The DOJ's complaint names short sellers as those harmed: those who "sold the May two-year notes short". They paid "artificially high and non-competitive prices" or accepted "artificially low ... special rates".
  - **What a holder got.** A holder who kept the note to its end on 31 May 1993 was paid as the note said. The overpayment was a lower yield for whoever bought in the squeeze, never a smaller payment.
  - **Old bonds can be squeezed too.** In May 1986 the 9 1/4% bond of February 2016 was (Joint Report B-1 n.1). So never say "Laura's old bonds cannot be squeezed".
  - **Judge's note:** the biggest scandal in the Treasury market's history moved prices and repo rates, and the people it hurt had sold notes they did not own. A buy-and-hold owner sat on the other side.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The scandal changed the price rule and the policing. The 35% caps are older, and the auction the agencies preferred never came.
  - **The price rule.** The uniform-price method was tested on two- and five-year notes from September 1992 (Malvey and Archibald 1998). On 28 Oct 1998 the Treasury "decided to expand the use of uniform-price auctions to the sale of all marketable Treasury securities", starting with the 2 Nov 1998 cash management bill (Treasury RR-2782). Today every winning bidder pays one price.
  - **The 35% caps.** The award cap dates from 1962. The cap on a single-yield bid dates from July 1990, and it answered Salomon's own bid for more than 100% of a REFCORP bond issue (Joint Report B-48, C-2 n.3). Both still stand in 31 CFR 356.
  - **The open auction.** The ascending open auction the 1992 report favoured was not adopted. Treasury moved to the uniform-price sealed bid instead (Dupont and Sack 1999).
  - **New policing.** Large awards are confirmed with the customer. False written statements in a bid are now an explicit offence (1993 Act s.110). Treasury can ask for large-position reports (s.104; rules from 1996, 14 calls by Dec 2014). Fair-price and best-execution rules now cover dealers' Treasury sales (FINRA Rules 0150(d), 2121, 5310).
  - **Say:** "every Treasury auction since November 1998 sells at one price, a change the 1991 scandal set off"; never "the scandal created the 35% rule".
- **Claim 4: KEEP for trading costs in a normal market. CHANGE for a squeezed bond and for the day's rate move.**
  - **Trading costs.** Card Q88 row 36 has the dollars: about 3 cents per $100.
  - **In yield terms.** Each basis point the curve moves changes the ten's cost by about $300 (NUMBERS 8x(e): $299.68 on the $302,287 cost), about 10 cents per $100. So normal trading costs are about a third of one basis point (Keel arithmetic).
  - **A squeeze.** The 1991 squeeze added up to 43 cents per $100 to one two-year note, for weeks. That is the same share of value as a 4-basis-point move in the whole ten's cost, and about $215 on a $50,000 holding (Keel arithmetic on Jegadeesh).
  - **Judge's note:** on a purchase day the curve's move, not the dealer's spread, is the big number. Only a squeezed bond would add more than a few cents, and the team's price check is the place it would show.
- **The plain lesson for Laura.**
  - **She trades on three days only:** January 2027, January 2028 and the 2031 move. Each Treasury then pays what it says to its end (IPS l.13), so a scandal can change what she pays on a purchase day, never what she is paid.
  - **What protects the price she pays.** Many bidders at every auction at one price, under 35% caps, with primary dealers bound to bid their share, build the curve she is priced against. A dealer must sell to her at a "fair" price (Rule 2121) with best execution (Rule 5310). Trades are reported to regulators (TRACE since 10 Jul 2017), and weekly totals have been public since 10 Mar 2020. For the funds, exchange quotes and the trade-through rule apply, though the SEC proposed rescinding that rule in June 2026.
  - **What does not.** No public tape covers her old bonds, and no markup is printed on her confirmation (card Q88 rows 18-19). No trade-through rule covers bonds. Squeezes and spoofing still happen.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 10, 3e: a squeeze changes the price, never the payment.** The DOJ named short sellers as the victims of 1991; a holder to maturity got what the note said. It lets 3e say why a market abuse is not among IPS l.17's reasons, without telling the story (3e's parallel is the gilt crisis).
2. **Row 25, 2.5 or 3e: before 1993 most dealers selling Treasuries were under no fair-price rule; today FINRA's fair-price and best-execution rules cover Laura's Treasury purchases.** It answers "what protects the price she pays" with a dated, checkable fact.
3. **Row 45, 2.5: the team's 25-basis-point price rule is about the size of the 1991 squeeze.** It gives 2.5's price check a scale a judge knows. The guard matters as much as the fact: never claim the rule guards against manipulation.
4. **Row 28, 3e or a judge's question: the audit trail the SEC asked for in January 1992 arrived in July 2017.** It shows the team knows how recently the Treasury market became visible to its own regulators, beside Q88-018's point that no public tape covers Laura's old bonds.
5. **Row 35, Table 3 or 3e: the trade-through rule behind the fund purchases was proposed for repeal in June 2026.** It is a live, dated watch item for January 2028 and the 2031 move, and it is easy to check before 27 Nov.
