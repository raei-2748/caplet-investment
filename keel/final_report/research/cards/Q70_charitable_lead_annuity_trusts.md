- **Problem class:** funding a fixed-dollar charitable annuity for a set term from a pool set aside at the start, with what is left going back to the donor's side. **Field that solved it first:** US federal tax law of split-interest trusts (26 U.S.C. 170(f)(2)(B), 2055(e)(2)(B), 2522(c)(2)(B); Treas. Reg. 1.170A-6, in force for transfers after 21 May 1972; the section 7520 tables), with estate-planning practice and actuarial valuation supplying the sizing and the investing.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Closest in shape, opposite in sizing, investing and purpose.**
  - **The shape is the same.** A fixed dollar amount, paid at least yearly for a set term, from money set aside at the start, with the charity paid before any private use and what is left going back to the donor's side (rows 1, 3, 21).
  - **Sizing is opposite.** A CLAT is sized at the section 7520 rate, which the statute sets at 120% of Treasury mid-term yields. Laura's ten are sized so they are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Investing is opposite.** CLATs hold almost no government bonds: 2.9% of all charitable lead trusts' investments in 2012 (row 28). Laura's ten are all Treasuries.
  - **Purpose is opposite.** A CLAT counts as a success when something is left for the family. Laura's plan counts as a success when all ten are paid, and anything left is a by-product: $45,456 in 1 Jan 2033 money if rates hold (NUMBERS 8h).
  - **Judge's note:** US tax law already has a twin of Laura's ten payments. It is built to bet against the rate that Laura's plan refuses to rely on.
- **Claim 2: KILL. In tax law "guaranteed" means the amount is fixed, not that the money exists.**
  - **The definition.** A guaranteed annuity is an arrangement "under which a determinable amount is paid periodically"; "determinable" means the amount "can be ascertained as of the date of transfer" (Treas. Reg. 1.170A-6(c)(2)(i)(A)). Nothing requires the trust to hold enough to pay it.
  - **When it ran short.**
    - In Walton (115 T.C. 589), two $100m trusts of Wal-Mart stock promised $108,570,025.58 each. Each paid $94,104,550.57, "a $14,465,475.01 shortfall", after the stock fell from $27.6875 to $26.1875 (13.3% short after a 5.4% fall, Keel arithmetic). This was a GRAT, whose annuity goes to the donor, but the sizing is the same (row 12).
    - A practitioner's 2006 CLAT averaged 6.31% against its 6% rate and still ran dry in year 9 (row 11).
  - **Who bears it.** The donor has "no liability"; the "charity simply doesn't receive the full promised amount" (FRGA, 2025). A pledge paid through a CLAT "may not be met in full" (Lee, Berry & Hall, 2011).
  - **Overclaims in the field.** Some practitioners and the Walton family's spokesman still say the charity is paid "regardless" (row 14: CONFLICTING).
  - **Judge's note:** in a CLAT the deed is ink and the money is pencil. Laura's ten are ink in both places: a fixed amount, and Treasuries she owns that pay it.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with the fact that sharpens it.**
  - **The rate is above Treasury yields by law.** The 7520 rate is "120 percent of the Federal midterm rate". The mid-term rate is the average market yield on marketable US obligations of 3 to 9 years (26 U.S.C. 7520(a)(2), 1274(d)). So the assumed rate sits 20% above what Treasuries pay, by statute.
  - **What that does to a Treasury-only CLAT.** A zeroed-out 10-year CLAT at November 2026's 6.2% pays 13.72% of its money each year. Invested at the 5.08% mid-term rate, it pays nine payments in full and about a third of the tenth: about 93% of what it promised (Keel arithmetic, row 5).
  - **Laura's ten priced at the IRS rate.** At 6.2% the ten are worth $269,852 on 1 Jan 2027, against the $301,544 Treasuries charge (NUMBERS 1) and the $336,656 Laura's standard costs (NUMBERS 2). At the August-November 2020 rate of 0.4% the same ten are worth $479,507 (Keel arithmetic, row 6).
  - **Judge's note:** the tax code's rate for valuing a charity's fixed annuity is set 20% above Treasury yields by statute, so a charity's promise sized at it cannot be paid by Treasuries alone.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. Rare from day one; one practitioner does it late in the term; the name was not found.**
  - **What CLATs hold.** In filing year 2012, charitable lead trusts held $582.5m of US and state government obligations out of $20.27bn of investments, 2.9% (Keel arithmetic, IRS SOI). In 2006 it was 4.5%.
  - **What practitioners advise.** "Avoid: Cash or bonds (won't beat §7520)" (Reed Corp).
  - **The trade, measured.** For a $10m 10-year CLAT: 100% shares gave a median $4.6m left and an 86% chance of anything left; a 45/45/10 mix gave $2.8m and 93% (Lee et al.).
  - **The one documented move to bonds.** One firm moved a real CLAT's remaining $2,887,622 of payments into short bonds in 2018, holding $7,170,581, and teaches "As Maturity Approaches DERISK Annuity Payments" (row 24).
  - **Not found.** No source uses "defeased CLAT", and no CLAT was found that holds the charity's payments in a Treasury ladder from day one.
  - **Judge's note:** the CLAT world's best practice ends where Laura's plan starts: lock the promised payments in bonds once they are covered, and keep the rest for growth.
- **Claim 5: KILL as a qualification rule. CHANGE to two weaker rules.**
  - **No exhaustion test.** "No probability of exhaustion rules; contrast CRATs: Rev. Rul. 77-374" (Hall & Sega, 2016). There is no payout minimum or maximum either (IRS Pub. 6109).
  - **Weaker rule 1: a valuation test, not a qualification test.** Treas. Reg. 20.7520-3(b)(2)(i) cuts the value of an annuity "expected to exhaust the fund". It assumes the trust earns the 7520 rate, so a payout at or below that rate is "assumed to be sufficient to make all payments" (row 16). A trust holding one non-dividend stock is "sufficient ... even indefinitely" (row 17).
  - **Weaker rule 2: a standard of care.** Above 60%, the trust deed must bar jeopardizing investments (row 19). That standard is judged "at the time of making the investment", "not ... on the basis of hindsight" (row 20).
  - **The gap.** No rule requires a CLAT to be able to pay.
  - **Judge's note:** US law tests a trust that pays a donor for the chance it runs dry (5%), but not a trust that pays a charity. Laura applies the stricter test to the charity's money.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 36, with row 2 ("guaranteed" means determinable).** For 3a. US tax law's own word for this kind of promise turns out to mean only that the amount is fixed, and Laura's ten are the one version where the money is fixed too. It gives aha row 5 a sharper legal twin, and the conceit a turn: ink in the deed, pencil in the portfolio.
2. **Rows 4-6 (the 7520 rate is 120% of Treasury yields by statute; a Treasury-only CLAT pays about 93%; the ten priced at $269,852 to $479,507 by month).** For 3a and aha row 5. A judge who knows CLATs as a low-rate trick learns that the rate is built to be beaten, so a charity's promise sized at it cannot be paid by Treasuries alone.
3. **Row 31 (Onassis: funded last, paid nothing).** For 3a or 3.0, as one sentence. The most famous charitable lead trust in US history never paid a dollar because it was funded from what was left. Laura funds her ten first and leaves her flexibility to what is left.
4. **Row 24 with row 26 (a real CLAT moved its last $2.89m of payments into bonds in 2018; the trustee's trade priced at 86% vs 93%).** For 3b and 2.0. The CLAT world's best practice ends where Laura's plan starts, and its own numbers price the same trade the half kept makes.
5. **Row 18 (count only "the minimum amount it is evident" the charity will receive).** For 3c. An IRS regulation's own worked example states the bottom's rule: say only what is evident (for Laura, owned), not what a rate promises.

## Gaps

- **No named CLAT failure from 2008-09 was found at source.** The documented cases are the Walton GRAT (same sizing, 1995), a practitioner's illustration on index returns, and a January 2009 note on underwater GRATs. The IRS Form 5227 microdata (2018-2022, irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/) could count lead trusts filing a final return with nothing left; not attempted.
- **"Defeased CLAT" was not found in any source,** and no CLAT was found that holds its charity's payments in a Treasury ladder from day one. One anonymised late-term example only (row 24).
- **Journal of Taxation, Trusts & Estates and Estate Planning articles** (including "Potential self-dealing if charity's payments are at risk by back loading", Estate Planning, October 2010, cited on a 2025 slide) were not read: behind paywalls.
- **IRS Publication 1457 (Table B) was not read.** Row 5 and row 6 use the standard annuity formula, which is what Table B tabulates (25.7520-3 Example 5 uses Table B the same way).
- **The original Bloomberg article (2013)** was read only in syndicated reprints, and the Onassis will only in a transcription, not the Surrogate's Court copy.
- **Rev. Rul. 88-27** (commute-and-prepay disqualifies a CLAT) was read only in a secondary summary on a community foundation's site, not the ruling itself.
- **No study of what share of CLATs end with nothing left, or pay the charity less than promised,** was found.
