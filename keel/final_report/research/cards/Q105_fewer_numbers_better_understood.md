## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with sizes, and CHANGE one word: remove the figures the reader's question does not need, never the figure it does.**
  - **Deleting beats everything else tried.** Hospital reports with four off-topic measures deleted: comprehension 2.4 to 2.8 of 3 items, best choice 40% to 62% (Peters et al., 2007, N=303). Showing only the two options a patient faced: correct reading of the added benefit 51% to 77%, reading time 42 to 28 seconds (Zikmund-Fisher et al., 2008, N=1,619). Survival figures without the redundant mortality figures: total accuracy 50% to 63% (Zikmund-Fisher et al., 2010, N=832). Net returns only, not gross and net: PRIIPs testers recommended it because both "confused participants" (London Economics et al., 2015). Three-period past performance added to a key information document cut the share choosing the best-suited product by about 5 points (European Commission, 2020).
  - **Lab sizes.** Cutting words the learner does not need: median d = 0.97 in 13 of 14 experiments (Mayer, 2008), d = 0.86 in 23 of 23 (Mayer & Fiorella, 2014); adding formulas and computations to a 653-word lesson hurt problem solving, d about 0.69-0.97 (Mayer & Jackson, 2005). Field meta-analyses are smaller: g = -0.33 (2020), g = -0.16 overall and -0.19 for comprehension across 50 studies (Cheng et al., 2026).
  - **Where it breaks.** The drug facts box, the most famous "less is more" success, removed words and *added* numbers in a small table: correct answers on a drug's benefit 8% to 70% (Schwartz et al., 2009). Without the numbers, 65% overestimated a statin's benefit tenfold or more. Removing a figure the reader needs makes things worse: cutting the middle periods of past performance "inverts the proper relation between volatility and risk attitude" (EC, 2020); low-numerate readers shown one option at a time could not work out a comparison they needed (59% vs 74%, Zikmund-Fisher et al., 2011). A summary cut below its steps lost its edge (Mayer et al., 1996, Exp. 1).
  - **Judges are numerate.** Peters' high-numerate readers gained only from deletion (not from reordering), so the lever that works on Wharton's readers is cutting.
  - **Judge's note:** the best-known "less is more" document added numbers. It won by taking away the words around the numbers that mattered.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Tested documents converge on "the answer figure first, in the first lines, with its yardstick; the rest after the answer or in a table the prose points to". No tested document sets "one" as a limit.**
  - **Position doubles the effect, and the back of a document has none.** The same rate notice raised switching from 3% to 6% on the front page; "non-front page disclosures had no effect" (Adams et al., 2016, FCA field trials). Roughly half of US health-plan shoppers who saw the summary form did not remember its coverage examples, which sat "near the back"; Consumers Union asked to move them "towards the beginning of the form" (2013).
  - **Moving is weaker than deleting, and order matters.** Putting the four off-topic measures last did not reliably help choices (48% vs 40%, not significant; Peters et al., 2007). Formulas placed at the end of a lesson did no harm; interspersed, they hurt, by "disrupting the coherence between units of qualitative information" (Verkoeijen & Tabbers, 2009).
  - **How many.** ONS: an article carries "two or three headline facts and figures"; bullets use "the most important value". The tamoxifen facts box held 9 rows by 2 columns and readers got 4 of 5 questions right (Schwartz & Woloshin, 2013). The finalist's "one number" fits a 200-250-word part; one yardstick beside it is supported (Peters et al., 2009; Woloshin et al., 2002).
  - **Detail does help in one table.** A more detailed performance table beat a simpler one; participants "prefer less detailed information if it is presented in one large table" (London Economics et al., 2015).
  - **Judge's note:** in the FCA's trial the same sentence had twice the effect on page one and no effect inside. Where a figure sits matters as much as whether it is there.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Tested: a figure with its basis in one plain clause is understood and used better than one surrounded by its mechanics. Not tested: whether it is trusted more.**
  - **Supported.** One-line explanations of what a figure means raised good choices 71% vs 59% and 53% vs 34% (Schwartz & Woloshin, 2013). Formulas and computations interspersed in an explanation hurt understanding (Mayer & Jackson, 2005). Extra mildly relevant items make people's judgments "much less extreme" (Nisbett et al., 1981), and evaluators average a case, so "mildly favorable information dilutes the impact of highly favorable information" (Weaver et al., 2012).
  - **Trust, the closest found.** Focus groups only: "The word 'might' ruins everything... It's kind of sketchy" (KFF Health News, 2011); testers asked for "In this example, you would pay" over "You might pay" (JKM Research, 2011). No experiment found measures trust in a figure given one clause of basis against the same figure given its mechanics (UNVERIFIED as stated; Gaps).
  - **Limit.** A clause cannot rescue a figure read as a forecast: PRIIPs' four performance scenarios were read as predictions, and the FCA judged the narrative warning "too short and small to impact the way consumers were interpreting" them (FS19/01, 2019).
  - **Judge's note:** writers add supporting points because each one helps; readers average them, so each weaker one subtracts (the presenter's paradox).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 1 with rows 2-3 (Peters et al., 2007), for 3a.** Deleting off-topic figures raised best choices from 40% to 62%; moving them to the end did not reliably help; numerate readers gained only from deletion. This is the evidence for the finalist's "one number", and the reason the cut lands in Figure 1's note and the store, not in 3a's last sentence.
2. **Rows 36 and 38 (SBC coverage examples; FCA Occasional Paper 19), for 3a's and 3c's first sentences.** The same figure had double the effect on page one and none inside; half of readers missed the examples at the back. The reserve's size or the price goes in 3a's first sentence, and the figure co-sponsors count in 3c's.
3. **Rows 22-23 (Mayer & Jackson, 2005; Verkoeijen & Tabbers, 2009), for 3c.** Computations interspersed in a plain explanation hurt understanding; placed after it, they did not. The rule's rounding and cut leave the sentence that says who owns each end.
4. **Row 27 (the presenter's paradox), for 3a's outside evidence and for the team's own process.** Writers add, readers average, so each weaker figure or source lowers the case. It is the written reason Book 17 asks for when an idea goes to the store instead of the page.
5. **Row 32 (PRIIPs' performance scenarios read as forecasts), for Tables 2 and 3.** A regulator's own document failed because a "moderate" column read as a prediction and a short warning could not fix it. Table 2's columns are named by market, never "central".

## Gaps

- **Trust measured directly (claim 3).** No experiment was found that compares trust in a figure given one plain clause of basis with trust in the same figure given its mechanics. The closest are focus groups (rows 34-35), the dilution and averaging studies (rows 27-28) and Q71 row 26's precision study. Next round: search "source credibility" and "processing fluency" with financial figures (Reber-Schwarz is in Q14b row 7).
- **A tested cap on figures per paragraph.** None found. ONS's "two or three" per article and the drug box's 9 rows are conventions or single designs, not limits. Kosslyn's four units (Q14a) is about graphs. Cowan (2001), on how many items working memory holds, was not read.
- **Expert readers.** Almost every test used the public or students. Expertise reversal (Kalyuga) suggests some cuts that help novices may not help experts; no test on investment professionals reading a written case was found. Not read: Kalyuga (2005).
- **Not reached or not read at source:** the full texts of Schwartz et al. (2009) and Woloshin and Schwartz (2011) beyond abstracts and hosted copies in part; Hibbard et al. (2002) on evaluability in health plan reports; Zikmund-Fisher (2013) on precision in risk numbers; Nielsen (2008), the root of GOV.UK's "20 to 28%"; the ESAs' 2016 PRIIPs consumer testing; the 2009 UCITS key investor information testing; FCA mortgage Key Facts testing; Tiede et al. (2020) on the number of options, found but not read past its abstract.
- **A test on a range.** No study was found that tests one stated range with its basis against the same range with its rounding rule shown. Card Q71 covers how listeners hear a range; this card covers how many figures sit around it.
