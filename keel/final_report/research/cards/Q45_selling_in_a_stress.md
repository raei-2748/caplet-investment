# Q45. Selling in a stress: what the plan's three trade moments and TNA l.15's "liquid" cost when markets seize

- **Date:** Sun 15 Nov 2026, Sydney (sources read Sun 15 Nov 2026; fund pages show data as of 23 Oct to 13 Nov 2026).
- **Report parts:** 2.2 (the holdings and their coherence with TNA l.15), 2.4 (how the book would be traded), 3c (when the bottom and the top are owned; the 2031 move), 3e (assumptions and limits: trading costs), Table 3 row "Trades in 2027, 2028, 2031" (exhibits/tables.md l.94). Most rows are for the store only.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Not repeated:** CANON B22, B23, B24, C2, C16, C18, D5 (the TNA's iBond claims and their status); NUMBERS 3 (cash wait, the 0.5% haircut, the friction test at 0.75% and 1%) and 8p (the 40% fall rows); card Q36 rows 9-10 and 21 (closure mechanics; Boehmer and Finta's discount after a closure notice; "wait for NAV"); card Q26 rows 7, 11, 14, 21 and T16-T18 (no leverage so no forced sale; extension past 31 Dec; costs inside NAV; Rule 6c-11's "greater than 2% for more than seven consecutive trading days"); card Q30 Table 3 rows 4-5 and the T16/T18 merges; card Q15 rows 3 and 6; card Q24 rows 33-35 (where each holding trades; SIFMA holidays; cash dates); card Q39 row 8 (ICI's "Even government bond ETFs ... in excess of 100 basis points" quote; Haddad, Moreira and Muir's larger discounts for safer bonds).
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Treasury ETFs, including iShares iBonds Term Treasury funds, traded close to their net asset value in March 2020, unlike corporate bond ETFs."
  2. "The Treasury market itself was hard to sell in March 2020 ('dash for cash'), so even Treasuries cost more to sell in a stress."
  3. "Selling an odd lot of individual Treasuries (under $1 million) costs a small investor much more than the quoted price."
  4. "Selling half of VT (a $30,000-$40,000 position) before the 2031 meetings costs little even in a crash."
- **Problem class:** liquidation cost under stress for a small holder: what a seller (or buyer) of a fund share or a small bond lot gives up against the value of the holdings, in normal markets and in a seized one. **Field that solved it first:** market microstructure research on bond ETFs and the Treasury market in March 2020 (ICI, IOSCO, BIS, SEC staff, the Fed and the New York Fed), with trade-size cost work from TRACE (SEC economic analysis, Fed staff, MSRB) and the Bank of England's gilt study.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Treasury ETFs traded much nearer their value than corporate bond ETFs, but not "close", and the long ones least of all. The iBond funds' own March 2020 record was not found.
  - **Government bond ETFs as a group.** The largest median discount in March 2020 was -0.5%, the same as equity ETFs, against -2.0% for investment grade and high yield (IOSCO, US ETFs, SEC staff data). Asset-weighted, they were over 100bp below NAV on 11 and 12 March (ICI; already in Q39 row 8).
  - **Long Treasuries.** TLT "traded at a 5% discount in March", with "larger dislocation where bonds have a longer maturity" (ICE Data Services). On its own mutual fund twin, Vanguard's long Treasury ETF (VGLT) went from -0.85% (11 Mar) to +1.58% (19 Mar), against ±0.05% in early February (Keel arithmetic on Yahoo Finance prices).
  - **Short and intermediate Treasuries traded at a premium, not a discount.** VGSH and VGIT were up to +0.38% (12 Mar) and +0.49% (13 Mar) above their own mutual fund share classes, and never below -0.01% (Keel arithmetic, same data). A seller lost nothing. A buyer paid up.
  - **Corporate bond ETFs.** LQD was 5.08% below NAV on 19 Mar (MSCI). Investment grade bond ETFs averaged 5.3% below NAV that day, 6.9% for short-duration ones, and some high-yield ETFs were more than 9% below (BIS). Asset-weighted, investment grade was about 365bp below and high yield about 220bp (ICI).
  - **iBonds Treasury.** IBTA-IBTK launched 25 Feb 2020 (IBTH page), weeks before the stress. Their premium and discount history for March 2020 was not found (UNVERIFIED). IBTM itself began on 6 Jul 2022 and has no 2020 record.
  - **Why it matters little for Laura.** Held to the end, a fund pays NAV (CANON A8). Prices only matter on the plan's own trade days. By 2031 the late-2032 fund is a short-dated Treasury fund, the kind that went to a premium in 2020.
  - **Judge's note:** in March 2020, short Treasury funds traded above their holdings' value and long ones up to 5% below; corporate funds 5-9% below.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with sizes; the cost fell hardest on old, long bonds.**
  - **Spreads.** Dealer-to-customer bid-ask spreads rose "by a multiple of over 10" and interdealer spreads by about 5 (Duffie). The 10-year on-the-run's spread went from about 0.5 to 1.4 ticks by 13 Mar. The 30-year's spread rose to more than 6 times its post-crisis average. Price impact was 5-6 times normal on 12-13 Mar (IAWG; Fleming and Ruela).
  - **Off-the-run effective spreads,** round trip, for trades of $1m-$25m (New York Fed SR 1170, Table 3):
    - 2-year: on-the-run 0.66bp to 1.38bp; the 2nd off-the-run 2.23bp to 7.45bp.
    - 10-year: on-the-run 2.07bp to 5.13bp; the 1st off-the-run 3.73bp to 16.86bp.
    - 30-year: the 2nd off-the-run 12.35bp to 73.39bp; the 5th 18.69bp to 129.00bp.
  - **Laura's kind of bond:** old 30-year issues with 7-10 years left went from 40.36bp to 52.66bp; with 3-5 years left, from 17.92bp to 47.62bp.
  - **Who sold.** Foreign holders sold $287bn, mutual funds $266bn, and households including hedge funds $196bn, mostly in off-the-runs (SR 1170 citing Vissing-Jorgensen). The Fed bought about $1tn in 2020Q1. Liquidity was "significantly toward normal by mid April" (Duffie).
  - **Judge's note:** Laura's T-bonds are exactly the old 30-year issues that were hardest to sell in March 2020; she never has to sell them.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** For Treasuries, an odd lot costs only a little more than a large trade in normal markets. The claim holds for corporate and municipal odd lots, and partly for Laura's own deep off-the-run bonds.
  - **All Treasuries, Aug 2021-Jul 2022.** Retail-size trades (≤$100k) had an effective spread of 0.07%, against 0.04% for larger ones: "only 3 bps higher", the lowest of seven fixed income categories (SEC, Regulation Best Execution proposal, Table 17).
  - **Other categories in the same table.** Corporate 0.52% against 0.25%; municipal 0.57% against 0.29%; agency 0.35% against 0.15%.
  - **UK gilts.** The median cost of a £1-£100,000 trade was 0.5-1.4bp from the day's average price. After allowing for who the client is, small trades cost no more (Pintér, Wang and Zou).
  - **Retail pays more.** Trades under $1mm "may only be reasonably attributed to retail investors", and retail investors "pay higher effective bid-ask spreads" (Sokolinskiy; no number in the text read). 63.7% of dealer-to-customer Treasury trades are under $1m. 98.4% of interdealer trades are in round $1m lots, so an odd lot cannot use the interdealer order book (SR 1036).
  - **Laura's five bonds are original 30-year issues of 2007-2011.** At institutional size their effective spread is about 0.18%-0.40% round trip in normal times (SR 1170); retail-size costs for such issues were not found. A one-way sale of a $25,000 piece is about $22-$50 in normal markets and $66 at March 2020's mean. The deepest issues reached about $161 (Keel arithmetic, half of the effective spread).
  - **The iBond funds.** Quoted 30-day median spreads are 0.04% (IBTR, 13 Nov 2026) and 0.05% (IBTM, 23 Oct 2026): about $5-$6 one way on $25,000.
  - **What it means for TNA l.15.** "a level of liquidity which no odd-lot bond offers" holds against corporate and municipal odd lots and against old off-the-run Treasuries in normal markets. It overstates the gap for Treasuries in general (0.07% against the fund's 0.04-0.05%), and in a stress a Treasury fund's discount can cost more than the bond's spread. The cost bites only on a forced sale; the plan holds every bond to maturity.
  - **Judge's note:** small Treasury trades are the cheapest small trades in US bonds; the funds' edge is a public price every second, not a much lower cost.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, sized; the brief's position size is CHANGED.**
  - **The size.** VT is $36,989 on 1 Jan 2031 centrally, so the moved half is about $18,494. After a 40% fall it is about $8,949 (0.6 times the $29,829 bought; NUMBERS 4 and 8p). The brief's "$30,000-$40,000" is all of VT, not the half sold.
  - **Normal markets.** VT's 30-day median spread is 0.01% (0.013% on 10 Nov 2026). Selling half of VT and buying the Treasury fund costs about $6 centrally and $3 after the fall (Keel arithmetic).
  - **A March-2020 week.** VT's own gap moved from -0.31% (12 Mar) to +0.66% (17 Mar) against its mutual fund twin, from ±0.08% in February. US equity ETFs' median spread peaked at 0.8% (IOSCO). The short Treasury fund bought could be at a +0.49% premium. All in, about 1.45% of the moved half: about $267 centrally and $129 after a 40% fall. The severe bound is 2.29%: $421 and $204.
  - **The cushion.** The cost lowers the unrounded top by about the same amount. The margin above the stated top is $3,443 centrally and $3,250 after a fall (NUMBERS 4, 8p). The top is worked out after the move, on the holdings bought, so a trading cost can lower what is stated but cannot make a stated figure unowned. One exception is in row 37.
  - **The 2020 fall itself.** VT fell 34.2% from 12 Feb to 23 Mar 2020 and rose 25.0% from the low by 17 Apr (adjusted closes). That is close to 8p's 40% case. ETF discounts lasted "only up to two weeks" (IOSCO), well inside the IPS's "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031".
  - **Judge's note:** even in a March-2020 week, moving half the shares costs about 1.5% of what is moved: tens to a few hundred dollars, far less than one $5,000 step of the top.
- **TNA l.15 in one line:** "liquid" is safe to stand behind for VT and all five iBond funds in normal markets (spreads 0.01%-0.05%; IBTR now $112m). "No odd-lot bond offers" is safe only against corporate, municipal and old off-the-run bonds, never as "small Treasury lots are hard to sell". Neither the funds nor VT should ever be said to trade at their value in a crash.

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Treasury funds in March 2020: closer to value than corporate funds, not close (rows 1, 4-14)
- **What is it?** In March 2020 bond ETFs traded below the value of their holdings for about two weeks. Government bond ETFs' largest median discount was -0.5%, the same as equity ETFs; investment grade and high yield reached -2.0% (IOSCO). The long-dated Treasury ETFs were the outliers: TLT about 5% below NAV (ICE), VGLT -0.85% on 11 March (Keel arithmetic). Short and intermediate Treasury ETFs went the other way, to a premium of up to +0.38% (VGSH) and +0.49% (VGIT).
- **Did it work?** As an exit, yes. The funds traded every minute while the bonds barely traded. SEC staff: ETFs "generally functioned as expected" (row 10). As a store of value at its NAV, not always: a seller of a long Treasury fund on 11-12 March gave up about 1% or more.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Five of her ten payments and the whole range sit in iBond Treasury funds. On a sale day the price, not the NAV, is what she gets.
- **How is it not?** She holds each fund to its end and is paid NAV (CANON A8). Her funds hold 2032-2036 bonds, not 20-year bonds. By 2031 the late-2032 fund is a short Treasury fund, the kind that traded at a premium in 2020. The iBond funds' own March 2020 record was not found.
- **What Caplet takes.** 2.2 or 3e, one clause at most: in March 2020 short Treasury funds traded at or above the value of their holdings and corporate bond funds 5%-9% below. Never "Treasury ETFs traded at NAV in the crash". Store: rows 6-9, 11.

### Card 2. The dash for cash: even Treasuries cost more to sell, old long bonds most (rows 19-25)
- **What is it?** Over 9-18 March 2020, hedge funds, foreign holders and bond mutual funds sold Treasuries for cash: $287bn, $266bn and $196bn, mostly off-the-run (row 24). Dealers ran out of balance sheet. Customer spreads rose more than tenfold (Duffie). Older issues suffered most: a 30-year bond five issues old went from 18.69bp to 129.00bp round trip (SR 1170).
- **Did it work?** The market stopped working well for about ten days. The Fed bought about $1tn in 2020Q1, and liquidity was "significantly toward normal by mid April" (row 21).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her five T-bonds are original 30-year issues of 2007-2011: the deep off-the-runs that were hardest to sell. In 2027-2031 they have 6-15 years left. In March 2020 that kind of bond cost 0.5% round trip (row 23).
- **How is it not?** She never has to sell them: each is held to its payment, she has no leverage, and a market fall is not one of the IPS's two reasons (IPS l.17). A held-to-maturity bond's payment did not change in March 2020.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3e: "even Treasuries cost more to sell in a stress" is true, and it is the reason the plan never sells them. A judge may know the dash for cash; one clause shows the team does too. Store: rows 19-21, 24-25.

### Card 3. Small Treasury lots are cheap; TNA l.15's comparison holds for other bonds (rows 26-32)
- **What is it?** On TRACE data for August 2021 to July 2022, a retail-size Treasury trade (≤$100k) had an effective spread of 0.07%, against 0.04% for larger trades: the cheapest small trades in US fixed income. The same table shows corporate at 0.52%, municipal at 0.57% and agency at 0.35% for small trades (SEC, row 26). Gilts show the same pattern: £1-£100,000 trades cost about a basis point (row 30).
- **Did it work?** For small Treasury holders, yes: their prices are close to dealers' own (dispersion 1.38 times interdealer, row 27). Retail still pays more than institutions, and quoted spreads overstate the cost of old issues (row 29).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Each of her T-bond positions is under $100,000. CANON B24 asks for exactly this evidence before the report leans on TNA l.15's "a level of liquidity which no odd-lot bond offers".
- **How is it not?** The 0.07% is an average over all Treasuries, which bills and new issues dominate. Her bonds are deep off-the-run, about 0.18%-0.40% round trip even at institutional size (row 23). Against those, the funds' 0.04%-0.05% is a real edge in normal markets.
- **What Caplet takes.** CANON B24 moves from UNVERIFIED to "true against corporate, municipal and old off-the-run bonds; overstated for Treasuries in general". The report never repeats the TNA line. If it touches liquidity, say what is measured: each fund trades at a public price every business day with a spread of about 0.05%. Store: rows 28, 31-32.

### Card 4. The 2031 move in a crash costs tens to a few hundred dollars (rows 2, 14-16, 37)
- **What is it?** Before the meetings, half of VT is sold and the money buys the late-2032 Treasury fund (IPS l.15). VT's normal spread is 0.01%. In March 2020, VT's price moved from 0.31% below to 0.66% above its own fund's NAV (row 14). Equity ETFs' median spreads peaked at 0.8% (row 2).
- **Did it work?** In 2020 a seller of VT on the worst close lost about 0.3% against value, plus the spread. VT fell 34.2% in six weeks and was 25.0% off its low a month later (row 15).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** On register figures the moved half is about $18,494 centrally and $8,949 after a 40% fall (NUMBERS 4, 8p). A March-2020 week costs about 1.45% of it: $267 or $129 (row 37).
- **How is it not?** The top is worked out after the move, on the holdings actually bought, so a trading cost lowers what is stated; it never leaves a stated figure unowned. The cushion between the unrounded and stated top ($3,443 centrally, $3,250 after a fall) is more than ten times the stress cost. The IPS gives no fixed day, so a stressed fortnight can be waited out (row 3; Table 3: "Changes: the date, never past the 2031 meetings").
- **What Caplet takes.** 3c, a note: even in a March-2020 week, moving half the shares would cost about 1.5% of what moves, a few hundred dollars at most, far less than one $5,000 step of the top. Never "free" and never "protected from crashes". The brief's "$30,000-$40,000 position" is all of VT; the sale is half.

### Card 5. The buy leg: a Treasury fund's premium against the 0.5% haircut (rows 12, 37)
- **What is it?** In March 2020 short and intermediate Treasury ETFs traded up to about 0.5% above NAV (row 12). Add half of the government ETF median spread (0.25%, row 2), and buying such a fund cost about 0.74% over the value of its holdings.
- **Did it work?** For buyers in 2020 it was a real cost against NAV, gone by April (+0.01% to +0.13%).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Two purchases set the range: the floor in January 2028 and the moved half in 2031. NUMBERS 3 says "Each end is the money put into the late-2032 fund times what $1 of the fund's holdings pays", less 0.5% for "the fund's own frictions". If "money put in" means dollars spent, a premium over 0.5% sits outside the haircut. NUMBERS 3's own test shows a 0.75% friction on the whole value breaking an end in 5.0%/6.0% of house paths.
- **How is it not?** The fix is arithmetic, not strategy. Count the money put in as shares bought times that day's NAV, and the premium is a cost outside the figure, so nothing stated can be unowned. In normal markets the premium is about 0.06% (IBTM, row 18).
- **What Caplet takes.** For the numbers owner: word NUMBERS 3 as "shares bought times NAV on the trade day". For Table 3's trade row: "limit orders near each holding's value" can be read as within about 0.25% of NAV for the Treasury fund (half the haircut); Rule 6c-11's 2%-for-seven-days flag (Q26 row 21) is far looser than the plan's 0.5%. Not report prose.

### Card 6. IBTR is no longer a small fund (rows 17-18)
- **What is it?** IBTR, the 2037 rung's fund, had net assets of $112,281,580 on 13 Nov 2026, a 30-day median spread of 0.04% and a premium of 0.05%. It launched on 25 Mar 2026 and holds 4 bonds.
- **Did it work?** Its spread is now as tight as IBTM's (0.05%, $603m).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** CANON B22 and C18 call "liquid" weakest for IBTR on its $11.2m of 30 Jun 2026. That reason is gone.
- **How is it not?** A fund with 4 holdings and eight months of history has no stress record; IBTM has none before 2022 either.
- **What Caplet takes.** CANON B22 and C18 to be updated by the lead. In 2.2, if the size of a fund is mentioned, use the dated figure.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
- In March 2020, short and intermediate Treasury ETFs traded above the value of their holdings, by up to about 0.5%, while long Treasury ETFs fell to as much as 5% below and corporate bond ETFs to 5%-9% below. A seller of a short Treasury fund lost nothing; a buyer paid up (rows 8, 12-13).
- Small Treasury trades are the cheapest small trades in US bonds: 0.07% round trip for trades up to $100,000, against 0.52% for corporate bonds and 0.57% for municipal bonds (row 26).
- In the dash for cash, a 30-year Treasury five issues old went from about 0.19% to 1.29% round trip at institutional size, and dealer-to-customer spreads rose more than tenfold. Those old long bonds are exactly the kind Laura holds and never has to sell (rows 21-23).
- Moving half the shares in a March-2020 week would cost about $130-$270 on register figures, less than a tenth of the room between the unrounded and the stated top (row 37).
- The fund behind the 2037 payment grew tenfold in five months, to $112m, and now trades at a 0.04% spread (row 17).
