- **Problem class:** when does money set aside for a promise count as keeping the promise? Accountants call the question derecognition. **Field that worked it out:** US accounting standard-setting, three times over: company debt (FASB 1982, 1983, 1996), pension promises (FASB 1985), and the definition of a liability itself (Concepts Statements 3, 6 and 8, 1980-2021).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. The rule-makers changed their test, not their view of Treasuries.**
- (a) In 1983 America's accounting rule-makers asked who still carries the risk, and four of the seven said a company that puts matched Treasuries into a trust it cannot take back carries none, so its debt is gone; in 1996 they asked who controls the money, found the company still did, and put the debt back on its books.
- (b) Laura's ten pass the first test on the money from January 2028 and fail it only because she can take her Treasuries back; they fail the second test because the Treasuries are hers, which is exactly what ink's word "owned" says.
- (c) Neither test found anything wrong with the Treasuries, because none of the 1996 reasons questions them: the rule-makers changed only the question, from risk to whose money it is; so the report keeps Book 19's strong words for the Treasuries and drops only the words that say the promise is already gone.
- Decision served: the conceit's two-part definition of ink; the verbs in 3a. Report part: 2.0, 3a. Sources: rows 1-4, 8-10, 19. **Pedigree or aha:** the history is pedigree for an accounting judge; that ink's two halves ("owned" and "will pay it on its date") are the two eras' two tests is an aha, and it answers Book 19's "startling new perspective" with a source behind it.

**2. Laura's ladder is the case accountants ruled on for pensions in 1985 (the best companion).**
- (a) In 1985 the same rule-makers decided that a pension plan which buys high-quality bonds paying on the dates its pensions fall due has reduced its risks but settled nothing, because "the investment decision can be reversed", the employer still owes the pensions, and the bonds cannot cover pensioners living longer than expected.
- (b) Laura's ladder is that case with one difference that matters: her ten are fixed at $50,000 on fixed dates, so there is no lifespan to guess, and the risk a pension's bonds cannot cover does not exist for her; from January 2028 her Treasuries pay all ten even if cash waiting earns nothing. That leaves one reason her ten are not settled, that she could sell, and the IPS answers it only with its own rule of two reasons to change (l.17).
- (c) The parallel breaks because pensioners can go to court and Laura's residency has no one to do so yet; what the break teaches is that the plan's strength sits in the case's fixed payments and in the Treasuries, and its one soft spot is a promise kept by her own rule, which 3e names in one sentence.
- Decision served: the ladder (IPS l.13), IPS l.17's two reasons, 3e's limits. Report part: 3a, 3e. Sources: rows 11-14. **Pedigree or aha:** niche to most finance judges, pedigree to a pension accountant; an aha because it predicts something checkable (rows 11-13 applied to Laura fail on reversal and responsibility, never on risk) and shows that a fact in the case, the fixed amounts, is what lets Treasuries come so close to settling the ten.

**3. The 1996 reasons that apply to Laura are about whose money it is, not whether it pays.**
- (a) In 1996 the rule-makers listed six things a trust of Treasuries lacks, and the only one about safety is that if the company defaults on something, its lender can demand the debt early, and then the company "must make up the difference".
- (b) No one can bring Laura's payment dates forward, so that reason cannot happen to her; the reasons that do apply are that the residency signed nothing, cannot take the Treasuries, and has no claim on what is left over, which comes back to her (about $46,600 in 2033 money if rates do not fall, NUMBERS 8h).
- (c) This corrects the parent card, which read "make up the difference" as IPS l.17's two reasons: of the failures accountants name, only a Treasury default matches one of the two (the 1983 rule's "failure to realize scheduled cash flows from trust assets"), and a missed deposit matches none, because before January 2028 the money for the ten is not yet all set aside.
- Decision served: IPS l.17 and how 3e words it; WORDS row 24's "the money for each payment is already in Treasuries Laura owns". Report part: 3a, 3e. Sources: rows 6, 15-17. **Pedigree or aha:** a correction, store-first; a modest aha for a judge who knows the 1996 rule.

**4. "Met" is January's word; "owned" is today's.**
- (a) The case asks that the payments be "met with a high degree of certainty" (IPS l.11 quotes it), and in every rulebook read a payment is met only when the person owed it is paid: the 1983 dissenters wrote "For a debt to be satisfied, the creditor must be satisfied", and since 1996 that has been the rule.
- (b) So the report says the strongest true thing about now, that the money for each payment is already in Treasuries she owns and that from January 2028 they pay every payment on its date even if cash waiting earns nothing, and it uses "met" only for each January.
- (c) The line moves in 2031: once co-sponsors hear her figure and plan around it, accounting would call it an obligation, because others are "justified in relying" on it, and its pension rule counts a step only once done ("The probability of completion of the irrevocable action is not relevant"); that is the accounting reason the shares move into Treasuries before the meetings, so her figure is ink before anyone relies on it.
- Decision served: the verbs in 3a; 3c's order (move, then state); 3d. Report part: 3a, 3c, 3d. Sources: rows 5, 14, 20-24, 26-27. **Pedigree or aha:** the verdict on words is housekeeping; "reliance begins in 2031, and the money is owned before it begins" is a modest aha for 3c.

## Best three for the report

1. **Row 10 with rows 3 and 9: ink's two halves are the two eras' two tests.** "Will pay it on its date" is the 1983 risk test and "owned" is the 1996 control test; ink is money whose risk is gone and whose control is kept, which is why it is never "paid". It gives the conceit's definition a sourced, startling pedigree in one clause, and it serves 2.0.
2. **Rows 11-13: the 1985 pension ruling is Laura's case.** FASB called matched high-quality bonds "not a settlement because the investment decision can be reversed", and also because they leave mortality risk. Laura's fixed $50,000 payments have no mortality risk, so her one soft spot is her own decision, which IPS l.17 binds. That gives 3e its one honest sentence and shows why the case's fixed payments matter.
3. **Rows 14, 21-23: "met" is January's word, and reliance begins in 2031.** Accounting counts a step only once it is done, and a promise becomes an obligation when others rely on it. That is why 3a says "already in Treasuries she owns", never "already met", and why 3c moves the shares before Laura states her figure.
