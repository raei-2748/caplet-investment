## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KILL.** Forward rates are not fair forecasts of later short rates; they carry a premium that changes over time.
  - **The evidence.** Fama and Bliss (1987) split each forward rate into the expected change in the 1-year rate plus an expected premium (their eq. 6); forwards forecast the 1-year rate's change 2-4 years ahead, which they put down to "a mean-reverting tendency". Campbell and Shiller (1991): a steep curve forecasts rising short rates over the long run but a falling long yield over the short run, "inconsistent with the expectations theory". Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005): one combination of forward rates predicts bond excess returns "with R 2 up to 0.44"; the expectations hypothesis "is equivalent to the statement that excess returns should not be predictable".
  - **The size today.** On Fri 6 Nov 2026, the Fed's Kim-Wright model put the 10-year yield's term premium at 0.87 points and the premium in the forward rate ten years out at 1.58 points (FRED THREEFYTP10, THREEFFTP10). October 2026's 1.01 was the highest monthly average since April 2010. Since 1990 it averaged 0.83 (1990s 1.72, 2010s 0.22) and was below zero in 58 of 442 months (2012-13, 2016, 2019-21, 2023).
  - **Forwards as forecasts.** The New York Fed found that, over the long run, "the forward rate performs least well of the four methods" it tested; its model forecasts beat it "by as much as 3 percentage points and never less than 1" (Adrian, Crump, Diamond and Yu, 2016). The FOMC's own longer-run median for its rate is 3.2% (Sep 2026), against the register's 5.43% forwards for 2033-2042 (NUMBERS 8a).
  - **In the record (Keel arithmetic, approximate).** From 1962, each month's 1-year forward rate 5 years ahead was above the 1-year yield actually seen 5 years later by 1.28 points on average (69% of months), and 10 years ahead by 1.76 points (67%). But for start months before 1981 the forwards were below what came, by 1.55 and 2.02 points. From 1981 they were above, by 2.60 and 3.76. One history cannot split premium from surprise, which is why the models disagree on the size ("The sign of term premium estimates can at times be different", Li, Meldrum and Rodriguez, 2017).
  - **Judge's note:** "priced at market" means priced on the market's forward rates, which the New York Fed found to be the worst long-run forecast of rates it tested. A ladder sized there is sized on a figure that includes a premium.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** The 55.6% is one rate cycle, about four independent periods, and its size depends on how history is read.
  - **One cycle.** Of the 228 windows starting 1962-1980 (rates rising), 39 were short (17.1%). Of the 357 starting 1981-2010 (rates falling), 286 were (80.1%). Four long runs of start months (Sep 1980-Jul 1986, Feb 1987-Nov 1991, May 1994-Oct 2001, Nov 2004-May 2010) hold 286 of the 325 short windows.
  - **Few independent tries.** 777 months hold 4.05 non-overlapping 16-year windows. A mean of overlapping 16-year sums has the precision of about 3.4 independent ones. A Bartlett estimate on the short/not-short sequence gives about 13 (the sequence's month-to-month correlation is 0.91). With 4 the 95% interval on 55.6% runs from 18% to 88%; with 13, from 31% to 78%; treated as 585 independent tries it would be 51.5%-59.5%, which is false precision.
  - **How big when short.** Among the 325 short windows the median shortfall is $16,055 (middle half $7,296 to $22,459); 226 are short by more than $10,000 and 62 by more than $25,000; the worst is $45,266 (start Jul 2007). NUMBERS 2's "usually small (median $1,592)" is the median over all 585 windows, 44% of them at $0. Short windows starting before 1981 have a median of $3,645; from 1981, $17,062.
  - **Other readings of the same years.** History's moves with the trend taken out: 42.2% short (median $0, 1-in-20 $30,766, worst $43,093). Every move reversed: 41.2% (worst $26,594). History's forecast errors added to today's forwards: 67.2% (median $20,096, 1-in-20 $42,733, worst $51,399, start Jun 1982; before 1981 15.8%, from 1981 every window).
  - **Judge's note:** a cold reader expected the shortfall to be "$500 or $30,000"; the honest answer is that in the falling-rate era it was short in four windows out of five, typically by about $16,000.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE (the professor is right about the premium, wrong about the direction for this replay).**
  - **What the replay does.** The register's replay (A35) multiplies today's forwards by each window's 1-year yield relative to its start. A flat history means today's forwards come true. So the 55.6% measures the drift in the history (forty years of falls, and the 2009-15 near-zero years mapped in proportion), not a premium in today's curve. In the average window the 1-year yield 16 years on was 0.52 of its start (geometric mean).
  - **What a premium does.** Centre a trend-free history on today's forwards and 42.2% of windows are short: a ladder sized exactly at the market's central figure fails often even with no premium. Centre it 0.9 points lower (Kim-Wright's 10-year premium on 6 Nov) and 54.7% are short (median $965, 1-in-20 $35,427). Centre it 1.6 points lower (the forward premium ten years out) and 73.3% are (median $8,765).
  - **So.** The 55.6% sits about where a trend-free history around the Fed model's expected path lands. If the premium is as large as the far forwards suggest, it understates the risk to a ladder sized at forwards today. It does not overstate it. The robust statement is the direction and the size when short, not the percentage.
  - **Judge's note:** the term premium works for Laura on the bonds she holds and against her on the coupons she must reinvest, so the professor's point strengthens the case for sizing the payments without reinvestment.
- **Claim 4: KILL as evidence; KEEP as construction.** At zero interest the history cannot make the standard fail (NUMBERS 2 already says so). An insurer's reserves must meet "moderately adverse" conditions, defined as "unfavorable, but not extreme" (ASOP 22, sec. 2.9); on Keel's reading, sixteen years of waiting cash earning nothing sits at the extreme end, beyond that standard, and is chosen on purpose. Japan lived it: its overnight call rate averaged under 0.5% in every year from 1996 to 2024, below zero in every year 2016-2023, after 7.53% in 1991 (OECD via FRED). Say the standard "cannot fall short by design" and leave the 585 out of that sentence.
  - **Judge's note:** what an insurer's actuary may treat as extreme, Japan, one of the largest economies, lived through for 29 years.
- **Claim 5: KILL.** Only escrow verifiers size at zero (card Q20). Insurers size at risk-free forward rates and hold capital for the rest. The PRA's matching Test 1 accumulates shortfalls "at the risk-free rate" and allows up to "3% of the present value of liabilities" (about $9,046 on the ten's $301,544, Keel arithmetic). Solvency II's best estimate is "the probability-weighted average of future cash-flows" on the risk-free curve, with a risk margin and capital on top. The US's actuarial standards name reinvestment as an assumption to choose (ASOP 7, 2026), test "moderately adverse" conditions (ASOP 22) and run New York's seven scenarios (Q20). PBGC's 2021 rescue money is sized as if it earns 67 basis points above the average of three corporate-bond segment rates (29 CFR 4262.4(e)(2)).
  - **Judge's note:** a UK annuity insurer's first matching test would pass the ladder as written, sized at forwards. The insurer covers the rest with capital Laura does not have. Her extra Treasuries do that capital's job.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 12 with row 13 (3a, 3e).** "Short in about one start in six while rates rose, four in five while they fell, typically by about $16,000": the honest form of the 55.6%, and the answer to both of the cold reader's questions (overlap and size).
2. **Row 9 with row 5 (3a).** The market's forward rates were the worst long-run forecast the New York Fed tested, and on the freeze day a Fed model put 1-1.6 points of premium in the forwards for Laura's reinvestment years: "priced at market" is priced on a figure with a premium in it, which is why the payments are sized without reinvestment.
3. **Row 27 with row 29 (3a).** A UK annuity insurer's first matching test would pass the ladder as written; the insurer covers the rest with capital, and Laura's extra Treasuries are that capital. It shows the judge the design sits where regulated practice sits, then goes one step further for a client with no capital behind her.
4. **Row 17 (3e, or a footnote-sized clause in 3a).** Taking the trend out and allowing for today's premium, about as many windows fall short as the register says: the professor's term premium does not make the 55.6% an overstatement. It keeps the report from conceding a point it would otherwise lose.
5. **Row 24 with row 32 (3a).** What US insurer actuaries are not required to test ("not extreme"), Japan lived through for 29 years. It answers the finalist's "why would interest ever earn nothing" alongside Q2d's US record.

**For question 4 (what 3a's one or two sentences must carry; content notes, not report prose; the writers set the words):**
- Fact 1, the risk: sized at today's forward rates, the ten fall short in more than half of 585 overlapping 16-year windows since 1962, about four independent periods; nearly all of it in the falling-rate years after 1981 (four in five starts, typically about $16,000 short), little before (one in six).
- Fact 2, why the history is not the point: forward rates carry a premium over expected rates (a Fed model put it near a point on 6 Nov 2026), so a promise sized at them falls short about half the time or more by construction. The extra Treasuries therefore size the ten so they are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, which no history can break: by construction, not by evidence.
- A plain-words truth test the final sentence must pass: it names the overlap, the era split and the size; it never puts "585" next to the zero standard; it never says "55.6% chance".
