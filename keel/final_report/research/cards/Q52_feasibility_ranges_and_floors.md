- **Problem class:** stating a future sum of money to people who will act on it before all of it is in hand: a forecast with a floor, given with words of confidence.
  - **Field that solved it first:** campaign counsel. Its feasibility or planning study is the standard tool (AFP's Ready Reference), and its outcome surveys show how often goals are met.
  - **For the words:** linguistics' work on "modified numerals" ("at least three", "more than two"), from Geurts and Nouwen (2007) on.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Fewer than half give a range, and none gives a number for its confidence.
  - Of 20 recommendations read at source (2009-2026), 8 gave the board a range and 12 a single figure. Two of the 12 were worded as a floor (row 1).
  - AFP's checklist promises "A recommended overall dollar goal" (one figure). Kindsight promises "a likely fundraising range".
  - Confidence is given in words: "doable and feasible", "a high level of confidence", "reasonably optimistic", "achievable, however, tempered by realistic concerns" (row 3).
  - The only numbers are polls: the share of interviewees calling the goal realistic (CCS benchmark 39%) or AFP's "at least 80 percent of the board" (row 5).
  - Where a range is given, the confidence sits on its bottom. The gift chart is drawn for the bottom, and the top is "the potential to reach" (row 4).
  - **Judge's note:** campaign counsel will not put a number on its confidence. Laura can, because after the move both ends are owned: "certain" (NUMBERS 4).
- **Claim 2: KILL.** Laura's range is narrower than every recommended range read.
  - The eight ranges boards were given ran from 22% to 50% of their midpoint, median 28.6% (Keel arithmetic, row 2).
  - Laura's $95,000-$115,000 is 19.0% of its midpoint (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4).
  - Their ranges were mostly money no one had offered yet: interviews found about 10% to 53% of the goal (row 7). Hers is owned in Treasuries before she says it.
  - **Judge's note:** a judge may hear "a range" as hedging. In the fundraising world, Laura's range is narrower than any range we found a consultant giving a board, and it is the only one already bought.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** "At least" held only when the floor was fixed or owned.
  - **Ruth Lilly's gift to *Poetry*** was announced as "well in excess of $100 million". It was "a conservative" estimate "depending on the performance of Eli Lilly stock".
    - The trusts were funded entirely with Lilly stock. They had "a draft Investment Policy Statement" by March 2002 and still held "at least 75 percent of the portfolio in one stock (RISK!)".
    - They lost $102 million in 2002, and the charities sued and lost (rows 22-23).
    - The gift was later counted at about $200 million (row 24). For a second charity, the announced $120 million was later estimated at about $85 million (row 25).
  - **Fixed sums held:**
    - Knight: $500 million, then another $500 million (row 28).
    - Yin: NT$20億, unchanged while the project's cost rose from about NT$50億 to over NT$80億 (row 27).
  - **Kuo's NT$150億 hospital gift** needed NT$50億 more after 12 years of rising costs (row 26).
  - **The Giving Pledge,** a promise stated as a share of future wealth: 8 of 22 who died had kept it (row 29).
  - **Judge's note:** the decade's most famous "at least" gift to the arts lost $102 million in its first year. The floor sat in one share, and an investment policy on paper did not save it. Laura's bottom sits in a Treasury fund from January 2028.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE.** If one figure is used after the move, it should be the bare figure, never "at least".
  - Linguists find that "at least n" tells the listener the speaker does not know the exact amount.
  - In Geurts and Nouwen's experiment, only about half of readers accepted "Beryl had at least three sherries" when she had had three (row 31).
  - From a speaker who plainly knows, it sounds odd: "? A hexagon has at least five sides" (row 32).
  - After the move Laura knows exactly what she owns. The rule pays the stated top in every path (NUMBERS 4).
  - So "at least $115,000" would make her sound unsure. It would also hint at more money, which CANON D12 keeps outside the range.
  - "At least" is the honest word only while the top is unknown, which is the time before the move, when the plan states nothing.
  - **Judge's note:** "at least" sounds cautious, but to a listener it says "I don't know".
- **Claim 5: KEEP, with numbers.** Campaigns raise 106-116% of goal on average, but the worst reported reached 75% (row 18).
  - The goal is set to be beaten, which makes it a soft floor, not a forecast.
  - Laura's funds pay $123,476 against a $115,000 top centrally (107.4%, Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4), near the campaigns' average.
  - Her worst case is the top itself, and theirs is three-quarters of the goal.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 (3c, Table 2 note).** Boards are given ranges 22-50% of their midpoint (median 28.6%), and Laura's is 19.0%: narrower than all of them and owned. This answers "is a range a hedge?" with numbers from the co-sponsors' own world.
2. **Row 23 (3c, 3e).** The decade's best-known "at least $100 million" arts gift lost $102 million in a year because its floor sat in one share despite a written investment policy. In one story, it shows why Laura's bottom is a Treasury fund and not a share fund.
3. **Rows 31-32 (3d).** "At least" tells listeners the speaker does not know, and Laura, after the move, knows. This settles the co-sponsor wording if a single figure is ever used, and gives a judge a fact few finance reports use.
4. **Row 9 (3c, 3d).** A real feasibility report sets "a baseline ... with a stretch goal" and reassesses before going public. That is Laura's order of events, except that her stretch is bought before she speaks.
5. **Row 18 (3c).** Campaigns raise 106-116% of goal on average, but the worst reported reached only 75%. It shows what "confidence" means in fundraising, and how much further Laura's "certain" goes.
