## (b) Cards: the six strongest ideas

### Card 1. The royalty cut-off: no sale after mid-2027 reaches the deposit
- **What is it?** Real calendars pay royalties three to four months after each half-year, plus two to three weeks
  (row 15). So the last royalty cash before 1 January 2028 covers sales to 31 March 2027 (a PRH calendar) or 30 June
  2027 (calendar halves); one children's and graphic novel publisher's last pre-deposit payment date is 25 October 2027.
- **Did it work?** One PRH author's first royalty cheque came about 14 months after publication (row 15); Arcadia held
  35% back for a period (row 15); the AG model allows up to four periods (Q12 row 9).
- **How is Laura like it?** Royalties are part of "earnings from publishing advances" in the broad sense, and her books
  sell through the same channels (case p.1-2).
- **How is she not?** Her publishers, periods and whether her books have earned out are unknown and were not researched;
  an unearned advance pays no royalty at all.
- **What Caplet takes:** a recession that starts after mid-2027 cannot reach the deposit through royalties. That puts a
  date on the IPS's "later" (l.9): later often means after January 2028, when her income no longer enters the plan.

### Card 2. Speaking breaks first and mends in two to three years
- **What is it?** In 2020 speaking was the most common income authors lost (Q12 row 35; Q1 row 33). Virtual fees ran
  about 74% of in-person (row 11); 70% of speakers charged under $5,000 a gig (row 12).
- **Did it work?** It recovered: by 2022 the average keynote fee was about $8,000, up 60% in a year, and 90% of speakers
  earned again (row 12); most in-person fees had held at their old level by 2021 (row 11).
- **How is Laura like it?** She is a "public speaker" and "educator" whose deposit draws on "speaking engagements"
  (case p.1-2).
- **How is she not?** These surveys cover professional and celebrity speakers, not authors' school visits; her fees and
  bookings are unknown.
- **What Caplet takes:** speaking is the channel by which a 2027 downturn reaches the 2028 deposit. Licensing is not:
  licensed sales grew 7.75% from 2019 to 2021 (row 13).

### Card 3. The end of school aid hit class sets, not library shelves
- **What is it?** When pandemic school aid had to be committed by September 2024, school library budgets stayed flat
  and per-student spending fell 3% (row 7), while Scholastic's classroom materials fell 12% and then 28% (row 8).
  Industry-wide PreK-12 revenue fell 2.5% in 2025 (row 9).
- **Did it work?** As a test of "falls again when federal aid ends": yes for classroom buying, barely for libraries,
  and far smaller than 2012's -15.9% (Q12 row 19). Book fairs, paid for by families, rose (row 8).
- **How is Laura like it?** "Her books are read in classrooms around the world" (case p.1).
- **How is she not?** Whether her books sell as class sets or library copies is not known; school sales are only part of
  any author's income.
- **What Caplet takes:** school money falls on government dates (2011-12, 2024-25, the July 2025 freeze, row 10), each
  time in a rising share market. It belongs in the report as "later", not "during that same period".

### Card 4. At the till her genre held; in the bank account children's writers did not
- **What is it?** In 2008-09 non-manga graphic novels grew 39% (row 1); in 2020 the comics and graphic novel market grew
  about 6% (row 2) and children's revenue rose (row 3). Yet UK children's fiction writers were the genre most likely to
  report income down since 2008 (61% against 42% of all, row 4), and UK author/illustrators' median income fell about
  9% in the Covid year (row 5).
- **Did it work?** As evidence for "sales held, income did not": yes, with the gap explained by events, school sales and
  smaller new advances (44% said advances fell, row 4), not by retail royalties.
- **How is Laura like it?** A graphic novelist and illustrator, "bestselling" (case p.1).
- **How is she not?** UK data, self-reported; a bestselling author sits on the protected side of the advance market
  (Q12 row 6).
- **What Caplet takes:** the thesis holds for the income streams the deposit needs (speaking, new deals), not for her
  books' shop sales. Say so plainly in 2.3.

### Card 5. The joint bad state closes on 1 January 2027
- **What is it?** A downturn that cuts the deposit and lowers yields before the ladder is bought is the one state in
  which the IPS's "we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit" (l.13) leans on a shrinking deposit (row 20).
- **Did it work?** At the 6 Nov close the deposit could fall about 98% and still complete the ladder; 77% after a
  1-point fall in yields, 53% after 2 points (NUMBERS 5). After 1 Jan 2027 a yield fall reprices only the small part
  left for 2028.
- **How is Laura like it?** Her deposit is the residual of a career income (Q12 row 32), so it can fall by more than
  her income does (row 19).
- **How is she not?** No source here measures how far yields fall in a downturn; a 1-2 point fall in seven weeks would
  be extreme.
- **What Caplet takes:** the career-ladder overlap Q1 worried about has a date. It is a 3e risk line, not a reason to
  change the plan (CANON A34, D9).

### Card 6. Corporate co-sponsors cut in the same year; foundations do not
- **What is it?** In 2020, corporate giving fell 6.1% and arts giving 7.5% as profits and GDP fell, while foundations
  gave 17% more and shares ended the year up 18% (row 21). Corporate giving rebounded 23.8% in 2021.
- **Did it work?** It confirms Q4b: corporate giving follows profits (Q4b row 16), foundations move one to two years
  late and by little (Q4b rows 5-8).
- **How is Laura like it?** She approaches co-sponsors in 2031 for a facility funded in 2033 (case p.3).
- **How is she not?** US data; her co-sponsors may be Taiwanese, and Taiwan's corporate arts money looked steady in the
  pandemic years (row 22), which was not a recession there.
- **What Caplet takes:** "co-sponsors cut their budgets" (IPS l.9) is right for corporate sponsors in a profit downturn
  and wrong for foundations; the co-sponsor text can lean on foundations and grants for steadiness.
