- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**

- **Problem class:** proof of title to an intangible asset held through a chain of keepers. Ownership moved from holding a printed document to an entry in a chain of ledgers. The question is who keeps each ledger, who checks it, and what an outsider can see. **Field that solved it first:** the Treasury and the Federal Reserve, which began converting Treasuries to book entry in 1966-68, and the exchange's Central Certificate Service (1968), which became the Depository Trust Company (1973). The law followed: SIPA (1970), Rule 15c3-3 (1972), Section 17A (1975), Revised UCC Article 8 (1994) and TRADES (1997). Auditing (direct confirmation) and broker conduct rules (confirmations, statements) set what an outsider may rely on.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. No live marketable Treasury is paper, and none of Laura's ever was. Some paper Treasury obligations are still around, and her fund shares still rest on a certificate held by the depository.**
  - **Treasury's own words:** "Treasury marketable securities are now only issued electronically. If you have a paper Treasury marketable security, it has already matured and is no longer earning interest" (TreasuryDirect).
  - **The drafters of the 1994 Article 8:** "Treasury securities are genuinely 'uncertificated', that is, no paper certificates are issued" (Prefatory Note, A-12).
  - **The end of paper, step by step** (Garbade, 2004, p. 44):
    - 13- and 26-week bearer bills ended in 1977.
    - Bearer bonds ended in September 1982, and bearer notes after December 1982.
    - In August 1986 Treasury said it would issue no more registered notes or bonds.
    - About 94% of marketable debt was already in book entry by the end of 1980.
  - **Laura's five T-bonds** (issued 2007-2011, NUMBERS 8g) began life as computer records: "Book-entry security means a Treasury security maintained as a computer record" (31 CFR 357.2).
  - **Paper that remains: savings bonds.** These are not marketable, and none is Laura's.
    - Banks stopped selling paper savings bonds from 1 Jan 2012.
    - The last route to new paper, I bonds bought with a tax refund, closed on 1 Jan 2025.
    - Holders may keep, cash or convert the paper bonds they have.
  - **Her fund shares are immobilised, not paperless.**
    - IBTM, the other iBond funds and VT are "registered in the name of DTC or its nominee and deposited with, or on behalf of, DTC" (iShares SAI, 2026).
    - DTC says that more than 90% of US corporate and municipal securities are "represented by one or more physical certificates that are immobilized at the depository" (DTC, 2026).
    - Vanguard: holders "will not receive or be entitled to physical delivery of share certificates". If DTC stopped serving the funds, they would find a replacement or "issue and deliver printed certificates".
  - **Safe forms:** "no Treasury Laura holds has ever been paper" and "marketable Treasuries are issued only electronically". Never "no paper Treasury exists", and never "her fund shares have no certificate".
  - **Judge's note:** Treasuries went paperless partly because insurers stopped wanting to insure paper. Late in 1970, the leading insurer of securities thefts from banks said it would restrict or end its cover in 1971. About $30 million of bearer Treasuries had been lost or stolen in 1969. Dealers "could not bear the risk of uninsured losses", and the Fed hurried the book-entry conversion (Garbade, 2004, p. 43, note 58).
- **Claim 2: KEEP (the law and the record are in cards Q72, Q78 and Q157). This card adds why each protection exists and who checks it.**
  - **The rules were written against a failure that really happened.**
    - The SEC's 1971 study: "Many broker-dealers found substantial quantities of securities in their possession without knowing to whom they belonged" (p. 20).
    - Firms, "because of inadequate and inefficient recordkeeping and segregation systems and procedures, and the infrequent counting of securities in their possession, mishandled and misused customers' funds and securities" (H.R. Doc. 92-231, as quoted by the SEC in 2025).
    - Before SIPC, "more than 160 NYSE member organizations" went out of business (NYSE, 1971, p. 23).
  - **Each answer, in order.**
    - **SIPA, December 1970.** SIPC: "the paperwork crunch, brought on by unexpectedly high trading volume, was followed by a very severe decline in stock prices".
    - **Rule 15c3-3, adopted 1972 and in force from 15 Jan 1973.** It is meant to forbid brokers "from using customer assets to finance any part of their businesses". If a broker fails, customers' securities "should be readily available to be returned to the customers" (SEC, 2013).
    - **Rule 17a-13.** At least once each quarter, the broker must "physically examine and count all securities held" and verify the rest. The count is done by people "whose regular duties do not require them to have direct responsibility" for those securities.
    - **Daily reserve checks.** Since 30 June 2026, the 49 largest carrying brokers, with 99.3% of customer credits, compute their customer cash reserve daily instead of weekly.
  - **Limits stay as recorded.** A broker failure costs time, and a hole in the customer pool is shared pro rata before SIPC fills it (Q72, Q78, NUMBERS 8t). Say "held for her and returned ahead of the firm's creditors", never "in her name" (Q157).
  - **Judge's note:** in 1969 the paper existed but the ledger was wrong: firms held securities and did not know whose they were. Laura's Treasuries have no paper at all, and the ledger is the holding. The counting and segregation rules she relies on were written against exactly that failure.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. The rule does not say "monthly". A statement Laura hands over is her own copy. The check that counts is a statement or letter sent straight from her broker's ledger, because no record above the broker names her.**
  - **How often.** FINRA Rule 2231 requires a statement "with a frequency of not less than once every calendar quarter", showing "any securities positions, money balances, or account activity". Each trade also brings a written confirmation "at or before completion of such transaction" (SEC Rule 10b-10, which covers every security except US Savings Bonds and municipal securities, so Treasuries and ETF shares are included). Never "a monthly statement" as a rule.
  - **What a statement is.** UCC 8-501 comment 2: the legal event is the moment "an intermediary has acknowledged that it is carrying a position for the customer", and "The precise form in which the intermediary manifests that acknowledgment is left to private ordering". The broker's credit on its own books is the event. The statement reports it.
  - **No other ledger names her.**
    - Treasury and the Reserve Banks "treat the Participant as exclusively entitled" to transfer a bond and receive its payments (31 CFR 357.13(a)). The Participant is the clearing bank, not Laura.
    - Vanguard: "Vanguard will not have any record of your ownership. Your account information will be maintained by your broker".
    - So a co-sponsor cannot look Laura up at the Fed, at DTC or at the fund.
  - **What a co-sponsor can check, from strongest to weakest:**
    1. **A statement sent by the broker straight to the co-sponsor.** FINRA 2231.02 allows it only on the customer's "written instructions", and the broker keeps sending statements to her too.
    2. **A letter from the broker in answer to the co-sponsor's own request.** This is the auditor's standard. Auditors must "send the confirmation request directly to the confirming party and obtain the confirmation response directly from the confirming party" (PCAOB AS 2310.16).
    3. **The broker's own audited balance sheet.** It is the public part of its annual report, under Rule 17a-5(e)(3), and an independent accountant also examines a compliance report on Rule 15c3-3.
    4. **The trade confirmations.** A confirmation must say if the broker is "not a member of the Securities Investor Protection Corporation" (10b-10(a)(8)).
    5. **A copy Laura forwards.** It is the weakest check: Madoff's customers held statements that named Treasuries never bought (store Q213-025).
  - **Safe form for 3d** (it changes only the meaning of "statement" in the ARGUMENT_TREE line): "a statement from her broker, sent to them directly at her written request, at the 2031 meetings and before the December 2032 payout".
  - **Judge's note:** above Laura's broker, every record shows someone else. The Fed's books show a bank, the fund's register shows Cede & Co., and DTC's books show her broker. The one ledger that names her is her broker's, so the check worth offering is a statement that comes straight from it.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. The crisis started the chain and produced the customer protection rules within two years. The words "held for" are from 1994, after the law's first answer failed.**
  - **What came straight from the crisis:**
    - SIPA (December 1970) and Rule 15c3-3 (1972).
    - The NYSE's Central Certificate Service, "a computerized system for immobilizing stock certificates" (NYSE, 1971, p. 4).
    - DTC, "organized in 1973 in response to the 'paperwork crisis'" (DTC, 2026).
    - Section 17A (4 June 1975): "The Commission shall use its authority under this chapter to end the physical movement of securities certificates" in settlement between brokers.
  - **What the law tried first.** The 1978 Article 8 added "parallel provisions dealing with uncertificated securities", aiming at "a system in which issuers would no longer issue certificates" (Prefatory Note, A-9). That did not happen. "Instead, the solution thus far has been the development of the indirect holding system", and "Even after the 1978 amendments, the rules of Article 8 do not deal effectively with the indirect holding system" (A-10, A-13).
  - **When "held for" arrived.** It came only in the 1994 revision: the assets are "held by the securities intermediary for the entitlement holders" (8-503(a); Q72). That is 24 years after SIPA (Keel arithmetic). DTCC told Congress in March 2026 that the "securities entitlement" structure was "one of the solutions to that crisis".
  - **Safe form:** "the paperwork crisis gave customers their protection rules and gave the market its depository; the law's words 'held for' came with its 1994 rewrite". Never "the crisis wrote 'for her' into the law".

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 40 and 28 with 32 (3d, and 3e's custody sentence): only her broker's ledger names her.** The Fed's book shows a bank and "treat[s] the Participant as exclusively entitled". The fund's register shows Cede & Co., and Vanguard "will not have any record of your ownership". This gives the plain answer to how Laura proves ownership, and it tells co-sponsors where to look.
2. **Rows 37-38 (3d's Table 3 line): the statement should come from the broker, not through Laura.** FINRA 2231.02 lets her broker send statements straight to a co-sponsor on her written instruction. The auditor's standard (AS 2310.16) is a request and a reply that pass directly between the checker and the holder. After Madoff, a forwarded copy is the weakest proof, and a direct one is the strongest Laura can offer.
3. **Row 23 (2.x, a judge's aha): Treasuries went paperless partly because insurers would stop insuring the paper.** About $30 million of bearer Treasuries were lost or stolen in 1969. The leading insurer said it would restrict or end its theft cover in 1971, and the Fed sped up conversion. Laura's T-bonds never existed on paper (rows 25-26).
4. **Rows 5-6 with 15-17 (3e or 2.x, one clause): the protections were written against a real failure.** In 1969-70 firms "found substantial quantities of securities in their possession without knowing to whom they belonged". Segregation (15c3-3, 1972) and a quarterly count by people who do not handle the securities (17a-13) are the answer, and the cash reserve check is daily at the largest brokers from 30 June 2026.
5. **Rows 29-30 with 43 (wording guard for claim 4): "held for" is 1994's phrase, not 1970's.** The crisis produced customer protection within two years and a depository by 1973. The law first bet on certificate-free securities registered with the issuer (1978), which never arrived. The words that make Laura's Treasuries hers in law came 24 years after SIPA.
