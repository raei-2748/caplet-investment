- **Claims tested:**

- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** committing in advance to the only events that may change a course of action while results arrive along the way, so that the results seen cannot rewrite the plan. Sequential testing began in industrial inspection; Armitage and Bross took it to clinical trials in the 1950s (Jennison, 2004). Medicine then solved the practical problem in three layers: group sequential rules (Pocock 1977; O'Brien and Fleming 1979; Lan and DeMets 1983), independent monitoring committees (the Greenberg Report, finalised 1967), and regulators' codes that make both a condition of trust (ICH E9, 1998; FDA, 2006; reporting standards CONSORT and SPIRIT).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Medicine's own record says that naming the reasons in advance is necessary but does not by itself stop a rewrite.
  - Written rules are rewritten often: 62% of 102 trials changed, added or dropped a primary outcome against their protocol (Chan et al., 2004, row 23); 58 of 67 trials in five journals that endorse the reporting standard misreported their outcomes (COMPare, row 24).
  - A rule named in advance did not stop the damage that early action does: trials stopped early for benefit overstated the effect (ratio of relative risks 0.71) "independent of the presence of a statistical stopping rule" (Bassler et al., 2010, row 16).
  - What does stop the rewrite in medicine is three things beside the written rule: the people who could change the plan do not see the results along the way (rows 3, 25); an outside reader checks the result against the record (rows 23-24); and every extra look has a price set before looking (rows 12-15).
  - **Corrected claim, for the lead (not report prose):** naming the reasons in advance makes any other change visible as a rewrite; what stops the rewrite in Laura's plan is that the promised money needs no decision to be paid and neither reason needs a price to be read. Of medicine's three supports she has the second (the submitted IPS, the judges, and from 2031 co-sponsors who see the fund's statement), cannot have the first (prices are public), and needs no third (no action waits on a price) (row 31).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Like in four ways, unlike in three.
  - Like: fixed before any result could be seen (row 1); checkable facts with a date or a number ("nothing received by 31 March 2028"; "a payment over five business days late", Table 3 rows 1-2; row 26); both on the harm side, with no exit on good news, which is medicine's asymmetry taken to its end (row 6); and each with a bounded response written down in advance (rows 5, 9, 27).
  - Unlike: a trial's stops read the results, while both IPS reasons read only whether an input or an instrument failed, as a trial stopped for vaccine supply did (row 20); a trial needs early exits because waiting harms patients, and Laura's promised money loses nothing by waiting (row 28); and a trial has an independent monitor, while Laura's plan has none and cannot hide prices (row 30).
  - Stricter than medicine: "only" closes the list, where a monitoring committee may act on harms nobody foresaw because "Not all potential risks can be identified in advance" (FDA, 2006, row 7). That is why 3e keeps a second list, the ways each promise could break (card Q109), beside the two reasons.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** True for one thing, empty for the rest.
  - True for the top before the move: a figure read off an early run of prices is medicine's "random extreme" (row 19), and aha 7 measures it in the plan's own terms (a top named a year early would exceed what the move buys in about 11 of 100 futures).
  - Empty for the ten payments and the bottom: a market move is not evidence about them, since Treasuries held for her pay them whatever shares do (the deletion test). "A fall in the market is not one of them" is a fact of construction, not a statistical discipline (row 29).
- **Claim 4: KILL as a named parallel or a reference; KEEP the logic as plain clauses about the plan.** 3e's one parallel is the 2022 gilt crisis (ARGUMENT_TREE branch 6). Medicine imagery is out of every deliverable (EXPECTATIONS 3.6, INSTRUCTIONS I85), and card Q29-016 bans "trial", "pilot" and "CONSORT" in the report. What may land, in the plan's own words: both reasons can be read without looking at a price; a rise is not a reason either; after a default "change" means hold and wait; the watch tier changes a date or an instrument, never the plan.
- **What the comparison shows a judge about 3e and Table 3** (for the lead's reasoning):
  - Table 3's two rows have the form a trial protocol is asked for (SPIRIT 2013 item 21b, row 22): who watches, the event that triggers, and what it may and may never change, written before the event.
  - 3e's assumptions are the plan's design assumptions. Watching them is quality monitoring, which in a trial "has no impact on type I error" (row 2): a wrong assumption moves a figure not yet stated, never the plan (D19's second list).
  - The closed list is honest only beside the second list (row 7; card Q109).
- **Judge's note (the aha):** in a trial a change is trusted when the people who make it have not seen the results along the way. Laura sees prices every day, so the plan does the next best thing: neither of its two reasons to change can be read off a price, and no promised dollar waits on anyone's reaction to one.
- **Judge's note (fixed income or statistics reader):** the field that measured what early looks cost found that a stopping rule written in advance did not stop early stops from overstating results; only stopping later did (rows 16-17). So the plan's protection for a stated figure is its date (she states the top once it is held), not the sentence in l.17.
