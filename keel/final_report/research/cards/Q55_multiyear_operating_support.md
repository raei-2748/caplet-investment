- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A big funder's multi-year operating grant is money set aside at the start."
  2. "Multi-year operating support is common, so ten fixed years is an ordinary promise."
  3. "A written promise of ten yearly gifts binds the giver, in the US and in Taiwan."
  4. "If Laura's promise has no conditions, the residency can count all ten payments on its books."
  5. "A donor-advised fund's scheduled grants are a reliable way to promise ten yearly gifts."
- **Problem class:** making a promised stream of future payments reliable to the people who receive it. That takes two separate things: the giver can pay (assets set apart) and the giver must pay (an enforceable promise). **Field that solved it first:** contract and gift law (Anglo-American charitable-subscription law, Restatement s.90(2); civil-law gift rules, where a gift binds once delivered or notarised), with nonprofit accounting (ASC 958-605) deciding when a recipient may count a promise.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KILL.** Every multi-year operating programme read is paid from money the funder expects to have later, and each says so in writing.
  - **Arts Council England.** Each later year is "indicative and subject to the Government's Spending Review". "We can only guarantee future instalments of your investment as long as sufficient funds ... are available to us."
  - **Taiwan's NCAF.** Its two- and three-year operating grants approve year one only. The later years are "先行匡列" (earmarked) and set each year "參酌年度預算額度" (with regard to that year's budget). Every results notice adds that NCAF "得視實際情況按比例刪減或停止補助" (may cut pro rata or stop the grant).
  - **Private foundations.** All three keep the right to stop later payments: Hewlett ("Funding may be modified or discontinued"), Kresge ("modify, suspend, or discontinue") and MacArthur (may "discontinue or suspend funding").
  - **Where the money sits.** A foundation that books an unconditional grant owes it from its general pool (Q7c: "owed, not owned").
  - **Why it matters.** A quarter of foundation leaders who want to give more multi-year unrestricted money hold back because "precommitting funds could create financial risk" (CEP 2025, Figure 27, N=122).
  - **Laura is the reverse.** Her ten are owned before they are promised: $444,545 of Treasuries on 1 Jan 2033 against $399,089 owed at forwards, 111.4% funded (NUMBERS 8h, MODEL).
  - **Judge's note:** the grants a residency would call "secure multi-year funding" are, in their own documents, pencil ("indicative"; "按比例刪減或停止"). Laura's ten are ink. The funders' own leaders name the missing piece: money set aside before the promise.
- **Claim 2: KILL.** Multi-year operating support is uncommon, short, and often thinner per year.
  - **How common.** In the decade before 2020, 12.4% of grants were both multiyear and general operating support (CEP GPR data). In 2026, 80% of Exponent Philanthropy's foundations made single-year operating grants, against 32% multi-year. The multi-year grants averaged about $68,000 across three years, against about $51,000 for one year (as reported by CEP, 2026).
  - **How long.** Terms found: NCAF at most 3 years, and only 5 of 93 troupes in 2026 got more than one. MacArthur Chicago "typically for three years" (an earlier evaluation said five). Hewlett letters run three years; grantees ask for "five- to seven-year grants". Creative Australia runs four years with "no transitional funding". Ford BUILD runs five years. Arts Council England was three years, stretched to five by yearly extensions.
  - **The longest fixed schedule found** is Doris Duke's $525,000 "allocated over seven years", paid to individual artists.
  - **Laura's ten fixed years, owned,** are longer than any programme read.
  - **Judge's note:** a ten-year operating promise is far longer than practice. No arts funder read, public or private, fixes more than seven years.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Owning shows she can pay; only words make her bound, and the rules differ by place.
  - **In the US, it depends on the state.** Iowa binds a charitable subscription with no proof of reliance (Salsbury, 1974, read at source here). Massachusetts tells juries a pledge must be supported "either by consideration ... or by reliance" (King v. Trustees of BU, 1995).
  - **In Taiwan, a promised gift can be revoked** for whatever has not been handed over, unless it is notarised (Civil Code art. 408).
  - **A promise of yearly gifts ends at the giver's death** "unless the donor has expressed a contrary intent" (art. 415).
  - **The giver may refuse** if paying would "seriously affect his means of livelihood" (art. 418).
  - **A notarial deed for a fixed sum** that states it may be enforced directly needs no lawsuit, and binds successors (Notary Act art. 13).
  - **Even then, collection depends on the giver's assets** (a Taiwan law firm's note). That is where the Treasuries come in.
  - **Limit.** The case puts "Taiwan's legal rules" out of scope (CASE_MAP A13), so this is context for one clause at most, never legal advice.
  - **Judge's note:** in Taiwan, a promise of ten yearly gifts lapses at the giver's death unless it says otherwise. The plan already answers "can she pay?". "Must she, and after her?" needs one sentence in her letter.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, with detail.** An unconditional promise is booked in full when made, at present value.
  - **Each dated payment is that year's support.** ASC 958-605-45-5: "by specifying future payment dates, donors indicate that their gifts are to support activities in each period in which a payment is scheduled". The ladder already has this shape.
  - **The value depends on who promises.** Collectability turns on "the donor's creditworthiness" (958-605-30-4).
  - **The words make it unconditional.** "A fixed payment schedule or specified amount alone doesn't establish an unconditional promise" (YHB). Foundations write accounting-only conditions into a separate section (Hewlett, Kresge).
- **Claim 5: KILL.**
  - **The money stops being hers.** A donor-advised fund is "owned and controlled by a sponsoring organization" (26 USC 4966). Fidelity Charitable's trustees "have exclusive ownership and control".
  - **Schedules can stop.** Recurring grants "may be discontinued for insufficient funds" (NPT).
  - **Not a binding pledge.** Sponsors help with "multi-year commitments that are not treated as a legally-binding pledge" (NCFP).
  - **The tax-law contrast.** US tax law does know a fixed charitable stream, the lead trust's "guaranteed annuity" (its own example: "$5,000 a year ... to Y Charity for a period of 10 years"). But it is given away irrevocably at the start, which the frozen plan does not do.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 1 and 9 (3a or 3d; a conceit turn):** Arts Council England's later years are "indicative", and NCAF's "按比例刪減或停止". That is two national funders saying in their own words that their multi-year money is pencil. Laura's ten are ink.
2. **Row 20 (3a, the aha):** a quarter of the foundation leaders who want to give more multi-year money hold back because "precommitting funds could create financial risk". Laura met that risk by buying the money first.
3. **Row 45 with row 10 (3d's one evidence clause, or 3a):** no arts funder read fixes more than seven years. In Taiwan only 5 of 93 top troupes held any multi-year operating promise in 2026, at most three years. Ten owned years is rare, and the numbers show it.
4. **Rows 35-36 with 38 (3d or 3e, one clause, A13 limit):** in Taiwan a promised gift can be taken back until it is paid, and yearly gifts lapse at the giver's death unless the promise says otherwise. The Treasuries show she can pay. One written, notarised line makes her bound, after her too.
5. **Rows 29-30 (3e, what to monitor):** BUILD's grantees were hurt most by not knowing what came after the grant. Laura's last payment is fixed in 2042 from the start. Saying so in 2031 turns a cliff into a date.
