- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "For a set of promises that must ALL be paid, adding issuers raises the chance that at least one fails." Reliability engineering calls this a series system. Is 1 - 0.99^10 right?
  2. "Credit markets price exactly this. A first-to-default basket's spread rises with the number of names and falls as their defaults become more correlated. So one issuer is one event, not ten."
  3. "Institutions that must pay fixed promises on dates concentrate the matching assets in their own government on purpose."
  4. "With one issuer a US default hits all ten at once. The concentration swaps ten small independent risks for one shared one." What does that mean for Laura, and would a judge find the series point surprising or obvious?
- **Problem class:** the reliability of a set that fails if any one part fails (a series system, also called a weakest-link or first-failure system), and how dependence between the parts changes it.
- **Field that solved it first:**
  - Reliability engineering wrote the product rule and its independence assumption (military handbooks, then NIST/SEMATECH).
  - Structural reliability wrote the bounds under dependence (Cornell's simple bounds, as given by Thoft-Christensen and Sørensen in 1982).
  - Credit derivatives priced it from 1999-2000: Li's copula paper on first-to-default baskets, then Hull and White, and Fed Board staff (Gibson).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, now sourced. The arithmetic holds; add the bounds, which make it sharper.**
  - **The rule.** NIST: "The series model is used to go from individual components to the entire system". The system "fails when the first component failure occurs". R_S = the product of the R_i.
  - **The handbook.** MIL-HDBK-338B s.5.4.2.1: "The successful operation of the system depends on the proper functioning of all the system components" (eq. 5.59).
  - **The check.** 1 - 0.99^10 = 9.56% (Keel arithmetic). The register's "9.6%, about 1 time in 10" stands.
  - **The product rule assumes independence.** NIST: "Each component operates or fails independently of every other one".
  - **Under dependence the answer moves inside bounds.** A series system's failure chance lies between the largest single chance and 1 - product(1 - p_i). The "lower bound corresponds to perfect dependence between all elements and the upper bound to no dependence" (Thoft-Christensen and Sørensen 1982).
  - **For ten 1% promises** (Keel arithmetic):
    - One shared issuer is the lower bound: 1%.
    - Ten independent issuers: 9.56%.
    - The worst any arrangement can do (failures never overlapping) is 10%.
    - So independence sits 95% of the way from the best case to the worst.
  - **What engineers do with it.** They apportion the system target down the chain by the nth root (MIL-HDBK-338B s.6). Ten rungs that together must be 99% need 99.9% each (Keel arithmetic).
  - **Judge's note:** for promises that must all be paid, spreading them over ten safe borrowers is almost the worst arrangement possible: 9.6% against a ceiling of 10%. Putting all ten on one borrower is the best: the chance any breaks is the chance that one borrower fails.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with dealers' and the Fed's own numbers.**
  - **Li (2000):** a 5-name first-to-default contract is worth 0.5823 at zero correlation and 0.1648 at perfect correlation. The second is exactly one name's value (Keel arithmetic reproduces both). "We see that the value of the contract decreases as the correlation increases."
  - **Hull and White (2004):** for ten names with a 1% default intensity, the first-to-default spread is 603bp at correlation 0, 440 at 0.3 and 293 at 0.6. One name alone is about 60bp (Keel arithmetic, the credit triangle 1% x 60%), so the independent basket costs about ten names.
  - **Gibson (2004, Federal Reserve Board):** a real five-name basket (General Motors 146bp the widest) has a par spread of 352bp at correlation 0, which is the sum of the five. It is 301bp at 0.3 and 168bp at 0.9, falling toward the widest single name. Gibson's Table 9 "shows why first-to-default baskets are a 'correlation product.'"
  - **Martin, Uzuner and Ma (2018)** state the bounds in words: "The FtD spread is the sum of the constituent spreads if the underlying default risks are independent, whereas an FtD spread must be at least that of the widest constituent."
  - **Real issuers.** S&P's US corporate history (1981-2024) gives AA issuers 0.53% to 1.34% default over 6 to 15 years. Ten AA companies, one behind each payment, independent: about a 9.3% chance at least one defaults. The rates are AAA 7.8%, A 15.2% and BBB 32.7% (Keel arithmetic). The aha's hypothetical "99% each" is roughly an AA company.
  - **Judge's note:** dealers price exactly this. A basket that pays on the first default costs about the sum of its names when they are independent and about one name when they move as one. Laura's ten rungs are a basket of one name.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. True for holders with no capital behind each promise; false for insurers, who pool and hold capital.**
  - **US law sends promised money to the government when nothing stands behind it but the asset:**
    - FDIC's Deposit Insurance Fund: "shall be invested in obligations of the United States".
    - Court registry funds: "public debt securities with maturities suitable to the needs of the funds", a ladder by statute.
    - Bankruptcy trustees: anything not US-backed needs a surety bond.
    - GENIUS Act stablecoin reserves (2025): cash, insured deposits and Treasuries of 93 days or less, but no corporate bonds.
    - Escrows (Q54) and 130(d) (placed) take the same line.
  - **Bank liquidity.** The Basel liquidity rule lets "Level 1" government assets fill the buffer "without limit". It caps everything else at 40%, with equities in a 50%-haircut sub-tier.
  - **Central banks.** The median central bank holds 40% in government bonds and 0% in corporate bonds, emerging-market bonds and equity (World Bank RAMP 2019). In the crisis the survey covers, 53.6% cut bonds without a government guarantee and only 8.9% cut long AAA government bonds (IMF WP 13/99). To hold dollars safely, even the ECB holds "US and Japanese government bonds".
  - **But UK annuity writers diversify credit.** They are the closest match to Laura's dated payments. Their matching portfolios are about 60% sovereign plus corporate bonds and about 40% illiquid lending (Bank of England 2026). The average is 34% corporate bonds against 15% gilts (PwC MALIR survey, YE2024). They can, because they hold regulatory capital, pool thousands of lives and haircut every spread (the fundamental spread).
  - **Judge's note:** the rule is not "institutions concentrate". It is "whoever has no capital behind a promise holds the government". Laura has none beyond the extra Treasuries, so her ten are escrow-kind, not insurer-kind.
- **Claim 4: KEEP the swap, said once and plainly. The alternatives would not escape it.**
  - **What concentration does in prices.** It lowers the chance that any promise breaks and raises the chance that many break together. In Hull and White's ten-name basket, the 5th-to-default spread rises from 0 to 31bp as correlation goes from 0 to 0.6.
  - **For Laura in plain words.** The ten now stand or fall together. If they fell, it would be because the US government itself did not pay on time. That is the one event the IPS already names as a reason to change the plan.
  - **Other issuers would not escape that world.** S&P: a sovereign default "would lead to a significant increase in nonsovereign defaults". A company's bond is "a sort of FtD" on the company and its government (Martin et al.).
  - **2011.** In the one modern event about the US's own credit, the August 2011 downgrade, Treasury yields fell 10-23bp while the S&P 500 fell 6.8% (FSOC 2012).
  - **Surprising or obvious?** Surprising before, obvious after.
    - People underrate the failure chance of a system that breaks if any part breaks: "the funnel-like structure of disjunctions leads to underestimation" (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; the conjunctive half is in Q14a-026).
    - Once the arithmetic is said, it is plain.
    - Use: one clause of arithmetic, one sentence of meaning, then on to "diversify Laura".
  - **Judge's note:** when the US itself was downgraded in 2011, its bonds went up and shares went down. The one shared risk is the one every other dollar promise shares too.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 6 (2.3).** The best aha in the card: for promises that must all be paid, spreading them over independent borrowers is almost the worst arrangement possible (9.6% against a 10% ceiling), and one borrower is the best. It sharpens the register's row 2 without adding a number the judge must hold.
2. **Row 15 (2.3, footnote or one sentence).** A Federal Reserve Board table with real names shows a five-name basket's price falling from the sum of the names (352bp) to near the riskiest one (168bp against 146bp) as defaults move together. It is outside proof that markets price the series point.
3. **Row 31 (2.3 or 3e).** It changes claim 3 into a sharper rule: holders with capital and a pool diversify credit, and holders with nothing behind each promise hold the government. That is the judge's likely objection ("insurers hold corporate bonds"), answered before it is asked, and it places Laura with escrows.
4. **Row 34 (2.3, the turn to "diversify Laura").** In August 2011 the US was downgraded and its bonds still rose while shares fell 6.8%. The one shared risk does not arrive with falling Treasury prices. This is IPS l.9's counterbalance shown on its hardest day.
5. **Row 24 (2.3, one clause).** The fund that guarantees every insured US deposit must by law hold only US government obligations. It is a strikingly dissimilar parallel that makes 89.55% in one issuer read as deliberate.
