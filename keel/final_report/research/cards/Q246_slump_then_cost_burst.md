- **(a) KEEP "more than 40%"; the levels hold on a third read; print one calendar.** MSCI's service returned the same four levels to every digit as card Q85 (16 Nov) and NUMBERS 9 (23 Nov): 31 Dec 1999 341.6261, 31 Jan 2000 322.8336, 31 Dec 2002 190.7965, 31 Jan 2003 185.0329 (rows 1-2).   - **One calendar.** The tree pairs -42.7% (end-January dates) with a building window that ends in December (Laura's calendar). On Laura's calendar the pair is -44.2% with January 2003 to December 2004 (+18.4%, +27.2%); on end-January dates it is -42.7% with January 2003 to January 2005 (8q's purchase +36 to +60 months: +17.9%, +28.0%).   - **Two register figures to replace (asked for by NUMBERS 9):** five years on, world shares were **16.7% below cost on the price series** (31 Dec 1999 to 31 Dec 2004), not "about 10%" (9.8% was a price-then-gross splice); after the meetings they **rose 53.8% (end-January 2003 to end-December 2004) or 49.1% on Laura's calendar**, not "56-57%" (rows 4-5).
  - **Judge's note:** the fall at the meetings (-44%) was almost the whole fall: the month-end low (Sep 2002) was only four months earlier and 48.4% below the March 2000 peak (row 3).
- **(b) KEEP the figures and the window; CHANGE the picture of "then rose".** DGBAS's own CSV and FRED, read at source: the general index went from 55.05 (January 2003) to 65.18 (December 2004), **+18.40% in NT$**; the Taiwan dollar from 34.5714 to 32.1703 per US$, 7.46% stronger; **+27.24% in US$** (rows 6-8). The building-construction sub-index, the part nearest her kind of work, rose 18.35% (row 7). But:
  - it was a burst, not a drift: 11.6% came in three months, December 2003 to March 2004, when rebar rose 37.8% (71.0% from January 2003) and metal products 43.9% over the window; the cause was world steel (CRS: US steel prices "double the level of mid-2003" by the end of March 2004; BLS: US import prices of iron and steel mill products +69.1% in 2004) (rows 11-13);
  - it began before the share low: the index rose from January 2002 (3.65% from its May 2001 low by December 2002), so the slump and the burst overlapped by about a year (row 14);
  - the US$ figure depends on the end month: +22.7% to the cost peak (October 2004), +27.2% to December 2004, +28.0% to January 2005; the last 4.5 points came from the Taiwan dollar in November-December 2004 (row 15).
  - **Judge's note:** a reader pictures a slow two-year climb; the record shows one quarter of steel prices doing most of it, which is why the plan's answer is money kept back rather than a forecast.
- **(c) KEEP the four episodes as labels; CHANGE what they are used for.** Re-derived from today's two reads: 406 two-year windows since January 1991, **20 at 28% or more**, in eight runs of start months that make the four episodes exactly as NUMBERS 8x(d) has them; the worst is +38.92%, July 2006 to July 2008 (row 17). But they are the list of the largest rises, not a list of slumps then bursts. **Only one of the four followed a fall in world shares:** over the 36 months before each window's start, world shares moved -47.7% to -14.8% before 2002-05, **+52.5% to +83.0% before 2006-08**, +22.2% to +26.0% before 2019-21, and US shares (MSCI serves nothing before 1997) +26.6% before 1991-93 (rows 18-19).
  - **The windows aha 9 needs are the post-slump ones:** since 2000, 20 meetings months followed a three-year fall in world shares of 30% or more, in three runs (August 2002 to September 2003, February to May 2009, June to July 2010). The US-dollar cost of the same Taiwan building then rose over the next two years by a median 27.4%, 23.7% and 12.7% (lowest 12.2%), against a median of 3.0% for all 406 windows since 1991 (rows 20-23).
  - **Two slumps, two engines:** after 2000-03 the building cost rose (steel); after 2008-09 the Taiwan dollar did (February 2009 to February 2011: index +4.6%, Taiwan dollar 16.9% stronger, +22.3% in US$). The second is 8q's "meetings 2009" failure, and it is not in the four episodes because it peaked at 24.6% (row 21).
  - **Judge's note:** the four episodes answer "how big", not "how often after a crash". The honest count for aha 9 is two: both world-share falls of 30% or more since 2000 were followed within two years by a building about a quarter dearer in US dollars, once through steel and once through the Taiwan dollar. Two events, overlapping windows: a dated count, never odds, and "can", never "tends to".
- **(d) ADD Hong Kong after 2008; KILL "1973-74 then 1974-75" as a then-case.**
  - **Hong Kong 2009-12, read at source (rows 26-29).** The Hong Kong dollar has been linked to the US dollar since 17 October 1983, within HK$7.75-7.85 (HKMA), so a Hong Kong tender price is in effect a US-dollar price. World shares (MSCI ACWI price) bought at the start of 2006 were 26.5% down at the start of 2009; Hong Kong's building works tender prices then rose 17.9% (Q1 2009 to Q4 2010). The next two January purchases met the same shape: -18.6% then +24.2% (2010-11), -18.0% then +17.5% (2011-12). The slump first cut tender prices 29.8% (Q3 2008 to Q2 2009); the recovery then lifted them 43.2% to Q4 2011.
  - **1973-75 (rows 30-32):** the burst came with the slump, not after it. US construction materials rose 29.2% from January 1973 to December 1974 (consumer prices +21.8%) while US share prices fell 45.0%; Hong Kong's tender prices rose 34.8% (Q4 1972 to Q2 1974) and then fell 35.7% by Q3 1975. After the slump, US materials rose 15.6% over 1975-76 against consumer prices' 11.7%: an inflation-paced rise, not a burst.
  - **The same 2004 burst elsewhere (rows 33-34):** US construction materials rose 15.2% from January 2003 to December 2004 (consumer prices +4.7%), the same world steel burst, so not an independent case; and Hong Kong's tender prices fell 2.6% over the same window, so not every economy felt it.
  - **Judge's note:** a smart reader expects a crash to cheapen building (demand falls). In Hong Kong it did, for a year; the two years after the meetings are what Laura's window catches, and there the rebound ran the other way.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 36 (with rows 20-22), 3e aha 9.** The count the aha has lacked: both 30% world slumps since 2000 were followed within two years by a Taiwan building about a quarter dearer in US dollars, by two different engines. It turns "this happened once" into "this happened after each of the two slumps we can check", still told as dated events.
2. **Row 18 (with row 24), 3e.** Only one of the four episodes followed a share slump; the worst came after a boom. It keeps the writers from citing "four times since 1991" as the aha's frequency, and it shows the second route (a burst large enough to beat even a grown half kept).
3. **Rows 27-29, 3e claim (d).** Hong Kong after 2008: the same shape outside Taiwan, with no currency part because of the peg, and a twist a judge will like: the slump first made building cheaper by 30%, then the recovery made it 43% dearer, and Laura's window meets the recovery.
4. **Rows 11-13, 3e and Table 3 row 7.** The burst was one quarter of world steel (rebar +37.8% in three months; US steel "double the level of mid-2003"). It explains why money kept back, not a forecast or a Taiwan-only watch, is the answer.
5. **Rows 4-5 and 10, for the register (NUMBERS 9's open items).** Replace "about 10% down" with 16.7% on the price series and "56-57%" with 53.8% (or 49.1% on Laura's calendar), and print one calendar per sentence (-44.2% with January 2003 to December 2004).

- **World shares before 1997.** MSCI's level service starts on 1 January 1997, so the share move before the 1991-93 episode is a US stand-in (OECD US share prices); MSCI World for 1987-91 and 1972-75 was not read at source.

- **The mechanism after 2008-09** (a weaker US dollar and a commodity rebound after world slumps; BIS's "standard pattern") was not re-read today; card Q131 row 31 read it on 18 Nov. Nothing here says a slump causes a burst.
