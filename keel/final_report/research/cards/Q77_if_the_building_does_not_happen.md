- **Problem class:** conditional and failed gifts: what happens to a promise or a transfer when the purpose it was made for fails, is delayed or changes.
- **Field that solved it first:** the law of charitable subscriptions and charitable trusts: English cy-près, American subscription cases from the 1840s and Cardozo's Allegheny College (1927). ## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with one change. It holds because she still owns the money, and a careless pledge could undo it.**
  - **What the plan does.** Until IBTM ends "on or about December 15, 2032" (CANON B11), both ends of the range sit in Treasuries in Laura's own name: "money you already own" (IPS l.15).
    - The case has her decide the contribution only "After establishing the operating reserve in 2033" (case l.101). In 2031 she says only what "she expects to contribute" (case l.110).
    - If the building is cancelled first, nothing has left her account. Centrally, the two funds' $123,476 and the half kept ($21,347) are all hers (NUMBERS 4; Keel reading, no new figure; row 3).
  - **What the law does to a written promise.** The risk is in the paperwork, not the money.
    - Allegheny College (N.Y. 1927) enforced a named, part-paid pledge. The court found consideration in the college's duty "to perpetuate the name of the founder of the memorial" (row 7).
    - Delaware held a donor who promised "If you build the hospital" to his word once building was under way, although the plans had changed (Danby, 1954, row 9).
    - A notarised promise in Taiwan cannot be revoked (Q55 row 35).
  - **So the change:** "stays hers" holds if her 2031 statement names the facility as its condition. In accounting terms that condition is a barrier with a "right of release" (row 10).
    - It does not hold if she signs an unconditional pledge to a body that outlives the building.
    - Any part already paid falls under claim 2. Taiwan lets a donor revoke only "the portion has not been transferred" (row 11).
  - **Judge's note:** most founders rely on a return clause, and Garden Bridge's donors found that a clause cannot return money already spent (rows 28-30). Laura's money never leaves her until the building needs it, so a cancelled building costs her nothing she owns.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. The claim is right about the donor's own rights and wrong about what the law does when the agreement says nothing. That default differs by country.**
  - **United States, no clause: the money stays in charity.**
    - Under UPMIFA, a donor's consent can release a restriction but "may not allow a fund to be used for a purpose other than a charitable purpose of the institution" (row 13).
    - A court may redirect the money cy pres (rows 14, 18).
    - The donor cannot sue unless she "expressly reserved the right to do so" (Herzog, 1997, row 16).
    - In 1904 a New York court found that money raised for a home that "would not be built" belonged to the charity (row 12).
  - **United States, with a clause or a regulator.**
    - A reverter clause "prevails over" cy pres while the donor lives, or for 21 years (Uniform Trust Code s.413, row 15).
    - New Hampshire's regulator puts return first: money raised to construct a building that cannot be completed "must be returned to the donor", redirected with the donor's written consent, or sent to a court (row 17).
    - Vanderbilt could remove a 1933 donor's name from a dormitory only by repaying the gift at today's prices (row 20).
  - **England, no clause: the money goes back.** A failed appeal's money is applied cy-près only if the donor cannot be found after advertisements and inquiries, or has signed a disclaimer of the right to have it returned (Charities Act 2011 s.63, row 21).
  - **Taiwan.**
    - A gift "with a charge", such as a duty to build, that the donee does not perform can be revoked and reclaimed (Civil Code arts. 412 and 419, row 22).
    - Money that has become a foundation's property never goes home: on dissolution its remaining property "不得歸屬於自然人" (may not go to any natural person; Foundations Act art. 33, row 23).
  - **Public capital grants come back by statute.** Massachusetts' cultural facilities grants are "repaid immediately" if the building stops being a cultural facility (row 25). Federal real property needs "disposition instructions" (2 CFR 200.311, row 26).
  - **Judge's note:** the intuition that a donor gets her money back when the building dies is the English rule, not the American one. In the US, money already given for a cancelled building usually stays in charity unless the donor wrote otherwise.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with two changes.**
  - **Why it holds.** The case dates the ten payments "one at the beginning of each year from 2033 through 2042" (case l.88-89) and ties them to no building.
    - The holdings end in cash on their own: the T-bonds mature and the iBond funds terminate.
    - Nothing is sold because the building is late. The waiting cash sits in the Treasury-bill ETF, where every waiting dollar already sits (NUMBERS 0; TNA l.5; row 5).
  - **Change 1:** the planned 2031 move still sells half of VT (IPS l.15). That sale is planned, not caused by a delay.
  - **Change 2:** a delay has a price. At the register's central Taiwan building-cost growth of 3.0% a year (NUMBERS 8d), each year of delay takes about $3,450 of buying power from a $115,000 contribution. Across the 1.5%-5.0% band it is $1,725-$5,750 (Keel arithmetic, before what the waiting money earns; row 6). Cloud Gate waited nearly seven years for its new building (row 41).
  - **If the whole residency is cancelled,** the payments have no payee and the reserve stays hers: $444,545 before the 2033 payment at today's forwards (NUMBERS 8h; row 4). The IPS's two reasons (IPS l.17) are reasons to change the holdings. A cancellation changes who is paid, not what is held.
- **Claim 4: five lines a careful founder writes, each found in real documents (rows 8-10, 19, 28-38, 42):**
  - the purpose and the condition: the facility is built;
  - when the money is paid: at a construction milestone, not before;
  - a start-by date after which the promise lapses (Laredo: "does not begin within twenty-four (24) months", row 35);
  - what a smaller or moved project means for the figure (Mississippi, row 19);
  - who gets unspent money back, and how fast (Piedmont: "within 30 days", row 36).
  - Laura needs only the first three, because she pays nothing in advance (row 39).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 40 with row 3 (3b): the aha.** In the US, money already given for a cancelled building usually stays in charity; in Taiwan, once it is a foundation's, it never comes back. Laura's contribution stays in Treasuries in her name until December 2032, so a cancellation costs her nothing she owns. This is the plan's design, not a clause, and it extends the conceit: ink is ownership, and ink not yet spent is still hers.
2. **Rows 28-30 (3b, 3e): Garden Bridge's return clauses.** The careful donors wrote "return in the event of the project not proceeding". About £7 million then became a legal claim, because £53.5 million had been spent first. This is the strongest case for paying only when the building exists.
3. **Row 31 (3e): the Elk Grove zoo, 2025.** It was cancelled because of "increased construction costs", and its donors were offered the choice to "reclaim or reaffirm". It is a current, plain example of the risk the IPS's flexibility names (building costs that overrun). Laura's owned money needs no such letter.
4. **Rows 1, 5 and 6 (3a, 3e, Table 3): a delay moves no payment and forces no sale.** It does cost buying power: about $3,450 a year on $115,000 at the register's 3.0% (Keel arithmetic). This answers the case's request for assumptions about cash-flow timing, and adds a Table 3 watch: "facility start date against the contribution's date".
5. **Rows 9, 10, 35 and 39 (3d): one condition in her 2031 words.** The figure is for the facility, payable when it is built, with a start-by date. Danby shows that unwritten conditions do not count, FASB that a written barrier makes the promise conditional, and Laredo that a start-by date keeps a delay from running on forever.
