## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. The market pays Laura for a risk she does not carry, and it is roughly what lets the ten fit inside her first deposit

- (a) Modigliani and Sutch argued in 1966 that each investor has a "habitat", the maturity that matches when its own debts fall due, and leaves it only for an extra return; Vayanos and Vila (2021) added the people who are paid for leaving theirs: traders ("arbitrageurs") who hold long bonds they do not need, carry the risk that the price falls before they sell, and in the authors' words "earn rents" from it "through bond risk premia". That extra return is the term premium: the extra yield a long Treasury pays over what the market expects short-term rates to average until it matures.
- (b) Laura's ten January dates make Treasuries ending in 2032-2041 her habitat, so for her the dated Treasury is the riskless holding and rolling short bills is the risky one (store H7-003); but the bond's price is set for the traders, who must be paid for a price risk that she never runs because she holds each bond to its end. In late 2026 two Federal Reserve models put that payment at about 0.6 of a point (ACM, 30 Oct) to 0.87 of a point (Kim-Wright, 6 Nov) on a 10-year Treasury, and on the register's own costs of the ladder at lower yields that is roughly $19,000 to $28,000 of the January 2027 purchase (Keel arithmetic): without it the ten would have cost about $321,000 to $330,000 on 6 Nov 2026, and the 2028 deposit would have had to complete $21,000 to $30,000 of them instead of $2,287.
- (c) It breaks in three places, and each teaches something: the premium is a model's estimate, not a price anyone quotes (two public copies of the ACM file differ by about 0.06 of a point in every month of 2026, and Kim-Wright's figure is a quarter of a point higher still); it is collected once, at purchase, and locked, so it says nothing about later purchases; and it runs the other way on the coupons, which are reinvested later at short rates the premium says will on average be lower than today's forward rates (store Q64-034). So the plan never leaned on being paid: it priced every coupon's wait at zero interest (NUMBERS 2), which makes the premium a gift on the purchase price, never support for any payment.
- **Decision it serves:** the first deposit buys all ten as dated Treasuries in January 2027 (IPS l.11, l.13: "roughly $300,000 – in other words, your entire first deposit").
- **Report part:** 3a; one clause available to 2.3.
- **Source:** Vayanos and Vila (2021), abstract and pp. 2-3, 8; McElhattan (1975), p. 28, for Modigliani and Sutch; ACM via the eco3min mirror (30 Oct 2026, 0.62) and the macrodatahub mirror (Oct 2026, 0.68); Kim-Wright via store Q64-005 (6 Nov 2026, 0.87); NUMBERS 1 ($302,287 at the 6 Nov curve, $317,742 at 0.5 points lower, $334,092 at 1 point lower). Keel arithmetic: interpolating NUMBERS 1 at 0.62 and 0.87 of a point gives $321,666 and $329,841, so $19,379 and $27,554 more; a parallel shift stands in for a premium that differs by maturity.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the idea that long bonds pay a premium is pedigree to any fixed-income judge (H3-015). The aha is its size and its place in this plan: the market pays Laura about $20,000 to $28,000 to hold the one asset that is riskless for her, because it prices that asset for someone for whom it is risky.

### Idea 2. Strangers set the price of her habitat, and a habitat investor buys whatever the price

- (a) Britain's Pensions Act 2004 judged pension funds on the gap between the market value of their assets and of their promises, with the promises valued at long inflation-linked gilt yields, so the funds crowded into long gilts; by January 2006 the gilt maturing in 2055 yielded 0.48% above inflation, against a historical average of about 3%, longer gilts yielded less than shorter ones, and the government responded by selling 50-year gilts (Greenwood and Vayanos 2010; Gromb and Vayanos 2010).
- (b) Laura's habitat in US Treasuries is not crowded: on 6 Nov 2026 the 30-year yield stood 0.40 of a point above the 10-year (NUMBERS 0), both Fed models put the premium above zero, and across 26 countries the gap between 30-year and 10-year yields is smaller where pension and insurance money is larger (Greenwood and Vissing-Jorgensen 2018). So the same plan, bought in London gilts in January 2006, would have paid for its safety; bought in US Treasuries in January 2027, it is paid for it.
- (c) It breaks on what Laura can do about the price, which is nothing: her plan holds at most 0.022% of the late-2032 fund (NUMBERS 8w), and in Vayanos and Vila's calibration it takes central-bank purchases of 12% of GDP to lower the 10-year yield by 25 to 30 hundredths of a point. The break teaches the decision's real ground: UK pension funds kept buying long gilts at 0.48% because the dates were fixed, and IPS l.11 puts the payment money "straight into Treasuries, because it's fixed and non-negotiable", a rule that would stand in a year when she was charged; only the price would change.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.11's rule (promised money into Treasuries) and the one-time purchase in January 2027, which locks the price she was offered.
- **Report part:** 3a (one sentence on why the rule does not depend on the premium); 3e (the premium as an estimate).
- **Source:** Greenwood and Vayanos (2010), sections I and II and the LSE version's footnote 3; Gromb and Vayanos (2010), "Pension Reform and the Term Structure"; Greenwood and Vissing-Jorgensen (2018), abstract; Vayanos and Vila (2021), p. 5; NUMBERS 0 and 8w.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha for most readers, niche even among finance judges: Greenwood and Vayanos note that preferred habitat was "typically being relegated to a paragraph in MBA-level textbooks". The UK contrast shows the plan's choice was never a bet on being paid.

### Idea 3. Step short, never long: why every rung ends before its payment and none after

- (a) In Modigliani and Sutch's account, as a 1975 Fed economist summarised it, an investor who leaves its habitat takes a risk on either side: a long bond held past the date must be sold first, which carries "a risk of capital gain or loss over the holding period", while a bond that ends too early leaves money to reinvest at a rate nobody knows yet.
- (b) IPS l.13's "each maturing just before it's needed" always errs to the short side, by 17 days for the funds and up to 320 days for the bond behind the 2038 payment (CANON B8), because the short side's worst case can be priced in advance: the plan assumes the waiting money earns nothing after the waiting fund's fee, and the extra Treasuries ($36,774) are sized so all ten are still paid (NUMBERS 2). A bond ending after its payment would have to be sold in a market no one can see in 2027, and that loss has no figure to set aside.
- (c) It breaks because the theory's habitat asset is a bond that pays everything on the date (Vayanos and Vila's habitat investors "demand zero-coupon bonds with specific maturities"), while Laura's bonds and funds pay interest for years before each date: by 1 January 2033 that interest is worth $93,941, nearly two of the ten payments (NUMBERS 2). So much of her money lives on the short side of her habitat for years, not days, and zero-coupon Treasuries are off Wharton's permitted list this year, which is why the extra Treasuries exist at all.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.13 ("just before"); the extra Treasuries' size (NUMBERS 2); TNA note 1's 10.5-month gap.
- **Report part:** 2.2 (one clause on "just before"); 3a.
- **Source:** McElhattan (1975), p. 28; Vayanos and Vila (2021), p. 2; CANON B8; NUMBERS 2; TNA l.5.
- **Pedigree or aha:** half aha. That holding to maturity removes price risk is textbook; that the theory explains why the plan may end months early but never a day late, and prices only the early side, is not.

### Idea 4. The same gap, the opposite answer: German insurers chase duration as yields fall, Laura pays for her gap once

- (a) Duration is the average time until a holding's money arrives, weighted by how much arrives when. In 2014 German insurers' promises ran much longer than their bonds (liabilities 20.5 rising to 25.2 years, government bonds 11.3 rising to 15.7), and because each fall in yields lengthened the promises faster than the bonds, the insurers bought more long bonds as yields fell: their demand "became upward-sloping", and they made about 40% of German residents' net bond purchases while holding 12.5% of their bonds (Domanski, Shin and Sushko 2015).
- (b) Laura has a gap of the same kind, because her coupons arrive early: her payments' duration is 9.94 years and her holdings' 7.76 (NUMBERS 1). But the plan never closes it by buying longer bonds as yields fall; it covers it once, with extra Treasuries sized for interest at zero, so after January 2028 a fall in yields asks nothing more of her, and IPS l.17 lists no market move as a reason to trade. This explains TNA note 1's "duration-matching", which NUMBERS 1 says needs explaining: Caplet matches dates, not durations, giving each payment its own Treasury ending before it, and a date match needs no re-tuning.
- (c) It breaks on length: German life policies average 31 years (Möhlmann 2021), so the insurers' promises run past most bonds and they must manage duration, while Laura's last payment, January 2042, falls inside the Treasury market's own maturities. Her plan does hold one moment of the insurers' behaviour, since if yields fall before January 2027 the first deposit buys less and the 2028 deposit buys the rest (IPS l.13), but it buys a fixed number of dollars due on fixed dates, once, and never chases duration again.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.13 (complete once with the 2028 deposit), l.17 (no market reason to trade); the extra Treasuries; TNA note 1's wording.
- **Report part:** 3a; 2.3.
- **Source:** Domanski, Shin and Sushko (2015), findings 1-5 and conclusion; Shin (2017); Möhlmann (2021), Table and text; NUMBERS 1 and 2; TNA l.7.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Cash-flow matching against immunisation is pedigree to an actuary; that one European insurance sector's falling-yield buying and Laura's extra Treasuries answer the same gap in opposite ways, and that this is what "duration-matching" in the Trading Notes really means, is new.

### Idea 5. Her habitat was set by her own promise and is measured by what it pays, not set by a regulator and measured at market

- (a) Britain made its pension funds into long-gilt habitat investors by a measuring rule: the regulator looked at each plan's "accounting deficit", the difference between the market values of its assets and its liabilities (Greenwood and Vayanos 2010), so as yields fell the measured gap widened and the funds bought long gilts, and later, borrowing against those gilts, they were forced to sell them in the 2022 collateral calls (store H7-037, Q2a).
- (b) Laura's dates come from the case, and her measure is what each Treasury will pay on its date: IPS l.15 calls the floor the fund's "payout", and the range rule counts it as units held times the holdings' remaining coupons and principal, added up undiscounted, with nothing earning interest (NUMBERS 0). So a fall in the fund's price between 2028 and 2032 changes no figure she has stated, and no measure in the plan ever forces a trade.
- (c) It breaks once, in 2031: the move sells half the shares at that day's market price, so one measure in the plan is a market value, but only for money not yet in Treasuries, and once it is moved it is counted by payout again. The teaching: market value decides what she can add to her stated figure, payout decides what she has already stated.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15 ("payout"), the range rule's measure (NUMBERS 0), IPS l.17.
- **Report part:** 3c (one clause on what the bottom is counted in); 3e.
- **Source:** Greenwood and Vayanos (2010), section I; Gromb and Vayanos (2010); NUMBERS 0 ("How Laura measures an end on the day"); IPS l.15, l.17; store H7-037.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree that market-value rules drove UK pension buying; the aha is that the range rule measures the floor the way a habitat investor would, which is why the bottom cannot move once stated.

## Best three for the report

1. **Row 10 (idea 1), 3a.** The market pays Laura roughly $19,000 to $28,000 on the January 2027 purchase to hold the one asset that is riskless for her, because it prices that asset for traders for whom it is risky; that is roughly why the ten fit inside the first deposit. Concrete, dated, from the register's own figures, and it turns H3-015's slogan into a sum.
2. **Rows 20 and 26 (idea 4), 3a or 2.3.** German insurers and Laura have the same kind of gap; the insurers chased duration as yields fell and moved a market, while Caplet paid for its gap once with extra Treasuries. It also explains what the Trading Notes meant by "duration-matching": matching dates, which never needs re-tuning.
3. **Row 14 (idea 2), 3a, one sentence.** In January 2006 the same plan in British gilts would have been charged for its safety, not paid, and pension funds bought anyway; the IPS's rule never depended on being paid, only the price did.
