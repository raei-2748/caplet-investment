- **Problem class:** what a person whose income is a skewed, cyclical lottery prize should promise to an organisation whose costs are hours of human time. **Field that solved it first:** cultural economics (Baumol and Bowen 1966; Throsby 1994; Abbing 2002) and the sociology of artistic labour (Menger 1999), on foundations laid by Adam Smith's chapter on wages (1776, I.10).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Throsby's day job: in this plan, higher Treasury yields pay for more shares

- **(a) The parallel.** David Throsby modelled an artist who takes a day job only until rent and food are covered and spends every other hour on art, so when the day job pays better she works fewer hours at it and more at her art (Throsby 1994, as Rengers 2002, ch. 2, and Menger 1999, p. 562, state the model).
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - Laura's plan works the same way: Treasuries are bought only up to what the ten payments need, and everything left goes to the facility.
  - So when yields rise, the payments cost less and the range rises at both ends. On the 13 Nov curve, about 0.3 points higher than 6 Nov:
    - no top-up is needed, and $6,203 of the first deposit is left over;
    - VT gets $31,544 rather than $29,594;
    - the range is $105,000-$125,000 rather than $95,000-$115,000 (NUMBERS 4 "On a later curve", a note and never a re-size).
  - A fall of 1.30 points before January 2027 does the reverse: the payments take half the 2028 deposit ($75,001), VT gets nothing and Laura states one figure, $85,000 (NUMBERS 5(e)).
  - A finance reader expects higher bond yields to pull money into bonds. Here they push money into shares, because the bonds are bought to a fixed need, not to a fixed share of the portfolio.
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - Artists obey the model only loosely:
    - Rengers and Madden: +2.6 hours a week of art for creators when the outside wage doubles.
    - Casacuberta and Gandelman: "no significant increase in arts hours" among Uruguayan musicians.
  - The reason is that an artist's survival line bends. Laura's cannot: ten dated $50,000 payments are a floor no year can shave, so the plan obeys Throsby more strictly than any artist does.
  - The response also runs only on the days money is placed (January 2027, January 2028 and the move before 2031). Once a Treasury is held, later yields move nothing in the payments: IPS l.13's "no price fluctuations for us to fear".
- **Decision served:** IPS l.11's order (promised money first, only unpromised money in shares) and l.13's completion with the 2028 deposit.
- **Report part:** 3e and Table 3's row on yields before January 2027 (one name); 3c's dated curve.
- **Source:** Throsby (1994); Rengers (2002); Casacuberta and Gandelman (2012); NUMBERS.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha.**
  - A pension actuary knows that rising rates help a funded plan.
  - No finance judge will have met that fact as an artist's working week.
  - It predicts a fact the reader can check in the register: a week of higher yields stated a higher range.

### 2. She earns in Rosen's economy and spends in Baumol's

- **(a) The parallel.**
  - Sherwin Rosen showed that an author puts in "the same effort whether 10 or 1,000 people ... buy the book", so "small differences in talent become magnified in larger earnings differences" (1981).
  - Baumol and Bowen showed that a live performance takes as many hours as it always did, so its costs rise faster than prices, "neither a matter of bad luck nor mismanagement" (1966, p. 162).
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - Laura earns in the first economy and will spend in the second.
  - Her income scales, so it swings with the economy (top earners' incomes are about 2.4 times as cyclical, card Q1b-012; arts sole proprietors' receipts fell 7.8% in 2009, card Q148).
  - A residency's costs are people's time, and they do not fall in a recession. Taiwan's monthly minimum wage stayed at NT$17,280 through 2008-2010, while average monthly earnings in industry and services fell 4.92% in 2009 (MOL; Keel arithmetic).
  - So the payments cannot be paid out of income still to be earned. The plan buys all ten from the 2027 deposit, money already earned. The 2028 deposit, which is still Rosen money and could fall in a recession year, carries only what can wait: at today's rates the ten meet the escrow standard with a 2028 deposit 73.9% smaller (NUMBERS 8u).
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - Her own making is Baumol work too: a 200-page graphic novel takes one to two and a half years (card Q12b-017). So she is Rosen only in selling, and the swing in her income comes from the selling.
  - The break teaches what the plan hedges: not her art, but the market that pays for it.
  - The same scaling makes her income large enough to fund a residency and too unstable to run one.
- **Decision served:** IPS l.9 (a career is a holding she cannot sell, and it falls in the same recession as shares); l.13 (the first deposit buys all ten); the missed deposit as one of the two reasons to change.
- **Report part:** 2.3's lead.
- **Source:** Rosen (1981); Baumol and Bowen (1966); MOL (Taiwan) minimum wage and earnings tables.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha as a pair.** Each half is pedigree to an economist. Pairing them for one person, whose money crosses from the scalable economy to the unscalable one, is new.

### 3. A stock she can't sell, and can't insure either

- **(a) The parallel.**
  - Pierre-Michel Menger found that artists face "extremely skewed" earnings.
  - They manage that risk by holding several jobs, "a cycling pattern of allocation of occupational time between various kindred activities".
  - He also found that "Only some of the occupational risks in the arts are insurable" (1999).
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - The pitch says Laura's career is a stock she can't sell. It is also one she can't insure.
  - Her three incomes (advances, speaking, licences) are kindred activities. They spread the risk of one book failing, but not the risk of a recession (IPS l.9).
  - The insurance schemes leave authors out:
    - France gives performers and technicians their own unemployment scheme (more than 312,000 people in 2023), yet "Le statut d'artiste-auteur ne permet pas l'accès aux allocations chômage".
    - In the US, self-employed writers had no unemployment cover until the emergency Pandemic Unemployment Assistance of 27 March 2020, which ended on 6 September 2021.
  - So the portfolio is her only holding outside the arts. The only insurance she can build is a portfolio that does not fall when her career does.
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - Kindred incomes can split: in 2020 Australian children's authors' book income rose while their events income fell (card Q56-012). So her three incomes are not one stock in every downturn.
  - The break teaches that the plan does not need to know which kind of recession comes: a Treasury held for each date pays in either kind.
- **Decision served:** pitch l.3 and IPS l.9 ("a counterbalance ... definitely not a contributor").
- **Report part:** 2.3 or 3a.
- **Source:** Menger (1999); Service Public (2026); Sénat (2025); Congressional Research Service (2021).
- **Pedigree or aha: aha in its second half.** "Can't sell" is Caplet's own line. "Can't insure, not even in France" is new, and it turns the pitch's image into a checked fact.

### 4. In the arts the last donor is the artist, so certain money is the residents' money

- **(a) The parallel.**
  - Baumol and Bowen found off-Broadway ran on actors "whose willingness to work for so little money is as real a financial contribution as the gift of any patron".
  - Hans Abbing found that artists' own unpaid work "relieves some of the effects of the cost disease" (2002, thesis 60).
  - So when an arts budget runs short, the artists make up the difference.
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - For a residency, a short year falls on residents: fewer places, shorter stays or smaller stipends. A plan that let a market fall reach the payments would make the residents its last donor.
  - IPS l.11 ("none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price") means no resident ever pays for a market fall.
  - A 2022-24 New York trial measured what certain money does for artists:
    - 2,400 artists were paid $1,000 a month for 18 months;
    - they worked 3.9 more hours a week on art and 2.4 fewer on other jobs;
    - their arts earnings stayed flat; the authors read this as "less commercially viable but more personally meaningful projects" (Noonan and Woronkowicz 2026).
  - That is Caplet's closing line measured: certain money is what lets artists take risks on the page.
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - The payments are certain in dollars, not in what dollars buy. At 2.4% US inflation the 2042 payment buys about 70% of what $50,000 buys today (NUMBERS 8d; less at Taiwan's wage pace, card Q102).
  - By default, then, the residents become the last donor again, slowly, unless others fill the gap. The case names who: "co-sponsors, grants, collaborators, program fees".
  - The trial is not a clean test of a residency:
    - residents are chosen on merit, not need;
    - stays run for months, not 18 months;
    - its comparison group was matched, with only part of it randomly assigned, and its first-survey response rate was 23.3% against 55.8% for those paid.
- **Decision served:** IPS l.11 and l.19; why "high certainty" is taken one step further; 3e's limit and 3d's line on who carries the rest.
- **Report part:** 3a (why), with the limit in 3e.
- **Source:** Baumol and Bowen (1966); Abbing (2002); Noonan and Woronkowicz (2026); NUMBERS 8d; the case.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha.** Finance judges know the cost disease as a cost problem. "The last donor is the artist" turns it into a statement of who would bear the plan's risk.

### 5. Adam Smith's lottery, and the house not insured from fire

- **(a) The parallel.**
  - Adam Smith wrote that in professions like poetry "the chance of gain is by every man more or less overvalued".
  - He wrote that "the contempt of risk and the presumptuous hope of success" are strongest "at the age at which young people choose their professions".
  - He found the same temper in insurance: "nineteen houses in twenty, or rather, perhaps, ninety-nine in a hundred, are not insured from fire" (1776, I.10).
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - The IPS opens on Smith's prediction: "You'd think that someone with your taste for thoughtful risk ... would usually own shares".
  - The temper that chooses an arts career is the temper that leaves the house uninsured.
  - Caplet's plan insures the house (the promises, owned in Treasuries) and gives the taste for risk only money nobody is owed (the half of the shares promised to no one, IPS l.15).
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - Laura is not Smith's young entrant: she has drawn a prize, and her risk is "thoughtful", not presumptuous.
  - The break teaches the plan's point: having won one draw, her career still holds a ticket in the next one (idea 2). So the promised money need not buy her a second ticket.
- **Decision served:** IPS l.9's turn ("You'd think ... until we stopped") and l.15's half kept.
- **Report part:** 2.3's opening sentence or 3.0 (one name).
- **Source:** Smith (1776), Book I, ch. 10; Menger (1999) on Marshall's lottery; Abbing (2002), thesis 38.
- **Pedigree or aha: pedigree for an economist.** Chapter I.10 is the founding text on why risky trades pay what they pay. For most finance judges it is an erudite surprise: a 1776 text predicts the IPS's first sentence.

### 6. Vanbrugh's opera fund: only a fixed promise can be inked

- **(a) The parallel.**
  - In March 1720 Vanbrugh estimated that the fund behind London's Royal Academy of Music stood at £20,000. He thought that was enough to keep opera going "till Musick takes such Root, as to Subsist with less aid".
  - Baumol and Bowen add: "Instead of less support, however, it constantly needed more" (1966, p. 161).
  - They draw the rule: any group that supports the arts "can expect no respite".
- **(b) What it shows.**
  - The cost disease breaks an open promise to keep an arts house going until it stands alone, because the house's costs rise faster than prices.
  - Laura's promise is ten fixed dollar sums on fixed dates. That is the only kind of promise that can be bought in full in advance, and so inked.
  - The case fixes it ("not adjusted for inflation") and leaves "any operating support beyond Laura's commitment" to others.
- **(c) Where it breaks.**
  - Fixing her share does not cure the disease; it moves it. Every dollar of cost growth above $50,000 a year, and every year after 2042 (outside the case's scope), lands on co-sponsors. So 3d must say plainly that her share is fixed in dollars and does not rise.
  - The fixity is the case's rule, not Caplet's choice. The plan's choice is to buy the fixed promise now.
  - The break teaches the trade at the plan's heart: a promise that grows with the residency's needs can never be owned, while a fixed one can be owned in full before the doors open.
- **Decision served:** why the ten are bought in January 2027 (IPS l.11, l.13); 3d's words on what her share is and is not; the conceit (Vanbrugh's promise could only ever be pencil).
- **Report part:** 3d (one name), or the conceit's turn in 3a.
- **Source:** Baumol and Bowen (1966), pp. 161 and the introduction; the case.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha.** Almost no finance judge knows the 1720 fund. It gives the conceit's lesson in one historical sentence.
