## (b) Cards: the eight strongest ideas

### Card 1. The stock-or-bond test
- **What is it?** A career counts as "a stock" only if its income is both volatile AND moves with a market index; low on
  both is "a bond"; in between is a mix plus single-name risk (Ibbotson, Milevsky, Chen & Zhu 2007, about p.18). Milevsky's
  barber Enzo has a variable income that "isn't connected to the market's fortunes and thus is more bond-like".
- **Did it work?** It is the standard practitioner frame: the CFA Institute (2012 blog: "a stockbroker should invest less in
  the stock market than a tenured professor"), Morningstar's engine (career = 30% stocks, 70% bonds) and Blanchett & Straehl
  (2015: equity falls with "riskier employment", 61% at 25 to 26% at 65). Rows 11-14.
- **How is Laura like it?** Volatility: yes, hugely (author income is a lottery: UK top 10% take about 47-70% of all income,
  row 39). Correlation: yes for the institutional money (advances, school budgets, events, licences), with a lag (rows 30-36).
- **How is she not?** Her readers behave like Enzo's customers: bookstore sales fell only 1.8% and 0.8% in 2008-09 while
  retail fell 8.2% (row 29). So she is "in between, leaning stock", not a pure share.
- **What Caplet takes:** the test gives a one-line, judge-proof definition of what the IPS asserts, and the honest refinement
  ("the cyclical half of her income"). It also shows Caplet's move is mainstream advice, applied to an unusual job.

### Card 2. Crowding out, and why the textbook default is the opposite (the main clash)
- **What is it?** Steady income acts like a bond you already own, so you should hold MORE shares; income that moves with
  shares acts like shares you already own, so you should hold FEWER (Campbell, Cocco, Gomes & Maenhout 2001, p.441).
- **Did it work?** The default in the books and industry is bond-like: Campbell & Viceira (human wealth "sufficiently
  stable" to tilt towards risky assets), Vanguard's target-date funds ("a more stable or bond-like asset"), Bodie, Merton &
  Samuelson's $500,000 example (mostly riskless). Campbell (2000): income risk shrinks the tilt but does "not reverse it"
  without correlation. Viceira (2001): correlation reverses it. Rows 3-6, 15.
- **How is Laura like it?** She has no paycheck and no employer to absorb shocks (Fagereng, Guiso & Pistaferri 2018), and her
  income is business income, which real owners hedge by holding fewer shares (Heaton & Lucas 2000). Rows 21, 24.
- **How is she not?** Davis & Willen find occupation-level income is uncorrelated with the broad market; what tracks a career
  is its own industry (row 23). Huggett & Kaplan put the stock part of a typical career under 35% (row 10).
- **What Caplet takes:** name the default and say why Laura is the exception (self-employed, no paycheck, institutional
  buyers who cut later). The thesis must stand on correlation, not on lumpiness. CLASH noted: the theory's default
  conclusion is the opposite of the IPS; the Final Report should meet it head-on, not hide it.

### Card 3. Horizon: the link is weak this year and strong over a career
- **What is it?** Wages and shares are cointegrated: over one year they barely move together, over decades they cannot drift
  apart (Benzoni, Collin-Dufresne & Goldstein 2007). The measured link is with LAST year's market: 0.52 for college
  graduates, same-year "strikingly low and insignificant" (Campbell, Cocco, Gomes & Maenhout 2001, Table 11.1). Rows 7, 9.
- **Did it work?** It explains why young people hold few shares despite decades to wait (their human capital is already
  stock-like). Mean-reversion speed 0.05 to 0.20 a year; Keel arithmetic: a half-life of about 3.5 to 14 years.
- **How is Laura like it?** Early career (graduated 2018, case p.1), where the model says human capital is most share-like.
  A college graduate: the 0.52 group.
- **How is she not?** The portfolio only sees her career for 12 months (the 2028 deposit). Over that short window the link is
  weak. Aggregate US data, not creatives; NYSE, not world shares.
- **What Caplet takes:** this is why the IPS word "later" matters: it is the version the data supports. The Final Report
  should say "over a downturn" not "in the same month". CLASH (partly) noted: over one year the effect is small.

### Card 4. Same downturn, different clocks
- **What is it?** In 2008-12 shares and a writer's income fell in the same downturn but on different clocks: shares first,
  the institutional money one to three years later, while shares were already rising.
- **Did it work?** S&P 500 -36.55% in 2008, +25.94% in 2009, +14.82% in 2010 (Damodaran). Meanwhile: HMH froze new books in
  Nov 2008 ("turned off the spigot"); UK advances "reduced to a quarter" in 2009 while sales fell about 5%; US el-hi sales
  -13.8% in 2009; licensing royalties -5.6% in 2008 and -8.7% in 2009; school library resources -9.4% in 2010 (-25.5% in
  high-poverty schools); school library budgets still cut in 2012. Rows 28, 30, 31, 34, 35, 44.
- **How is Laura like it?** Her income is exactly these streams: "publishing advances, speaking engagements, licensing"
  (case p.2) and books "read in classrooms" (case p.1).
- **How is she not?** These are industry series, not her income; one recession; 2001 shows almost nothing (row 27).
- **What Caplet takes:** the strongest evidence for the IPS's exact words "later and smaller". A judge will not have seen a
  school-library budget series in an investment report. Pair it with Card 3: the lag is what the academic data predicts.

### Card 5. Readers are defensive; institutions are not (and 2020 broke the pattern)
- **What is it?** The household side of her market (readers) barely moves in recessions; the institutional side (publishers'
  advances, schools, event budgets, licensees) does.
- **Did it work?** Bookstores -1.8% and -0.8% in 2008-09 against retail -8.2%; unit sales "fell meaningfully in only one year"
  (2009). In 2020 print units rose 8.2% and graphic novels 29%, but PreK-12 instructional materials fell 19.6% and 46.3% of
  authors lost speaking income. Rows 29, 32, 33.
- **How is Laura like it?** She has both halves; most authors earn about half their writing-related income outside books
  (Authors Guild 2023, row 38).
- **How is she not?** 2020 was a lockdown, not a normal recession, and in it her own genre boomed and her breakout came
  (row 46).
- **What Caplet takes:** "do people read more in recessions?" answered honestly: no general rule; book buying is defensive,
  not counter-cyclical. CLASH (partly) noted: 2020 book sales rose while shares crashed; only the school and speaking money
  fit "later and smaller" that year.

### Card 6. The danger is the left tail, and it sits at the top
- **What is it?** Recessions do not widen income shocks so much as skew them: "large upward earnings movements become less
  likely... large drops... more likely" (Guvenen, Ozkan & Song 2014). Earnings risk to the cycle is highest at the top:
  GDP beta 3.70 for men at the 99.9th percentile, 1.09 at the median man, 0.69 at the median woman (Guvenen et al. 2017).
  Rows 16, 18.
- **Did it work?** Catherine (2022) shows this skew explains why the young hold few shares; Catherine, Sodini & Zhang (2024)
  find Swedish workers with worse left-tail risk in bad markets hold less equity (row 19).
- **How is Laura like it?** A bestselling author is at the top of a winner-take-all ladder (row 39). Her risk is that the
  next hit does not come, or a big drop does, in the same years shares fall.
- **How is she not?** These are employees in tax records, and beta to GDP, not to shares. Top earners in services are less
  cyclical than average (row 17).
- **What Caplet takes:** a precise reading of "high degree of certainty": it is a promise about the left tail, which is why
  the payments cannot rest on assets that fall when her left tail is fattest. Good for the CERTAINTY definition.

### Card 7. Real people hedge this way, and real people were hurt when they did not
- **What is it?** Revealed hedging: people with risky, cyclical income hold fewer shares; people who tied savings to their
  income source lost both together.
- **Did it work?** Households with "high and variable business income hold less wealth in stocks" (Heaton & Lucas 2000). A
  household moving to a high wage-volatility industry cuts its risky share "by up to 35%, or USD 15,575" (Betermier et al.,
  Sweden). Enron: 62% of 401(k) assets in Enron stock, price from over $80 to under 70 cents, "not only their jobs... but
  also a significant portion of their savings" (GAO). Bear Stearns staff lost "more than $3 billion" in a month. Ordinary
  families too: 39% of US households hit by unemployment, negative equity or arrears in 2008-10 (Hurd & Rohwedder).
  Rows 21, 22, 41-43.
- **How is Laura like it?** She made the Betermier switch: salaried tech product manager to self-employed author (case p.1).
- **How is she not?** Owning your employer's stock is a far tighter link than a career and a world fund. VT holds publishers
  only at a tiny weight (row 23).
- **What Caplet takes:** the plan copies what careful households actually do, not a theory. Use Enron and Bear Stearns as the
  principle, never as a claim about the size of Laura's risk.

### Card 8. The case switches off the flexibility channel, so the risk lands on the 2028 deposit and the floor
- **What is it?** Bodie, Merton & Samuelson (1992): a worker who can work more after a bad year can take more market risk.
  The case removes that option: no money in or out before 2033 except the two deposits (case p.2). Huggett & Kaplan: a
  career is worth far less than its Treasury-discounted value. Rows 2, 10, 47.
- **Did it work?** Theory, widely taught. Applied here, it narrows the thesis: the portfolio is exposed to her career only
  through the $150,000 deposit at the start of 2028.
- **How is Laura like it?** A recession in 2027 could shrink the deposit while falling yields (10-year Treasury +20.10% total
  return in 2008) raise the cost of finishing the ladder. MODEL (25 Sep 2026 curve): a 1-point yield fall adds about
  $30,600, about a fifth of the deposit. So the ten payments survive unless the deposit falls by roughly four-fifths. Row 48.
- **How is she not?** A recession need not cut yields; the deposit is set by the case, which gives no sign it is at risk.
- **What Caplet takes:** the exact place her career risk lands: on the floor and the band, not on the ten payments. CLASH
  (partly) noted: "the bottom will remain constant" holds once the floor is bought in Jan 2028; before then a recession can
  lower it, and the IPS's two reasons name a "missed" deposit, not a smaller one. A risk to state in the Final Report, not
  a change.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **Her income falls after shares, and stays down while shares recover.** In 2009 shares rose 25.94% while US school
   publishing fell 13.8%, licensing royalties fell 8.7% and advances were cut to a quarter; school library money fell 9.4%
   in 2010 and was still being cut in 2012 (rows 28, 30, 34, 35). The academic data agree: the link is with LAST year's
   market (0.52 for college graduates, same-year insignificant; Campbell, Cocco, Gomes & Maenhout 2001). Most teams will
   say "correlated"; very few will say "lagged", which is what the IPS's "later and smaller" actually claims.
2. **The textbook says the opposite, and the case is what makes Caplet right.** Campbell & Viceira, Vanguard and Bodie,
   Merton & Samuelson all lean young workers towards MORE shares, partly because they can work harder after a bad year. The
   case forbids any money in or out before 2033, so that insurance never reaches the portfolio, and Laura has no paycheck
   for the default to rest on. Naming the default and showing why it does not apply is rarer and stronger than ignoring it.
3. **Her risk is the left tail, not the average, and it lands on the 2028 deposit.** Recessions mainly make big drops more
   likely and breakouts less likely (Guvenen, Ozkan & Song 2014), and top earners carry the most cycle risk (beta 3.70 at the
   99.9th percentile). Laura's own breakout came in the 2020 crash year, so her career is a hit-driven single stock, not an
   index. The one place it touches the portfolio is the 2028 deposit, in the same years falling yields raise the ladder's
   cost: the payments survive (a 1-point fall costs about a fifth of the deposit, MODEL), the floor takes the hit.
