- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A promise can be raised later, never taken back, so under uncertainty a promise should start low."
  2. "Her flexibility is worth more than its dollar value because it can still be used for whichever need appears."
  3. "The value of keeping a choice open is measured and used in practice, not only a theorem."
  4. "Holding back is the safe side: stating less now has no cost."
- **Problem class:** a decision made in steps, under uncertainty, where one act cannot be undone and news arrives between the steps (the "irreversibility effect"). **Field that solved it first:** environmental economics, twice in the same year: Arrow and Fisher (1974) on wilderness, and Henry (1974) on Paris's ring road and Notre-Dame. The investment-timing literature then turned it into the "option to wait" (Pindyck 1991; Dixit and Pindyck 1994), and practice tested it (CFO surveys, land prices, oil wells, venture rounds, the Thames flood plan). The counter-case comes from strategic commitment (Kulatilaka and Perotti 1998), negotiation (Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001) and environmental policy with two irreversibilities (Pindyck 2001).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Two words are wrong: "start low" and "raised".**
  - **What the theory says.** Replacing uncertain returns by their averages "will here, systematically and unduly, favor irreversible decisions" (Henry 1974, p. 1007). The fix is to take "the loss of options" off the benefit of the act that cannot be undone (Arrow and Fisher 1974, p. 319), not to pick a low number. And "the bias against development is due solely to the restriction on reversibility" (p. 315): if a stated figure could be withdrawn at no cost, the theory would give no reason to hold anything back. Laura's reason is the case's own (l.114: promising more than she can give "could damage her credibility").
  - **What the plan does is not "start low".** It states what the late-2032 fund will pay once it is owned. The bottom comes from the fund bought in January 2028 and the top from the same fund bought again with the moved half: $95,000 to $115,000 at today's rates (NUMBERS 4). It promises nothing else. Her flexibility (about $21,200 of shares) plus anything the funds pay above the top (about $7,500) stays promised to no one (NUMBERS 8o; IPS l.15).
  - **"Raised later" is the wrong verb for the plan.** The stated range is never raised. Money added later from her flexibility is "Laura's separate choice, outside the announced range" (CANON D12), and the contribution never exceeds the stated top (WORDS). Arrow and Fisher's own words fit this: the plan "can be revised (at least in the direction of additional development)" (p. 316). That means money added, not a figure restated. Real donors do add in steps: one lead donor gave $5 million in 2013, $25 million in 2017 and $6 million in 2021-2022 to one university's arts programme (row 36), and the funders in Marx and Matthews's games give each period (row 23).
  - **The second irreversibility.** "There are two kinds of irreversibilities, and they work in opposite directions" (Pindyck 2001, p. 2). For Laura, the first is her word. The second is a co-sponsor's decision made on her first figure: a co-sponsor who walks away in 2031 may not come back (Keel inference). The store's fundraising experiments measure this side: bigger first money draws more giving (List and Lucking-Reiley; Huck, Rasul and Shephard). So holding back pays only while it costs less than it buys. The IPS's half sits in the middle of a nearly one-for-one trade (NUMBERS 8o).
  - **Say:** "she states only what she owns, and keeps the rest promised to no one". **Never:** "start low", "under-promise", "raise her promise later".
  - **Judge's note:** the economics behind holding back never says "promise low". It says a plan built on averages promises too much, because an average cannot see what a promise rules out (Henry 1974).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. A dollar is never worth more than a dollar; one unpromised dollar can wait for whichever of three jobs turns up.**
  - **Money's value depends on its likely use.** Corporate finance measures what a firm's extra dollar of cash is worth "depending upon the likely use of that dollar". The value "declines with larger cash holdings, higher leverage, better access to capital markets" (Faulkender and Wang 2006). A cushion is worth most when it is small and other money is hard to raise. That fits a founder with a fixed budget, but it says nothing about "more than a dollar". The paper's average figure was not read (Gaps).
  - **Why one pot for three uses is efficient.** In stock-keeping, one pooled stock costs less than separate stocks: "the expected holding and penalty costs in a decentralized system exceed those in a centralized system", and "the magnitude of the saving depends on the correlation of demands" (Eppen 1979). This adds the primary source to the store's pooled-buffer rows (H4-057, H5-038).
  - **The pool's limit, in Laura's numbers.** The saving disappears when the needs come together. Building costs and the Taiwan dollar together rose by more than 28% in 1 of 20 two-year spans; on a $115,000 top that needs $32,191, "about all of her flexibility plus anything above the top" (NUMBERS 8q, label corrected Thu 19 Nov).
  - **Henry's definition gives the honest wording.** A decision is irreversible "if it significantly reduces for a long time the variety of choices that would be possible in the future" (p. 1006). What her flexibility keeps is a variety of choices, not extra dollars.
  - **Say:** "promised to no one, each dollar can go to whichever need comes first". **Never:** "worth more than its value", "a free option".
  - **Judge's note:** flexibility does not make a dollar worth more than a dollar. It lets one dollar wait for whichever of three jobs turns up first, and one pot for three jobs can be smaller than three pots unless the jobs come together.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with modest sizes.**
  - **Firms say they use it.** More than one-fourth of 392 US finance chiefs "claimed to be using real options", "somewhat surprisingly". The authors add that the main use is probably "a qualitative strategic planning tool" (Graham and Harvey 2002). Only 2% of UK and Irish firms used it usually or almost always (Geddes 1999, as cited by Philippe 2005).
  - **Prices show it.** Seattle land prices carry "a premium for the option to wait to invest that has a mean value of 6 percent in the sample" (Quigg 1993).
  - **Behaviour shows it.** Texas oil drillers drill fewer new wells when expected price volatility is high, which "accords with predictions from real options theory" (Kellogg 2014). Venture investors wait longer between rounds when market uncertainty is high; competition and project-specific uncertainty push them to invest sooner (Li 2008).
  - **Governments plan this way.** London's flood plan reviews every 5 years and is updated every 10, and "The timing of the phases may change" (Environment Agency, TE2100).
  - **The sizes in theory are modest.** The right to wait is worth $86 on an $800 factory in Pindyck's (1991) example, about 11% of its cost (Keel arithmetic). In Henry's "moderate" case it is worth about 13% of returns.
  - **Judge's note:** the option to wait is real and priced, and its measured size is a few percent to about a tenth. That is a reason to keep some money back, never a reason to keep most of it.
- **Claim 4: KILL.**
  - **Waiting can be lost or costly.** "Firms do not always have an opportunity to delay investments" and "There may be a cost to delay" (Pindyck 1991). "Competition does destroy each firm's option to wait" (Dixit and Pindyck 1994, p. 18).
  - **Early commitment can be worth more under uncertainty.** Where an early move wins a strategic edge, more uncertainty favours committing early: "Our results contradict the view that volatility is a strong disincentive for investment" (Kulatilaka and Perotti 1998).
  - **The first figure anchors.** First offers "were a strong predictor of final settlement prices" (Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001). The fundraising experiments already in the store measure the same pull for gifts.
  - **The irreversibility effect itself can fail.** It fails with "a low elasticity of intertemporal substitution", and "will hold if the value of information increases in the degree of flexibility" (Narain, Hanemann and Fisher 2005).
  - **The plan's own price of waiting.** Each $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,267 of what she keeps, and her total barely moves (NUMBERS 8o).
  - **Judge's note:** the price of waiting is paid by the co-sponsors' planning, and in the plan's own numbers it is close to dollar for dollar. The IPS keeps half; it does not claim half is the best split (never "optimal").

## Cards for the strongest rows

**Card 1. Averages promise too much (Henry 1974; Arrow and Fisher 1974; rows 1-7).**
- What is it? In the same year, two economists showed that when one act cannot be undone and news is coming, a decision made on averages picks that act "more often than he should" (Henry, writing about Paris's ring road and Notre-Dame). Arrow and Fisher found the same bias for a wilderness and said the point holds "without reference to environmental effects".
- Did it work? It became the base of a field: Dixit and Pindyck's book, environmental policy and real-options practice (rows 24-29). Henry put a size on a "moderate" case: the flexible choice stayed as good with returns 13% lower.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her stated figure cannot be taken back (case l.114), news arrives between 2031 and 2033, and the IPS states only owned money. So it never promises on an average.
- How is it not? Her "act" is a promise, not a demolition. She cannot wait: the case fixes 2031. Her choice is how much to state, not when.
- What Caplet takes: one sentence for 3b that a judge will not expect. The economics never says "promise low". It says a plan built on averages promises too much, because an average cannot see what a promise rules out.

**Card 2. The co-sponsors are investors too (Pindyck 1991; row 14).**
- What is it? Pindyck's lesson for anyone facing an irreversible investment: "stability and credibility may be more important than tax incentives or interest rates".
- Did it work? It is the standard explanation of why uncertainty slows building and hiring. Texas drillers (row 27) and venture rounds (row 28) behave as it predicts.
- How is Laura's case like it? Co-sponsors decide in 2031 whether to put money into a building they cannot unbuild. A figure that cannot move lowers their uncertainty more than a larger figure that might.
- How is it not? Pindyck writes about firms and governments. The step to co-sponsors is Keel's reading, and the store's experiments show that size matters too (bigger first money draws more).
- What Caplet takes: 3d can say why the owned figure helps the co-sponsors, not only Laura: it is the one number in their plans that will not move.

**Card 3. Two one-way doors (Pindyck 2001; Kulatilaka and Perotti 1998; Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001; rows 17, 19, 21-22).**
- What is it? Waiting has its own irreversibility. A policy delayed lets damage build ("two kinds of irreversibilities ... in opposite directions"). A firm that waits can lose its strategic edge, and a first figure anchors what follows.
- Did it work? These are theory and laboratory results, with field support from the store's fundraising experiments (bigger leads draw more giving).
- How is Laura's case like it? Stating too much spends her credibility; stating too little can lose a co-sponsor for good. Holding back is not free.
- How is it not? Co-sponsors are partners, not rivals or bargaining opponents, and the plan leads with its top figure, which is owned.
- What Caplet takes: 3e names the cost of her flexibility honestly (a smaller promised figure than all the shares would give), and 3b calls half the middle of a trade, never the safe side.

**Card 4. One pot, three jobs (Eppen 1979; Faulkender and Wang 2006; NUMBERS 8q; rows 30-32).**
- What is it? A pooled stock costs less than separate stocks, and the saving depends on how the demands move together. A dollar of cash is worth more where it is scarce and other money is hard to raise.
- Did it work? Pooling is standard practice in stock-keeping and the store already applies it to buffers (H4-057). The value-of-cash result is a large-sample finding about firms.
- How is Laura's case like it? IPS l.15 gives one pot three uses. Unpromised, each dollar waits for whichever comes first, so one pot can be smaller than three.
- How is it not? When two needs come together the pool runs out. Building costs and the Taiwan dollar together need about all of it in 1 of 20 two-year spans (NUMBERS 8q).
- What Caplet takes: replace "worth more than its dollar value" with "one dollar waiting for whichever of three jobs comes first", and put the joint limit in 3e.

**Card 5. The price of the choice kept (NUMBERS 8o; rows 33-34; GOLDMINE 23).**
- What is it? The plan's own trade: each $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,267 of what Laura keeps, and her total barely moves.
- Did it work? It is a model result, reproduced across 200,000 simulated futures and two histories.
- How is Laura's case like it? The decision in IPS l.15 is not about how much she has; it is about how much of it is still a choice. That is exactly what the quasi-option literature prices.
- How is it not? The literature gives no rule for the share to keep (Q5c, Q5 card 7). Half is Caplet's judgment.
- What Caplet takes: GOLDMINE 23 lands in 3b as one line: promising more would not make her richer, only less free, at about $5,300 of choice for each $5,000 of promise.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 (Henry's bias), 3b.** It answers "why not promise more?" with a 1974 result a judge will not expect: averages favour the act you cannot undo.
2. **Row 33 (the option's price in NUMBERS 8o), 3b.** It places GOLDMINE 23: each $5,000 more promised costs about $5,300 of choice, and the total barely moves.
