- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** payoff design for a protected minimum with some share upside, and who stands behind the minimum. ## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Both halves are right about the mechanics; "leaves the shares uncapped" needs its other half.
  - **How the products do it.** A buffer ETF holds a package of exchange-traded FLEX options on an S&P 500 fund: a put spread (or, for a 100% buffer, a put) gives the protection, and a call sold "as far out of the money as possible, while still generating enough premium to cover the cost of the buffer" pays for it; "The sale of this call is what creates the upside cap" (Innovator). Innovator's November 2026 funds: a 9% buffer with a 20.27% cap (BNOV), and a 100% buffer with an 8.74% cap (ZNOV), both for one year and 0.79% a year. The outcome holds only for a holder from the first day to the last; returns are on price only, "the Fund will not receive the benefit of any dividends" (ZNOV prospectus).
  - **What Laura does instead.** Each promise is paid by a Treasury she owns: the ten from January 2027-2028, the bottom of the range from January 2028, both ends after the 2031 move (NUMBERS 4). No option is bought or sold; nothing is reset each year.
  - **The other half of "uncapped".** VT is uncapped, but what co-sponsors receive is capped too: the contribution never exceeds the top stated in 2031. Both designs cap. The difference is who keeps the gain above the cap. In a buffer fund it goes to whoever bought the call the fund sold. In Laura's plan it stays hers: the excess over the stated top joins her flexibility ($7,503 plus the half kept $21,179 = $28,682 centrally, "her flexibility plus anything above the top", NUMBERS 4 and the Thu 19 Nov label).
  - **What is protected differs.** A 100% buffer fund protects its starting value each year, less fees (net buffer 99.21%, ZNOV). Laura protects dated figures, not her deposit: if VT went to zero the 2028 money would return $102,365 on $110,838 (NUMBERS 4, "Not capital-protected"; Keel arithmetic: 7.6% less).
  - **One year against five.** On 1 Oct 2026 (1-year Treasury 4.44%) a one-year floor cost about $95.75 per $100, leaving $4.25 for shares; a buffer fund with a 100% buffer and a 9.43% cap (ZOCT) beat that Treasury-plus-shares mix whenever the S&P 500's price rose more than about 5.3% in the year, and lost to it in every fall and every flat year (about 4.3 points at zero). Laura's floor runs five years (January 2028 to late 2032): it costs $81,244 to pay $102,365, so 26.7% of the money left goes to VT (NUMBERS headline and section 4; Keel arithmetic). Innovator's 100% buffer funds run six months, one year or two years, none five (Innovator brief).
  - **Judge's note:** the industry and Laura both cap the promise; only Laura keeps the gain above the cap, and only Laura's protection is bought once for the date it is needed, not every year.
- **Claim 2: KEEP for notes; CHANGE the wording; add the third kind.**
  - **Notes.** The store already holds the credit point (Q3a rows 14-16: an issuer's guarantee is "only as good as the financial strength of the company"). New here is the price: 64 issues of one popular retail structured equity product were sold at prices "almost 8% greater" than their fair value, with a mean expected return "slightly below zero" (Henderson and Pearson 2011); three equity-linked notes carried hidden costs of 11.1%-17.2%, and a plain Treasury-plus-stock portfolio beat them 94%-97% of the time (McCann and Luo 2006).
  - **The wording.** A Treasury is also a promise, the US government's. The true line is whose promise and how it is held: a bank's note is the bank's unsecured promise, a debt owed to her; a Treasury held for her is the government's bond, owned by her and kept apart from the broker's own assets (the law's word is "owned", UCC 8-503; the report says "held for her", never "in her name"). IPS l.15's own words are "money you already own".
  - **The ETF kind.** A buffer ETF is not a bank's promise: Innovator says its funds "are not backed by the faith and credit of an issuing institution, so they are not exposed to credit risk", and the options' "OCC acts as guarantor and central counterparty". But the prospectus also says "The Fund does not provide principal protection" and "Since the Buffer is not guaranteed, a shareholder may lose their entire investment." The ETF wrapper took the bank out; it kept the cap, the fee, the lost dividends and the yearly reset.
  - **Judge's note:** three generations sold the same promise: kept by trading (1987), by a bank's credit (2008), by options re-bought every year (2018 on). Laura's is kept by owning the bond that pays it.
- **Claim 3: KEEP the attribution; CHANGE the wording to Marks's own.**
  - **His words** (Oaktree memo, 22 Oct 2024, p. 2): "That's why this kind of investing is called 'fixed income' – the income is fixed. For the purposes of this memo, however, it might help to think of it as 'fixed outcome' investing." Next line: "This isn't a difference in degree; it's a difference in kind." The slogan "fixed outcome, not fixed income" is a paraphrase; quote the sentence, never the slogan.
  - **Coinage.** Marks does not claim it and credits no one; no earlier use was found in this run. Say "Marks's phrase" or "as Marks puts it", never "coined by Marks".
  - **His own caveat** in the same paragraph: the return is known "assuming the borrower makes the promised payments when due". For Treasuries the report adds no such caveat (Books 3, 5, 19).
  - **Scope for Laura.** True of the five T-bonds held to maturity. The iBond funds pay what their bonds pay less costs, never a fixed sum (CANON A8; NUMBERS 3); say "a fixed outcome" of the ladder as a whole, not of each fund's payout.
  - **Judge's note:** the industry named its option funds "Defined Outcome", the bond's own promise; AQR says investors "erroneously infer that a buffered fund is able to provide a similarly defined outcome" as a bond. Laura bought the bond.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE (UNVERIFIED either way); never write "closed to her anyway".**
  - Wharton bans "Derivatives, including options, futures, and swaps" and permits "Exchange-traded funds available on WInS" (Final Report instructions). A buffer ETF is an ETF that holds options. No Wharton text says whether such an ETF counts as a derivative. Wharton's 24 Oct FAQ banned "Individual TIPS" and "Individual STRIPS", which separates an instrument from an ETF holding it; read the same way, a buffer ETF listed on WInS could be permitted. Whether WInS listed any is UNVERIFIED (Keel never opens WInS).
  - So the honest line is: options themselves were not allowed; a fund that holds them was a design not taken. That is the stronger story for 3b (a refusal, not a rule).
- **Claim 5: KEEP what the store has; add one fact.** After October 1987 the price of protection changed shape: index option implied volatility was "largely flat" before the crash and "downward sloping" after it (Constantinides, Jackwerth and Perrakis, citing Rubinstein 1994 and Jackwerth and Rubinstein 1996). Every put a buffer fund buys today is priced in that post-1987 world, and option sellers are paid a "volatility risk premium" for it (Israelov 2019). Constantinides and co-authors also argue the post-crash smile is not "too steep", so say "priced higher", never "overpriced".
- **What the studies say, in one line each:** AQR: buffer funds lagged a beta-matched mix of their index and Treasury bills by 0.8% on average across three drawdowns, and more than 80% lagged in two of them (Asness et al. 2025); median cost to cumulative return 6.4% for a median drawdown benefit of 0.5% (Asness and Villalon 2025). Israelov: 36.5% in the S&P 500 and 63.5% in cash matched a put-protected index's return "with less than half the volatility". Morningstar: 59 of 88 buffer ETFs beat a 60/40 fund over 2021-2025, by 0.75% a year on average, mostly from 2022, and they fit long-term investors worst. The products do what they say (target-tracking studies); the cost is the cap, the fee and the dividends.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 42 (with rows 2 and 16), 3b.** Both designs cap what is promised; only Laura keeps what is above the cap. It turns the obvious objection ("why not a protected fund?") into a point for the plan, with register figures.
2. **Row 32 (with 33-34), 3a.** Marks's sentence quoted exactly, scoped to the bonds and the ladder, with "as Marks puts it" in place of "coined". It names the standard in a famous practitioner's words and closes store H1-031's open check.
3. **Row 21 with row 36, 3a or 3b.** The industry borrowed the bond's promise ("Defined Outcome") for a one-year options package, and AQR says buyers wrongly think it works like a bond. Laura bought the bond itself. This is the aha in one contrast.
4. **Row 17 (with row 7), 3b or 2.2.** A floor bought for five years leaves 26.7% of the money for shares; a one-year floor leaves about 4%, and no full-buffer product runs past two years. Time paid for her shares. The products have no five-year term, so they fail the date test.
5. **Rows 19 and 25 (one clause), 3b.** The evidence: buffer funds lagged a simple index-plus-bills mix (AQR), and holding less in shares beat buying puts (Israelov). Row 23's fairer Morningstar figure goes beside them if there is room, so the report never claims the products always lose.
