**Claims tested (each to KEEP, KILL or CHANGE).**
1. "For money promised to no one, one world fund at market weights is the right default against a home-biased (US)
   fund."
2. "International diversification fails when it is needed most (correlations rise in falls), so a world fund does not
   protect the top in a crash; what it protects is the long-run outcome."
3. "A world fund (Taiwan about 2% of ACWI) does something for a Taiwan-dollar cost."
4. "One fund's fee (0.06%) and simplicity beat a hand-built mix for an individual's unpromised money."

**Problem class and the field that solved it first.** International portfolio choice for a surplus pot whose possible
uses are partly in a foreign currency. International finance solved the first half: the international CAPM says every
investor holds the world market portfolio (Solnik 1974, as reported by Coeurdacier and Rey 2013), and French and
Poterba (1991) named the gap between that and what investors hold "home bias" and priced it as a belief about returns.
The crash half belongs to the tail-dependence literature (Longin and Solnik 2001; Forbes and Rigobon 2002); the horizon
half to Asness, Israelov and Liew (2011); the currency half to international asset pricing with real exchange-rate
risk (Adler and Dumas 1983, as reported) and to currency-overlay research (Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros and Viceira
2010). The individual investor's version is the practitioners' (Bogle, Swensen, Ellis, Vanguard's research), read here
for what each says about one global fund.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: KEEP, with its frame changed.**
   - The real choice is 62.4% US (VT, fact sheet of 30 Sep 2026) against 100% US, so it touches 37.6% of her
     flexibility: about $8,000 of the central $21,347 on 1 Jan 2033 (NUMBERS 8k; Keel arithmetic).
   - Holding mostly at home needs a belief, not a fact: in Dec 1989 US investors held 93.8% of their shares at home,
     Japanese 98.1%, British 82.0%; against a value-weighted world portfolio that takes about 90 basis points a year of
     home optimism for Americans, 250 for Japanese and over 400 for British investors (French and Poterba 1991a).
   - Over 1900-2005 the world index earned 5.75% a year real at a 17.23% standard deviation, less risk than every
     country but Canada; the US 6.52% at 20.19% (Dimson, Marsh and Staunton 2006). Over 1974-2024 global beat home on
     Sharpe ratio in "the vast majority" of 32 countries, and the US was the exception (UBS Yearbook 2025).
   - The largest market can fail for decades: Japan was 45% of the world market at the 1989 peak (Reuters 2024; 25.4%
     after cross-holdings, French and Poterba 1991b), lost 5.23% a year real through the 1990s (DMS 2006), and the
     Nikkei took 34 years to close above 38,915.87. The US is about 62% today, and its concentration the highest for
     at least 100 years (UBS Yearbook 2026).
   - Change: call VT the default or "a helpful starting point" (Vanguard 2021's words), never "right" or best. No
     study says a world fund beats a US fund over Laura's five years (the US won 1974-2024), and the newest lifecycle
     study goes further abroad than VT (about one-third home, two-thirds international for a US couple: Anarkulova,
     Cederburg and O'Doherty 2026). The evidence supports "not less abroad than market weight", not "exactly market
     weight".
2. **Claim 2: KEEP the first half; CHANGE the second.**
   - The crash half holds: international correlation "increases in bear markets, but not in bull markets" (Longin and
     Solnik 2001, five largest markets, monthly, 1958-1996). In each of 22 countries' worst month (1950-2008) the local
     market fell 27.0% on average and the global portfolio 17.2%; for the US (Oct 1987) -21.4% against -21.0%
     (Asness, Israelov and Liew 2011, Table 1). A world fund does nothing for the top in a crash; the plan's answer is
     the move of half into Treasuries before 2031 (IPS l.15).
   - Change "the long-run outcome" to "her flexibility against one country's long slump over the five years she holds
     it": country-specific growth explains about 1% of quarterly returns and 39% of 15-year returns, and the global
     portfolio's edge opens after a couple of years (AIL 2011; updated to 2022 by Asness, Ilmanen and Villalon 2023).
     Their global portfolio is equal-weighted and they say cap weights "reduce those benefits": VT is cap-weighted with
     62.4% in one country.
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE (the number and the hope).**
   - Taiwan is 3.5% of VT (fact sheet of 30 Sep 2026), its third-largest country, and 3.23% of MSCI ACWI (30 Oct
     2026), not "about 2%".
   - A world fund does almost nothing for a Taiwan-dollar cost: the Taiwan slice of the central flexibility is about
     $750, and a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar lifts it about $75 against an $11,500 need (0.65%); even if every non-US
     currency rose 10% at once, the non-US part (about $8,000) gains about $800, 7% of the need (Keel arithmetic).
   - For a dollar investor the foreign currency inside a world fund tends to fall when shares fall: the US dollar
     "moved against world equity markets" in 1975-2005 (Campbell et al. 2010); currency adds about 6 points of risk
     (UBS 2026) and has "no intrinsic return" (Vanguard 2021).
   - What the record does show: the two threats to her flexibility have tended to come apart. In 4 of the 5 US share
     falls of 15% or more since Oct 1983 the Taiwan dollar weakened (US dollars needed for a fixed NT$ budget down 0.4%
     to 13.2%; 1987 the exception, up 4.6%); across 492 two-year windows the correlation is +0.09 (NUMBERS 8j's figure,
     reproduced); in the 68 windows where US shares fell over two years, none saw a Taiwan dollar 10% or more stronger,
     against 15.8% of rising windows (Keel arithmetic). Few independent episodes; the plan does not rely on it. The
     IPS's answer stays her flexibility, tested against a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar (8j: 93.2% house).
4. **Claim 4: KEEP, re-weighted: simplicity decides, cost does not.**
   - VT 0.06% against VTI 0.03% and VXUS 0.05% blended at VT's own split (0.0375%): about $7 a year on $29,829, about
     $34 over five years; a US-only fund saves about $9 a year (Keel arithmetic on Vanguard's pages).
   - What one fund buys: market weights by construction, so no rebalancing rule the IPS never wrote, and a 2031 move
     that is one instruction (sell half of one fund).
   - Practitioners: Ellis, "wide-range indexing global markets makes good sense for everyone", "comfortable taking
     market capitalization as the basis"; Swensen, low-cost index funds with 20 of his 50 equity points abroad; Bogle
     dissents (at most 20% abroad, because "we spend our money in dollars"), but this money is spent, if at all, in
     Taiwan dollars, so his own reason does not reach it. Taxes (foreign tax credit) are outside the case's scope.

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. Home bias priced as a belief (French and Poterba 1991a; rows 1-3).**
- What is it? Two economists took what American, Japanese and British investors actually held at the end of 1989
  (93.8%, 98.1% and 82.0% at home) and asked what each would have to believe about returns for that to make sense.
  Against a value-weighted world portfolio, Americans needed about 90 basis points a year of optimism about home and
  110 of pessimism about Japan; Japanese investors 250 basis points; British over 400. Taxes, costs and legal limits
  were too small to explain it, so "This lack of diversification appears to be the result of investor choices".
- Did it work? It named the puzzle. Thirty years later the measure is still large (US 77.2% at home against a 32.6%
  weight in 2008; Coeurdacier and Rey 2013), and Vanguard still calls market weight "a helpful starting point" from
  which "many investors will consider an allocation below".
- How is Laura's case like it? A US fund for her flexibility would be the 1989 American's choice, and it would need the
  same unstated belief: that US shares beat the rest by roughly a point a year. The plan states one world figure (7.5%,
  TNA l.23) and no home premium.
- How is it not? The belief is not always wrong: over 1974-2024 the US was the one large market where staying home won
  (UBS 2025). And the question touches only 37.6% of her flexibility, about $8,000 at the centre (row 22).
- What Caplet takes: for 2.3, one sentence: the world fund is the default because a home tilt is a forecast the plan
  does not make; never "right" or "best", say "starting point". For 3b, the size (row 22) if a judge asks why not the
  S&P 500.

**Card 2. Japan, 1989: the largest market can fail, and market weights hold most of it at the top (rows 7-9, 4).**
- What is it? At the end of 1989 Japanese shares were 45% of the world market on headline weights, ahead of the US at
  33% (Reuters 2024); corrected for companies owning each other, 25.4% against 37.2% (French and Poterba 1991b). The
  Nikkei closed at 38,915.87 on 29 Dec 1989 and did not close higher until 22 Feb 2024. Japanese shares lost 5.23% a
  year in real terms through the 1990s (DMS 2006).
- Did it work? For a home-biased Japanese saver it was a 34-year trap. For a world investor it was a large but diluted
  loss, and the rest of the world carried the portfolio (the US gained 14.24% a year real in the same decade, DMS 2006).
- How is Laura's case like it? The US is now the largest market at about 62%, its concentration the highest for at
  least 100 years (UBS 2026). A US fund would be the 1989 Japanese position; VT holds the US at 62.4% and the rest at
  37.6%.
- How is it not? A world fund at market weights would have held more Japan than US at the top, so it does not avoid the
  biggest bubble; it dilutes it. Today's float-adjusted indexes cut the cross-holding double count (Keel inference).
  And Laura holds for five years, not 34.
- What Caplet takes: for 2.3 or 3e, one fact a judge remembers (the 34 years), framed as what one fund at market
  weights does: it never bets on which country, so it never holds only the one that fails. Not a prediction about the
  US.

**Card 3. Diversification fails in the crash and works in the slump (Longin and Solnik 2001; Asness, Israelov and Liew
2011; rows 10-15).**
- What is it? Correlation between national markets "increases in bear markets, but not in bull markets" (Longin and
  Solnik). In each of 22 countries' worst month since 1950 the global portfolio still fell (17.2% against 27.0%); in
  the US's worst month, Oct 1987, it fell 21.0% against 21.4%. Over longer horizons country-specific growth takes over
  (1% of quarterly returns, 39% of 15-year returns), and that "is precisely what international diversification protects
  investors against" (AIL).
- Did it work? The 2023 update to 2022 holds the pattern (Asness, Ilmanen and Villalon). Forbes and Rigobon (2002) show
  part of the "crisis rise" is a volatility effect, but markets move together in crashes either way.
- How is Laura's case like it? Claim 2 is right that VT does nothing for the top in a crash, and the IPS never asks it
  to: it moves half into Treasuries before 2031 (l.15). After the move a crash reaches only her flexibility.
- How is it not? Laura holds VT from Jan 2028 to 2033, five years: inside AIL's range where the gap opens, but short
  of their 15 years. Their global portfolio is equal-weighted, and they say cap weights "reduce those benefits"; VT is
  cap-weighted.
- What Caplet takes: the changed claim for 2.3: the world fund protects her flexibility against one country's long
  slump, not against a crash; the crash is met by the move into Treasuries. One sentence each; the US row of Table 1
  (-21.4% against -21.0%) if one number is wanted.

**Card 4. Bogle's reason, turned (rows 17-18).**
- What is it? The founder of index investing told Americans to keep at most 20% abroad: "We Americans save our money in
  dollars; we spend our money in dollars; we earn our money in dollars; and so we should invest our money largely in
  dollars." He would "not give the same advice to non-U.S. investors".
- Did it work? Over his own window it did: from 1993 US shares beat the rest (his figures: 9.4% against 5.2% a year to
  2016). Vanguard, his own firm, recommends more abroad; the 1974-2024 Yearbook result agrees with him for the US alone.
- How is Laura's case like it? Laura is American (case p.1: raised in Texas) and her payments are in US dollars, so
  for her Treasuries Bogle's reason holds, and the plan holds them in dollars.
- How is it not? Her flexibility is the money for "a stronger Taiwan dollar" and building costs in Taiwan (IPS l.15);
  if it is spent, it is spent in Taiwan dollars. Bogle's reason (invest where you spend) does not reach it, so it
  argues against an all-US fund here, not for it. It does not argue for a Taiwan tilt either, since the equity route to
  a currency fails in data (Q39 r22).
- What Caplet takes: the answer to the strongest practitioner objection, in his own terms, for 2.3 (one clause) or the
  Reflection if the team argued it. Quote Bogle once; never imply he would have approved VT.

**Card 5. What a world fund does for a Taiwan-dollar cost: almost nothing (rows 21-25).**
- What is it? Taiwan is 3.5% of VT (30 Sep 2026; 3.23% of MSCI ACWI on 30 Oct), not 2%. On the central path the Taiwan
  slice of her flexibility is about $750; a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar adds about $75 against an $11,500 need (0.65%).
  Even if every non-US currency rose 10% together, the 37.6% abroad adds about $800, 7% of the need.
- Did it work? The research says the currency inside foreign shares is mostly noise with no long-run return (Vanguard
  2021; about 6 points of extra risk, UBS 2026), and for a dollar investor it tends to fall when shares fall (the dollar
  "moved against world equity markets", Campbell et al. 2010).
- How is Laura's case like it? The IPS already names the currency as a use of her flexibility (l.15), and NUMBERS 8j
  tests it directly (a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar covered in 93.2% of house paths, 98.1% against the real currency
  since 1983).
- How is it not? The arithmetic holds local share prices fixed and treats currencies as moving together; neither is
  true. It sizes the effect; it does not forecast it.
- What Caplet takes: for 3b, nothing new in the prose: the currency is met by her flexibility's size, which 8j tests;
  never say or imply that VT offsets the Taiwan dollar. For 3e, the corrected Taiwan weight if a judge asks.

**Card 6. The two threats have tended to come apart (row 26).**
- What is it? On the repo's monthly series since Oct 1983, in four of the five US share falls of 15% or more the Taiwan
  dollar weakened (2000-03 by 11.7%, 2007-09 by 5.4%, 2020 by 0.4%, 2022 by 15.2%), so a fixed NT$ budget cost fewer US
  dollars just when shares were down; 1987 went the other way (Taiwan dollar 4.4% stronger). In the 68 two-year windows
  in which US shares fell, none had a Taiwan dollar 10% or more stronger; in rising windows 15.8% did.
- Did it work? As a pattern only. The correlation over 492 overlapping windows is +0.09 (the same as NUMBERS 8j's), close
  to none; the "68 windows" come from about three falls.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her flexibility is in VT and falls with shares; one of its uses (a stronger Taiwan
  dollar) has tended not to arrive in the same years. That differs from the other two uses in the store: co-sponsors
  fall short in slumps and building costs rise in the same year in inflationary falls (Q5e verdict 2).
- How is it not? US shares, not VT; prices without dividends; 1987 (inside the 1986-1988 appreciation, 8j's largest
  two-year move at +29.4%) shows the exception can be large. The plan does not need the pattern, and the report must not lean on it.
- What Caplet takes: for 3e or Table 3's note, at most one plain line ("in the record since 1983, falls in shares and a
  stronger Taiwan dollar have rarely come together; the plan does not rely on it"), with 8j's direct test beside it.

**Card 7. One fund as a process, not a fee (rows 27, 29, 19-20).**
- What is it? VT costs 0.06%; the same exposure in VTI and VXUS costs about 0.0375%, about $7 a year on $29,829; a US
  fund saves about $9 a year. What one fund buys is not price: it holds market weights by construction, so nothing
  drifts and nothing needs a rebalancing rule, and the 2031 move is one instruction.
- Did it work? Bogle's own test of rebalancing found the gain was "noise" (9.49% against 9.71% a year over 20 years).
  Ellis holds one global fund himself and judges indexing by whether the investor can stay with it; Swensen wanted
  index funds and rebalancing, with 40% of his shares abroad.
- How is Laura's case like it? IPS l.17 allows only two reasons to change the plan; a two-fund mix would bring a split
  and a rebalancing decision every year, which is a third reason to act that Caplet never wrote.
- How is it not? Swensen's discipline is rebalancing, which this plan does not do; Ellis does not get upset at a US-only
  choice, because "how people feel is a very important part of the reality". WInS's $25 commission and the foreign tax credit (row 28) are outside Laura's real plan.
- What Caplet takes: for 2.3 or 2.5, half a sentence: one fund because it needs no decision until the 2031 move, not
  because it is cheapest. The fee is already in Trading Note 3; say it once.

## (c) What would surprise a judge

- Taiwan, where the building will stand, is the third-largest country in Laura's world fund (3.5%, ahead of the UK at
  3.1%), and still a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar adds only about $75 to her flexibility against an $11,500 need.
- The founder of index investing argued against world funds for Americans because they spend in dollars; this money,
  if spent, is spent in Taiwan dollars, so his own reason points the other way.
- At the 1989 peak a world fund at headline weights held more Japan (45%) than US (33%); the Nikkei then took 34 years
  to regain its close. Market weights do not dodge the largest market's bubble; they only stop it being the whole bet.
- In the US's worst month since 1950 (Oct 1987) a global portfolio fell 21.0% against the US's 21.4%: the world fund is
  no help in a crash, which is why the IPS moves half into Treasuries before the meetings.
- In four of the five US share falls of 15% or more since 1983 the Taiwan dollar weakened; the choice between VT and a
  US fund moves her flexibility by hundreds of dollars on plausible return gaps, against a spread from market luck of
  about $9,900 to $41,300.

## Method (Keel arithmetic)

- Row 22: $21,347 (NUMBERS 8k, central half kept, 1 Jan 2033) x 37.6% (100% - 62.4% US) = $8,026; x 3.5% (Taiwan) =
  $747.
- Row 23: need = 10% of the $115,000 contribution = $11,500 (8d and 8j convention: 10% more US dollars). Taiwan slice
  $747 x 10% = $75 (0.65% of the need); non-US part $8,026 x 10% = $803 (7.0%). Local share prices held fixed.
- Row 26: monthly averages, S&P Composite price (repo Shiller mirror) and Taiwan dollars per US dollar (repo FRED
  EXTAUS series), Oct 1983 to Sep 2026, 516 common months. Falls: from a running peak to the lowest month before the
  index regained that peak, kept when 15% or more. Windows: every 24-month span (492), share change against the change
  in US dollars needed for a fixed NT$ budget (start rate / end rate - 1). Script kept outside the repo (scratchpad
  `q6d/twd_drawdowns.py`); results: falls 1987-08 to 1987-12 (-26.8%, US dollars needed +4.6%), 2000-08 to 2003-02
  (-43.7%, -10.4%), 2007-10 to 2009-03 (-50.8%, -5.1%), 2020-01 to 2020-03 (-19.1%, -0.4%), 2021-12 to 2022-10
  (-20.3%, -13.2%); correlation 0.091; share-down windows 68, median -3.6%, none at +10% or more; share-up windows 424,
  median +1.7%, 15.8% at +10% or more.
- Row 27: 0.624 x 0.03% + 0.376 x 0.05% = 0.0375%; (0.06% - 0.0375%) x $29,829 = $6.71 a year, about $34 over five
  years without growth; 0.03% x $29,829 = $8.95 a year for VTI alone.
- Row 30: 0.376 x 2 points = 0.75% a year; 1.0075^5 - 1 = 3.8%, x $21,347 = about $815. AQR's 4.6%: 1/1.046^5 = 0.799,
  so the non-US part ends about 20.1% lower relative to a US fund; 0.376 x 20.1% = 7.6%, x $21,347 = about $1,616.

- The broad dollar index (FRED) was refused by the proxy, so how far the Taiwan dollar moves with the dollar's broad
  swings (which is what a world fund's currency part would capture) is not measured.
- Row 26 uses US shares, prices without dividends, and overlapping windows from about three falls; the S&P mirror is
  not checked against Shiller's primary file.
- AIL's five-year figures (row 14), Swensen's currency sentence (row 19), Ellis's "about half" (row 20), MSCI's 3.28%
