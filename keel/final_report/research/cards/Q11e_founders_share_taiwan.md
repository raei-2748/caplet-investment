  2. "Taiwan's public subsidies for private cultural facilities fund only part of a project, so the founder's own share sets the project's size."

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Headline case: the Paul Chiang Art Center (江賢二藝術園區), Jinzun, Taitung; opened 15 Mar 2025.**
  - **The founder came first.** The artist and his wife bought all the land for phases 1 and 2, in stages, from selling paintings and houses. They also gave their own house and land.
    - The co-founder's words: 「沒有他們帶頭付出，其他都將無可能」 ("without their leading contribution, nothing else would have been possible") (row 1).
  - **The first co-sponsor.** Even then, "錢在哪裡都不知道" (no one knew where the money for the buildings would come from) until one family offered at a dinner to pay for one building.
  - **The rest followed.** 「有了首例後…陸續加入」: once there was a first case, four more sponsors joined one after another (row 2).
  - **The buildings.** All five were paid for by co-sponsors and carry their names (row 3).
  - **No values.** No total cost, and no value for the founder's share, is published (row 5).
- **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
  - **The order holds** in every private case found. The founder supplied the site or the building first; co-sponsors, then the state, came after.
    - The cases: Paul Chiang (rows 1-3); Chishang Granary (rows 8-9); 二呆藝館, built in 1989 with the founder's life savings of over NT$10m (row 11); the Juming Museum, built over 12 years "獨資" (alone) (row 12); Grand View (Q9c row 32).
    - No case was found where a co-sponsor led and the founder followed (row 31).
  - **"Largest single share" is not shown.**
    - The best case publishes no values. Its five buildings were all paid for by others (rows 3, 5).
    - The Alliance, Paul Chiang's operator, began with one NT$2m seed and nine equal NT$2m followers (row 7).
    - In the one programme with figures, private owners paid 48.3% of NT$1.09bn of refits, with the state paying the rest (row 18).
    - Most Taiwanese residencies are public-led (Q9c; not repeated).
  - **Changed claim:** in the private Taiwanese arts places found, the founder put in the site or building first and others followed. The founder's share by value is rarely published, and in the one large programme with figures, owners paid about half.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. It depends on the programme.**
  - **Undesignated private old buildings (the main programme with private capital).**
    - The cap is 50% of eligible items, up to 80% in special cases.
    - Priority goes to 「配合款額度高者」 (higher matching money).
    - The owner raises any shortfall and 「不得要求追加補助數額」 (may not ask for more) (rows 15-16).
    - In practice owners paid 48.3% overall. In the 153 works cases the median owner share was exactly 50.0%, and only 4 owners paid a fifth or less (row 18; Keel arithmetic on the MoC list).
    - The programme stopped taking new cases in June 2022 (row 20).
  - **Designated heritage.** The owner pays at least 5%, or nothing after giving 10+ years' right of use. The central share for a private national monument reaches 95% (row 21).
  - **So** the founder's share sets the size for an undesignated building (the state matches about one for one at most) and does not set it for a designated monument.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The claim is right on order and wrong on amount.
  - **Paul Chiang.** Families and companies paid all five buildings and land for a plaza, each building named for its sponsor (row 3). GlobalWafers gave NT$5m a year in 2023 and 2024 for "both the software preparation and hardware construction" (row 6).
  - **Chishang.** The owner paid the refit. A company's arts foundation paid for the lighting, and a fund manager pays operations (rows 8-9).
  - **Changed claim:** Taiwanese corporate and family money does pay for buildings, but only after a founder's site exists. It comes per building or per year, with names attached.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE (context only, never an estimate).**
  - **Her range in NT$.** $95,000-$115,000 is NT$3.11m-3.77m at 32.768 NT$ per US$ (the register's 6 Nov rate; Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4 and 8j).
  - **Against owners' parts.** In the MoC's subsidised refits, the owner's part had a median of NT$2.58m and an upper quartile of NT$4.08m (2018-2022 prices). 109 of 153 owners put in less than NT$3.77m (row 25).
  - **The nearest residency-type case.** The 卯澳 stone houses cost NT$7.47m in all; the owner's part was NT$4.13m (US$125,885) (row 24).
  - **Public residencies.** Refits run NT$20m-40m (rows 27-29), and NCAF's planned arts village NT$150m (row 30).
  - **So** the range is meaningful at the scale of a small rural refit, or of an owner's half of a mid-size one, and is a minor share of anything village-sized.
- **The one note a judge could use:** Taitung, 2025. The artist's land came first, bought by selling paintings and his house. One family then offered a building at a dinner; "once there was a first case", four more followed, and all five buildings carry their names. Laura's figure is owned before she asks (IPS l.15), so it can be the first case.

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Paul Chiang Art Center: land first, then a first building, then the rest (rows 1-7)
- **What is it?** An art park on a Taitung hillside, opened 15 Mar 2025.
  - **The founders.** The artist and his wife bought all the land for phases 1 and 2 in stages, from selling paintings and houses, and gave their own house and land.
  - **The first sponsor.** The co-founder says no one knew where the buildings would come from until one family offered a building at a dinner.
  - **The rest.** 「有了首例後」: once there was a first case, four more sponsors followed. All five buildings carry sponsors' names.
  - **A company.** GlobalWafers gave NT$5m a year in 2023 and 2024 for building and programme together.
  - **The operator.** The Alliance itself began with one NT$2m seed and nine equal NT$2m followers.
- **Did it work?** Yes: it opened with five buildings paid for, after 14 years from idea to opening, more than ten revisions of the development application, rising costs and a pandemic. No total cost or founder's share by value is published.
- **How is Laura's case like it?**
  - The founder's money exists, and is owned, before anyone else is asked.
  - The first co-sponsor follows something already there.
  - The co-founder credits the founders' "帶頭付出" (leading contribution) for everything that followed.
- **How is it not?**
  - The founders gave land, a house and works, not cash, at a scale far beyond $115,000.
  - The buildings were paid for by others, so the founders' share may not be the largest.
  - The chain ran through one network of Taiwanese business leaders, which Laura does not have and which the case does not give her.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3b, the order and its reason in a co-founder's words: others followed "once there was a first case". In 3d, Laura's owned figure can be that first case (IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"). Never claim she supplies the largest share (row 32).

### Card 2. Chishang Granary: the owner refits, a foundation runs it, companies pay parts (rows 8-10)
- **What is it?**
  - A 60-year-old rice granary in Chishang, Taitung, opened in Dec 2017 as an art museum of about 200 ping.
  - The rice-mill owner paid for the architect's redesign and refit himself, then entrusted the building to the Taiwan Good foundation for NT$1 a month.
  - A company's arts foundation paid for the lighting; a fund manager funds operations.
- **Did it work?** It opened and runs. The county's platform calls every step "民間自主" (private effort). No cost figure is published.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The capital comes from the founder, the running money from others, and the operator is separate: the same split Q11d row 30 recommends for 3d (Laura's money is capital; the operating plan is the residency's).
- **How is it not?** The owner already had the building and his business is local. Laura gives money to a facility she will not own or run, and no co-sponsor role is fixed in the case.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3d, a Taiwanese pattern of roles (founder's capital, a foundation's operation, companies' parts), as a store note. It supports claim 3's "programme money" half and limits its "not the lead capital" half.

### Card 3. The MoC's old-building programme: half and half, with overruns on the owner (rows 15-20)
- **What is it?** The Ministry's 2018-2022 programme for undesignated private buildings built by 1971.
  - **The cap.** 50% of eligible items as a rule, up to 80%.
  - **Priority.** Higher matching money goes first.
  - **Overruns.** The owner raises any shortfall and "may not ask for more".
  - **Strings.** Payment comes in three parts, and the building is kept for 3 to 10 years.
  - **The list.** About 248 cases, NT$1.09bn in all: owners paid 48.3%, and the median owner share in works cases was exactly 50.0%.
- **Did it work?** It funded about 248 cases, then closed to new ones in June 2022. A refit planned on it after that date found no programme.
- **How is Laura's case like it?**
  - A capped public share with the overrun on the owner is the risk IPS l.15 names first ("building costs that overrun").
  - A programme closing is a form of "a co-sponsor falling short".
  - The founder's money sets how much the state can add.
- **How is it not?** The programme is for private owners restoring their own old buildings, mostly homes, shops and small venues. A residency facility in Taiwan might be public, heritage, or new-built, and Laura is not its owner.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3e, as assumptions: a public share in Taiwan is capped, paid in parts, and can close; overruns are the owner's. For 3b, one Taiwanese fact behind "her money comes first": the state matched private owners about one for one and favoured those who put in more.

### Card 4. Designated heritage: the founder can pay almost nothing (rows 21-22)
- **What is it?**
  - Under the heritage bureau's rules, a private owner of a designated monument self-funds at least 5%, or nothing after giving 10+ years' right of use.
  - The central share is capped by the local fiscal tier (50%-90%), and reaches 95% for a private national monument.
  - Taichung copies the 5% floor.
- **Did it work?** It is the standing system (amended 2025-26). The Kinmen house in row 28 was repaired for NT$22.88m in public money and now hosts artists.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** It shows the limit of claim 2: whether a founder's share sets the size depends on the building's legal status.
- **How is it not?** Nothing in the case says the facility is in a heritage building, and the facility's cost and site are out of scope.
- **What Caplet takes.** A guard for the store: never write that in Taiwan "the founder's share always sets the size". Say it holds for undesignated private buildings.

### Card 5. Founders who gave everything, and the half Laura keeps (rows 11-13, 33)
- **What is it?**
  - 趙二呆 built his Penghu art hall in 1989 with his life savings of over NT$10m, on land the county gave.
  - 朱銘 built his museum over 12 years with his life savings and refused outside money.
  - 嚴長壽 praises the Paul Chiang founders for 「傾其所有」 (giving all they had).
- **Did it work?** The buildings stand. 二呆藝館 needed NT$23.18m of public repair money 37 years later. Juming now seeks sponsors for upkeep "beyond what the museum can bear alone".
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A founder's own money first, before anyone else's.
- **How is it not?** These are artists building their own museums. Laura's contribution goes to a shared facility, with ten payments owed first and half the shares kept for the facility's own risks (IPS l.15).
- **What Caplet takes.**
  - **The risk.** A Taiwanese reader may weigh "half promised to no one" against founders who gave everything (Clash, row 33).
  - **The answer.** IPS l.15's three uses, and "according to their means" (Q32).
  - **The fact.** Both Taiwanese all-in founders' buildings later needed others' money for repair or upkeep, which is what a kept margin is for. Store note, never a comparison that criticises them.

### Card 6. The scale of $95,000-$115,000 in Taiwan (rows 14, 24-30)
- **What is it?** Laura's range is NT$3.11m-3.77m at 32.768 (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4 and 8j). Some local yardsticks:
  - **Small art-space fit-outs:** NT$0.6m-2m (row 14).
  - **Taitung old-building refits:** NT$1.29m-3.28m in all (row 26).
  - **Owners' parts in subsidised refits:** a median of NT$2.58m and an upper quartile of NT$4.08m (row 25).
  - **The 卯澳 residency-type refit:** NT$7.47m in all, with the owner's part NT$4.13m (row 24).
  - **Public residency refits:** NT$20m-40m (rows 27-29).
  - **NCAF's planned village:** NT$150m (row 30).
- **Did it work?** As yardsticks only. Prices are 2001-2022 nominal, and building costs have risen since (NUMBERS 8d gives the range as NT$2.59m-3.14m of today's building at 3%).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** "Meaningful" can be shown locally. Her range is more than the median private owner put into a state-matched refit, and on its own it would have paid the whole of 40-54 of the 153 works cases.
- **How is it not?** No figure here is her facility's cost, which the case puts out of scope (l.164-166). The facility may be new-built or public, and the NT$ value moves with the rate (NUMBERS 8j).
- **What Caplet takes.** At most one clause in 3b, if the lead wants a local yardstick: her range is about what a private owner in Taiwan put into a small state-matched refit. Otherwise store only, beside Q11d row 32 and Q9c row 35.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
- In Taiwan's one programme with published figures for private owners' refits (about 248 cases, NT$1.09bn), owners paid 48.3%. The median owner share was exactly 50.0%: the state matched owners almost one for one, favoured those who put in more, and left every overrun to them (rows 15-18).
- At the Paul Chiang Art Center, the co-founder says no one knew where the buildings would come from, even with the land bought, until one family offered a building at a dinner. "Once there was a first case", four more followed, and all five buildings bear sponsors' names (rows 2-3).
- In a designated monument, a private owner may pay 5%, or nothing at all after lending it for ten years (row 21).
- The owner of a 60-year-old rice granary in Chishang paid for its refit as an art museum himself, then handed it to a foundation for NT$1 a month (row 8).
- The Juming Museum's founder refused outside money for 12 years. Twenty-three years after opening, the museum asks sponsors for NT$200,000 to NT$12m to keep his outdoor works in repair (rows 12-13).
