- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "The extra Treasuries are the plan's contingency."
  2. "Her flexibility backs up the payments if something goes wrong."
  3. "The safest margin is sized to a probability, like a grid's one day in ten years."
  4. "A margin written into the plan is enough; who sets it and what it is called are details."
  5. "A margin should stay the same size until the commitment ends."
- **Problem class:** sizing, naming and governing a margin above a commitment that must hold (reserve adequacy under named bad cases). **Field that solved it first:** the sea. Lloyd's Register's voluntary rule of 1835 (3 inches of freeboard per foot of hold), made law with statutory tables by the Merchant Shipping Act 1890. Today the fullest written versions are aviation fuel rules (ICAO Annex 6, 4.3.6) and spacecraft mass margins (NASA Goddard GSFC-STD-1000).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. The extra Treasuries are not contingency. In a pilot's terms they are alternate fuel: sized to one named bad case, burned in full only there, and planned to come back intact otherwise. The plan's contingency is the 0.5% cut.**
  - **Aviation names each part of the fuel by its job.** ICAO Annex 6 4.3.6.3: contingency fuel "to compensate for unforeseen factors", 5% of trip fuel and never less than five minutes' holding at 450 m; then destination alternate fuel; then final reserve fuel (30 minutes for a turbine aeroplane); then discretionary fuel "at the discretion of the pilot-in-command".
  - **Contingency is expected to be spent.** EASA's Fuel Implementation Manual (25 Nov 2025) says that under statistical contingency fuel "you should expect to statistically burn some or all of this fuel". It says the 3%, 5% and 20-minute figures "have no science behind them", and that "Coverage" of 95% means 95% of past flights "landed with all their Destination Alternate and Final Reserve Fuel intact".
  - **Builders say the same.** AACE: contingency "is expected to be expended" (store Q5-006). AACE 18R-97 sets the estimate plus contingency "typically to achieve a 50% probability of project cost overrun versus underrun".
  - **Laura's extra Treasuries** ($35,894 in January 2028, NUMBERS 2) are sized to one bad case: nothing earns interest, and every waiting dollar pays the ETF's fee. In that world they cover $55,920 and nothing is left. At today's forwards they are never drawn, and $45,456 in 2033 money comes back (NUMBERS 8h). That is alternate fuel.
  - **The 0.5% cut on each end of the range** is the true contingency. It covers costs beyond the 0.07% fee, about 0.27% shown by five ended iBond funds (NUMBERS 0), so part of it is expected to be used.
  - **Judge's note:** the plan's biggest margin is the one it expects to hand back; its smallest is the one it expects to spend. A builder's contingency works the other way.
- **Claim 2: KILL. Her flexibility is discretionary fuel: the owner's own cushion for her own project, never a reserve for the payments.**
  - **Discretionary fuel has its own rule.** It is "the extra amount of fuel to be carried at the discretion of the pilot-in-command" (ICAO 4.3.6.3 g). Using fuel "for purposes other than originally intended during pre-flight planning shall require a re-analysis" (4.3.6.7).
  - **NASA keeps margins from being stacked.** Goddard: "additional reserves are not added to margins. The lead systems engineer holds the overall system margins" (GPR 7123.1C).
  - **Dams set the standard by who bears the loss.** FEMA (2004): the PMF is the design flood where failure in smaller floods is "unacceptable". A smaller flood may do only where the consequences are "acceptable", for example where "the risk is only to the dam owners' facilities".
  - **Laura.** IPS l.15 gives her flexibility three jobs: overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short. IPS l.11 says no payment depends on a share price, and VT's job is "never the payments" (CANON A26).
  - **The case that proves it.** Mon 9 Nov's 3-point margin would have left the ten payments to the half of VT kept in 35 of 585 historical windows. Keel's call of Tue 10 Nov, made inside the IPS's completion clause, removed that case (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Judge's note:** in dam safety, who carries the loss sets the standard. The payments reach the residency's people, so they get the strictest bound; a fall in her flexibility lands on her alone, so it may sit in shares.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Probability is the right tool for the 2031 range and the wrong one for the ten payments. Grids and dams draw that line too.**
  - **How grids size their reserve.** NERC's reference margin levels come from a "0.1/year loss-of-load study" or a deterministic rule, with defaults of 15% (thermal) and 10% (hydro) (LTRA 2025).
  - **NERC itself warns** that shortfalls arise "despite meeting this traditional resource adequacy criteria" (State of Reliability 2025).
  - **February 2021** brought 23,418 MW of firm load shed, "the largest controlled firm load shed event in U.S. history". More than 4.5 million people in Texas lost power and at least 210 died. "81 percent of freeze-related generating unit outages occurred at temperatures above the units' stated ambient design temperature" (FERC-NERC).
  - **Dams set the design flood by consequence** (FEMA P-94, 2013): High hazard takes the Probable Maximum Flood, Significant the 1,000-year flood, Low the 100-year flood. FEMA also recommends that "percentages of hydrologic events (e.g. 50% Probable Maximum Flood) be discontinued".
  - **Laura's plan follows the same split.**
    - The payments are sized to a bound, zero interest after fees. They are paid in all 585 historical windows and all seven Regulation 126 scenarios (NUMBERS 2).
    - The range comes from a written rule.
    - Only the range carries a confidence, which is what Wharton asks for.
  - **Judge's note:** in 2021 the grid's reserve failed because its plants were counted at their labels. Laura counts each holding at what it pays if no interest is earned, and VT at zero for the bottom.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. A margin holds only when a rule, not the person who gains from cutting it, sets the mark, and when fixed words say "watch" and "act".**
  - **Britain's first legal load line (1876)** marked "the maximum load-line in salt water to which the owner intends to load the ship for that voyage". Owners chose where to paint it: "Some apparently painted them on the funnel!" (Lloyd's Register Foundation). It was "widely derided as being a farce and a delusion" (Glover, 2020).
  - **The 1890 Act** changed one phrase, to "to which it shall be lawful to load the ship", fixed by the Load Line Committee's tables.
  - **Avianca 052 (25 Jan 1990) had enough fuel at the start.** The NTSB found "the airplane still had sufficient fuel to complete its flight safely". It then held three times for about 1 hour 17 minutes. The crew said it was "running out of fuel" but never declared an emergency.
  - **The NTSB's findings.** Probable cause: the failure "to communicate an emergency fuel situation". Contributing: the "lack of standardized understandable terminology for pilots and controllers for minimum and emergency fuel states".
  - **ICAO now has two fixed phrases.** MINIMUM FUEL is "not an emergency situation but an indication that an emergency situation is possible should any additional delay occur". MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY FUEL means act now.
  - **Laura's equivalents.** The range rule on owned holdings is the 1890 mark (NUMBERS 4). Table 3's watches are her MINIMUM FUEL. The two reasons are her MAYDAY: "A fall in the market is not one of them" (IPS l.17).
  - **Judge's note:** Britain's first legal load line failed because the owner drew it, and it worked once a table drew it. Laura's range is the second kind: a rule draws it, not her hopes.
- **Claim 5: KEEP, with the plan's own version.** Spacecraft margins burn down by plan as estimates become measurements, and so does Laura's need.
  - **Goddard's mass margins** are 15% before SRR, more than 10% at PDR, 5% at CDR, more than 2% at SIR and 0 in operations (GSFC-STD-1000 Rev I, Table 1.06-1). The Air Force's tailoring of AIAA S-120A asks for growth allowance plus margin above 30% at go-ahead, falling to above 1% at the final design (SMC-T-002).
  - **Laura's burn-down.** Each 1 January only money above what every later payment needs at zero interest can come back: $8,813 in 2033 and $58,603 in all (NUMBERS 8h). This is Keel's reading; the IPS is silent. Kept instead, the reserve's funded ratio after each payment rises from 113.0% to 248.2%.
  - **A margin has a price.** Apollo's lunar module cut weight after its design was "95 percent complete" and became "so fragile and vulnerable to damage" (SP-4205). NASA's manager warned against "ultra-conservatism as an expedient to problem solving" (SP-4009). Laura's standard cost $13,220 more than Mon 9 Nov's call and lowered the floor payout from $115,929 to $103,178 (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Judge's note:** a spacecraft's margin shrinks because its estimates turn into measurements. Laura's shrinks because each payment made removes a wait that could have earned too little.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 3, 9 and 39 (aviation's named fuels): 3a and 3.0.** They give a judge the vocabulary for the plan's three margins in one sentence: the 0.5% cut is contingency, expected to be partly spent; the extra Treasuries are alternate fuel, used in full only in their named bad case and otherwise handed back; her flexibility is the captain's discretionary fuel. A builder's contingency works the other way round. Aha: "the biggest margin is the one the plan expects back".
2. **Rows 12-14 (the 1876 line drawn by owners, the 1890 line drawn by tables): 3c and 3d.** Britain's first legal load line marked what "the owner intends", some owners painted it on the funnel, and it was called "a farce and a delusion". It worked only once a rule drew it ("lawful"). This is the strongest outside evidence for the range rule: Laura's figures are drawn by a rule on owned holdings, not by her hopes. It also fits the conceit as a fact, not a second image.
3. **Row 26 (FEMA: who bears the loss sets the flood): 3b.** A smaller design flood is allowed only where "the risk is only to the dam owners' facilities". This explains in one stroke why the payments sit in Treasuries and her flexibility in shares: the standard follows who carries the loss, not how likely the loss is.
4. **Row 25 (FEMA ends "50% Probable Maximum Flood"): 3a.** A fraction of the bound is not a standard. It is the outside case for Keel's Tue 10 Nov call to replace the 3-point margin ($22,192 short at 0%, 35 of 585 windows) with the full zero-interest bound, inside the IPS's completion clause.
5. **Rows 6-7 (MINIMUM FUEL and MAYDAY; Avianca 052): 3e and Table 3.** A margin that was enough at the start was lost because nobody used the agreed words. Table 3's watches and the two reasons are Laura's two fixed phrases, and the report can show which is which.
