- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** when may a payment that misses its date still count as kept? English land law solved it first: Littleton's strict "law day" for mortgages (written before 1481), then the Court of Chancery's equity of redemption (settled in Charles I's reign), then Parliament for penalty bonds (1696, 1705), then bond indentures and rating agencies, which turned the court's discretion into stated grace periods.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**Idea 1. The law forgave a late payment only when the payee had wanted money and nothing else.**
(a) Since the 1600s English courts have let a borrower who missed his day keep his land by paying late with interest and costs, because the lender had only ever wanted his money back, but they have refused when the date itself was the bargain: in 1997 a Hong Kong buyer who paid ten minutes late lost both the flat and his deposit (Union Eagle).
(b) Laura's residency is the second kind of payee, since money that reaches it in March does not pay for a season that began in January; that is why the plan counts a payment "late, on its 1 January" as broken (NUMBERS 8u) and buys each date with a Treasury that matures before it (IPS l.13).
(c) The parallel breaks because equity needed a court and a creditor who could be paid interest, and the residency has neither; the break teaches that nobody will ever forgive the plan for being late, so any delay the plan can live with has to sit somewhere other than the residency's 1 January.
- *The test in the law's own words:* relief where "the penalty is designed only to secure money, and the court gives him all that he expected or desired"; none where forfeitures "were never intended by way of compensation, for there can be none" (Lord Macclesfield in Peachy v Duke of Somerset, 1720, quoted in Makdessi para 5). Lord Eldon's warning in Hill v Barclay (1811), as Lord Wilberforce put it: forcing someone to take money "even with interest, at a date when receipt had lost its usefulness". That is the residency's position exactly.
- **Decision served:** ten Treasuries, each maturing just before its payment (IPS l.13); the standard that a payment is broken if late on its 1 January (8u). **Source:** Makdessi paras 4-6, 10; Shiloh Spinners pp. 722-724; Union Eagle; NUMBERS 8u. **Part:** 3a, one sentence on why a date is bought. **Pedigree or aha:** aha for a finance judge: cash-flow matching is pedigree, but the legal reason a date must be bought (lateness is forgivable only where interest can repair it, and a season cannot be repaired) is not in the finance canon; a law-trained judge may know Union Eagle.

**Idea 2. Antonio's bond was the forgivable kind; the first plan's 1 January 2033 was not.**
(a) Shylock's "single bond" is a penalty bond with a condition, repay "on such a day, / In such a place, such sum or sums as are / Express'd in the condition" or forfeit, which is the instrument English equity spent the next two centuries relieving; in Act 4 Bassanio tenders "twice the sum" in court, the step the Administration of Justice Act 1705 later made a defence for any debtor who paid "the actual loss, together with interest and costs, into court".
(b) So Antonio's late ships were the kind of lateness the law learned to forgive, and the first plan's danger was the kind it never could: in the model every miss was settled on 1 January 2033, the day all ten had to be bought and the first paid, with no money due after January 2028 to make it good, and in 97.5% of the misses the shares had lost value over 2027-2030 rather than merely arriving late (NUMBERS 8s).
(c) The parallel breaks because the model has no share returns after 2032, so it cannot say whether the shares would have come back; the break teaches that the first plan had to buy all ten on its one day, which turned a fall in prices into what Littleton called a dead pledge, "taken from him for ever", with no ship still at sea and no court to plead to.
- *What the whole play adds:* when the play was written, the common law "enforced the bonds according to their letter" (Makdessi para 4), and Shylock asks for exactly that, payment "according to the tenour"; the Duke's plea that he "loose the forfeiture" is the Chancellor's relief, a century early. The parent card read Act 5 as "solvent but late"; the trial scene shows the money was already in court (Portia's, tendered by Bassanio) before the ships came home.
- **Decision served:** buying the ten in January 2027, not at the first payment (IPS l.13); 3f's account of the first plan. **Source:** Merchant of Venice 1.3, 4.1, 5.1; Makdessi paras 4-7; Littleton s. 332; NUMBERS 8s. **Part:** 3f, beside "the first plan's payments were in pencil"; never "one of ten dates" (8s). **Pedigree or aha:** aha; judges remember the pound of flesh, almost none that the trial scene stages a tender in court that English law would later accept.

**Idea 3. The plan puts every grace before the day someone relies on the money.**
(a) Equity could forgive a late payment because the lender would still be made whole when it came, and when its forgiveness ran on without limit Lord Eldon extended foreclosure periods "with considerable regret" because it was "frequently a severe grievance to the mortgagee", the person relying on the date.
(b) Caplet's plan keeps the rule in its own calendar: a ladder short in January 2027 is completed with the 2028 deposit (IPS l.13), five years before the first payment; the deposit counts as missed only if nothing has arrived by 31 March 2028 (Table 3 row 1); a trade day may slip day by day but "never past the 2031 meetings" (row 5); a Treasury may be up to five business days late, a line that falls 7-9 days before each 1 January paid from an iBond fund (row 2; card Q138 row 41); and the residency's 1 January gets no grace at all (NUMBERS 8u).
(c) The parallel breaks because in law a creditor or a court grants grace to a debtor, and in the plan nobody grants anything to the United States: the five days only tell Laura's team when to re-check, never to sell (Table 3 row 2); the break teaches that the line is a rule for the plan's own conduct, and that S&P's borrowed number fits only because every rung pays before its payment.
- *The check it predicts, and it holds:* if the rule is right, no grace in Table 3 should run past a date someone relies on. Rows 1, 2 and 5 and NUMBERS 8u all end before reliance (the payments from 2033, the co-sponsor meetings in 2031, each 1 January); no exception found. The first plan had one buying day, and it was also the first payment's day, after the last deposit: no grace could sit behind it.
- *IPS l.13 read this way:* "we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit" is an equity of redemption written into the plan: late (January 2028, not 2027), whole (the top-up, $2,388 at today's rates, NUMBERS 0), and before anyone relies on it (2033).
- **Decision served:** buying the ten in January 2027 with the 2028 deposit behind them (IPS l.13); Table 3's dated definitions of IPS l.17's two reasons (31 March 2028; five business days). **Source:** Perine v Dunn (Kent, 1819); Clark v Reyburn (1869), quoting Spence; Union Eagle; Table 3; NUMBERS 8u; card Q138 row 41. **Part:** 3e (the two reasons), with Table 3. **Pedigree or aha:** aha; a judge can check it against Table 3 in one look, and it explains why the plan's slack exists where it does.

**Idea 4. Late inside a grace period is forgiven only if whole: Russia failed one 30-day grace three ways in 2022.**
(a) Russia paid dollar bonds in roubles on the due day and S&P cut it to selective default before the 30 days ran out; it then paid in dollars within the grace period but left out about $1.9 million of interest for the delay, and the derivatives market's committee ruled a failure to pay as of 19 May 2022; in June its coupons were sent but never reached holders within the grace, and Moody's called a default.
(b) For Laura this says that "paid" has three parts, the whole sum, in the promised money, received by the holder; Table 3 row 2 tests only the payer's side, the receipt side is the custody line and the residency's own 1 January, and the interest a late Treasury does not pay is already counted at nothing by the payments' standard (NUMBERS 2; card Q138 row 15).
(c) The parallel breaks because Russia's bonds promised interest for a delay (its Condition 5) and dollars it could no longer move, while a Treasury promises no interest for a delay and pays in the currency its own government issues; the break teaches that the only one of Russia's three failures that applies plainly to a Treasury is a delay, which is the one the five-business-day line is built to catch.
- **Decision served:** Table 3 row 2's definition of "a Treasury default"; 3e's honest limits. **Source:** EMEA Determinations Committee (7 June 2022); Reuters (9 April 2022); Alston & Bird (13 July 2022); Reuters (27 June 2022). **Part:** 3e, one clause at most, else the store. **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to a sovereign-debt judge; the $1.9 million detail is niche and carries the 1705 Act's rule (interest for the wait) into 2022.
