- **Problem class:** a precommitted exit or review rule under a known bias (people stay too long on a losing course, and also leave a sound one in a panic). **Field that solved it first, in written form:** among the fields read, clinical-trial monitoring has the most developed written form: stopping boundaries set in the protocol before any data, a named committee that watches, and a stated choice between a binding rule and a non-binding guideline (FDA 2006, 2019). The behavioural evidence comes from organisational behaviour (Staw 1976, 1981; Simonson and Staw 1992; Boulding, Morgan and Staelin 1997); the practitioners' names are Heath and Heath's "tripwire" (2013) and Duke's "kill criteria" (2022); aviation's stabilized-approach gates show what happens when a well-made criterion is ignored.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Both labels misfit, in different ways.**
  - **The two reasons have kill criteria's form but not their job.** Duke's best criteria "combine two things: a state and a date". Table 3 gives each reason both: "nothing received by 31 March 2028" and "a payment over five business days late". But kill criteria exist to make someone act *before* "it's no longer a decision" (Thaler, quoted by Duke): they fire on a forecast that the course is not worth pursuing. The IPS's two reasons fire only once it already is no longer a decision: the deposit did not come, or the Treasury did not pay (builds on Q7b row 33). Neither response kills anything either: row 1 says "no range until paid" and "never a bond or fund sold early"; row 2 says "cover re-checked and the range restated on what was paid" and "never a forced sale" (ARGUMENT_TREE: after a default, "hold and wait").
  - **The real work of IPS l.17 is the exclusion.** The kill-criteria literature is built against staying too long (Staw; Boulding: 0 of 13 executives pulled a failing product without help). L.17 is built the other way, against leaving a sound plan in a crash: "A fall in the market is not one of them." Kill criteria say when to leave; l.17 says when not to.
  - **"Tripwires that change nothing" is wrong for two rows and too generous for the rest.** Table 3's own heading says "a date or an instrument may change, never the plan". Rows 4 and 5 are if-then rules with the action already chosen (an early fund end: swap the fund, costs from the 0.5% margin; a halt: wait day by day, "never past the 2031 meetings"): implementation intentions (Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006), not tripwires. Row 3 (debt-limit deadline) is a tripwire whose answer is fixed as "nothing", so its job is to stop a panic act. Row 6 (yields and shares) has no trigger at all, on purpose ("None"). Only row 7 (a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031) is a tripwire in Heath and Heath's sense: it starts a look and leaves the decision to Laura.
  - **The names that fit, from the field that wrote them down first:** the two reasons and the exclusion are *binding* rules; rows 3-7 are *non-binding* guidelines (FDA 2019's "binding" and "nonbinding" futility rules). The report should use neither "kill criteria" nor "tripwire": plain words do it ("the only two things that change the plan"; "start a closer look; some move a date or swap a fund; none changes the plan").
  - **Judge's note:** a judge who knows "kill criteria" expects l.17 to be a list of exits; it is a list of the exits that remain once a market fall is ruled out, and both are moments when the money simply did not arrive.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE (reason 2 passes all six tests; reason 1 passes five and partly the sixth, with one gap).**
  - The literature's test, assembled from the sources (Keel synthesis, row 27): a measurable state; a date; set before outcomes are known; a named owner, best not the person who made the first decision; a response chosen in advance and stated as binding or not; matched to real danger so it is not tuned out.
  - **Reason 2 (a Treasury payment more than five business days late):** state yes; date yes (rolling: each due date plus five business days); set before yes; owner yes, "Team", not Laura (decoupled, as Boulding's best condition was); response yes; matched to danger yes (five days stops a holiday or a one-day systems slip counting, Q7b row 37).
  - **Reason 1 (nothing received by 31 March 2028):** state yes (a clean benchmark, "nothing"); date yes; set before yes (named in the IPS 6 Nov 2026, dated in Table 3 on 11 Nov 2026, both before any outcome); response yes; matched to danger yes. **Owner: partly.** The "Who" is Laura, whose own deposit it is, so the watcher is the actor; Duke's and Boulding's advice is that the person who checks the stop should not be the one whose choice is being judged.
  - **The gap a Duke-minded judge could name:** the early signal "Confirmed by 1 Dec 2027" is a carry-on condition with no stated action if confirmation is missing on that day. By CANON A34 the report adds no rule; it can say, as a limit, that the plan hears four months early (Q111 row 15) and acts only on 31 March 2028.
  - **By design, not a gap:** the state is binary, so a smaller deposit never fires it. The order of purchase handles any size (CANON D9; aha 2: the ten payments still stand if the deposit arrives about three-quarters smaller).
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Rows 4 and 5 pass every test and are better than tripwires; rows 3, 6 and 7 each have one weakness, two of them deliberate.**
  - Row 3 (debt-limit deadline): its state rests on an estimate (the X-date, the Treasury's or the CBO's), so it is the least measurable row; its fixed answer, "nothing before it", means that ignoring it costs nothing.
  - Row 6 (yields and share prices): no trigger, deliberately. Finance backs the choice (a price-based exit lowers expected return when prices follow a random walk, Kaminski and Lo 2014) and so does aviation's evidence (criteria that fire without real danger get "tuned out ... about 97 percent of the time", FSF 2014).
  - Row 7 (her flexibility): the currency half is a true tripwire (measurable, a 2031 baseline, Laura's call). The building-cost half has a carry-on band ("five-year building costs 1.5% to 5.0% a year") but no look-closer trigger (declined, SKELETON Sat 14 Nov, CANON A34), and a five-year average is slow: in October 2026 it read 3.19%, inside the band, while the latest year was +7.18% (8d). By the timeliness test it would show a burst late. Said as a limit in 3e's notes ("costs rise in bursts" is already there), never as a new trigger.
  - Every row names a "Who", but "Team" is a group; McGrath and MacMillan ask for "a specific person". In the report "we" is Caplet as adviser, as in IPS l.17.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. A rule written in advance helps, but about half the people who agree to one break it.**
  - Boulding, Morgan and Staelin (1997): of 17 senior executives who agreed to the experimenters' stopping rule, 9 pulled the failing product (53%) and 8 broke the agreement; of 11 who wrote their own rule, 5 broke it ("I make the rules, I can break the rules") and 3 wrote rules "so ambiguous that, effectively, they failed to constitute a stopping rule". A new decision-maker did best (17 of 31, 55%). "None of our proposed solutions fully eliminates the problem."
  - Aviation: with stabilized-approach criteria written in advance, measurable and owned by the pilot, "only around 3 percent" of unstable approaches end in a go-around (FSF 2017).
  - **So the plan's main promise must not rest on anyone keeping a rule, and it does not:** held to the end, the Treasuries pay without any decision (Q7b row 34). Table 3's rules matter only for the few acts left.
  - **Judge's note:** in the experiment, nearly half the executives who signed up to a stopping rule broke it; Laura's payments need nobody to keep one.
- **Claim 5: KILL.** Keeping a market fall out is finance, not temperament. For the Treasuries no price path changes what they pay at maturity (CANON A8). For the shares, a loss limit lowers expected return when returns are unforecastable: "If prices follow random walks, stop-loss rules always decrease expected returns" (Kaminski and Lo 2014; they add value only with momentum, so say "not free", never "never works"). And a reason that fired in every bad year without endangering a payment would teach everyone to ignore the list, as stabilized-approach criteria taught pilots (FSF 2014).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 33 (3e): kill criteria say when to leave; l.17 says when not to.** The judge who expects a list of exits finds a list of the exits left once a market fall is ruled out, and both are moments when the money fails to arrive (with row 3's Thaler line as the key: they fire only when it is "no longer a decision").
2. **Row 16 with row 15 (3e): even a stopping rule people agree to is broken about half the time,** and self-written rules fail outright; so the plan's main promise rests on no rule at all, because held to the end the Treasuries pay without anyone deciding anything.
3. **Row 25 with row 24 (3e, Table 3): a criterion that fires without real danger gets tuned out,** which is why a market fall is kept out of the reasons; and the aviation fix (should-be gates, one must-be gate) is Table 3's two levels. Evidence only; 3e's parallel stays the gilt crisis.
4. **Row 22 (Table 3, 3e): binding and non-binding, the field's own names,** replace "kill criteria and tripwires"; a binding rule buys its advantage only if always kept, as l.17's exclusion does for the stated top.
5. **Rows 28-31 (Table 3): the six-part grading,** which shows reason 2 passing every test, reason 1 passing five with the owner test only partly met and one gap (no action if "Confirmed by 1 Dec 2027" is missing), and row 7's building-cost watch reading a slow average of a cost that rises in bursts. Each is said as an evaluation, never as a new rule (CANON A34).
