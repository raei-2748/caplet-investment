- **Problem class:** a fixed promise whose payer is not the person relying on it. If a promise is backed by someone else's yearly budget, or by a pooled fund's hoped-for returns, how safe is it, and what makes it safe? **Field that solved it first:** public-pension law and actuarial funding. Taiwan's Constitutional Court (Interpretations No. 781-783, 23 Aug 2019) graded how far each part of a pension is protected by whose money pays it. Taiwan's labour law (Labor Standards Act art. 56 para. 2, 2015) answered employers' failed promises by requiring next year's pensions to be held in full before the year starts.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** It is true in substance, but "when the money ran short" fuses two different things.
  - **What was cut, and who paid for it.**
    - The 18% preferential deposit rate (18%優惠存款) was paid by the public budget, not by the pension funds that were running out.
    - It began in 1960 for lump-sum pensions (1974 for civil-service insurance payouts). It was first set at 1.5 times Bank of Taiwan's one-year rate and fixed at 18% in 1983.
    - That year the one-year rate was 7.55%. By 2016 it was 1.07%, so the budget's bill grew. For civil servants it peaked at about NT$19.8bn (2012).
    - The 2016 bills were NT$18.7bn for civil servants, NT$29.8bn for teachers and NT$20.2bn for the military, about NT$68.7bn in all (Keel arithmetic on Interpretations 781-783).
  - **How it was cut.** Statutes in force from 1 Jul 2018 cut the rate:
    - civil servants' monthly pensioners got 9% to 2020, then 0% from 2021;
    - the part needed to keep a pension at the legal floor stayed at 18% (Civil Servants Retirement Act art. 36).
    - The savings were put into the funds, whose own cash flow had turned negative and which were due to run out in 2020 (military), 2029 (teachers) and 2031 (civil servants).
  - **What was not cut.** The court upheld the cuts because the 18% interest and the old-system pensions were "恩給制之範疇", the range of benefits the state pays from its budget. The reform "未扣減及於個人在職時之提撥費用部分": it did not touch what workers had paid in themselves (Interpretation 782). Military savers get their deposit principal back in year 11, "符合有本金始有孳息原則" (no interest without principal; Interpretation 781).
  - **Then the promise moved again.**
    - On 12 Dec 2025 the legislature voted 52-50 to stop the yearly cuts. Replacement-rate caps were frozen at the 2023 level, so 35 years of service gives 69% instead of falling to 60%. The change took effect on 28 Dec 2025.
    - Three bodies have asked the Constitutional Court to rule. No ruling had been made as of 6 Aug 2026.
    - The Executive Yuan says that whether the back pay already paid will be clawed back "depends on the ruling".
  - **Say instead:** "Taiwan cut by law a fixed 18% rate that the public budget paid; what retirees had paid in themselves was untouched; seven years later a new vote moved the promise back."
  - **Judge's note:** Taiwan's Constitutional Court drew the line IPS l.11 draws. What retirees had paid in was given "較高之保障" (higher protection). What the budget paid could be rewritten by the legislature.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** In law, yes. In practice, the treasury has never paid it.
  - **The law.** Labor Pension Act art. 23 para. 2 in its official English: returns "shall not be less than the interest rate of a two-year fixed term deposit by local banks; in the event of any deficiency, the Treasury shall make up the shortfall."
  - **How it is tested.** The guarantee is cumulative, from the first contribution to withdrawal (Enforcement Rules art. 32). Principal "不會因為基金運用虧損而減少" (does not fall when the fund loses; Bureau of Labor Insurance).
  - **The record.** The new fund earned less than the guaranteed rate in 8 of its 21 years, including -6.06% in 2008 and -6.67% in 2022. Over the whole period it earned NT$2.76 trillion against a guaranteed NT$427bn (National Audit Office, FY2025).
  - **Who covered the shortfalls.** They were absorbed inside the fund, whose rules let late-payment fines (滯納金) be spent on them. The Legislative Yuan's Budget Center says there was "未發生由政府預算撥補之情況" (no budget top-up has ever happened). It warns this "恐失去訂定保證收益之初衷" (may defeat the guarantee's purpose). Its 2016 and 2020 reports say the same.
  - **The one Taiwan floor that did trigger.** This was the civil-service pension fund's three-year test. The treasury owed NT$4.88bn after 2008-2010. It paid in budget instalments from 2010 to 2017, after the legislature demanded payment within five years.
  - **Say instead:** "Taiwan writes its labour pension floor on the national treasury; the floor has held because the fund out-earned it, and the one public floor the treasury did have to pay took eight budgets."
  - **Judge's note:** a Taiwanese reader's best-known "treasury guarantee" has never been paid from the budget. The one that was paid arrived late and in instalments.
- **Claim 3: KEEP NARROWLY, as Keel's inference, and reword.**
  - **What no source shows.** No source tells us what a Taiwanese co-sponsor reads first. Co-sponsors never see the IPS: 3d is Laura's own words to them.
  - **What the sources do show.** Within one decade, Taiwanese adults watched four public fights turn on whose money stands behind a promise:
    - the 2018 reform;
    - the 2019 rulings, which ranked promises by funding source;
    - the 2025 reversal, whose cost the Ministry of Civil Service said is shifted to "不是退撫基金參加者的其他納稅人" (taxpayers who are not members of the fund);
    - the 2026 Labor Insurance Act, which says "The central government shall bear the ultimate payment responsibility" while setting each year's top-up "依政府財政及本保險財務狀況" (by the government's fiscal condition and the insurance's finances).
  - **So,** in 3a, to the judge: IPS l.11's "anybody else's money" is the line Taiwan's own court drew in 2019. In 3d, Laura's first sentence answers "whose money?" with "ten years of operating money already owned". Never write that a co-sponsor "would read it first".
- **Exact disanalogies (must travel with any use):**
  - **Public pensions are not a private founder's promise.** Taiwan's are statutory benefits paid from yearly budgets or pooled funds, and changed by vote. Laura's ten payments are paid by bonds she already owns: "Held to the end, these pay what they say" (IPS l.13).
  - **What l.11's "anybody else's money" means.** It means money that is not Laura's: a co-sponsor's, a donor's or any third party's pledge. The US Treasuries she owns are not "someone else's money" in that sense. They are the instrument itself: a debt owed to her, with the amount and the date written in it, which the report may call guaranteed. A missed Treasury payment would be a default, one of the IPS's only two reasons to change the plan (l.17), not a budget decision.
  - **"Guarantee" does not carry over.** Taiwan's court read "政府負最後支付保證責任" (the government bears the final payment guarantee) as guaranteeing the adjusted payments. So the report never borrows the word from a pension context, never likens her Treasuries to a "government guarantee" of a Taiwanese pension, and names the instrument and the dates instead.
  - **Floors.** Taiwan's minimum-pension floor still rests on the budget. Laura's bottom rests on a Treasury fund she holds, measured once at purchase, so "the bottom never moves after January 2028" (NUMBERS section 4 note).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 10 (with row 11), 3a.**
   - The finding: Taiwan's public pension fund was built in 1979 on the hope that returns would fill a gap left by contributions under half the actuaries' rate. The hope missed (it needed 27.7% and earned 2.44%), and the funds began to run dry.
   - Why it matters: IPS l.11 is the opposite design, and the judge sees it tested in Laura's own country.
2. **Row 29 (with row 30), 3a, and a candidate for 3d's one Taiwan half-clause.**
   - The finding: after factory closures left workers unpaid, Taiwan's law since 2015 makes an employer hold next year's pensions in full by March. Laura holds all ten years from January 2027.
   - Why it matters: it is client knowledge and an aha in one line. The lead weighs it against Q9c's clause and keeps only one.
3. **Row 1 (with row 2), 3a and 3.0.**
   - The finding: Taiwan's Constitutional Court ranked pension promises by whose money pays them. What people paid in was untouched, and what the budget paid was cut.
   - Why it matters: this gives Caplet's structural definition of high certainty a Taiwanese precedent.
4. **Row 28, 3d background and 3a.**
   - The finding: Taiwan's newest national promise (Labor Insurance, 2026) is written as certain in principle, yet its size is voted each year.
   - Why it matters: it is the exact contrast to opening 3d with ten years already owned.
5. **Rows 23 and 26, 3a (claim 2's correction).**
   - The finding: the labour pension's "treasury guarantee" has never drawn on the budget. The one Taiwan floor the treasury did pay took eight budgets.
   - Why it matters: someone else's money arrives on someone else's schedule. This is also why IPS l.15 keeps half the shares for "a co-sponsor falling short".
