## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. A forward rate is a rate only a borrower can lock in, so for Laura the only reinvestment rate that needs no forecast is none

- (a) In 1983 Shiller, Campbell, Schoenholtz and Weiss showed how anyone can guarantee themselves today's forward rate for a future year: buy a long bond and, at the same time, issue (borrow through) shorter bonds whose payments cancel the long bond's coupons, which works only "If we can both borrow and lend at the rates given in the term structure"; the CFA curriculum gives the same two readings of a forward rate, a "breakeven interest rate" and "a rate that can be locked in by extending maturity".
- (b) Laura's plan only lends: it buys Treasuries and holds them, and never borrows or sells short (WInS bans margin and shorting, and her real plan may use only what WInS lists), so the interest her ten Treasuries pay before 2033 can only be reinvested at whatever rates come, and the 5.12% a year the ten need (NUMBERS 8a) is a break-even she can see but cannot buy. The one reinvestment rate she can count on without anyone's forecast is none at all, which is exactly the standard the extra Treasuries are sized to (NUMBERS 2).
- (c) It breaks in one place, and the break is the plan: a lender can come close to a lock without borrowing by buying more principal now, so that less depends on coupons later, and that is what the extra Treasuries are (more of the same Treasuries and funds, bought in January 2028). The fixed-income judge's own correction to another row says it in seven words, "with coupon bonds, certainty is bought by over-funding" (attack 23), so the $36,774 is the price of the lock Laura cannot buy.
- **Decision it serves:** the zero-interest standard for the ten payments and the $36,774 of extra Treasuries bought with the 2028 deposit's first dollars (NUMBERS 2; IPS l.11).
- **Report part:** 3a, one sentence before the price.
- **Source:** Shiller, Campbell, Schoenholtz & Weiss (1983), BPEA, section on forward rates; CFA Institute (2014), Reading 32; NUMBERS 2 and 8a; `competition/COMPETITION.md` section 4.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to a bond desk (they lock forwards with swaps every day); an aha to a general finance judge, because it turns "why zero?" from caution into arithmetic: zero is the only rate a lender who cannot borrow is ever sure of.

### Idea 2. The week trading froze, forecasters expected lower rates than the forward rates implied, by about a point by 2029

- (a) The Philadelphia Fed's survey of 32 professional forecasters, answered by 11 November 2026 and released on 16 November, put the 3-month Treasury bill at a median 4.1% in 2027, 3.9% in 2028 and 3.5% in 2029, while 6 November's prices implied one-year rates of 4.34%, 4.66% and 4.70% for those years (the register's engine, Keel arithmetic); after allowing about 0.15 points for bills paying less than one-year notes, the gap is about 0.2, 0.7 and 1.1 points.
- (b) So the plan's central case is not "what the market expects": it is the rates implied by today's prices, which the textbook calls "an upwardly biased estimate of expected future spot rates" when lenders want extra pay for lending longer (CFA, liquidity preference), and which the New York Fed's survey-based study finds are driven mainly by term premiums "Beyond the three year maturity" (Crump, Eusepi and Moench 2018). Every sentence that now says "expected" (FACTS l.26, l.71) should say "implied by today's prices", and the $46,600 that comes back by 2042 is the forward-rate case, not the expected one.
- (c) It breaks because forecasters miss too: survey long-run rate forecasts have differed from model forecasts "by up to two percentage points" (same study), and in 2022-23 forward rates "underestimated the speed and extent of policy tightening" (Federal Reserve FEDS 2026-026), so the survey does not prove rates will be lower, only that the forward curve is not a forecast. That is the point of the zero standard: it needs neither the curve nor the forecasters to be right.
- **Decision it serves:** how the report names its central case (3a, 3c, 3e) and the fact sheet's wording.
- **Report part:** 3e, one clause; the wording everywhere.
- **Source:** Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia (2026), SPF Q4 2026, summary table rows 6-7; CFA Institute (2014), Reading 32; Crump, Eusepi & Moench (2018), sections 4 and B.3; engine forwards (`numbers/engine.py`, 6 Nov curve).
- **Pedigree or aha:** the expectations hypothesis's failure is pedigree; the same-week number is an aha, because it shows the difference on Laura's own dates rather than citing a 1987 paper.

### Idea 3. Forward rates miss most in the recession the IPS already fears, so relying on them would add a fourth loss to the same bad years

- (a) Rate futures overshot the rates that followed from 1988 to 2005, with excess returns "positive on average and strongly countercyclical", and "right before recessions, when the Fed has already started easing, fed funds futures keep forecasting high funds rates" (Piazzesi and Swanson 2008); a later study of 1990-2021 traces most of the overshoot to "a handful of large negative shocks such as the Great Financial Crisis in 2008 and the COVID-19 shock", which came "hand in hand with deteriorating financial conditions, declining stock prices" (Schmeling, Schrimpf and Steffensen 2022).
- (b) Caplet's IPS (l.9) says that in a recession her advances come later and smaller, shares fall and co-sponsors cut their budgets, so her portfolio should counterbalance her career; a ladder that needed forward rates would add a fourth loss in those same years, interest on waiting money below what was priced, and the only money left to fill that gap would be her shares (NUMBERS 2 item 4). So the zero standard is the IPS's own counterbalance argument applied to the ten Treasuries' coupons.
- (c) It breaks on timing and on one decade: the futures evidence covers months to a year, while Laura's coupons wait up to sixteen years, so it bites only when low rates last (they did in 2009-15 and 2020-21, card Q81), and the 1990-2021 pattern that forecasters "never underestimated short rate hikes by 100 basis points or more" broke in 2022. What survives is the direction that matters to her: when the misses were large, they were cuts, in crashes.
- **Decision it serves:** the zero standard (NUMBERS 2), tied to IPS l.9 and l.11.
- **Report part:** 3a, as the reason for the standard, one sentence.
- **Source:** Piazzesi & Swanson (2008), JME, abstract and sections 2-4; Schmeling, Schrimpf & Steffensen (2022), BIS WP 996 (JFE 146(3)), sections 1, 4, 6 and Table IA.5's note; Cochrane & Piazzesi (2005), abstract ("countercyclical").
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Finance judges know the term premium; few connect the timing of forward-rate misses to the career risk the IPS opened with.

### Idea 4. The side that locks in a fixed rate has been paid for thirty years; waiting cash is the other side, so a plan that waits pays that premium unless it needs nothing from it

- (a) Schmeling and co-authors note that "investors have earned positive excess returns by entering into contracts that lock in fixed rates today while paying the realized short rate in the future", and the Federal Reserve Board's own staff long subtracted a premium before reading futures as forecasts (a rule of thumb of 1 basis point a month for fed funds futures, Piazzesi and Swanson 2008).
- (b) Laura's ten Treasuries are the fixed side, and the market pays her for holding them (card Q195); but every coupon that waits for its payment date earns whatever short rate comes, the floating side that has lost on average, so the premium she earns on the bonds she gives back on the coupons (card Q64 row 34 makes the same point in theory; this is the record). The extra Treasuries move that money from the floating side to the fixed side before 2033.
- (c) It breaks because a money-market contract and a coupon left in a bill fund are not the same instrument, and the record is short (1988-2021, a few big easing cycles), so it gives a direction, not a size. That is why the report should use it as a reason and never as a figure.
- **Decision it serves:** the extra Treasuries as more of the same Treasuries and funds, rather than a bigger cash buffer (NUMBERS 2, the backstop).
- **Report part:** store, or one clause in 3a if idea 1 is cut.
- **Source:** Schmeling, Schrimpf & Steffensen (2022), section 1; Piazzesi & Swanson (2008), section 5; cards Q195 and Q64.
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche pedigree; it makes idea 1 concrete for a bond judge.

### Idea 5. History agrees on the direction of the miss but cannot give its size, and a curve's slope is not the tell

- (a) On the Federal Reserve's monthly Treasury yields since 1962 (Keel arithmetic), the one-year forward rate for one to ten years ahead stood above the one-year yield later seen by 1.46 points on average, in 66% of 657 start months; but before 1981 it stood below in 92% of start months and from 1981 above in 97%, so the record is two eras, not 657 tries (this reproduces card Q64 row 11 within 0.1 point).
- (b) The fixed-income judge called November 2026's curve "the textbook case where forwards overstate expected short rates"; on 6 November the one-year yield was 4.33% and the ten-year 4.86% (a slope of 0.53 points, the middle of history's range; his "about 4.5%" and "about 5.5%" are 25 September's 1-year and 20-year), and in months with a slope like that the forwards overshot by 1.11 points on average but only 57% of the time, against 2.00 points and 82% when the slope was above a point. So the judge is right about the direction and overstates the steepness, and the case for zero does not need his steepness.
- (c) It breaks as evidence because each era sets the answer: within the middle-slope months the forwards were too low before 1981 and too high after it, so no history can split premium from surprise (card Q64). It teaches the report to rest the zero standard on ideas 1 to 3 and keep any replay to one clause in 3e.
- **Decision it serves:** what the report says about the 585-month replay (professor attack 6) and how far it leans on history.
- **Report part:** 3e at most.
- **Source:** FRED DGS1-DGS30 (repo copies to 25 Sep 2026), Keel arithmetic; treasury.gov par curve, 6 Nov 2026 (repo CSV).
- **Pedigree or aha:** neither; a check that keeps the report from over-claiming in either direction.

## The attacks, settled

- **Fixed-income attack 8 ("forward rates called expectations"; "every coupon"): STANDS, and the report changes its words.** Forward rates are break-even rates; the textbook says they are "an upwardly biased estimate" of expected rates when term premiums are positive (CFA), and in the week of the freeze forecasters expected about 0.7 points less for 2028 and 1.1 points less for 2029 (idea 2). "Every coupon earns at least 5.12%" is also wrong as worded: NUMBERS 8a finds one flat rate by bisection, so the condition is on the average over each payment's waiting money, not on each coupon. The judge's curve figures are 25 September's, not 6 November's (idea 5); the attack does not depend on them.
  - **Replacement wording (content for the writers, not report prose).** First use: "the rates that 6 November's Treasury prices imply for later years, called forward rates: for each year, the rate that makes it come out the same whether you buy a Treasury that matures later, or one that matures sooner and reinvest the money when it does." Then "forward rates" or "the rates implied by today's prices", never "expected", "what the market expects" or "what the market then expected". The break-even: "the ten as bought pay in full only if their waiting money earns the same as a flat 5.12% a year". FACTS l.71 becomes "If waiting money earns the forward rates of 6 November 2026, the ten payments never need the extra Treasuries."
- **Fixed-income attack 7 (the zero test presented as IPS l.11's own): NEEDS A CHANGED CLAIM; the judge's remedy is right, his reason half right.** He is right that l.11 is a promise and the zero test is a measure the team chose; the case asks teams to define and evaluate "a high degree of funding certainty". He is wrong that the ladder as bought already met l.11: without the extra Treasuries, a stretch of low rates would leave a gap, and the only money left to fill it would be the half of the shares kept (NUMBERS 2 item 4), which l.11 rules out. So: "l.11 is the promise; we hold it to the escrow verifier's test, all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, because otherwise only her shares could fill a gap left by low rates." Price in the same sentence: $36,774 of the 2028 deposit, about 8% of all her money (Keel arithmetic on $450,000); at forward rates about $46,600 of it (1 January 2033 money) comes back by 2042; at a flat 4% the ten as bought would fall $13,906 short in all (NUMBERS 8a), so that much is spent.
- **Fixed-income attack 9 (is the test pooled?): FALLS ON THE FIGURE, NEEDS ONE CLAUSE (as ATTACKS.md D14 and "Answers that came back" already decided).** The test already pools the extra Treasuries: their coupons and principal wait in the bill fund for the next payment due and carry forward (NUMBERS 2, the backstop). Each of the ten keeps its own coupons for its own payment because the IPS buys one Treasury per payment (l.13) and the Trading Notes say "reinvested coupons fund the rest". Pooling all cash would size the extra Treasuries at $33,460, about $3,300 less (ATTACKS.md, from `NUMBERS_ANSWERS.md`; the register's own 0%-sized comparison, P100, is $2,849 cheaper, NUMBERS 2), not $16,000; the $16,000 design (B, $319,687) buys only eight Treasuries in January 2027, which l.13 rules out.
- **Professor attack 6 (the replay used rate levels, not forward-rate errors): STANDS ON METHOD; the direction is the professor's.** The replay applies each window's moves in the one-year yield, in proportion, to today's forward rates (`keel/models/ASSUMPTIONS.md` A35), so a flat history means the forwards come true: it assumes no premium. ATTACKS.md D14's "rate levels, not errors against each start's forwards" should read "history's moves in the one-year yield, applied in proportion to today's forward rates": the replay never used history's levels. Card Q64's forward-error version (approximate) was harsher, 67.2% of windows short against 55.6%. The report should lead with ideas 1-3 and the zero standard, which needs no replay; if the history stays, one clause in 3e names its method ("history's rate moves applied to the rates implied by today's prices") and never puts 585 next to the zero standard (NUMBERS 2: "not separate evidence").
