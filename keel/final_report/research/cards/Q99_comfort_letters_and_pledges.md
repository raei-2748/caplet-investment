- **Problem class:** what wording makes a promise of future money believable to the person who budgets on it, when the person promising gets nothing back. **Field that solved it first:** commercial law on comfort letters and on "intention to create legal relations" (Rose & Frank, House of Lords, 1924; the London, Sydney and Melbourne comfort-letter cases of 1989), and US charity law on charitable subscriptions (Restatement (Second) of Contracts s.90(2), 1981). Civil-law codes answer the same question by statute (Taiwan Civil Code arts. 153 and 406-409; Germany's BGB s.518; France's Code civil art. 2322, 2006).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** A statement of what she owns is, in a court's words, exactly what a comfort letter is: "a statement of present fact and not a promise as to future conduct" (Kleinwort Benson, Court of Appeal, 1989). In every system read, three things turn words into a promise.
  - **A promise verb.** "The real contrast is between the words of promise, namely 'We will not' in para 2, and the words of statement of fact, 'It is our policy' in para 3" (Kleinwort). A Melbourne judge, the same year: "words of promise, such as 'we agree', 'we undertake', or even 'we promise'" (TLI). Taiwan's High Court in 2008 read "best efforts ... all reasonable action" as no promise at all.
  - **The necessary points: amount, purpose, time.** Taiwan Civil Code art. 153's 必要之點. A pledge with no amount or time stays 「道德期待」, a moral expectation (碩豐 2026b). Florida (1974) asked the pledge to "recite with particularity the specific purpose", and held it "insufficient if the pledge designated the general operating fund".
  - **How a reasonable listener hears it.** "what it is that would objectively be conveyed by what was said or done, having regard to the circumstances" (High Court of Australia, 2002).
  - **So her words carry two sentences of two kinds.** A fact: what is held for her, since when, and that it pays out in December 2032. A promise: she will give $115,000 to the facility. The fact makes the promise believable; the promise makes the fact count. WORDS' ban on "intend", "expect", "hope" and "aim" for the top is the law's own line (rows 2, 11, 36).
  - **Judge's note:** the most famous comfort letter failed in the very event it was written for. Malaysia Mining's "It is our policy" was, by its own telex after the tin market collapsed in October 1985, a policy that could be "reviewed in the light of changing circumstances". Laura's IPS names the one thing that is never her reason: "A fall in the market is not one of them" (l.17).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** In the funders' own offices a signed pledge is not "committed" but "secured".
  - **The funder test (16 Nov).** The four funder readers defined "secured" as cash received, a signed pledge or donation agreement, or money in a restricted or project account. They filed Laura's figure, stated from Treasuries held for her at her own broker, as "committed" ("earmarked but still hers"). Two said a signed pledge letter would move it to secured.
  - **The law under that box is thin.** A signed charity pledge binds in New Jersey and Iowa. It failed against donors' estates in Maryland (1979) and Florida (1974): two $50,000 pledges with $80,000 unpaid, and "In consideration of and to induce the subscriptions of others" printed on the card. In Taiwan a gift promise can be withdrawn until the money moves unless it is notarised (Q83).
  - **The claim becomes:** funders count paper and restriction (signed, restricted to the project) as secured, and money the giver owns, stated in a meeting, as committed.
  - **What the report does.** It never calls either end "secured": it says what is held and since when. SKELETON 3d's "the written form funders count" is a signed letter naming the facility as the purpose (rows 18, 29, 34-35).
  - **Judge's note:** a funder's "secured" box measures paper, not money. A signed pledge that a Florida court let lapse with its donor files one box above Treasuries that pay out in December 2032 whatever markets do.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE the wording; store only.** It is true for New South Wales.
  - **Sydney.** Banque Brussels Lambert v ANI (1989) held a parent's "it is our practice to ensure" letter promissory. It is read here through its verbatim quotation in Gate Gourmet (2004) at [189], which followed it ([212], [217]).
  - **Melbourne.** A Victorian judge held a comfort letter not binding the same year (Commonwealth Bank v TLI, 29 Jun 1989).
  - **Wording.** Say "a New South Wales court has held", never "Australian law has held".
  - **The lesson.** Two almost identical sentences, "It is our policy to ensure" and "it is our practice to ensure", lost in London and won in Sydney in 1989. Never rest a figure's believability on how a court reads words (row 37).
  - **Where it can go.** The team rule gives the Australian angle no place in the report unless it earns one. The London-Sydney split can go in one clause, with no flag.
- **Claim 4: KILL.** The name decides nothing.
  - **France.** Its Code civil made the lettre d'intention a binding personal security in 2006: "l'engagement de faire ou de ne pas faire".
  - **England.** "Intention" is the weak word (rows 1, 11).
  - **Taiwan.** One 意向書 was held binding, another a preliminary contract (secondary reports; rulings not read at source).
  - **Sydney.** Gate Gourmet's "Letter of Support" bound, and its earlier draft had said "guarantee".
  - **So** courts and funders read the verb, the necessary points and the money, never the heading.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 4 (3d or 3c; the conceit's turn for the part).** Malaysia Mining's "It is our policy" was "reviewed in the light of changing circumstances" when the tin market crashed in 1985, against IPS l.17's "A fall in the market is not one of them". The parallel is strikingly dissimilar: a London tin crash and a Taiwanese residency's co-sponsor meeting. Its turn is plain: a policy is pencil.
2. **Row 36 (3d's spine).** The fact alone is a comfort letter and the promise alone is a pledge card. The two together, in SKELETON 3d's order, give the law's own reason for WORDS' bans on "intend" and "expect".
3. **Row 35 (3c or 3d, a judge's aha).** A funder's "secured" box measures paper, not money. A signed pledge that a Florida court let lapse files above Treasuries that pay out in December 2032. This is why the report says "held since" and never argues with the box.
4. **Row 22 (3d, client knowledge of Taiwan).** The Taiwan Sugar case: "best efforts" was read as no promise, and the bank's own credit file said "joint guarantor: none". In Taiwan, how the reader files a letter decides its weight.
5. **Row 29 (3d, the ask's wording).** A Taiwanese court refused to treat a NT$1m gift as restricted, because no words or dedicated account tied it to the hospital. Naming the facility as the purpose answers funder reader 3's first question.
