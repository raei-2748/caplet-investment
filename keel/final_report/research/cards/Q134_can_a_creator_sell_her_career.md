- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Part of a creator's career can be sold (music catalogues and royalty streams trade; Royalty Exchange lists some book royalties; Bowie sold his future royalties in 1997), so 'can't sell' is true of her future work and largely of book royalties, but not of a whole creative career."
  2. "When royalty assets were sold, their prices fell when rates rose and when the creative cycle turned, the two forces the IPS names (l.9's recession, l.13's falling yields)."

  4. (added) "Because her career can't be sold, the plan never relies on it."
- **Problem class:** the boundary between a person and her property: which claims on a creator's future income the law and markets let her sell, and at what price. **Field that solved it first:** US law (the 1867 peonage statute; equity's refusal to order personal service; bankruptcy's line between a debtor's property and her later earnings), then economics (Friedman 1955 on equity in people; Mayers 1972 on "nonmarketable assets"), then the royalty markets (Mills Music Trust 1964, Bowie bonds 1997, Royalty Exchange since about 2012) and the failed markets in careers (Fantex 2013-17, income share agreements 2016-22).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. "Can't sell" is exact for the work she has not yet done, and that is the part that pays the 2028 deposit. It is wrong for finished books.**
  - **Finished work sells.** A slice of the author's royalties on a best-selling programming book series sold at auction for $565,000 on 20 Dec 2021, on $103,333 of royalties in the previous 12 months, for "Life of author + 70 years" (Royalty Exchange). That is about 5.5 years of income, a first-year yield of 18.3%, on a day the 10-year Treasury yielded 1.43% (Keel arithmetic). Across 2,453 Royalty Exchange auctions the median was 6.0 times last year's income, and streams under five years old fetched 4.6 times (AltStreet). A share of song royalties has traded as Mills Music Trust units since 1964.
  - **Creditors can take finished work too.** A 2026 bankruptcy order states the rule: "where a debtor musician composes a song pre-petition and earns royalties on that song post-petition, the post-petition royalties from that song belong to the bankruptcy estate" (citing *Cusano v. Klein*, 9th Cir. 2001). But the estate excludes "earnings from services performed by an individual debtor after the commencement of the case" (11 U.S.C. 541(a)(6)).
  - **The work itself cannot be sold or forced.** Peonage contracts "in liquidation of any debt or obligation" are "null and void" (42 U.S.C. 1994, from 1867). "A promise to render personal service will not be specifically enforced" (Restatement (Second) of Contracts 367(1)). Friedman (1955): "in a non-slave state, the individual embodying the investment cannot be bought and sold".
  - **Slices of the future are sold one book at a time.** An advance contract sells rights in a book not yet written (UK law vests "future copyright" on creation, CDPA 1988 s.91). An option clause sells only a first look: the Authors Guild model gives the publisher "no longer than 60 days" of exclusive negotiation.
  - **Every attempt to sell a whole career failed.** Fantex postponed its first athlete IPO when the player needed "season-ending back surgery" (12 Nov 2013). Its insiders bought 52% of its last $59.3m offering (2016), and it deregistered in January 2017 with 160 holders of record. US regulators ruled that income share agreements "are credit" (CFPB 2021), and Purdue paused its programme in 2022.
  - **Judge's note:** US bankruptcy law draws the IPS's line exactly. Royalties on a finished song belong to her creditors; what she earns from work after the case belongs to her. The case's deposit comes from "publishing advances, speaking engagements, licensing, and other entrepreneurial ventures" (case p.2): work still to be done, the part no one can buy.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Rates hit sold royalty assets in two ways, through prices and through missing buyers. The creative cycle hit them more through long declines than through recessions.**
  - **Rates.** In the institutional market prices fell (Q96 rows 14-29; Shot Tower's recorded-music multiple kept drifting, 13.8x in 2023 to 13x in 2024). In the small-royalty auction market, median multiples stayed in a 4.7x-6.4x band from 2014 to 2026. Dated sales fell from 200 (2019) to 38 (2021) and 21 (2022), and fewer than 15 in 2023 and in 2024 (AltStreet; only 884 of 2,552 deals carry dates). Edison (June 2023): "sellers pull deals when pricing expectations are not met".
  - **The creative cycle.** Moody's put the Bowie bonds under review for "weakness in sales for recorded music as well as the recent downgrade of an entity that provides credit support to the transaction" (2003). That entity was EMI, so the guarantor came from the same industry and weakened in the same slump. Mills Music Trust's receipts fell 26.4% from 2005 to 2008, before the crash, then fell 12.8% in 2008 (shares -36.55%) and rose 4.0% in 2009. In 2020 they were 1.9% below 2018 (Keel arithmetic on its 10-Ks).
  - **Who sells.** 52% of sold royalty streams were earning less than the year before, and 74% were below their peak year (AltStreet, 1,153 listings). Streams are usually sold on the way down.
  - **Judge's note:** a finished book's royalties are not a reserve. They are sold at about five or six years' income, most often when they are falling, and in 2022-24 the buyers partly went away.
- **Claim 3: KEEP the "largest non-tradable asset"; CHANGE "unsellable by law".**
  - **The largest asset.** Human capital was "the largest asset across all income groups, constituting 64 percent of total wealth in 2018" (World Bank, *Changing Wealth of Nations 2021*). Palacios (2015) puts it at "93% of aggregate wealth" in an equilibrium model (Karolyi's summary).
  - **The term.** "Nonmarketable assets" entered asset pricing through Mayers's 1972 chapter in Jensen's volume (Karolyi 2015; the chapter itself not read).
  - **The law bars selling the person and forcing the work, not selling income.** Friedman saw "no legal obstacle" to buying "a share in an individual's earning capacity", though it is "economically equivalent to ... partial slavery". When it was tried, regulators called it a loan (CFPB 2021), and economists found adverse selection "has unraveled private markets for these products" (Herbst & Hendren 2024). So the law forbids the slavery half, and the economics, and the regulators' word "credit", stopped the rest.
  - **Judge's note:** for the average worker a career is a bond, not a stock. Fama and Schwert (1977) found its links to market returns "weak", and Palacios found human capital "more like a bond than a stock". Caplet's "giant stock" is true of the exception the case describes: the part of her career that pays the 2028 deposit (Q96 row 40).
- **Claim 4: KILL. The plan relies on her career once, at a known date and amount.** "'None' needs the 2028 deposit's first dollars. It holds with $150,000 and with $75,000, not with no deposit". With no deposit the ten are $57,455 short at zero interest, and "A missing deposit is one of the IPS's two reasons to change course" (NUMBERS, section on the first plan; IPS l.17). With half the deposit the plan still meets its standard in 99.965% of house paths.
  - **Judge's note:** the plan does not pretend her career is out of the picture. It takes one dated dollar figure from her career; at today's rates that deposit could arrive 73.9% smaller and all ten would still be paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS, the promise table); and it names a missed deposit as a reason to change course.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 21 (bankruptcy draws the IPS's line).** 2.3: royalties on a finished song belong to a debtor's creditors, but "earnings from services performed by an individual debtor after the commencement of the case" do not. Why: it gives "can't sell" a precise legal edge that a judge will not expect. The work still to be done is the part that is not property, and it is exactly what the case's deposit comes from.
2. **Row 40 (three tiers of sellability).** 2.3: it answers the devil's advocate ("royalties trade, Bowie sold his") in one sentence without weakening l.3. Why: it turns a possible objection into a sharper version of Caplet's own pitch.
3. **Row 39 (claim 4 killed: the plan relies on her career once).** 3e area (2): the deposit is the career's one dated input; half of it still meets the standard in 99.965% of house paths, at today's rates a deposit 73.9% smaller still pays all ten, and its failure is a named reason to change course (IPS l.17). Why: it shows the team priced its own premise rather than assuming the career away.
4. **Rows 30-32 (Fantex).** 2.3, one clause: the only company that floated shares in people's careers postponed its first IPO when the player needed back surgery, had its own insiders buy half its last offering, and deregistered in January 2017. Why: "a stock she can't sell" stops being a figure of speech once a judge sees a real attempt fail.
5. **Row 1 with row 7 (finished books sell at about 5.5 years' income, usually on the way down).** 3e: a best-selling book series' royalties sold for $565,000 on $103,333 a year when the 10-year Treasury yielded 1.43%, and most sold streams were already falling. Why: it explains in numbers why a backlist is never counted as a reserve, without advising Laura to sell anything.
