## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Osaka could not see inside a warehouse, so it judged every bill on its date as if it were backed. Laura's rule checks the other half of ink before she speaks.

(a) What Osaka did:
- A lord's Osaka warehouse kept its rice stock secret, and a backed bill looked exactly like an empty one. So the shogunate's 1761 ban on bills "beyond the rice held" could not be enforced in the market.
- In the first month of 1771 the shogunate changed the test. A bill was empty only if delivery failed "without reason". The town magistrate then held each defaulted bill to be backed, under what the historian Yasuo Takatsuki calls the established rule that "not a single empty bill exists". The magistrate made the lord deliver or settle.
- Osaka's lenders lent against bills because they knew this court, not because they knew any warehouse's stock (Kusama Isuke, for 35 years a clerk at Osaka's largest money-dealer).

(b) What it shows about Laura's plan:
- Ink has two halves: a figure owned in Treasuries, and Treasuries that will pay it on its date (CONCEIT). Osaka's lasting rule tested only the second half, and only after the date. Store row Q157-042 found the mirror image: the law's owned/owed line matches only the first half.
- Laura's co-sponsors book her figure the way Osaka booked a bill, at face. All four funders in the 16 Nov test filed her stated figure as "committed". They then raise their own money around it from 2031, and the payment arrives only in December 2032.
- So the "owned" half has to be checked by Laura before she speaks. That is IPS l.15's "money you already own". It is also why nothing is stated before the move: a top stated a year early would buy less than stated in 10.6% of house paths (NUMBERS section 4).

(c) Where it breaks:
- Osaka's after-the-fact test served a holder who could wait for the date and then go to the magistrate. A co-sponsor that finds Laura's figure short in December 2032 has already raised and planned its money around it.
- The one Osaka fix that did check the stock was an inspector's seal on every bill from 1783. It was abolished in 1787 after the great domains boycotted it, because a warehouse's interior was the lord's own ground.
- Laura's two Treasury funds publish their holdings every business day by rule (store Q26-021). So her check needs nobody's consent, and nothing stops her from making it before she speaks.

- **Decision:** Laura states a figure only once it is owned in Treasuries (CONCEIT; IPS l.15). The move is fixed to the first trading day of 2031, and no top is stated before it (NUMBERS section 4).
- **Report part:** 2.0, as one clause behind the working word: ink is "owned" and "pays on its date", and Osaka's court could test only the second. Or 3c, beside the 10.6% figure. Use one outside name per part only.

### 2. The 1773 "government guarantee" was a declaration that was never paid. What lasted was the court.

(a) What happened in 1772-1782:
- In 1772 the shogunate asked Osaka's two leading money-dealers to take in doubtful bills with cheap public silver, and they refused. The silver offered was 1,000 kan, against about 40,000 kan of the lords' yearly rice sales. They also warned that lords who knew someone would take their bills would issue more.
- They advised the shogunate only to "declare" that it would buy bad bills. In the sixth month of 1773 it declared that it would, "so that townspeople suffer no loss".
- The historian Yasuo Takatsuki finds no trace that public silver was ever spent. The magistrate ordered settlements instead, and the buyback was abolished in 1782.

(b) What it shows about Laura's plan:
- Parent idea 3's "the fix worked only when the government stood behind the paper" rests on the economist Ulrike Schaede's "began guaranteeing all rice bills in 1773". Read whole, the sources say the backing was a promise of someone else's money.
- That is pencil by the report's own definition. It is also what IPS l.11 refuses to rest a payment on: "a share price, or ... anybody else's money".
- Laura's plan has no backstop and needs none for figures she owns.

(c) Where it breaks:
- It breaks where the parent card put its weight, on the second reason to change. Osaka's paper had two parties: a lord who wrote it, and a shogunate that said it would stand behind it.
- A Treasury has one issuer, the United States, and no one above it. So IPS l.17 names a default as one of the two reasons to change the plan, not a risk that someone covers.
- The second reason therefore matches Osaka only by absence. Osaka shows that a backstop for a lord's paper turned out to be words, and the plan claims no backstop at all.

- **Decision:** the second reason to change (IPS l.17; CANON A34). No payment and no stated figure leans on a third party's promise (IPS l.11).
- **Report part:** 3e's note on the two reasons, as a guard. Never write "Osaka's bills were made safe by a government guarantee". Store row 11 records the conflict.

### 3. Kaga's fire: who carries a loss in storage. Laura carries it by stating less than she owns.

(a) What happened:
- A rice bill began as a warehouse receipt. The lord's warehouse kept the rice, the holder owned it, and the holder carried the risk of fire or flood.
- Early in the 1700s a fire burnt the Osaka warehouses of two domains. Murakami at once declared all its bills good. Kaga said its bills carried no guarantee against fire or flood, until holders protested so sharply that Kaga replaced that clause with "a full guarantee" (Schaede, citing Miyamoto).

(b) What it shows about Laura's plan:
- Laura's top is held through a Treasury fund, and a fund's owner carries its costs and accidents, as a receipt's holder carried fire. The plan puts those on Laura, not her co-sponsors, by stating less than the late-2032 fund pays.
- With waiting cash earning nothing, the fund pays $117,731 on its two purchases. The 0.5% margin holds back $589 and rounding down holds back $2,142, so the room under the $115,000 top is $2,731 (NUMBERS section 3).
- At today's forwards the fund pays $122,503, and the $7,503 excess goes to her kept half (NUMBERS section 4).
- That is the decision to "state less than she owns", and Kaga is its reason: the holders of a stated figure would not accept a clause saying the loss is theirs.

(c) Where it breaks, on size and speed:
- The margin is sized for slow costs, about 0.27% beyond the fees (NUMBERS section 0). A fire is sudden.
- Laura's sudden loss is the fund ending early. An end on 2 January 2031, with waiting cash earning nothing, leaves the top $4,589 short, more than the room. At today's market rates the same end leaves it $7,295 over (NUMBERS section 3).
- So the margin is Murakami's answer for slow damage only. The sudden case is why Table 3 row 4 watches for an early end all year.

- **Decision:** the range rule's 0.5% margin and rounding down (NUMBERS sections 0 and 4); the excess going to the half kept; Table 3 row 4's watch.

### 4. Companion: an airline may oversell seats because not everyone shows. Nobody holding Laura's top fails to show.

(a) How overselling works:
- An airline may sell more seats than a plane has, because some ticket holders never show.
- When too many do show, US law makes it pay each passenger bumped against their will up to 400% of the one-way fare, capped at $2,150 (14 CFR 250.5). In April-June 2026 this happened to 0.15 in every 10,000 passengers (DOT).
- Osaka's empty bills ran on the same bet. In 1737 Hiroshima's warehouse held rice for only 30% of its bills, because holders had trusted it as a "sure warehouse" and left their bills unclaimed.

(b) What it shows about Laura's plan:
- Laura's top cannot be oversold, because every holder of it will show. In the 16 Nov funder test all four readers booked the figure she stated as "committed", and both readers given one figure booked the $115,000 top. The building then draws the whole contribution in December 2032.
- The relation predicts the plan's shape, and the prediction can be checked: a claim that will be made in full must be owned in full. Only the part promised to no one, her kept half, can stay in shares (IPS l.11, l.15).

(c) Where it breaks, on the remedy:
- Overselling is lawful because a bumped passenger is paid in cash the same day, at a price the law sets. A building that comes up short has nothing to be paid with except Laura's kept half.
- The break also shows that Laura does carry one oversold promise, from the other side. IPS l.15 holds her kept half for "a co-sponsor falling short", because co-sponsors' pledges, like tickets, may not show (store Q83: a Taiwan pledge can be withdrawn until notarised or paid).

- **Decision:** the stated top is owned in full; the kept half covers others' pledges (IPS l.15).
