- **Problem class:** provenance and attestation. A mark on an object can mean "made by", "held by" or "closed", and the question is what keeps such a mark believable. A second class is ordering irreversible steps in printing.
- **Field that solved it first:** Chinese connoisseurship and cataloguing. Collectors' seals backed by catalogues ran from the Song to the Qing; the Shiqu Baoji catalogue began in 1745. Museum provenance research studies them today. The print trade solved registration twice: the Chinese douban clamp and the Japanese kento.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The seal stamped "when the work is finished" is the artist's seal, and the artist's seal is a signature.
  - **The source says so.** FACTS_PLAIN's own source places the seal "below or near the signature when the work is completed" (Smithsonian, quoted in Q47 A15). The Asian Art Museum has the artist "sign it and stamp one or more seals beneath the signature" (Q47 A15). HKMoA calls it "the artist's seal, representing the artist's signature" (Chu, 2008).
  - **So V2.2's 3d turn reads as "signed".** "Her statement is a seal, not a sketch" says that Laura signs. That is exactly the misreading the definition avoids by saying "owned" (cards Q42-Q44).
  - **The seal that means "held" is the collector's.** It is stamped after the work is in hand: "a record of viewing or ownership" (HKMoA). Collectors' seals outnumber the maker's on old scrolls (Rietberg: "an ever-expanding feature"). One Freer album leaf carries ten collector seals and no artist seal.
  - **The truest form is the catalogue seal.** Qianlong stamped 「石渠寶笈」 on a work once it had been entered in his catalogue, 「代表這件作品已被編進了目錄」 (邱建一).
  - **What to do.** If a seal stays in 3d, it is the holder's seal, never the maker's. Otherwise Q82's "OK to print" or Q83's art. 408 takes the slot (CONCEIT V2.3-V2.5).
  - **Judge's note:** the red seal a reader pictures at the foot of a finished Chinese painting is the painter's signature. The seal that means "this is held" is the collector's, stamped after the work is in hand.
- **Cahill's evidence on seals (new; the strongest single find).** Hsiang Yuan-pien put "as many as fifty" seals on a single scroll but compiled no catalogue. So forgers stamped copies of his seals "over thousands of their wares", and "no one now is much impressed by the presence of his seals on a painting" (Cahill, 1963). Liang Qingbiao's "sparsely and carefully placed seals" kept their worth.
  - **This is the case's warning in craft form.** Promising more than she can contribute could "damage her credibility" (case l.114).
  - **Laura's two ends can be checked against a record.** Table 1 lists every holding, the bonds by CUSIP (card Q38).
  - **Judge's note:** a collector's seal with no record behind it was forged until it meant nothing. A stated figure is believed only while a record shows what stands behind it.
- **Claim 2: KEEP as a convention, with two limits.**
  - **The convention.** 「墨要新磨」: ink is ground fresh (arte.gov.tw). Ground ink left standing turns to 宿墨 in 7-8 hours in summer and about a day in winter, as the glue film around the soot breaks down. The Nara ink-makers' union advises 「宿墨はすてた方が無難です」, that stale ink is safer thrown away. So ink is kept as a solid stick and turned to liquid just before use, as the IPS keeps each payment in a Treasury "maturing just before it's needed" (l.13).
  - **Limit 1: a master broke the convention on purpose.** Huang Binhong used 宿墨 by choice (Wang Bomin).
  - **Limit 2: not every rung is "just before".** The Feb-2037 bond's money waits 320 days, or 10.5 months (NUMBERS l.1403, l.1096). Waiting cash keeps every dollar; only its interest is pencil. Say "waits", never "spoils".
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Chinese colour woodblock (douban, used for New Year prints) uses no registration marks.
  - **How it works.** "There are no registrations marks used at all": the whole stack of paper is clamped once, at one edge (woodblock.com encyclopedia). "When all the papers are printed and dried, the printing of the next color can start" (Pietzcker). Suzhou's Taohuawu New Year print process names the step 「選紙上料（夾紙）」, choosing and clamping the paper (ihchina).
  - **Why it fits 3c.** It is a true frame for "two inkings, two dates": the sheet is fixed once, and each colour is laid in its own pass on its own day.
  - **Its honest limit.** Registration "wanders a little" (woodblock.com). Card Q3c's IBTA ended 15 days early, so the turn does not promise precision.
  - **Kento.** The marks are Japanese (Musashino Art University). They keep 3e's existing turn, not this one.
  - **Judge's note:** Chinese colour prints use no registration marks. The paper is clamped once, and each colour waits for its own pass.
- **Claim 4: KILL.**
  - **No pencil draft.** Chen Uen drew without one: 「很難想像鄭問沒有打草稿，而是用水彩打底」. He spent longer thinking about a picture than drawing it, and the brush was 「最後一哩路」, the last mile (鍾孟舜, 故宮文物月刊 424, National Palace Museum, 2018).
  - **A verified Taiwanese source for "the idea comes before the brush".** It sits where 意在筆先 now sits, with that phrase's attribution doubted and no verified English wording (Q47 A18).
  - **What it maps to.** The ten payments were never pencil: the thinking came first (the 27 Sep test, the order of claims), and the purchase in January 2027 was the last mile.
- **Claim 5: KILL as a report turn (store colour only).**
  - **The type counts conflict.** AP (2010) gives "2 million pieces of lead type"; Taipei Times (2017) gives "more than 10 million lead character pieces" and "120,000 molds".
  - **The one working parallel collides with another term.** It is "out of sorts" (no letter can be in two lines at once, so no dollar is counted twice). The matrix-and-recast idea collides with the store's "matrix" and with Ri Xing's own unrepairable matrices.
