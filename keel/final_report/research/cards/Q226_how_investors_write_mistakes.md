- **Claims tested:**
  1. "An account of a mistake convinces when it names what the writer believed, what it cost, what changed it, and what they now do differently." (CHANGE: the four parts are there, but not in that order, and the fourth is the one most often missing or turned into a general lesson.)
  2. "The best accounts judge the decision by what was knowable then, not by how it turned out." (CHANGE: Marks says it of decisions in general; Buffett and Ackman, writing about their own, count the result and say what they could not see, and Marks says it is "far from always" right.)
  3. "A team reflection should show a change of mind for a reason, not a confession." (KEEP, with a sharper test: the reason must be one the writer wrote down before the result, and it must say what stays unchanged.)
- **Problem class:** self-report credibility: how a writer who stands to look bad persuades a reader that the lesson is real. **Field that solved it first:** investment letters and memos (Buffett from 1977 on, Marks's memos from 1990); aviation and medicine's blameless post-mortems came later.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1, CHANGE.** In seven accounts read (Buffett 1989, 2007, 2014 twice; Marks 2020 and 2023; Ackman 2017) every one names a belief and a cost. "What changed it" is a stated reason in five (Berkshire's textile bargain, Ackman's four lessons, Marks 2004 and 1979, Dexter as "durable advantage vanished"). "What I now do differently" is the weak part: Buffett's 2007 Dexter passage ends "I'll make more mistakes in the future", with no new rule; his 1989 list ends in a mechanism (the "institutional imperative") and a structural fix, not a promise to try harder. The convincing order is: the belief with its reason, what the facts did, the cost counted in a way the reader can check, then the one thing that is now different, kept to what is actually different.
2. **Claim 2, CHANGE.** Marks (13 Jan 2020): "you can't tell the quality of a decision from the outcome", quoting Duke: "If we aren't wrong just because things didn't work out, then we aren't right just because things turned out well." But the writers who convince do not use that as a defence of their own call: Buffett on Dexter writes "And I simply didn't see that coming" and counts the cost at what the shares are worth now ($5.7 billion), the harshest reading; Ackman says "In retrospect, we misjudged". They state what could not be seen and still own the cost. Marks himself dates his own error as timing ("I started complaining too soon", 2004). So say what was known then and what the team now knows, and never use "knowable" to shrink the cost.
3. **Claim 3, KEEP.** None of the seven is a confession for its own sake: each ties the admission to a changed decision or a stated test (Buffett: "it's a good idea to review past mistakes before committing new ones", 1989). Ackman's letter is the nearest to a confession and is the one with an apology ("I deeply and profoundly apologize"); it also gives four lessons and a sizing rule. For 3f: IPS l.9 tells the change in one sentence ("that was our plan - until we stopped and considered..."); the report's account is stronger if it adds the reason's source (a writer's income arrives later and smaller), the one thing it did to the plan (the 2028 deposit's first job), and one thing it did not change.

**Judge's note.** A smart reader would expect the best mistake accounts to be the most self-critical. They are the most specific: Buffett's Dexter passage is credible because it changes across letters, from 1993's praise ("one of the best-managed companies Charlie and I have seen") to 2007's "the worst deal that I've made", and prices the cost twice ($433 million paid; $3.5 billion once the shares' later value is counted). The account that names a number, a date and one thing no longer done convinces more than one that names a feeling.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 23 (shape across seven accounts), 3f.** Gives the 27 Nov writers an order of telling for the copout: position and objection, reasons weighed, decision, what is understood now, what remains uncertain.
2. **Row 25 (the fourth part is the weak one), 3f.** The reflection convinces if it names one concrete thing now done differently, not a general learning.
3. **Rows 5 and 6 (Dexter across letters and counted twice), 3f.** A before shown as it was held, and a cost stated plainly before the larger count, is the shape of an honest change of mind.
4. **Rows 7 and 13 (Claim 2 changed), 3f.** Judge the decision by what was known then and still own the cost; Marks's Duke line is the reference.
5. **Row 26 (say what did not change), 3f.** Ends the copout on what the decision left standing, which also answers the criterion's "uncertainty".
