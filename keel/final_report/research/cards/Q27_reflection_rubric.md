## Claims tested

1. CHANGE (tested as keep / kill / change): "Reflection rubrics reward articulated learning tied to a changed action
   over description, so each event must end on what changed and why." Sub-question: which scheme is most reliable
   between raters, by reported inter-rater agreement?
2. CHANGE: "A team's reflection is judged on how it disagreed and settled things, and reviews of successes teach as
   much as reviews of failures." Sub-question: should one of the three or four events be a success?
3. KEEP: "The 16 Nov readers should score each event on Wharton's own words first, then on the best rubric from the
   literature."
4. Asked in the brief: does the literature support SKELETON 3f's plan (two sentences on last year; three or four events
   each with a before and after number; a closing paragraph on which recommendation changed because of which event;
   one line on roles)?

Problem class: measuring the quality of reflective writing reliably (educational measurement), and learning from a
team's own events (organisational learning). Fields that solved it: medical and teacher education (reflection rubrics
tested for agreement between raters); applied psychology (after-event reviews; team learning behaviour); US Army
doctrine (the after action review).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The schemes do reward learning tied to action: DEAL ends on "What will I do in light of it?";
  Hatton and Smith define reflection as "deliberate thinking about action with a view to its improvement"; the Army
  review ends on "how the unit should complete the task differently next time". But "what changed" is too narrow. The
  REFLECT rubric puts "confirmatory learning" ("Frames of reference or meaning structures are confirmed. Requires
  critical reflection.") at the top level beside transformative learning, and Bell et al. object that "Reflection
  isn't only about transformation". New wording: each event ends on what changed, or what was tested and kept, and why.
- **Most reliable scheme: none is good; analytic beats holistic.** One reader's agreement with another, as an
  intraclass correlation (ICC), single rater:
  - REFLECT: 0.632 at its developers (0.376 to 0.748 across rounds; abstract only); 0.448 overall and 0.350 to 0.452
    by criterion in an independent replication; 0.50 by criterion but 0.36 for one global score in a second study.
  - Kember's four levels, scored holistically: 0.43 in that second study. Kember et al. (2008) report "very good
    agreement" (abstract; the figure itself not seen).
  - Kember's 1999 coding: Cronbach's alpha 0.802, but initial agreement between coders only 50% to 79%.
  - DEAL (Ash and Clayton), Moon and Hatton and Smith publish no agreement statistic; they used consensus or a second
    coder plus discussion.
  - So: score criterion by criterion, never one overall mark, and rank on the mean of three readers. On the
    Spearman-Brown formula (my arithmetic), a single-reader ICC of 0.45 becomes about 0.71 for the mean of three
    (0.36 becomes 0.63).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Wharton names "collaboration, disagreements" among five things, so how the team disagreed is
  judged as one part, not the whole. No study of how judges read a team's written reflection was found (gap).
  - Success reviews help, modestly. Ellis and Davidi: reviewing successes and failures beat failures only (98
    soldiers, two companies, quasi-experiment; eta squared 0.049; possible regression to the mean).
  - The Army: "To sustain success, the unit needs to know what it performs well" (FM 7-0, K-38).
  - Yes, one of the three or four events should be a success or a decision kept, told by its decision points, with
    what was luck named. Register 8b already names one piece of luck: orders filled "within two trading days of the
    year's cheapest close", which "is luck of timing, not skill".
- **Claim 3: KEEP.** Wharton's words name the criteria; the literature supplies the anchors.
  `RERANK_RUBRIC.md` has five criteria (A influence, B reasoning, C disagreement, D uncertainty and learning, E new and
  checkable), anchors at 1, 3 and 5, the VOID rule, blind rules, two caps, tie rules and a set check.
- **3f plan: mostly supported, with two changes.**
  - "A before and after number" becomes "a before and after state, in register figures where they exist". A decision
    kept has no after-number, and only about seven events have register figures at both ends (Q21).
  - Add one success told with its luck check.
  - Last year's lessons, the closing paragraph on what changed, and one line on roles are each supported (section c).

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Score criterion by criterion, and average three readers (rows 14-22)
- **What is it?** Rubrics for reflective writing, tested for agreement between raters.
- **Did it work?** Only moderately.
  - The best reported single-reader agreement is ICC 0.632 (REFLECT's developers, abstract).
  - An independent replication got 0.448, and a second study 0.50 by criterion against 0.36 for one global score.
  - Holistic levels (Kember) did worse (0.43). DEAL, Moon and Hatton and Smith report no statistic.
  - Grierson et al. think good agreement came from raters building a shared reading of the anchors.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Three readers place about 25 short event sheets on a scale, as raters place
  narratives.
- **How is it not?** The sheets are short factual records, not personal essays, and the readers rank events rather
  than judge people (Moniz's 14-sample problem does not apply). The three readers share one design, so their errors
  are not independent and the gain from averaging is smaller than the formula says.
- **What Caplet takes.** `RERANK_RUBRIC.md`: five criteria scored separately; anchors read first; the mean of three
  readers; a gap under 1.0 out of 25 is a tie; one line of explanation where two readers differ by 2 or more.
  The old RANKING (W, V, C, S, each 1-10) scored vividness on uncorrected texts. Three of its top ten carry figures or
  titles now VOID (X026 2nd, X006 8th, X087 10th), and so does X028 (14th). The new sheet drops "vivid" as a criterion;
  vividness is a drafting job for 27 Nov.

### Card 2. "Tested and kept" counts at the top (rows 9-10, 16-17)
- **What is it?** REFLECT's confirmatory learning, Bell's objection to grading by transformation, and Hatton and
  Smith's finding that critical reflection was often "brief and superficial" while dialogic reflection was rich.
- **Did it work?** The rubric was used in published studies (rows 18-20). The critique has no test of its own.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Several of the strongest events in the pool are decisions tested and kept:
  - X100: a pre-set test the gold-and-property mix failed (0.8991 against a 0.90 bar, failing the every-run clause);
  - X053: written October triggers that never fired;
  - X098: a "rule" traced to its sources, found dead, and the plan kept.
- **How is it not?** REFLECT asks for critical reflection behind the confirmation. A held decision with no real test
  behind it is habit, not reflection. X100's test came five days before the objection (Q21), so it cannot be told as a
  test written to settle that objection.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3f: "a before and after state; register figures at both ends where they exist; a decision
  tested and kept is told by its test". Criterion A at 5 and D at 5. This changes Q21's rubric row 4 (self-critique),
  which scored "confirms what the team already thought" as a 3: after a real, dated test, it can be a 5.

### Card 3. Review a success too, and name the luck (rows 23-25)
- **What is it?** Reviews that cover both what went right and what went wrong.
- **Did it work?** Ellis and Davidi: performance rose more with success-and-failure reviews (eta squared .049). After
  two weeks, the soldiers' success maps held 9.11 ideas against 7.37 for failures. The Army's review states the same
  rule (K-38, K-43). The meta-analyses are positive (abstracts only).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A team that only tells mistakes shows half its learning. Wharton asks for
  "decision-making" and "learning", not for errors alone.
- **How is it not?** Soldiers on navigation tasks with right answers. Caplet's successes are judgements whose outcomes
  are still unknown (the plan runs to 2042). One quasi-experiment with a possible regression-to-the-mean artefact.
- **What Caplet takes.** One of the three or four events is a success or a decision kept, told by its decision point
  and with a luck check. Register 8b supplies one: the fills on 7-8 Oct came "within two trading days of the year's
  cheapest close (5 Oct)", which "is luck of timing, not skill". It may be told only that way, if told at all.
  Criterion D at 1 penalises crediting skill for timing.

### Card 4. Disagreement voiced in the open, told fairly (rows 7, 26, 28, 29)
- **What is it?** Edmondson's learning behaviour (differences discussed "openly rather than privately"), the Army's
  "Permit respectful disagreement", and Ash and Clayton's fairness test.
- **Did it work?** Learning behaviour predicted outsiders' ratings of team performance (adj. R squared .26 in 51
  teams), though cross-sectional.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Wharton asks for "disagreements" by name.
- **How is it not?** No study shows how judges score disagreement in a written team reflection (gap). The record
  rarely says who argued what: the journal's "[who argued what]" is blank (Q21 d).
- **What Caplet takes.** Criterion C: two real positions, the losing one put so its holder would accept it, and how it
  was settled.
  - Events in the pool with a recorded objection: X100 (the "copout" objection), X059 (the vote on the scaled book),
    X082 (an outside verdict).
  - A disagreement within the record: the 2020 counter-example sat in Caplet's notes on 14 Oct, and the IPS chose the
    career framing anyway (Q21 claim 2).
  - Nothing is invented. Blind sheets use roles; the 3f draft may add names Caplet supplies.

### Card 5. Do not perform reflection (rows 11, 19)
- **What is it?** Hatton and Smith saw that criteria "impose a particular text construction". Stock phrases were coded
  as reflection, and assessed writing is "altered to meet the perceived requirements". Grierson et al. warn that
  criteria invite writing aimed at "scoring well".
- **Did it work?** It is an observation, not a test, but two independent groups made it.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A 600-word reflection read by judges is assessed writing.
- **How is it not?** Wharton's judges read for substance under five criteria, not a coding scheme.
- **What Caplet takes.** Readers on 16 Nov score facts on the sheet, not phrasing. On 27 Nov each 3f draft is checked
  on the five criteria, and stock reflective phrases are cut. This is consistent with the plain-English rules in
  `keel/ips_rewrite/drafts/D35/README.md`.

## (c) Verdicts

### Claim 1: CHANGE
- **Holds.** Every scheme read ends on action:
  - DEAL's fourth question;
  - Hatton and Smith's definition;
  - Moon's top level ("points for learning are noted");
  - the Army's K-10.
  Description alone sits at the bottom of every scheme: Moon's "descriptive writing", Hatton and Smith's "not
  reflective", Kember's "habitual action".
- **Fails.** "What changed" excludes confirmation after a real test, which REFLECT scores at the top and Bell defends.
  Hatton and Smith found critical (transforming) reflection rare and often thin.
- **Most reliable between raters.** No scheme reaches "good" (ICC 0.75 or more) outside its own developers. By the
  figures read:
  - analytic REFLECT by criterion (0.50 in Soemantri; 0.632 at its developers, abstract);
  - then holistic Kember levels (0.43);
  - then one global REFLECT score (0.36).
  The replication (0.448 overall) shows the developers' figure did not travel. DEAL, Moon and Hatton and Smith report
  none.
- **New wording.** "Each event ends on what it changed, or on what was tested and kept, in the recommendation, and
  why."

### Claim 2: CHANGE
- **"Judged on how it disagreed and settled things."** Disagreement is one of five things Wharton names, so it is one
  criterion (C), not the whole test. The evidence for open disagreement mattering comes from work teams (Edmondson),
  not from judges reading reflections. No study of how assessors read team reflections was found; a Consensus search
  returned group-assessment reviews only (gap).
- **"Successes teach as much as failures."** Partly supported. Ellis and Davidi found success-and-failure reviews
  better than failure-only, by a small effect, in one quasi-experiment. The success-and-failure group started lower
  (46.40 against 54.41) and ended higher (76.42 against 71.30), so part of the gap may be regression to the mean; and
  before the reviews began, failures produced richer maps (7.12 ideas against 4.20). The Army asks for equal attention
  to both. "As much" is stronger than the evidence; "also" is supported.
- **Should one event be a success?** Yes: one of three or four. It must be told by its decision point, with the luck
  named. Candidates on the sheet's terms (the lead's call after the blind scores): a decision kept after a real,
  dated test (X100, X053, X098). Keeping the plan through the IPS rewrite (X103) had no test behind it, so it is not
  one.

### Claim 3: KEEP
- **Order.** Wharton's words first, as the names of criteria A to E, quoted in `RERANK_RUBRIC.md`. The literature
  second, as the anchors.
- **Built in.**
  - Five criteria, each with anchors at 1, 3 and 5.
  - Two caps: post-submission events at most 3 on A; an event retelling a Trading Note 1 on E.
  - The VOID rule: read only from the corrected sheet; no credit for a void figure or title.
  - Blind rules: no origin, no candidate ids, roles not names.
  - Ties and the set check.
- **Why five, not Q21's ten.**
  - Rows 3 (structure) and 10 (few things) of Q21's rubric judge the finished 3f, not a single event, so they move to
    the 27 Nov draft checks.
  - Q21's row 7 (hindsight within the rules) folds into D and the post-submission cap.
  - Q21's row 9 (specific and real) folds into E.

### Does the literature support Keel's 3f plan (SKELETON.md part 3f)?
- **Two sentences on last year's lessons: supported.** The Army agenda starts from "what was supposed to happen"
  (K-6). Edmondson notes that "a team's history" shapes what it learns. Keep it to two sentences: it is context, not
  one of the events.
- **Three or four events: supported.** The Army allows organising by "key events, themes, or issues" when time is short
  (K-29). The panel's few-things advice is in Q21 rows 6-7.
- **"Each with a before and after number": change.** Make it a before and after state, with register figures at both
  ends where they exist. A decision kept is told by its test. Two reasons:
  - Confirmation scores at the top (rows 16-17).
  - Only about seven events have register figures at both ends, four of them one story about the ladder's cost (Q21
    claim 1).
  The meta-analysis finding that objective review media help (row 30, abstract) supports using figures where they
  exist, not forcing them.
- **A closing paragraph on which recommendation changed because of which event: supported**, with one edit: "changed
  or was kept after a test". DEAL's fourth question, the Army's K-10, and Wharton's "influenced" all point here.
- **One line on roles: supported.** The Army names "who is responsible for making the recommended changes" (K-10).
  Use Q21 claim 3's narrower wording. The Army's no-names norm (K-38) is for internal reviews and does not bind an
