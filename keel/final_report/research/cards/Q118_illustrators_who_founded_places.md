# Q118. How did picture-book and comics creators who founded places pay to run them, and did any set the running money aside before opening?

- **Date:** Tue 17 Nov 2026, evening, Sydney. All sources were read Tue 17 Nov 2026.
- **Report parts:**
  - 3.0: the recommendation, with running money first.
  - 3a: the operating commitment, and why it is owned before 2033.
  - LAURA: Laura beside the creators most like her, the people who make picture books and comics.
  - RESIDENCY: how places founded by creators actually ran.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Not repeated:**
  - Q101: artists' foundations (Aspen's advice to fund early years "without art sales", Judd, Tiffany, Joan Mitchell, Rauschenberg's Captiva, Jentel).
  - Q100: Taiwan's museums founded by artists. Its 13 cases had "none ... owned even one year of running costs set aside before opening". This card adds people who make picture books and comics.
  - Q67: founders who stated a floor. Its claim 5 says the running money killed the failures. This card adds the creators' cases.
  - Q18 row 36 / store Q18-036: Carle's founding words and the $250,000 added to the endowment through the founders' challenge grant. That row is not repeated here.
  - Q9, Q9b, Q9c, Q11b, Q11e, Q52, Q95, Q108.
  - A grep of the store returned 0 hits for each of these: Chihiro, Sendak, Keats, Tezuka, 手塚, Rockwell, Mort Walker, Quentin Blake, May Gibbs, Klovharu, 幾米, 鄭問, 劉興欽 and 敖幼祥. "Carle" returned 2 hits (Q18-036, and a Carleton citation). "Schulz" returned only Schulz-Hardt (Q91). "Cartoon" returned only advances and inking (Q12b, Q44, Q95).
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Creators who founded places that lasted paid for the running costs first (a foundation or an endowment) and the building second."
  2. "Places that ran on the creator's yearly income shrank or closed when the income fell or the creator died."
  3. "So Laura's ten payments, bought first, put her in the lineage of the places that lasted."
- **Problem class:** how to fund the running costs of a place founded by one creator. The creator's income is uneven, and the creator may die before the place does.
- **Field that solved it first:** museum and foundation accounting.
  - Japanese public-interest foundations keep their running money in named, ring-fenced "specified assets" (特定資産), separate from building funds.
  - US museums split an endowment into a board-designated part, which the board can vote to spend, and a permanent part (Form 990 Schedule D).
  - Neither ties the money to dated years of running costs. Matching money to dated payments comes from sinking-fund and escrow practice (card Q62).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KILL as stated.** None of the 16 places and foundations read today, all founded by or named for a creator, held a sum for running costs before opening. Most paid for the building first and expected the running money to follow.
  - **Schulz Museum (2002).**
    - The heirs paid "about $8 million" to build it.
    - The $1 million a year to run it was to come from memberships, admission, grants, program fees, the shop and "an endowment fund that's yet to be created" (Press Democrat, 2002).
    - The director expected "a minimum of 200,000 visitors its first year". Actual visitors were 70,000, then 55,000 (Press Democrat, 2004): 35% of that minimum, then 28% (Keel arithmetic).
  - **Eric Carle Museum (2002).**
    - "The $18-million project" included an endowment, but $18 million was what the museum was "seeking in sponsorships" (PW, 2001).
    - Ten years on, the endowment held $1,184,873 at the start of FY2012 (IRS 990, Schedule D). That was 45% of the year's $2,631,141 of spending. Investment income was $26,980, about 1% of spending (Keel arithmetic on ProPublica).
  - **Norman Rockwell Museum.** After forty years it had a $3 million endowment against a $4.7 million budget. The director expected "to borrow from the endowment to balance the books" (Berkshire Eagle, 2009).
  - **Quentin Blake Centre.** It bought its building "with a donation in 2019" and raised £12.5 million to convert it. Its working capital came as a £1 million social investment (AHF, a loan-type fund).
  - **The one partial exception: Chihiro (1977).**
    - The family gave the foundation the works, the materials, "a part of the copyright" and the site in 1976. The museum opened 15 months later.
    - So the source of the running money came first. But it was a stream of royalties, not a sum owned.
    - Its separate running reserves (¥1.85bn in 2024) were built up over the next 47 years.
  - **Changed claim:** "Creators' places that lasted were given a lasting income (copyright, or a founder's foundation that kept giving) before or soon after opening. None owned its running money before opening."
  - **Judge's note:** the 2002 plan for the Schulz Museum's running money is a list of exactly the sources the case says Laura's team "may not rely on": co-sponsors, grants, program fees.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** A creator's death closed none of these places, and royalties outlived their creators. What closed or shrank places was losing a promise, or losing a site.
  - **Royalties outlived the creator.**
    - Keats died in 1983. In tax year 2015 his foundation's other income, the line where its royalties fall, was 67.6% of revenue (ProPublica; Keel arithmetic).
    - Chihiro died in 1974. Fifty years later her foundation runs two museums.
    - Sendak's foundation held $261,470 in 2009 and $53.3 million in 2014, after his estate reached it.
  - **What closed places.**
    - Mort Walker's museum. "Marvel Enterprises, which gave the Museum $100,000 as the first payment towards a pledge of $1 million, filed for bankruptcy". A candy deal that "could have potentially earned the Museum $500,000 a year" ended when the firm "went out of business in September 1997". The museum closed at the end of 2002 (IJOCA, 2026).
    - The Cartoon Art Museum, which Schulz's 1987 gift had endowed, closed in September 2015 "when the rent abruptly more than doubled" (NPQ, 2016).
    - Taiwan's 劉興欽 comics hall had "連年虧損" (losses year after year), closed when its lease ended, and lost its park in 2025 because the buildings had no use licence (zh.wikipedia; UNVERIFIED).
  - **Money alone did not keep a residency running.**
    - The Sendak Fellowship had no fellows from 2018 to 2022, while its foundation held $55-57 million (Instrumentl, an IRS-data site).
    - In 2025 the foundation sold art at Christie's so the funds would "maintain the foundation's house in Ridgefield ... and the programs there" (NYT, 2025).
  - **The creator's yearly giving did fall, and the place survived by asking others.**
    - The Carles' first foundation paid out $674,300 in 2011 and $21,650 in 2015.
    - The museum's revenue was below its spending in 8 of the 12 years from FY2012 to FY2023 (Keel arithmetic), and it lived on gifts.
  - **Judge's note:** places did not close because their creator died. They closed because their running money was someone else's promise (a sponsor's pledge, a landlord's lease, a forecast of ticket sales).
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** There is no lineage to join.
  - In none of the 16 cases did the running money come before the doors opened as a sum the place owned.
  - The nearest is Chihiro. Its 2024 balance sheet keeps two running reserves (¥1,300,266,731 for public programmes and ¥549,102,452 for the foundation's operations) apart from a building fund (¥436,750,090) and a repair reserve (¥79,143,000).
  - That is the same split the IPS makes: "The building is a different matter" (l.15). But Chihiro's reserves were invested and built up after opening. In 2025 its board replaced three reserve rules with one new rule after a change in the law.
  - Laura's ten payments are owned in Treasuries from January 2027, six years before the first payment falls due, and none of them can be relabelled.
  - Say: "The places that lasted were given an income that outlived their founder. Laura's plan goes one step further: the running money is owned, with dates, before the doors open." Never say "the lineage of the places that lasted".
  - **Judge's note:** the best-run creator foundation found keeps its running money and its building money in separate named pots, as the IPS does. It took decades to fill them. Laura's are filled before the residency opens.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 9 (Schulz: the building was owned, while the running money depended on "memberships, admission, grants, program fees ... and an endowment fund that's yet to be created").** Serves 3.0 and 3a.
   - The closest real precedent for a beloved creator's place built its plan on the exact sources the case bars Laura from relying on.
   - Laura's plan turns that order round, and row 10 shows what the forecast was worth (35% of its minimum in year one).
2. **Row 2 (Carle: ten years after opening, the endowment was 45% of one year's spending, and investment income was about 1%).** Serves 3a and RESERVE2033.
   - Picture-book makers founding an arts place are the people most like Laura.
   - Set beside her $444,545 reserve on 1 Jan 2033 (111.4% of the ten payments), it shows the size of the step without boasting.
3. **Row 35 (Mort Walker: Marvel paid $100,000 of a $1 million pledge, then went bankrupt, and the museum closed in 2002).** Serves 3.0 and COSPONSOR.
   - Of all the cases, this one shows most clearly the difference between money owned and money promised. It is the conceit's point made with a real cartoonist's museum.
   - It is also why Laura states only what she owns.
4. **Row 17 (Chihiro keeps its running reserves, ¥1.85bn, in pots separate from its building fund).** Serves 3a and LAURA.
   - The best-run creator foundation found makes the same split as IPS l.15, "The building is a different matter".
   - It took 47 years to fill those pots. Laura's are filled before opening.
5. **Row 23 (Sendak: art sold in 2025 "to maintain the foundation's house ... and the programs there", while row 21's residency stopped for five years).** Serves 3a and RESIDENCY.
   - Even $40-57 million behind an illustrator's residency did not keep it running steadily or stop a sale to pay the running costs.
   - IPS l.11 ensures Laura never faces that choice for her ten payments.
