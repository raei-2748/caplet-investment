### 1. Production stages, order, colour on a copy

- **MakingComics.com, "Overview Of The Comic Creation Process", 16 Jan 2014**, https://makingcomics.com/2014/01/16/overview-comic-creation-process
  "Pencilling happens first, followed by inking and finally coloring of the comic." / "Stage 5: Letters ... After the comic art is complete a letterer inserts dialogue balloons/boxes into the panels of the comic and places all of the text."
- **Book Riot, "Adventures of a Comic Book Newbie: Talk to Me about Writing/Pencils/Inks/Colors/Letters!", 6 Nov 2014**, https://bookriot.com/adventures-of-a-comic-book-newbie-talk-to-me-about-writingpencilsinkscolorsletters
  "Once the inker is done with their work, they scan the pages in at high resolution and pass the files along to the colorist, the next person in the process." / "They may hire flatters, people who outline the different segments of a page and fill them with placeholder colors, but it's the colorists who choose the color palette for each comic and add all of the shading and texture to those colors." / "Finally, when the editors ... have approved the colorist's work, the colored files are passed on to the letterer, the final link in the chain."
- **Comic Book Herald, "How Do Superhero Comics Get Made?", 9 May 2019**, https://www.comicbookherald.com/how-do-superhero-comics-get-made/
  "Before colors get applied, colorists either lay down flat fields of color or else work with a 'flatter' to do it for them." / Colors: "This is the final step in the illustrative end of artwork, adding shading and finalizing light sources". It also says: "With the concept art approved in the pitch and the layouts approved by author and editor, it's time to commit. Pencils are the first pass at the artwork".
- **How Products Are Made, "Comic Book" (Gale; also on Encyclopedia.com)**, https://www.madehow.com/Volume-6/Comic-Book.html
  "The original artwork is first photocopied and then scanned into a computer. The scanned copy becomes an electronic file that forms a digital outline of the page to be colored." (Note: this older account puts lettering before colouring.)
- **Todd Klein (Eisner-winning letterer), "Digital Lettering for Comics", Todd's Blog, 26 Oct 2023**, https://kleinletters.com/Blog/digital-lettering-for-comics

- **Todd Klein, "Computer Lettering"**, https://kleinletters.com/ComputerLettering.html

- **CBR, "Letterer Appreciation Day" roundtable, 1 Sep 2017**, https://www.cbr.com/letterer-appreciation-day-all-star-lettering-roundtable

Verdict notes: stages TRUE. "Letters last" PARTLY: often described last and sits on top, but modern letterers commonly work in parallel with the colourist, and historically letters went onto the pencilled board before inking. Colour on a copy TRUE.

### 2. Non-photo blue

- **Wikipedia, "Non-photo blue"** (secondary; the cleanest statement found), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-photo_blue
  "Non-photo blue (or non-repro blue) is a common tool in the graphic design and print industry, being a particular shade of blue that cannot be detected by graphic arts camera film. ... It also allows artists to lay down sketch lines without the need to erase after inking." / "More recently, with digital image scanning and image manipulation, non-photo blue fulfills its function in a different way. ... Most scanners will detect the light blue lines. However, shifting to greyscale and increasing the contrast and brightness of the scanned image reduces the appearance of the blue tones. Another common approach involves replacing the blue channel with another channel – typically the red channel."
- **Jim Campbell (professional letterer), "Forgotten Wisdom, Part Two: Bleeding What Now?"** (blog, c. Jan 2012), https://clintflickerlettering.blogspot.com/2012/01/forgotten-wisdom-part-two-bleeding-what.html
  "(By the way, the rules are printed in non-repro blue to make it easy to separate them from the pencils. If you scan the pencils in grayscale rather than RGB, someone has to manually remove them with the eraser tool!)"
- **Rivkah LaFille (comics artist), "Printing Bluelines for Inking", 14 Jul 2021**, https://www.rivkah.com/lets-make-magic/bluelines
  "Then, when you went to Ye Olden Xerox Machine and made a copy, the blue was a shade that didn't reproduce, and you'd be left with copies of only the black." / "Unlike a Xerox machine, your scanner is not going to automatically drop out your bluelines. You will have to do so manually in photoshop."
- **Gary Martin, *The Art of Comic Book Inking* (Dark Horse), manuscript rewrite by Anina Bennett hosted as her writing sample**, https://www.timetunnel.bigredhair.com/work/writing_samples/InkingBook.pdf
  "Nonphoto-blue pencil: Also called 'nonrepro blue.' Look for one that won't show up on photocopies." / "The inked line is what's reproduced in the finished comic book, since pencil lines are erased after inking."
  (Caution: this is a pre-publication manuscript, not the printed book. Cite the published book only after checking a copy.)

Verdict: TRUE for camera film and photocopiers. For scans PARTLY: the blue is removed on purpose by a channel swap, threshold or filter. Ordinary graphite pencils are erased after inking. "The pencil never shows in print" is true in outcome, but because someone removes it.

### 3. Correcting ink

- **Gary Martin manuscript (as above)**: "White ink: FW white and Pro White (opaque watercolors) work on most black inks; standard white-outs like Liquid Paper do not." / "You can use white ink to correct mistakes as well as to add negative-space details." / "Even when you're doing a rush job, take time to erase the pencil marks from your inked pages and to white out any stray ink lines or splotches."
- **comic tools (cartoonist's blog), "How to get the perfect white-out consistency", 14 Mar 2009**, http://www.comic-tools.com/2009/03/this-week-how-to-get-perfect-white-out.html
  "WHITE-out is another word we cartoonists use for what is really a specialized guache for correcting ink drawings. ... There's really only two kinds worth buying, Deleter, which I use, ... And Pro white".
- **Nate D Sanders Auctions, "Original Comic Art", 20 Aug 2020**, https://natedsandersauctionblog.com/comic-art
  On a 1975 Fantastic Four splash page: "Some white-out present as common with original comic art."
- **downthetubes.net on a Heritage Auctions lot, 12 Jun 2025**, https://downthetubes.net/original-calvin-and-hobbes-art-by-bill-watterson-included-in-upcoming-heritage-auction
  "Staple holes, adhesive staining, margin notes, handling marks, white corrective paint, and the taped and pasted-up header, text and other elements give you a glimpse into artist Jim Starlin's creative process for the cover of Captain Marvel No. 33 (Marvel, 1974)".
- **ComicArtTracker, "Original comic art glossary"**, https://comicarttracker.com/faq-en/original-comic-art-glossary
  "Some paste-ups may have original hand-drawn art on them, such as corrections to a character's head or expression."
- **Artwod, "How to Fix Inking Mistakes Without Starting Over"** (craft blog, undated), https://artwod.com/blog/how-to-fix-inking-mistakes-without-throwing-the-page-away
  "On the original page you'll see the correction if you look closely. In a scan or a photo with decent contrast, it vanishes." / Patching: "This is standard practice in comics. Plenty of published pages are quietly covered in patches."

Verdict: TRUE. Ink cannot be erased; it is covered (white paint) or replaced (pasted patch). The printed page hides the fix; the original art keeps it, and collectors and auction houses describe it.

### 4. Trim, bleed, safe area

- **Aditya Bidikar (professional comics letterer), "The Comics Creator's Technical Handbook (Part 1)", Strange Animals newsletter, 9 Apr 2021**, https://buttondown.com/adityab/archive/strange-animals-2021-8-the-comics-creators
  "Standard American comics are printed at 6.625 x 10.1875 inches (i.e. 6 5/8 inches by 10 3/16 inches)." / "If your art extends to the edge of the page, but you're only supplying files at trim size, then your bleed is automatically white, and if the cutting machine cuts the page slightly outside where you want it, you're left with white space where you don't want it." / "In comics, the added area for bleed is usually 0.125 or 1/8 inches, so based on our trim size, our bleed size is 6.875 x 10.4375 inches" / "But, what if the cutting machine accidentally cuts too far inside from your trim size and cuts off important art or words from your page? To avoid this, you're supposed to keep all important art and all your lettering within the 'safe area'. This is a slightly flexible measure ... the standard one in my experience is 5.875 x 9.4375 inches".
- **PrintNinja (offset comic printer), "File Setup for Full Bleed Printing", updated 15 Jun 2026**, https://printninja.com/file-setup-for-full-bleed-printing
  "This is because there is a standard cutting variance of up to 1mm inherent in professional printing processes. By incorporating an extra 0.125" of artwork into your design, you'll create a buffer to accommodate for this variance." / "Note that the variance can be 1-2mm in either direction." / "If you are looking to print uniform borders in your project (for example: comic book panels), we recommend keeping them 0.25" inside of the trim line, or double the normal safe zone, to account for the 1-2mm trimming variance."
- **PrintNinja, "How to Make a Modern Comic Book", 23 Apr 2020**, https://printninja.com/make-a-modern-comic-book
  "Here, you can see the standard comic book trim size is 6.625 inches x 10.25 inches."
- **Mixam (printer), "Full bleed printing file setup"**, https://mixam.de/support/bleed
  "During the printing process, manufacturing variance can occur, meaning a cutting blade can sometimes cut the paper on either side of the trim line. Reasons for manufacturing variance include slight blade misalignment or paper moving due to pressure from the cutting blade."
- **Blambot (Nate Piekos, letterer), "Original Art Dimensions for American Standard Comics"**, https://blambot.com/en-gb/pages/original-art-dimensions-for-american-standard-comics
  "SAFE AREA - The innermost line on your bristol board. This is the line that your panel borders will butt against. (And all important lettering that you don't want to be trimmed off should go inside here!)" / "TRIM LINE - This is where the comic page will be cut by the printer after the art is reduced."
- **Jim Campbell (as above)**: "Because the guillotining process cannot be 100% accurate, you have to draw your artwork larger than the finished size and allow a margin of error."
- **Sure Print, 15 Mar 2024**, https://sureprintanddesign.ca/article/bleeds-and-margins-with-comic-book-printing : "to allow for the 1–2 mm trimming tolerance".
- Other trim figures: Critical Blast reference sheet "Standard American comic 6.625 × 10.25"; Art of the Comic Book "Trim: 6.625 x 10.125".

Verdict: definitions and purpose TRUE; bleed 1/8 inch TRUE; trim height PARTLY (10.125-10.25 depending on source); blade tolerance 1-2 mm as stated by printers; safe margin varies from 1/8 to 1/2 inch.

### 7. Advances paid in stages

- **Authors Guild, Model Trade Book Contract, "Advance Against Royalties" commentary**, https://go.authorsguild.org/contract_sections/4
  "Today, publishers insist on paying the advance in installments, rather than all upfront. Milestones for triggering payment of an installment include signing of the agreement, partial manuscript delivery, publisher's acceptance of the manuscript, publication, publication of a paperback edition, or simply a year after publication." / "When most of the advance is paid after delivery or even publication, it can no longer be considered an advance."
- **Association of Illustrators (UK industry body), "Publishing Contracts - The Basics"**, https://theaoi.com/resources/contracts/publishing-contracts-the-basics
  "Advance payments for books are usually divided into three sections. Ideally this should be payment on: signature of the contract; delivery of roughs; delivery and acceptance of the artwork".
- **Kate McKean (literary agent), "Book Contracts: Let's Talk Money", Catapult, 16 Sep 2021**, https://magazine.catapult.co/dont-write-alone/stories/book-contracts-money-royalties-advance-payout-subrights-literary-agent-kate-mckean-money-week
  "If your book needs three rounds of edits, you don't get paid until it's done. That's what the acceptance part means." / "For illustrated books, you very often have to wait until all the files are ready for the printer."
- **Lori Richmond (picture-book author-illustrator), 11 Mar 2019**, https://www.loridraws.com/blog/how-childrens-book-advances-and-royalties-work
  "...it's usually broken down to something like: payment 1 on signing, payment 2 on delivery of sketches, and payment 3 on approval of final art." / "You might have a period of YEARS that passes between all payments of the advance."
- **Scott Kurtz (cartoonist), "Making a Graphic Novel, One Step at a Time", 22 Jan 2026**, https://scottkurtz.substack.com/p/making-a-graphic-novel-one-step-at
  "Most traditional publishing advances are paid in installments. A common structure looks like this: Upon signing the contract; Upon delivery of the final script; Upon delivery of the completed book; Upon publication".

Verdict: TRUE. An illustrator's later instalments are conditional on delivery and acceptance.

**Reduction ratio (arithmetic on the sources above):**
- Marvel spec: board trim 9.9375 x 15.28125 to a printed trim of 6.625 x 10.1875 is exactly 2/3 (66.7%) on both sides; the art is drawn at 150%. The same factor turns its bleed (10.5 x 15.75) into 7 x 10.5 and its copy-safe area (9 x 14.0625) into 6 x 9.375.
- Older margin layout (TCJ, Art of the Comic Book): 10 x 15 live area to a 6 x 9 printed live area = 60% (drawn at about 167%, TCJ's "one and two thirds up").
- The claim as worded (10 x 15 to 6.625 x 10.1875) gives 66.25% across and 67.9% down: close to two-thirds but not one exact scale, because it compares one publisher's board box with another's printed trim.

Verdict: PARTLY. The 11 x 17 board and the roughly two-thirds reduction are TRUE. On a modern full-bleed board the "about 10 x 15" box is the trim, and the live or safe area is about 9 x 13.4 to 9 x 14.1. On an older margin layout, 10 x 15 was the whole live area. The exact figures differ by board maker and publisher.

## Craft mechanisms that match the plan's financial ideas (all sourced above)

1. **Reserve against returns.** Authors Guild Model Contract, "Reserve Against Returns" (https://go.authorsguild.org/contract_sections/5): "During the first [4] accounting periods of sales, Publisher has the right to retain a reasonable amount of the royalties earned by Author as a reserve against the return of unsold copies of the Work, not to exceed [35%] of amounts due. Thereafter, only actual returns will be deducted in calculating royalties." This is Laura's own industry setting aside earned money against a known, limited risk and releasing it later: the operating reserve, in her trade's terms.
2. **Margin at an edge.** The bleed (1/8 inch, about 3.2 mm) is wider than the blade's stated 1-2 mm wander, and lettering stays inside the safe area so that the blade can never reach it. The buffer is sized to beat the error. It does not just match it.
3. **Commit only what you own.** An advance instalment is released only on delivery and acceptance ("you don't get paid until it's done"). Image solicits only after "three completed issues". In both cases the promise comes after the work is inked.
4. **A promise to people who pre-commit.** Shops order non-returnable copies about two months ahead. After FOC, print runs are fixed. A book more than about 30 days late can be made returnable or cancelled. The co-sponsors in 2031 are in the shops' position.
5. **Fixed bottom, variable top.** Colour is laid on a scan and never touches the inked original. Letters sit on their own layer. The fixed layer comes first and stays untouched.
6. **The pencil is removed on purpose.** Non-photo blue is dropped out by a filter before printing. It does not vanish by itself.
7. **Corrections leave a record.** White paint and patches disappear in print but stay on the original art, and auction houses list them.
8. **Laura's own sentence:** "I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves." The advance is fixed and royalties are unknown.
9. **Drawn large, printed small.** The art is drawn at about 150% and shrunk to about two-thirds, so a 1/16-inch wobble on the board prints at about 1/24 inch (arithmetic from the sizes in item 10). No source was found that says reduction is done in order to hide flaws, so do not claim that as the reason.
