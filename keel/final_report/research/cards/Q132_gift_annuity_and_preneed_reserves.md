- **Problem class:** licensing a promise of fixed future payments to people who cannot check the promiser: the law makes the promiser set aside a priced, walled-off reserve, with a margin and an examiner, before or as the promise is made. **Field that solved it first:** US state insurance law for "charitable annuity societies" (New York's segregated annuity funds, regulated since at least the old Insurance Law s.45; California's "Grants and Annuities Societies" chapter, enacted 1935), then state funeral law (pre-need trusting) and medical-liability law (court-ordered periodic payments).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Yes to a walled-off, actuarially sized reserve held before the promise; no to "restricted to named assets" in New York; the regulation cited is the wrong one.**
  - **Before the promise.** New York: no charity "shall make or issue in this state any annuity contract before obtaining a permit", and it must file its forms and rate schedule "before making such agreement" (s.1110(a), (d)). California and Washington set the reserve on granting the certificate (Cal. Ins. Code s.11521; RCW 48.38.020(1)).
  - **Sized.** New York: admitted assets at least the reserves plus "a surplus of ten per centum" (or $100,000), and 25% for small issuers that skip the permit (s.1110(b), (d)). Florida, Washington and Arkansas also ask reserves plus 10%. California's reserve uses the 2012 Individual Annuity Reserve Table at 4% for annuities issued from 2017 (Bulletin 2016-6); the 1949 statute caps the rate at what "is currently yielded on safe securities".
  - **Walled off.** "segregated as separate and distinct funds ... shall not be applied to pay its debts" (New York); "legally and physically segregated" (California, Washington).
  - **Named assets: only California and Florida.** California: the insurers' list (first item "Obligations of the United States of America"), with listed shares and funds capped at the lesser of net worth or 50%. Florida: stock at most 50% of required reserves and surplus, 10% in any one name. New York has used the "prudent investor standard" since at least 2001 and is "not ... subject to the investment limitations"; New Jersey moved to its Prudent Investor Act in 2003; Washington uses the 1830 prudent-man words. In 1976 New York had capped common stock at 10% of the fund.
  - **The citation.** The reserve rests on Insurance Law s.4217 and, for valuation rates since 2020, 11 NYCRR 103.5 (Regulation 213). DFS's list names Part 93 "Aircraft Reinsurance by Life Insurers": drop "Part 93 (Regulation 30)".
  - **Judge's note:** New York prices how generous a charity's fixed promise may be from "the ten year treasury bond plus two percent". Laura's ten are priced at the Treasury curve itself, with nothing added.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Partial trusting, yes; "invested badly" was fraud into the seller's own affiliate; $600 million is an allegation.**
  - **The rule then.** NPS could keep up to 20% of each payment and had to put 80% in trust with a bank trustee (RSMo 436.027, 436.031.1, repealed 2009; 8th Cir. 2017).
  - **What the trust held.** Missouri's board, 30 Apr 2008: the accounts held "a substantially and significantly lower amount than the 80%", and "the trust primarily consists of term insurance policies and not cash". The policies were the Cassitys' own Texas insurer's (Lincoln Memorial), bought with trust money and borrowed against "without the trustee's written approval" (8th Cir.).
  - **Size.** Prosecutors: "more than $450 million in losses", "some 97,000 customers in more than 16 states", 1992-2008. The receivers' 2009 suit alleged "a more-than $600 million racket" (NOLHGA). A jury awarded $355.5 million of compensatory damages against a bank trustee; the 8th Circuit sent it back in 2017 for trust-law damages.
  - **The fix (Missouri SB 1, effective 28 Aug 2009).** All payments into trust within 60 days; at most 5% plus 10% released, so at least 85% stays (the state: "In the past, the requirement was 80 percent"). The trustee may not be under the seller's control, investing follows the prudent investor standard, and the board may audit without waiting for a complaint ("By that time, it was often too late").
  - **Another state: New York trusts all of it.** "Any and all moneys" go within ten business days into an interest-bearing bank account or "an investment backed by the government of the United States", never commingled, and they "shall continue to be the money of the person making such payment" (GBL s.453).
  - **Judge's note:** the biggest US pre-need failure was not a market loss. The reserve held the promiser's own affiliate's paper. Laura's reserve holds the US Treasury's.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, one level further.**
  - **Court-ordered fixed schedules.** Texas medical-liability law (2003) funds a court's periodic-payment judgment by an insurer's annuity, "an obligation of the United States", liability insurance, or other funding the court approves. Whatever security remains goes back to the defendant (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code s.74.505). California makes a defendant who is not adequately insured "post security adequate to assure full payment" (CCP s.667.7).
  - **Pre-need.** New York names US-government-backed investments as the only investment besides insured deposits (GBL s.453).
  - **Matching to dates.** No gift annuity or pre-need rule asks for it: a lifetime annuity's end date is unknown, so the law prices it with a mortality table instead. Date matching appears only in IRC s.130(d) ("reasonably related") and the lottery's prize rule, both already in the store.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. Laura's reserve is like the law's in form, stricter in what it holds, and alone where the law gives a charity a second line.**
  - **Form.** On 1 Jan 2033 her reserve is $445,655 against the ten's value of $399,089, 111.7% funded, so the extra Treasuries are about 11.7% of the ten (NUMBERS l.13, 8h; Keel arithmetic). That is about the 10% surplus four states require, on a different basis: her figures are at forward rates, the law's at a statutory rate.
  - **What it holds.** Hers holds 0% shares. Charities' gift annuity reserves averaged 57% "Stocks/mutual funds" in 2021 (ACGA survey via PG Calc), and the ACGA's own rates assume 40% equities.
  - **The second line.** Washington: "The general assets of the insurer or institution ... shall be liable ... to the extent that the separate reserve fund is inadequate". Charities used that line after 2008, moving money from endowments and operating budgets into their reserves. The case gives Laura no second line: "Teams may not rely on co-sponsors, grants, program fees, or other outside funding" (case l.91-92).
  - **What it means.** Laura's margin has to sit in the assets themselves (Treasuries matched to the dates, sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing; NUMBERS 2), not in a surplus over a pool of shares. Never "meets New York's test": she is not a charity, and her figures are on a different basis.
  - **Judge's note:** the law lets a charity back fixed payments with shares because it has a whole balance sheet behind the reserve. Laura has none, so hers holds none.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 15 (with 25-26), 3a: the law lets a charity back fixed payments with shares because its whole balance sheet stands behind the reserve; the case forbids Laura a second line, so her reserve holds none.** The aha that turns "why all Treasuries?" from caution into arithmetic of who stands behind whom.
2. **Row 7, 3a or 3e: in 2022 the New York Philharmonic's legal gift annuity reserve, 95.88% shares, was $2,483 under its 110% line.** A real, named, recent case of a walled reserve for fixed payments failing its own test in a falling year; one clause.
3. **Rows 23-24, 3a: charities' reserves for fixed payments average 57% shares, and the industry's rates assume 40%; "annuity payments ... are not reduced when market values decrease."** The industry's own reason, in its own words, for Laura's 0%.
4. **Row 4, 3a: New York prices a charity's maximum fixed promise from "the ten year treasury bond plus two percent"; Laura's ten are priced at the Treasury curve itself.** Shows the IPS's "one step further" (l.11) against a statute a judge can check.
5. **Rows 33 and 36, Table 3 and 3e: NPS's trust held the seller's own affiliate's term policies; Missouri's fix was the whole sum set aside, a keeper the seller does not control, and checks that do not wait for a complaint.** Gives Table 3's yearly reserve row its reason; extends card Q62's circularity with the largest US pre-need failure.
