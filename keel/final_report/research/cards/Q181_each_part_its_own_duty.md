- Problem class: when does a set of independent parts have a property none of the parts has? ## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. The court tested the eraser by imagining it gone, which is the plan's deletion test; the same judgment's mark of a real combination, "parts used in common", finds the plan's one shared fund

(a) In 1875 the US Supreme Court refused a patent on the pencil with an eraser in its end by asking what each part would do if the other were missing: the lead "performs the same operation and in the same manner as it would do if there were no rubber at the other end".
(b) Laura's plan passes that test on purpose: put VT at zero and the ten payments are unchanged and the range is one figure, the bottom (NUMBERS 4), because, as Trading Note 2 says, each payment "rests on a dedicated instrument".
(c) It breaks on the court's own list of what a real combination has, "relation between the instruments", "reciprocal action" and "parts used in common": the plan has two parts used in common, the late-2032 fund, which pays the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top (Trading Note 2: it "does two jobs"; NUMBERS 8w), and the extra Treasuries, one pool behind all ten payments (8a). The court called a shared handle a convenience and never asked what breaks with it, and deleting a part cannot show that; breaking the shared part does: if that fund ended early in 2031 and cash waiting earned nothing, the top would be up to $4,589 short, the bottom up to $2,887, and the ten $1,827 short, landing on the 2036 payment, where the extra Treasuries have nothing to spare (8w; at today's market rates nothing is short).

### 2. "Two and two have been added together, and still they make only four": the test a patent must pass is the test a promise must fail

(a) In 1950 the Court said old parts earn a patent "only when the whole in some way exceeds the sum of its parts", and of a supermarket checkout built from a counter, a sliding frame and guide rails it said: "Two and two have been added together, and still they make only four"; in 2007 (KSR) it said joining familiar parts by known methods is likely obvious "when it does no more than yield predictable results".
(b) A promise to co-sponsors has to fail that test, and the plan does: the top Laura states is the bottom's figure plus the shares moved into Treasuries, counted on one basis and rounded down once (WORDS), so a co-sponsor holding Table 1 can add it up, and the plan's most striking result, that none of the ten payments depends on a share price (IPS l.11), comes from old parts "each performing the same function it had been known to perform", the Court's words in 1976 for a combination that is not new.
(c) The parallel no longer rests on the parent card's word: in 1950 the same Court called "combination" and "aggregation" labels for a verdict whose use as tests "results in nothing but confusion", in 1983 the patent appeals court found a "synergism" requirement "nowhere found in the statute", and the examiners' manual now says no claim is rejected for "aggregation". What survives is "predictable results", and it gives the report a rule of words: the parts do what they are known to do and the figures add, and the plan is never called new, clever or a synergy.

### 3. The parts do depend on each other, but one way only: the plan's merit is its order of purchase and its fit to Laura, two things patent law never credits

(a) The pencil judgment said the law needs more than a change "of the order in which they are used", yet its own examples of real combinations include parts that work "sometimes successively", and in 2007 the Court added that "neither the particular motivation nor the avowed purpose of the patentee controls".
(b) Laura's plan has an order of another kind: not an order of use, like turning a pencil over, but an order of claim on scarce money. The 2028 deposit first completes the ten and buys the extra Treasuries, then the late-2032 fund, then VT, and the 2031 move takes its size from what VT is worth that day, so each part's size depends on the parts bought before it and never the reverse; that is why a deposit about three-quarters smaller still pays all ten if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 8u, 5) and the cut lands on the building money (CANON D9). The reason for the order is Laura's income (IPS l.9: her career is "a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell"), the very thing the patent test is told to ignore.
(c) The court's missing mark, "reciprocal action", draws the exact line: the plan's parts depend forward, never back, so no fall in one part can force a sale of another (IPS l.17: "A fall in the market is not one of them"). The break also tells the report where to show the merit: the pencil's order never changed an outcome, and the plan's order changes nothing while money is plentiful, so the order is shown with a short deposit, never with the central case.

### 4. Best companion: Simon's two watchmakers show, from the other side, that the order of assembly decides what survives

(a) In 1962 Herbert Simon told of two watchmakers: Tempus built each watch of about 1,000 parts in one go, and when he was interrupted the half-built watch "immediately fell to pieces and had to be reassembled from the elements"; Hora built "subassemblies of about ten elements each", so with a 1-in-100 chance of an interruption at each part added, Tempus took "about four thousand times as long" (pp. 470-471).
(b) Patent law says the order in which old parts are put together adds nothing to the thing; Simon shows that the order of assembly decides whether the thing survives being built, because each finished subassembly is a stable intermediate form. Laura's plan reaches three finished, owned forms on three dates (January 2027, January 2028 and the move before the 2031 meetings), and Simon predicts what NUMBERS confirms: a move in markets alone can reach the payments only before the January 2028 purchases ("any move in yields once the first slice is bought in January 2028" breaks nothing, 8u) and the range only before the move ("After the move nothing changes either end", section 4). The team's first plan was Tempus: nothing about the payments was finished before 1 January 2033, when all ten had to be bought (8s).
(c) It breaks on the interruptions: Simon's are small, frequent and independent, one chance per part, while Laura's shocks are rare and hit every unfinished part at once (one share market, one yield curve), and a few reach finished parts too, a US default and an early end of the one fund that carries three promises (idea 1). Simon's own word for systems like this, "nearly decomposable" (strong ties inside each part, weak ties between parts), is the accurate one for the plan, and it replaces the parent card's "the parts never interact".
