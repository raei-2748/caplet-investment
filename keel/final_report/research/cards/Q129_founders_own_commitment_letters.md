- **Claims tested:**
  1. "The founders' commitment letters that read as most credible state one amount or share, its purpose and its timing in the first person, and name what stays uncertain in a single clause, rather than in a paragraph of caveats."
  2. "Founders of arts places who announced their own contribution did so with a figure and a condition, not a promise of the whole."
  3. "Fundraising research finds that a donor's own words with a concrete figure persuade more than institutional language."
- **Problem class:** making a giver's own written promise believable to the people who must plan around it (a credibility signal in a cheap-talk setting). **Field that solved it first:** the genre itself was set by the Giving Pledge letters from 2010; the evidence on which words carry information comes from text analysis of peer-to-peer loan requests and crowdfunding pitches (LIWC, Pennebaker's group; Prosper and Kickstarter studies) and Loewenstein's donor experiments.
- **Disanalogy to carry everywhere:** a Giving Pledge letter is a moral promise of a share of future wealth, with no dollar figure and nothing owned against it. Laura's 2031 statement is a dollar range whose Treasuries she owns before she states it (IPS l.15's move; NUMBERS 4: about $95,000-$115,000 on today's figures, the top first). The letters are the nearest genre in voice, the farthest in substance.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The genre is first person, short and plain, but it almost never names a dollar figure, and it usually names no doubt at all. What makes it believed is money already moved, not the sentence.
  - **What was coded.** 23 letters read at givingpledge.org, chosen for spread: 10 from Asia or Oceania, including Taiwan (Samuel Yin), Korea, China and India; creators (George Lucas, Jeff Skoll), a novelist (MacKenzie Scott) and a fashion designer (Diane von Furstenberg); and arts-museum founders (the Broads). Counts are Keel arithmetic.
  - **What the letters state.**
    - First person: 23 of 23 ("we" in 5).
    - A dollar figure for the pledge itself: 0 of 23.
    - A share of wealth: 14 (10 as a number, 4 in words, such as "majority" or "almost all").
    - A period: 13, almost all "lifetime or at death".
    - A dated schedule: 1 (Buffett).
  - **Uncertainty.**
    - None named: 16.
    - One clause or one sentence: 6. Three of these are about how the money will be used, not whether it exists.
    - A full paragraph: 1 (Kim & Sul).
  - **The verb.** Of the 14 letters that state a share:
    - 5 use a promise ("will go", "hereby pledge that we will", "commit to placing").
    - 6 use exactly the words 3d bans ("intention", "intent", "I expect", "I plan", "my plans").
    - 3 describe a present practice.
  - **The proof.** 9 of 23 point to money already moved or a vehicle already funded.
  - **The outcome.** The letter with no share, no date and no figure, MacKenzie Scott's, has the largest record in the sample: over $26bn in 2,700+ gifts. Its credibility came from giving, not from wording.
  - Judge's note: none of the 23 pledges names a dollar figure. Laura's 2031 words would name one ($115,000, NUMBERS 4) only because the Treasuries behind it are already hers. The genre's "intention" and "at least" are free to a pledger and banned in Laura's mouth.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Founders of arts places stated what they themselves paid as a fact already done. The stone at Jin Yong's study reads "1400余万元 … 均由金庸一人承担". Tadao Ando said "設計・建設の費用は私が責任を持ちましょう". The condition usually came from the receiver, not the founder:
  - Osaka's council accepted Ando's building as a 負担付き寄附 (a gift with a condition).
  - Chicago sold Theaster Gates the bank for $1 with a $750,000 first investment required.
  - **The running years were the weak part every time.**
    - Ando had to raise about ¥50m a year by visiting about 85 firms himself.
    - Park Kyung-ni's writers' residency lived on yearly grants, cut 20% in 2017, with deficits of about ₩30m a year.
    - Jin Yong's study, given with no running money and no terms on its use, was leased at ¥450,000 a year and became a dining room with a ¥500-a-head minimum.
    - Lin Hwai-min's prize money started the Wanderer Project, but 「沒有他們，就沒有流浪者」 (without the Shih family's foundation, no Wanderers).
  - Laura's plan reverses the order: her $50,000 a year of running money is owned to 2042 first, and the building share is the part she states as a range. This adds East Asian founders, with their own words, to Q118 row 9's Schulz case.
  - Judge's note: the architect famous for the building said "It is not enough to just build a building. You have to nurture it". The plan owns that part before it states the building figure.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** No study read compares a donor's own words with institutional wording for the same gift, so the claim as written is unsupported (Gaps). What the studies do show:
  - **Concrete detail.** It raises giving only when it shows what the money does: $10.25 against $7.54, while low-impact detail gave $6.95 (Cryder, Loewenstein & Scheines 2013).
  - **Scale statistics.** These lower giving, even placed beside a story: $1.43 against $2.38 (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic 2007).
  - **Concrete and numerical words.** These help loan requests; self-justification hurts them (Larrimore et al. 2011).
  - **Personal stories.** Identity stories raise funding but predict repayment worse (Herzenstein et al. 2011).
  - **Hope and gap words.** Phrases of hope and of a funding gap ("hope to get", "short of our goal") mark unfunded Kickstarter projects. Phrases of a pledge made ("has pledged") mark funded ones (Mitra & Gilbert 2014).
  - Judge's note: the stories that raise loans best are the ones least tied to repayment. A co-sponsor should count what is held, and Laura's words lead with what is held.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 (3d):** six of the fourteen founders who state a share use exactly the verbs 3d bans ("intention", "expect", "plan"), and none names dollars (row 1). It shows a judge why Laura's words drop them: a pledge can, a figure that is owned need not.
2. **Row 4 (3d):** the genre's own test of belief is money already moved (9 of 23). Laura's Treasuries are that proof, dated and held for her, so the passage can lead with what is held without arguing.
3. **Row 25 (3.0, team's prose):** Ando says "It is not enough to just build a building. You have to nurture it". He raised twenty years of running money after the building. The plan owns its running share first.
4. **Row 30 (3a, 3d):** Korea's first writers' residency, founded by the author of Toji, ran on yearly grants that a review panel cut by 20%. Her daughter covers about ₩30m of deficit a year. It is a sharp, real contrast with ten years owned to 2042.
5. **Row 43 (3d):** across 45,810 Kickstarter pitches, "hope to get" and "short of our goal" mark unfunded projects and "has pledged" funded ones. This is field evidence behind 3d's word bans, with Herzenstein (row 39) as the judge's aha on stories against substance.

## Gaps

- **The sample of letters.**
  - The full pledger list did not load (the list page is cut off), so the 23 letters were chosen for spread, not drawn at random. Counts describe this sample only.
  - The page for Chen Tianqiao and Chrissy Luo returned 404.
  - No Taiwanese pledger other than Samuel Yin was found, though the FAQ names Taiwan.
  - Korean and Chinese letters (Kim & Sul, YOU Zhonghui, DONG Fangjun, Gensheng Niu) were coded from their English text on the page.
- **Outcomes not checked.** Whether Buffett and the other pledgers kept their schedules was not checked at source (Q52-029 holds the 2025 IPS study of deceased pledgers).
- **Claim 3's exact test.** No study was found that compares a giver's own first-person words with institutional wording for the same gift. The next round could read:
  - Kogut & Ritov (2005), the "singularity effect";
  - Meer (2011), on asks from peers;
  - Andreoni, Rao & Trachtman (2017), "Avoiding the ask";
  - Chatterjee & Hambrick (2007), on "I" in CEO letters;
  - Rittenhouse's candor rankings of shareholder letters.
- **Papers read only in part.**
  - Herzenstein et al. (2011) and Gao, Lin & Sias (2023) were read at abstract level only. The 29/24/12% default figures come from the University of Delaware release.
  - Mitra & Gilbert's phrase list was read partly in press coverage (Georgia Tech; UK Fundraising). "Even a dollar" and the error rates were read in the paper.
- **Jin Yong.** His CCTV interview quote is second hand (row 28). The stele's wording was read in a Chinanews report, not from a photograph.
- **Lin Hwai-min.** The National Cultural Award's sum was not on the Cloud Gate pages read. The 2015 prize is told two ways (row 34).
- **Theaster Gates.** His own cash share is not stated in the sources read, and the renovation total conflicts ($3.7m against $4.5m).
- **Taiwan.** No Taiwanese arts founder's announcement of a dollar contribution to a building with a condition was found beyond Q100, Q118 and Lin's prize money.
- **How pages were read.** Several news and city pages (Osaka, Hangzhou, Wonju, Cloud Gate) were read as page text returned by the search tool, not as full fetched pages.
