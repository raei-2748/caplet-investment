- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A reservoir's firm (or safe) yield is the draft it can supply through the worst drought on record (Rippl's mass curve, 1883); the operating reserve is sized the same way: all ten payments met even if cash waiting earns nothing."
  2. "Water utilities sell firm supply and interruptible surplus apart and contract only the firm part; co-sponsors count only what is owned, and her flexibility (the half of the shares promised to no one) is the interruptible part."
  3. "Firm-yield sizing has failed when a drought beat the drought of record (Cape Town's Day Zero 2018, Australia's Millennium Drought, Texas 2011, California): a standard set by history can be beaten, while the plan's standard (cash waiting earns nothing) is set below history."
  4. "The reservoir parallel supports the one conceit, pencil and ink."
- **Problem class:** sizing a store so that a fixed draw is met through the worst run of inflows (storage-yield or "critical period" analysis). **Field that solved it first:** water-resources engineering: Rippl's mass curve (1883), then the sequent peak algorithm and linear programming (USACE EM 1110-2-1420 lists all three), then probabilistic yield (Hazen 1914 onward).

## Verdicts in one screen

**Claim 1: CHANGE.** Three faults and one strong likeness.
- *Firm is not safe.* USACE: "Safe yield typically includes a factor of safety over the firm yield"; Texas's one-year safe yield leaves "a one-year supply in storage at the end of the drought of record" (Region B); California's Independent Science Board calls firm yield "sometimes misleadingly called 'safe yield'". Drop "(or safe)".
- *Rippl did not tie the method to a drought of record.* His 1883 point was that "it is not the year in which the least total rainfall occurs which gives the measure of the storage-capacity required, but the period in which the greatest fluctuation of supply and demand happens" (the Vienna case: the experts sized on the driest winter, 1857-58; his 37-year mass curve showed 1855-56 and 1858-59 needed more). Klemeš (1979): the method "is not in any way tied to the use of a historic record". "Firm yield" against "a repeat of the drought of record" is today's agency usage (USACE 2018, TWDB 2012), not Rippl's words.
- *The likeness is real in the arithmetic.* A running total of inflow less draw, the largest shortfall sets the store, solved by a drawing (Rippl), a sequent peak, or linear programming (USACE lists LP). NUMBERS 2 sizes the extra Treasuries ($35,894 in Jan 2028) with "an exact linear programme" so that all ten are paid "if nothing earns a cent and every waiting dollar ... loses 0.15% a year", and under that standard they have "exactly $0 spare". USACE's firm yield is the draw that "brings the stored water volume exactly to zero once". The plan's backstop is a firm-yield sizing.
- *The input is harsher than any reservoir is allowed.* A city's water plan refuses "no rain": Cape Town, Feb 2018, "Assuming it will not rain again is not realistic." Laura's reserve is sized on exactly that for its one uncertain inflow (waiting money earns nothing and pays 0.15% a year, for all sixteen years 2027-2042), which is harsher than the worst sixteen years of US bills on record (1934-49: +0.21% a year after the fee; NUMBERS 8a, Q81). It can afford this, at $35,894, because the main inflow, the Treasuries' coupons and principal, is written into the bonds.
- **Better wording:** "sized with a reservoir engineer's arithmetic, on a harsher input than any reservoir engineer is allowed to assume."
- **Judge's note:** a water engineer may not design for "it never rains again"; Laura's reserve is designed for exactly that on the one inflow it does not own, and the price is $35,894 of extra Treasuries, most of it returned to her if rain comes ($45,456 in 1 Jan 2033 money at today's forwards, NUMBERS 8h).
- **Judge's note:** Rippl's own lesson, 143 years old, is why the test holds zero for sixteen years and not one bad year: a store is emptied by the longest run of thin inflows, not by the worst single reading (Q81: the bill sat under 0.15% for 58 months running, Feb 2011 to Nov 2015).

**Claim 2: KEEP the first half reworded; CHANGE the second.**
- Utilities do sell firm and interruptible water apart and price them apart: LCRA firm water is "subject to a higher rate than interruptible"; Cape Town: "Agriculture typically pays less for water at a lower level of assurance". Only the firm part counts against needs: Texas Region H, "interruptible supplies and local supplies that are not firm during drought are not available for use in meeting needs"; the UK's deployable output "should not include the contributions from any demand or supply drought measures".
- But "contract only the firm part" is wrong: LCRA contracts interruptible water to irrigation customers, and California's SWP has "Article 21 interruptible water". The true rule is a law in Kansas: "the director shall not contract for withdrawals ... in excess of the yield capability ... computed to provide water through a drought having a 2% chance of occurrence" (K.S.A. 82a-1305 as quoted in KGS Bulletin 239), so "much of the average reservoir yield remains uncommitted".
- Her flexibility is **not** interruptible water. Interruptible water is sold to someone and cut first so firm customers keep theirs (LCRA 2012-2015). Her flexibility is "promised to no one" (IPS l.15) and is never a buffer for the ten payments (IPS l.11; Q59's kill). Its twin is Kansas's **uncommitted** yield: held back, promised to nobody, there for the owner's own uses (in her case overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short).
- The cautionary twin for the range is California's Table A: a stated maximum of 4,172,786 acre-feet a year, delivered at 5% in 2014, 2021 and 2022, about 40% on average 2015-2024 (Keel arithmetic). Laura's range is the opposite: the bottom is owned from January 2028, and the top owned before the meetings (IPS l.15).
- **Judge's note:** Kansas made into law what IPS l.15 says in a sentence: you may promise only the figure that holds in the bad case; the rest stays uncommitted.

**Claim 3: CHANGE ("failed" to "beaten", and two examples drop).**
- *Cape Town* planned at the national department's "1 in 50 year level of assurance"; the drought was estimated at "311 years, with 90% confidence that it actually falls between 105 and 1280 years". A probability standard was beaten, not a drought of record, and Day Zero never came: demand fell from about 1,200 to about 500 megalitres a day and the dams bottomed at about 20% (end April 2018) against a 13.5% Day Zero line.
- *Melbourne* (Millennium Drought): "In 2006, streamflows were almost 30% lower than the previous lowest ... far less than the worst-case forecasts of Victoria's water managers"; storage fell to 25.6% (June 2009). The record was beaten; the city did not run dry.
- *Texas 2011* was "the most intense one-year drought in Texas since at least 1895", yet the 1950s (77 months) stayed the statewide drought of record (TWDB 2017). Locally the record did fall: LCRA's 2007-2015 drought beat 1947-57, its firm yield was cut, interruptible water was stopped, and existing firm contracts were not touched. The town that ran dry (Spicewood Beach) ran on wells.
- *California's* SWP is contracts written above firm supply (Table A), not a firm-yield sizing that failed. Drop it from this claim.
- What survives: "a standard set by history can be beaten" (LCRA; USBR Foss, about a 30% chance; USGS Massachusetts; Archfield and Vogel). "Set below history" holds for US bills since 1934 (the test's -0.15% a year sits below every 12-month span, worst -0.126%, and below the worst sixteen years, +0.21%; replay: all ten paid in all 919 start months, at least $2,454 left; NUMBERS 8a). It is not a floor of nature: bills closed below zero on 8 days in 2015 and 2020, and three central banks ran negative rates for about eight years (Q2d). And today the payments' standard is a firm yield, not a safe yield: they carry no margin for fund costs beyond the 0.07% fee until the Wed 18 Nov re-size (about $236 short per 0.1% of the funds' payouts, NUMBERS 2). Never "cannot be beaten", and never say waiting cash has a floor at zero.
- **Better wording:** "Every water standard set by the worst drought seen has met a worse one somewhere. Laura's is set below anything US waiting cash has done since 1934."

**Claim 4 (the conceit): SUPPORTS as evidence, COMPETES as an image.**
- As evidence it sharpens the conceit: by the conceit's own definition, water's "firm" is pencil. USBR: "the firm yield is neither absolute nor fixed - it is an assumed volume based on assumed future conditions"; LCRA's fell by about 100,000 acre-feet in 2015 when a new drought beat the record. Laura's ink is a figure a Treasury she owns will pay on its date; no new record can rewrite it.
- As an image it competes. Voice round 3: "Keep one parallel per passage." "Reservoir" sits a syllable away from "operating reserve", and a fountain pen has an ink reservoir, a crossed image waiting to happen.
- **Recommend:** store and References only, or at most one plain evidence clause in 3e or Figure 1's note, never in a passage that carries a pencil-and-ink turn.
- **Judge's note:** in water, "firm" is a forecast that a worse drought can cut by a sixth; in Laura's plan the firm part is a list of Treasury payments no drought of any kind can cut.

**Where the likeness breaks (say these plainly if the parallel is used):**
1. A reservoir refills ("rest and refill"; it "can only fill from natural inflow"); the reserve only runs down. After January 2028 nothing goes in (NUMBERS 8h: "it changes only by running down").
2. A reservoir serves "an indefinitely long period" (Rippl) at a steady draw; the reserve serves ten dated $50,000 draws, 2033-2042, and then ends.
3. A reservoir's leftover water stays or spills; the reserve's leftover returns to Laura ($45,456 in 1 Jan 2033 money at today's forwards; $0 under the test; NUMBERS 8h).
4. A reservoir's inflow is all weather; the reserve's is mostly written into Treasuries. Its uncertain parts are the waiting rate and the costs of the five iBond funds, whose payouts follow their Treasuries but are not fixed to the dollar.
5. In a drought worse than the record, LCRA cuts every firm customer pro rata; the ten payments are fixed and never pro-rated (IPS l.11), and the plan's only exits are its two reasons (IPS l.17).
6. Interruptible water is a buffer for firm customers; her flexibility is not a buffer for the payments.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 27 (3e, or 3a's evidence):** Cape Town's "Assuming it will not rain again is not realistic", against a reserve sized on exactly that for its one uncertain inflow, and able to afford it ($35,894) because the rest is written into Treasuries. The cleanest aha in the card.
2. **Row 2 (3a, References):** Rippl, 1883: not the driest year but the worst run sets the store. It explains in one sentence why the test holds zero interest for sixteen years and not one bad year.
3. **Row 17 with row 38 (3c, 3d):** Kansas law forbids promising past the firm figure and leaves the rest "uncommitted"; California's Table A shows what happens when a stated figure sits above the firm one (5% delivered three times in nine years). Laura states only what she owns.
4. **Rows 7 and 8 (3a, Figure 1's note):** the backstop is sized as USACE sizes firm yield, to "exactly $0 spare" under its standard; firm, not safe, until the Wed 18 Nov re-size adds the margin for costs beyond the fee. Gives the report the precise word.
5. **Row 33 (3c, 3e):** USBR: firm yield "is neither absolute nor fixed - it is an assumed volume based on assumed future conditions". In water, "firm" is pencil; Laura's ink is a Treasury payment, so no new record can rewrite it.
