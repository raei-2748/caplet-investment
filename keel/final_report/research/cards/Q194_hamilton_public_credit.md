- **Corrections to the question as posed (each keeps a false fact out):**
  1. "To the letter" is not Hamilton's phrase in the 1790 report: the word "letter" does not occur in it (full text searched at constitution.org; not surfaced in the Founders Online text either). His words are "by good faith, by a punctual performance of contracts" and "scrupulously to fulfil the engagements of the government". Never put "to the letter" in quotation marks as his.
  2. Hamilton did not "pay first and in full". The Funding Act's own preamble uses two verbs: "fulfilling the engagements" for the foreign debt and "funding their domestic debt upon equitable and satisfactory terms" for the rest. The domestic debt was swapped into new stock worth, at market prices on 31 Dec 1790, about $75 for each $100 of old loan certificates (Hall and Sargent). The credit was built by restating the promise to what revenue could carry and then paying the restated promise punctually.
  3. "A promise kept for two centuries" is too long. Congress's own researchers write: "Under any reasonable definition of default, the federal government defaulted in 1814" (interest due in Boston on 1 Oct 1814 went unpaid). The honest span is "since the War of 1812". Never "the United States has never defaulted".
- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** how does a promiser whom nobody can force to pay make a promise that others will count at face? Public finance solved it first, in Britain after 1688 and in the United States in 1790: tie each promise to money that exists (a revenue), keep paying it on the day, and let the record do the rest; constitutional law then wrote the habit down (Article VI, 1787; the 14th Amendment, s.4, 1868).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**Idea 1. Hamilton's rule for credit, run one step further.**
(a) In 1790 Hamilton asked Congress to adopt as "a fundamental maxim" that "the creation of debt should always be accompanied with the means of extinguishment", because he had watched the opposite: the Confederation's Congress had borrowed for the war with no power to tax, and by April 1789 its debts sold for 15 to 25 cents on the dollar.
(b) Laura's rule goes one step further than Hamilton's: she does not make a promise alongside the money for it, she owns the money first and states the figure second (IPS l.15, "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"), so her figure never spends a day in the gap where the Confederation's promises lost most of their price.
(c) The parallel breaks on what "the means" were: the 1790 Act pledged "the faith of the United States" to provide "hereafter" whatever more money was needed, a promise backed by taxes not yet collected, which a government can make because it can tax; Laura's future income is a writer's and arrives "later and smaller" in the same recession that cuts co-sponsors' budgets (IPS l.9), so it can back nothing she states, and owning first is the only form of Hamilton's maxim open to her.
- *The order is the honesty, in Hamilton's own words:* buying the public debt at its market price would be "highly dishonorable to the government, if it were to precede a provision for funding the debt", and "altogether unexceptionable, after that had been made". The same act is honest or not by its place in the sequence. So is "$115,000": a forecast before the 2031 move, a fact after it (NUMBERS 3: no figure is stated before the move).
- **Decision served:** the conceit's rule (Laura states a figure only once it is inked) and IPS l.15's move before the 2031 meetings. **Source:** Hamilton (1790); Act of 4 Aug 1790, s.21; Sylla, Wilson and Wright (2009); Sargent (2012). **Part:** 2.0 (the rule beside the definition) or 3d (the team's sentence before her words). **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows Hamilton funded the debt; almost none knows the maxim, and the turn, that a state may promise first because it can tax later and a writer cannot, is not in the canon.

**Idea 2. Each part of ink's definition has been tested once in American history, and held since the War of 1812.**
(a) Ink is "a dollar figure owned in Treasuries that will pay it on its date", and history has tested each part once: in February 1790 the House rejected, 36 to 13, Madison's motion to pay each security's present owner only its market price and the rest to whoever first held it; in 1933 Congress cancelled the promise to pay certain bonds in gold, and holders were paid the dollar figure instead; in 1979 some bills were paid days late because of a paperwork failure (card Q138).
(b) Each test left standing the part it touched (the owner was paid, the dollar figure was paid, the late bills were paid), which is why a Treasury can count as ink, and why the gold-clause case cannot reach Laura's ten, since each is a fixed dollar figure and the 1933 break was in a promise of gold.
(c) The parallel breaks twice, and both breaks teach: in October 1814 the Treasury failed to pay interest due in Boston, which Congress's researchers call "an unambiguous default event", so the report's span is "since the War of 1812", never "never defaulted"; and five of her ten are iBond funds, which "do not seek to return any predetermined amount" (CANON C2) and end on a date their board can move, so the record backs the Treasuries the funds hold, not the funds, which is why the ten carry the plan's own 0.5% margin for fund costs beyond the fee (NUMBERS, Tue 17 Nov evening).
- *Why "owned" is Hamilton's word too:* he rejected discrimination because "the public will pay the sum expressed in the security, to the first holder, or his assignee", so that "the buyer may be safe in the purchase". A promise that pays whoever holds it is what lets a broker hold Treasuries for her (UCC 8-503; card Q157).
- *A date was the later addition:* 1790's debt was "an annuity ... redeemable at the pleasure of the government", with no day the holder could demand the principal. Laura could not have built her ladder from Hamilton's bonds. The option to repay early has since moved: no Treasury has been issued callable since 1985 (Q104-022), but each iBond fund's board may still end its fund early or merge it (CANON C2), the one place in her ladder where someone else holds 1790's "pleasure"; Table 3 row 4 watches it.
- **Decision served:** what counts as ink (WORDS: "owned in Treasuries (directly or through iBonds funds) ... held to that date"); the ten carrying the 0.5% margin. **Source:** Madison (1790) and the First Federal Congress Project; Perry (1935); CRS R44704; card Q138; CANON C2; NUMBERS 2. **Part:** 2.0, one clause after the definition ("ink, because the United States has paid every dollar figure it owed since the War of 1812"); the fund break to 3a. **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A bond judge knows 1979 and 1933 as trivia; nobody has lined them up as tests of an owner, a dollar figure and a date.

**Idea 3. American credit was founded on a restated promise, not on paying the old one in full.**
(a) Hamilton judged in 1790 that paying the old domestic debt at its own 6% "would require the extension of taxation to a degree ... which the true interest of the public creditors forbids", so he asked creditors to swap each $100 for $66.67 of 6% stock and $33.33 that paid nothing until after 1800, budgeted the debt at 4%, and priced the swap on his forecast that rates would fall ("Probabilities are always a rational ground of contract").
(b) This is what happens to a promise made before its money exists: it ends in a restatement, and the credit came only from keeping the smaller promise punctually afterwards, until the 6% stock was retired by 1825 and the whole interest-bearing debt in 1835; Laura's plan is built never to need that second step, because she states only what she owns, and a stated range is neither raised nor lowered (CONCEIT 3b).
(c) The parallel breaks because Hamilton had someone to ask: creditors could weigh his forecast and refuse (though the Act paid refusers, for 1791, only the rate accepters got), while co-sponsors cannot trade with Laura, they can only count her figure or not; with no one to restate to, she must be right the first time, which is why each end is rounded down to the next $5,000 after a 0.5% cut (NUMBERS 3).
- *Hamilton's own test for a restatement:* "no change in the rights of its creditors ought to be attempted without their voluntary consent; and that this consent ought to be voluntary in fact, as well as in name". The plan never needs to meet it.
- *Measured at market prices:* on 31 Dec 1790 the new securities for $100 of loan-office certificates were worth $75, for $100 of unpaid interest $45 (Hall and Sargent, Table 1). Against 1789's 15 to 25 cents, holders gained a great deal; against face, they took what Hall and Sargent call "substantial haircuts".
- **Decision served:** stating nothing before the move and rounding each end down (NUMBERS 3); the plan's refusal to put a forecast inside a stated figure (the 7.5% stays in Table 2's note). **Source:** Hamilton (1790); Act of 4 Aug 1790; Hall and Sargent (2013); Sylla, Wilson and Wright (2009); CRS R44704. **Part:** 3c or 3d, one sentence at most; the correction (never "Hamilton paid in full") binds every part. **Pedigree or aha:** aha for most finance judges, who learned that Hamilton paid the war debt at par; an economic historian will know Hall and Sargent.

**Idea 4. Laura has no record as a funder, so she borrows the oldest one there is.**
(a) Hamilton wrote that what a buyer will pay for a promise is "in a compound ratio to the immediate profit it afforded, and to the chance of the continuance of his profit", and he could watch that chance being priced: in Philadelphia the new 6% stock rose from 70 to 90 cents on the dollar between 9 and 22 December 1790 and reached par by summer 1791.
(b) A co-sponsor values Laura's figure the same way, by its size times the chance she keeps it, but she cannot raise that chance with a record of paying, because she has never funded a residency and has no years to build a record before the 2031 meetings; owning Treasuries lets her borrow the United States' record instead, so the chance behind her figure is the Treasury's, and co-sponsors can count her top at face (the funder test of Mon 16 Nov: co-sponsors count the top).
(c) The parallel breaks because Hamilton's promises traded and the market told him every day how far he was believed, while her figure has no price at all, so she would learn of doubt only when a co-sponsor declines; she cannot correct course on evidence, and has to be believed the first time, which only ownership achieves.
- *Why appearances matter here, in his words:* credit's "Opinion is the soul of it, and this is affected by appearances, as well as realities", and "Delay, by disseminating doubt, would sink the price of stock". Laura's plan does delay stating, to the move, on purpose; the delay is safe for her because nobody relies on her figure before 2031.
- **Decision served:** 3d's one countable figure, the top, with the two dates under it (January 2028 and the move). **Source:** Hamilton (1790); Sylla, Wright and Cowen (2009); Sylla, Wilson and Wright (2009); funder test (`research/panel/funder_test_2026-11-16/`). **Part:** 3d, the team's sentence before her words (never in her words). **Pedigree or aha:** aha. It answers the question a judge will have about a first-time founder ("why should anyone believe her?") with a fact about bonds rather than a claim about her character.

**Idea 5. No court can make the United States pay, so its record is the guarantee, and the paper under the record is its survival.**
(a) In 1935 the Supreme Court held in Perry that Congress "has not been vested with authority to alter or destroy" its bonds and that the 14th Amendment covers "whatever concerns the integrity of the public obligations", yet Perry recovered nothing, and the United States has since withdrawn its consent to be sued on gold-clause bonds at all (31 U.S.C. 5118(c)(1)).
(b) So nothing outside the United States enforces its promise; Hamilton had already said what does, that a government which contracts "exchanges the Character of Legislator for that of a moral Agent", and a Treasury is ink because the United States has chosen to pay every dollar figure it owed since the War of 1812, and has twice written that choice into its highest law; Laura stands where the United States stands, since no co-sponsor could make her pay a figure (card Q99), and she binds herself the one way open to her, by owning the money before she speaks.
(c) The parallel breaks at the paper itself: the same section that says the Union's debt "shall not be questioned" declares the Confederacy's debts "illegal and void", so a promise no court enforces is exactly as good as its issuer's survival; that is the one thing ownership cannot buy, which is why the IPS names a US default as its only premise (l.11, l.17) and the conceit calls it the paper, not a smudge (CONCEIT 3e).
- *Even the one rewrite kept the market:* over 80% of gold-clause holders rolled their money into new Treasury issues, and the cancellation had little effect on Treasury borrowing, though holders "lost money relative to what they had expected to receive" (CRS R44704).
- **Decision served:** IPS l.17's two reasons (a Treasury default is one); 3e's single statement of the premise. **Source:** Perry (1935); 31 U.S.C. 5118; Hamilton (1795); US Const. amend. XIV s.4; CRS R44704. **Part:** 3e, one plain sentence (said once, no safety clause elsewhere, Book 19); 2.0 may borrow "written twice into its highest law". **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for Perry (fixed-income and law judges know it); the pairing of the Union's unquestionable debt with the Confederacy's void one, and the consent statute, are niche.

**Idea 6. A rescue, once given, is read as a promise, so Laura's flexibility is never mentioned to co-sponsors.**
(a) Hamilton's 1790 assumption of the states' debts turned state creditors into backers of federal taxes, but, Sargent argues, it also "created unwarranted expectations about future federal bailouts": when eight states and Florida defaulted in 1841-42, British and Dutch creditors holding 70% of that debt argued that a federal guarantee, "while not explicit, had been implied", Congress refused, and the refusal cost the federal government its own credit in Europe in 1842.
(b) Laura's flexibility may cover "a co-sponsor falling short" (IPS l.15); if she told co-sponsors so, she would create the 1840s' implied guarantee, so the plan keeps that half of the shares pencil and unstated (CONCEIT 3b; anything she later gives from it is her separate choice, CANON D12), and her help, if it comes, is a decision that year, not a precedent.
(c) The parallel breaks because Hamilton's bailout was deliberate and bought something, creditors who would vote for federal taxes, while Laura gains nothing from co-sponsors who expect her to cover them; for her an implied promise is all cost, so the co-sponsor passage names one figure and says nothing about what else she has.
- **Decision served:** half the shares promised to no one (IPS l.15); 3d's one figure. **Source:** Sargent (2012), abstract and s.6.1; Henning and Kessler (2012). **Part:** 3d or 3b, one clause; never in her words. **Pedigree or aha:** niche. A macroeconomist knows Sargent's lecture; nobody has used it to explain why a donor's spare money should stay unmentioned.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 2 with rows 24-27 (2.0, one clause after the definition; the fund break to 3a):** each part of ink's definition (an owner, a dollar figure, a date) has been tested once in American history and kept, which tells a judge in one line why a Treasury counts as ink, and the two breaks (1814; the iBond fund wrapper) show exactly where the plan's own 0.5% margin begins.
2. **Idea 1 with rows 1, 4 and 7 (2.0 or 3d):** Hamilton's maxim joined each promise to its means; a state can promise first because it can tax later, while a writer whose income falls in a recession cannot, so Laura runs the maxim one step further and owns before she states. The judge sees the conceit's rule as the founding rule of American credit, tightened for a private person.
3. **Idea 4 with rows 3 and 5 (3d):** a first-time founder has no record of paying, and no years to build one before 2031, so she borrows the oldest record there is by owning its bonds. It answers "why should a co-sponsor believe her?" with a fact about bonds, not a claim about her character.
