## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KILL.** A date does not keep a rule. Taking the parts away from it does.
  - **Everest.** The rules were set in advance and broken on the day.
    - Hall's was "either 1:00 P.M. or 2:00 P.M. He never declared which", and Hall would "withhold making a final decision until summit day, after assessing the weather and other factors" (Krakauer, 1998).
    - Fischer's applied only to "anybody who wasn't within spitting distance of the summit by two P.M." (Beidleman, in Krakauer, 1998).
    - "Only four individuals adhered to the turnaround time philosophy." Seventeen climbers summited after 2:00 p.m. (Roberto, 2002).
  - **Airlines.** Gates are written before every flight, yet "Approximately 3 percent of unstable approaches or unstable landings result in a go-around" (Flight Safety Foundation, 2017).
  - **What kept rules, in the evidence:**
    - No judgment left for the day: automatic selling "greatly reduced" the disposition effect, reminders did not (Weber and Camerer, 1998; Fischbacher et al., 2017). Aviation's fix is "Minimizing the subjectivity of go-around decision making".
    - A keeper who does not share the goal: Hall waited two hours past his own time for Hansen.
    - A reason the keeper believes: "Pilots do not see current go-around policy criteria as realistic".
    - No public figure to defend yet: one of Kayes's warning signs is having to "face public expectations".
  - **Laura's plan has all four.**
    - Table 3 row 5 lets only the date change, and only for a trading halt or fund prices more than 0.5% off their value.
    - The team places the trade. Laura states her figure after it.
    - IPS l.15 gives the reason: "so whatever figure you choose is money you already own".
    - Nothing is stated before the move (NUMBERS 4).
  - **Replacement:** "The move is kept because nothing is left to decide on the day: the IPS fixed the step, the team places it on the first trading day, and no figure exists yet to defend."
  - **Judge's note:** the turnaround time that business schools teach was never one time, and its exception, "within spitting distance of the summit", switched on exactly where people take the most risk.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Waiting for any reason about prices is the failure. The stronger pull comes on the unkind day.
  - **What the rule guards against.** A turnaround exists for an ordinary bad turn that arrives late. The squall "was nothing extraordinary ... If it had hit two hours later, it's likely that nobody would have died" (Krakauer, 1998).
  - **Who broke it.** The late and the close, not the climbers having an easy day: Hansen after 4:00 p.m., Fischer at 3:40, and Hall waiting for Hansen. The one celebrated turn was Kropp's, on a windless day with the top "no more than sixty minutes above".
  - **The research puts the stronger pull below the goal:**
    - People are "more risk seeking when they are below their goal": 47% chose the risky plan against 24% (Heath et al., 1999).
    - A chance to break even is "especially attractive" after a loss (Thaler and Johnson, 1990).
    - Investors realised gains at 0.148 against losses at 0.098, about 1.5 times as readily (Odean, 1998; Keel arithmetic).
    - So selling half of VT after a rise is the easy sale. The hard day is after a fall, when the top has slipped just under a $5,000 step the team hoped for.
  - **The kind day has its own pull.** Expectations follow past returns but have predicted the wrong sign (Greenwood and Shleifer, 2014); the house money effect; "Several seasons of good weather" (Roberto, 2002).
  - **Replacement:** "Waiting for any reason about prices is the failure a dated move prevents: after a rise, to get more; after a fall, to get back to the step just missed. The second pull is the stronger one."
  - **Judge's note:** investors sell winners about one and a half times as readily as losers, so the move after a rally is the easy trade. The rule earns its keep in the year shares fall.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Rules that left judgment for the day broke. Laura's leaves one narrow exception about market plumbing (a halt, a price off net asset value), never about price direction.
  - **One wording flag for the lead, not a clash.** Table 3 row 5's outer limit says "never past the 2031 meetings". A reader can take that as "before the meetings end".
  - IPS l.15 says "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031", which means before the first meeting. NUMBERS 8r adds: "Only a move at the start of January is before every possible first meeting."
  - Suggest "never past the first 2031 meeting" (row 34).
- **Claim 4: KILL.** Against 1 January, a move on 1 April:
  - raises the mean unrounded top by $236 ($451 by 1 July);
  - leaves the central $115,000 unchanged;
  - states a lower top in 12.0% of house paths (15.7% by July) and a higher one in 16.4% (22.9%).
  - Where VT falls more than 10% between the dates, the top steps down in 56.3% of those paths (65.2%) (NUMBERS 8r).
  - The wait buys about $236 of mean at the cost of a 3-month bet on the one figure co-sponsors hear.
- **Claim 5: KILL.** Everest stays in the store.
  - 3e has its parallel and 3c has the RBA's (ATTACKS D6), and 3f takes none (D16).
  - The sign is opposite: the move banks the top (H2-053).
  - The Everest lesson is contested in the literature (Elmes and Frame, 2008) and by Boukreev's own close.
  - It is a story of deaths beside a gift plan.
  - What can be used is evidence and method, with no story told: the go-around rate and the automatic-selling result, for Table 3 row 5's wording and at most one clause in 3c.
