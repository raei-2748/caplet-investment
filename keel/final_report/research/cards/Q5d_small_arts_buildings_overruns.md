**Claim tested (keep, kill or change):** "For a SMALL arts or residency building (renovation or modest new build, well
under US$10 million), a flexibility of about a fifth to a quarter of the owner's own contribution sits near what the
evidence says such projects overrun at middle odds (P50) and short of high confidence (P80-P90)."

The two shares (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4): the half kept, $21,347, is 18.6% of the $115,000 contribution (the
brief's 18.5%); with the excess, $29,823, it is 25.9% (the brief's 26%).

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** Project cost forecasting, answered by reference class forecasting
(Kahneman and Tversky's "outside view", applied to projects by Flyvbjerg; Q5 rows 4-12). You take the final cost of a
class of similar finished projects against their approved budgets and read off the percentiles. Construction
management adds two warnings, and they decide this claim. First, the percentile depends on the stage at which the
budget was fixed: the investment decision, the grant award or the signed contract. Second, it depends on how "size" is
measured (Jørgensen et al., 2012). For small and renovated buildings the evidence comes from three places:
- public audit (the UK Public Accounts Committee; Taiwan's Legislative Yuan Budget Center and audit offices);
- Nordic public owners' databases (Norway, Denmark);
- construction statistics (Australia, UK).

## Verdicts in one screen

of the 90th percentile in all of them. Whether it reaches the 80th depends on the class and on the stage the budget is
measured from. This is illustration only (CANON A31). The table is Keel arithmetic on the sources' own percentiles.

- **The closest classes are small and renovated buildings measured from an approved budget** (Norway, and England's
  arts lottery buildings). In them, a fifth sits at about the 60th percentile and a quarter at about the 70th to 75th.
  The 80th is about 27% to 38%, so "short of P80-P90" holds. "Near P50" does not: the middle in these classes is 11%
  to 13%, and a fifth is 6 to 7 points above it.
- **The Danish cultural buildings and the Australian buildings measured from the signed contract are more
  reassuring.** There a fifth sits at about the 79th to 84th percentile and a quarter at about the 89th. But only 16 of
  Denmark's 110 projects cost under DKK 10m, and the contract stage comes late.
- **Small is no discount.** Smaller buildings overrun by more in percentage terms, and their tails are fatter: Norway's
  smallest-area quarter has a P90 of 42%, against 19% for the largest quarter. Larger projects overrun more often: 39%
  of Heritage Lottery Fund grants over £2m went over budget, against 17% of all. For renovation the difference lies in
  the tail (P90 52% against 30% for new build), not in the middle (11% against 4%). That middle gap is not significant
  in the regression, and Denmark found "no decisive differences".
- **Laura's stage comes before every baseline.** In 2031 her building is a brief at most (AACE Class 5, Q5 row 3), so
  the same flexibility sits at lower percentiles than the ones in this table.
- **In bad markets the half kept falls below the middle.** At the house model's 1-in-20 low it is $9,881, 8.6% of
  $115,000. That is below the small-building median: about P41 to P46 in Norway's small and renovation classes, with
  only 4 of England's 15 projects overrunning by that little or less. In the years shares fall over 2031-2033, its
  median is $15,423 (13.4%), which sits about at the median (P51 to P53). With the excess added, the 1-in-20 low is
  $16,193 (14.1%) and the median in falling years is $23,201 (20.2%) (NUMBERS 8f; Keel arithmetic).
- **All of this assumes overruns fall on her contribution in proportion.** Arts capital funders usually cap their
  grants and leave overruns to the owner. In England's arts lottery, supplementary grants paid about 35% of the
  overrun. In Taiwan's local venue programme (to 2024), anything above the approved budget was self-funded. If Laura
  pays last, the percentages above are percentages of the whole project, and they become larger shares of her gift
  (Q5 row 33; row 30 below).

**Changed claim (for the store, not prose).** "For a small arts or residency building, new or renovated, a flexibility
of a fifth to a quarter of the owner's contribution covers more than the typical overrun, measured from an approved
budget. It sits at about the 60th to 75th percentile in the closest classes, and at the 79th to 89th in the Danish
cultural buildings and from the signed contract. It covers less than the bad cases: it is short of the 90th
percentile in every class read. This holds only when overruns are shared in proportion, when the budget is already
approved, and in a middle market. At the 1-in-20 low, the half kept alone is below the middle."

**What Caplet takes**
- **3b.** The report may illustrate the overrun use against her flexibility (A31). If it does, the claim it can carry
  is "above the typical overrun, short of the bad ones", naming the class and the stage: small and renovated
  buildings, measured from an approved budget. Never "near the middle", and never "enough". One source suffices:
  Mæhlen et al. (2024).
- **3e.** The assumption "her flexibility: sized as half the shares, not to a cost" can carry one clause of evidence.
  For small buildings, the middle overrun from an approved budget is about 12%, and 1 in 10 overrun by more than about
  40% (Mæhlen et al., 2024). One limit also earns a clause: in 2031, Laura's building has no approved budget.
- **Table 3 row 7.** The row watches the Taiwan dollar and building costs (an index, 1.5% to 5.0% a year). It does not
  watch the project's own budget. The evidence says overruns show up in two places: when a renovation is opened up,
  and when the owner changes the brief (rows 10, 14, 25-27). Question for the lead: should "Carry on while" name the
  building's own budget at each design stage? It would be a watch only, with no trigger (A34).

**Clash.** NUMBERS 8j (line 1000) calls 13% and 17% "Q5's early-stage norms". For small buildings measured from an
approved budget, 13% is about the median (P52) and 17% about P59 (Keel arithmetic on Mæhlen's small class). They are
middle-odds figures, not early-stage or high-confidence ones. This is a label risk, not a number change; the numbers
owner decides. There is no clash with the FINAL IPS or the TNA. The pot-sharing risk stands as recorded (Q5 row 2;
row 29 below).

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. Norway's small and renovated buildings (Mæhlen et al., 2024).**
- What is it? A 2024 study of 215 finished buildings from three Norwegian public owners (2003-2020; 90% under 11,000
  m²). It compares each building's final cost with its budget at the investment decision, by size, type, price per m²
  and distinctiveness.
- Did it work? It gives what Q5 lacked: a published distribution for small and renovated buildings. For the
  smallest-area quarter, the middle overrun is 12% and 1 in 10 overrun by more than 42%. Renovations: 11% and 52%. The
  largest quarter: -1% and 19%. Area is a significant predictor (p<0.01), though the model explains only about a fifth
  of the variation.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her building is small and one-off. It is probably modest in price per m², and it may
  reuse an old building (Q9c). Each of those classes overruns more.
- How is it not? These are Norwegian public owners, with professional estimators and an approved budget. Laura is a
  private founder in Taiwan, and in 2031 she has no approved budget. The paper publishes no P80.
- What Caplet takes: the one source for 3b or 3e if overruns are illustrated. Measured from an approved budget, a fifth
  of her contribution sits at about the 60th percentile and a quarter at about the 70th to 75th for small and renovated
  buildings: above the typical overrun, below the bad ones (Keel arithmetic, row 5).

**Card 2. England's fifteen arts buildings and the Heritage Lottery Fund (Public Accounts Committee, 2004 and 2007).**
- What is it? Parliament's Public Accounts Committee reviewed the fifteen largest Arts Council lottery buildings in
  2004, and the Heritage Lottery Fund's projects in 2007.
- Did it work? The arts buildings overran 16.9% in total, with a median of 11.8%; 6 of the 15 overran by 20% or more.
  ACE raised its programme contingency from 5% to 7%, and supplementary grants met about 35% of the overrun. For the
  Heritage Lottery Fund, 17% of finished projects went over budget, rising to 39% of those with grants over £2m.
- How is Laura's case like it? Arts buildings with mixed funding, where the owner met part of each overrun.
- How is it not? All fifteen were large (grants over £5m), built in the 1990s lottery boom. The Heritage Lottery Fund
  measured from its grant, after its own development stage.
- What Caplet takes: a second class that agrees with Norway's small-building middle (about 12%) and 80th (about 27% to
  29%). It also gives two facts on who pays: funders met about a third of overruns, and bigger projects overrun more
  often, though smaller ones overrun by more (rows 1, 9, 19).

**Card 3. From the signed contract (Love et al., 2013).**
- What is it? 276 Australian projects, measured from contract award, with best-fit probability distributions by type
  and size.
- Did it work? For building projects of A$1m to 10m, 18% exceed a 20% overrun and 12% exceed 25%. Under A$1m, few
  overrun by more than 10%, but the mean is 24.9% with an SD of 64.8, driven by a handful of extreme cases.
- How is Laura's case like it? These are building projects of a residency's likely scale.
- How is it not? By the time a contract is signed the design is done, and Laura's 2031 comes years before that. The
  data are contractor-reported.
- What Caplet takes: the same fifth to a quarter sits at the 80th to 89th percentile only once a contract is signed.
  Any comparison must name the stage, and Laura's stage is the earliest (Q5 row 3).

**Card 4. When the roof comes off (HC 502; Realdania; Taiwan's Control Yuan building, Kinmen and C-LAB).**
- What is it? Evidence from renovations:
  - the Heritage Lottery Fund's "Once you take the roof off a historic building";
  - Norway's renovation tail (P90 52%);
  - Denmark's repairs (15% overran by more than 25%; unforeseen conditions caused 36% of their overrun);
  - Taiwan's Control Yuan monument repair (+22.47%: the trusses could not be assessed until the roof came off);
  - Kinmen's design changes after dismantling surveys;
  - C-LAB's +35.5% after a heritage review added monuments.
- Did it work? The buildings were finished. The cost of the unknown was paid late, by the owner or from reserve funds
  (Taiwan's second reserve fund).
- How is Laura's case like it? If her residency reuses an older building, most of the overrun risk is this kind.
- How is it not? The medians differ little from new build (Norway 11% against 4%, not significant; Denmark "no
  decisive differences"). These are public owners under heritage rules, and there is one small Taiwan case.
- What Caplet takes: the honest line is that renovation changes the tail more than the middle. For the lead (Table 3
  row 7): the building's budget after the opening-up survey is a natural thing to watch, as a watch, never a trigger
  (A34).

**Card 5. The funder caps, the owner pays (HC 253; HC 502; Canadian Heritage, 2024; Taiwan's venue programme).**
- What is it? How arts capital funders treat overruns:
  - England's arts lottery: supplementary grants met about 35% of the overrun.
  - Heritage Lottery Fund: increases went to about half the projects that overran, costing 3% of funding.
  - Canada's Cultural Spaces Fund: top-ups went to 28 projects in 2021-2023, after the pandemic jump in prices, and
    quotes must now be less than six months old.
  - Taiwan's local venue programme: anything over the approved budget is self-funded.
- Did it work? The funders limited their exposure, and the projects went ahead.
- How is Laura's case like it? She is the founder, and co-sponsors will likely give fixed sums, year by year, in NT$
  (card Q11d).
- How is it not? These are public funders with written rules. Laura's co-sponsors are private, and their terms will be
  set in the 2031 talks.
- What Caplet takes: the claim's comparison holds only if overruns are shared in proportion. Otherwise a fifth of her
  gift is a smaller share of the project's cost (row 30; Q5 row 33). Keep this a risk to record, not a sizing.

## (c) What would surprise a judge

- **Small buildings overrun by more, not less.** In Norway's public buildings, 1 in 10 of the smallest overran by more
  than 42%, against 19% for the largest. "It's only a small building" is the wrong comfort.
- **Taiwan's own watchdog overran on its own roof.** The Control Yuan's repair of its national-monument building came
  in 22.47% over its approved budget, because no one could see the state of the trusses until the roof came off.
- **The famous US arts-building study left out every project under $4m,** so its 69% to 82% overruns say nothing about
  a residency.

## (d) Gaps

- No overrun distribution was found for arts buildings under about US$2m. ACE's small grants (£100k-£500k) report scope,
  not overruns, and Love's under-A$1m class has only 14 projects.
- No Taiwan distribution for small venues or residencies. There is one Taiwan case with figures (the Control Yuan) and
  two qualitative audit notes. Audit reports on reusing idle spaces (閒置空間再利用) were not found with figures.
- Mæhlen et al. publish no P80 and no m² cut-offs for the quarters; the fits in row 5 are Keel arithmetic.
- Abstract only: Odeck (2004), Cantarelli et al. (2012), Jørgensen et al. (2012), Jahren and Ashe (1990), Shrestha et
  al. (2013), Wang et al. (2025), Rich (2026), Shrestha and Zeleke (2018). Shehu et al. (2014): search excerpt only.
  Abdul-Rahman et al. (2013), seen only as cited by Saidu and Shakantu (2017), is UNVERIFIED and left out of the table.
- The NAO's 2007 report on the Heritage Lottery Fund was not read: its archived PDF is blocked to crawlers. The Public
  Accounts Committee report on it was read.
- Woronkowicz's later work (Building Better Arts Facilities, Routledge, 2015) was seen only on the publisher's page; no
  size analysis was found. No Creative Australia or state arts-infrastructure evaluation with overrun data was found.
- Who carries overruns between Laura and her co-sponsors remains unknown (as in Q5's gaps).
