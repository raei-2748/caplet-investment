Claims tested:
1. Keep, kill or change: "For a fixed ten-year operating commitment, a dedicated reserve beats an endowment spending
   rule: a spending rule smooths what it pays but cannot promise a sum, and it cuts payouts when markets fall, just
   when the residency and its co-sponsors are weakest."
2. Keep, kill or change: "Endowments that promised too much broke on liquidity, not on returns."
3. Keep, kill or change: "Laura's design is the textbook answer for a finite commitment and the endowment model the

Problem class: paying an institution from a pool of money. Two shapes: a dated, fixed, nominal stream that ends (a

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **CHANGE.** Half of it holds. A spending rule pays a share of a smoothed value, not a sum (Yale: 80% of last year's
   spending plus 20% of 5.25% of the value two years back). Smoothing delays and softens cuts; it does not stop them.
   After 2008-09, Harvard's payout for operations fell from $1,416m by $96m and then $129m (Keel arithmetic: about
   -15.9% over two years). Yale's fell from $1,162.8m to $1,104.2m (-5.0%) and $982.6m (-11.0%), about -15.5% over two
   years. Across US colleges in FY2009, 30% cut spending dollars, by 21% on average; in FY2011, 49% cut their spending
   rate. Endowments also cut 20-22 basis points below their own rules per 10% negative shock. Two changes to the claim:
   "beats" becomes "does a different job" (a rule is built to spend forever and keep purchasing power; a dedicated
   reserve pays named sums on named dates and runs down), and "just when" becomes "one to three years after", which
   matters for Laura because a fall in 2031-32 would reach endowed co-sponsors' grants around 2033-35, the first
   operating years.
2. **CHANGE.** Returns started it; cash needs that did not match the assets decided who got into trouble, and the
   arts case that actually closed broke on running deficits. Harvard (-27.3% return; its general operating account
   fell from $6.58bn to $3.68bn; about $500m in cash to exit swaps on $1.138bn notional; $2.5bn of new debt; more than
   275 jobs cut) did not break; it borrowed, cut and slowed. Brandeis drew its whole $50m credit line in June 2009
   with $82.8m of unfunded commitments and $192.9m locked up; its trustees authorised selling art; none was sold.
   New York City Opera spent its endowment (about $55m "several years" before 2015 to about $4.8m; $24m withdrawn in
   2008-09 with court permission) on deficits that began in 2003, and filed for Chapter 11 on 3 Oct 2013. Better
   claim: in 2008-09 the endowed institutions in most trouble had cash calls (collateral, unfunded commitments,
   running deficits) that their assets could not meet on the day; the fall in returns exposed them.
3. **CHANGE.** The line is real, in law and in textbooks: UPMIFA's first spending factor is "the duration and
   preservation of the endowment fund", and it says a fund of limited duration should spend faster than an endowment
   rate; the CFA curriculum puts endowments in perpetuity and dated payouts under liability-driven investing; Tobin and
   Merton build endowment theory on a perpetual flow. It is not a clean either/or: Dybvig shows a perpetual endowment
   can protect a committed spending level by holding it in the safe asset (a dynamic rule close to CPPI; Laura's
   reserve is static and cash-flow matched, not CPPI). Size of the difference (Keel arithmetic): paying $50,000 a year
   forever at a 5% rule needs about $1,000,000; the ten operating payments need a reserve of $444,545 on 1 Jan 2033
   (NUMBERS 8h) because they end in 2042. The last clause must go: what the residency needs after 2042 is outside the
   case, and her flexibility is hers, not a residency endowment (CANON A30, A31, D12).

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Smoothed rules cut late, and by about a sixth (rows 2-7, 34)
- **What is it?** Most endowments pay a set rate on a moving average of their value; Yale and Stanford blend last
  year's dollars with a rate on an older value. The rule turns a market fall into a slow cut in dollars.
- **Did it work?** It worked at what it is for: no cliff. It did not hold the dollars. Harvard's payout for operations
  fell about 15.9% over FY2010-11 and Yale's about 15.5% (Keel arithmetic from their reports); in FY2009, 30% of US
  colleges cut spending dollars by 21% on average, and in FY2011, 49% still cut their rate.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her endowed co-sponsors, if any, will pay through rules like these, and the IPS
  expects co-sponsors to cut when shares fall (IPS l.9).
- **How is it not?** The ten operating payments are not a rule on a value: each is a Treasury that pays a set amount
  in its year. Taiwan's funders mostly give from yearly budgets (SKELETON 3d), which follow different clocks.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3e: one monitoring line, "endowed co-sponsors' returns in 2031-33 signal their programme
  money in 2033-35", with the Harvard and Yale sizes in a note. 3d: ask endowed co-sponsors for multi-year letters.
  Number: about one sixth over two years, starting one year after the fall.

### Card 2. Sum certain against fixed percentage (rows 2, 27, 8)
- **What is it?** US tax law separates paying "a sum certain", "a stated dollar amount", from paying "a fixed
  percentage of the net fair market value of the trust assets determined annually". An endowment rule is the second
  kind; trustees can also cut below it (20-22 basis points per 10% negative shock, Brown et al. 2014).
- **Did it work?** The law has used the two forms for decades; the annuity form is the one that promises dollars.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The ten operating payments are fixed nominal sums on fixed dates (case; CANON A33).
- **How is it not?** Laura's plan is not a charitable trust; the regulations are a naming aid, not a model.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a: the plain contrast in notes ("a fixed sum, not a share of whatever is there") for the
  writer to put in students' words. No exhibit. Never say "guaranteed" (WORDS).

### Card 3. 2008-09 at Harvard and Brandeis: cash calls, not only returns (rows 12-19)
- **What is it?** Harvard held its operating cash in the endowment's risky pool (general operating account $6,575m
  to $3,683m), paid about $500m in cash to exit swaps whose collateral calls created "liquidity pressures", borrowed
  $2.5bn and cut uncalled commitments by about $3bn. Brandeis drew its whole $50m credit line with $82.8m of unfunded
  commitments and $192.9m locked up, and its trustees authorised selling art.
- **Did it work?** Both survived. Harvard paid with debt (interest about $146m to almost $300m a year), jobs (over
  275) and slower building; Brandeis paid with two years of public dispute over the Rose and no art sold.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Fixed costs (the ten operating payments, a residency's staff) against a pool whose
  value moves.
- **How is it not?** The operating reserve has no borrowing, no derivatives, no capital calls and nothing locked up;
  after the top-up in January 2028 (NUMBERS 2, $38,282) the payments need no new money.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a: the separation of the operating reserve from her flexibility is the lesson Harvard
  wrote down itself in 2012 ("endowment dependence and volatility", "highly fixed cost structure"). 3e: no exhibit;
  one line in assumptions. Use -27.3% for Harvard's return.

### Card 4. The law treats a ten-year fund as its own thing (rows 25, 26, 22)
- **What is it?** UPMIFA lists "the duration and preservation of the endowment fund" first among the spending factors
  and says a fund of limited duration should spend faster than an endowment rate; a gift is presumed permanent unless
  its words limit the term.
- **Did it work?** It is a uniform act that states adopt one by one (Massachusetts enacted it on 2 Jul 2009, effective
  30 Jun 2009, per Brandeis's statements); how many states adopted it was not checked.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The operating reserve is a fund of limited duration: $444,545 on 1 Jan 2033 paying
  $50,000 a year (NUMBERS 8h), 11.2% in the first year (Keel arithmetic), running to $74,123 left by 2042.
- **How is it not?** Laura's money is personal, not a charity's donor fund; analogy only. Taiwan law is out of scope.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a or 3e: the reason the reserve is meant to run down is a recognised principle, not a
  shortcut. 3d note: pledge wording should name the term of any co-sponsor money.

### Card 5. The perpetual-or-finite line is real but not either/or (rows 28-33)
- **What is it?** Tobin and Merton build endowment theory on a perpetual flow; the CFA curriculum files endowments
  under perpetuity and dated payouts under liability-driven investing. Dybvig shows a perpetual fund can still protect
  a committed spending level by holding its value in the safe asset, rebalanced as markets move.
- **Did it work?** As theory, yes; Dybvig's own paper says the rule does worst in up-and-down markets.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The ten operating payments are the committed part, held in Treasuries; her
  flexibility is the part in shares.
- **How is it not?** Laura's reserve is bought once and cash-flow matched, not rebalanced; it is not CPPI and not
  immunised (CANON C12, A33; EXPECTATIONS 2.2). And it ends in 2042.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3e: the scope line that the plan funds ten years by design; the size of the alternative
  (about $1,000,000 for $50,000 a year forever at 5%, Keel arithmetic) only if it earns its place. Never offer her
  flexibility as a residency endowment.

### Card 6. New York City Opera: an endowment spent on running deficits (row 23)
- **What is it?** An arts institution with a deficit from 2003 drew its endowment down from about $55m to about
  $4.8m, including $24m in 2008-09 with court permission, and filed for Chapter 11 on 3 Oct 2013; a $7m emergency
  appeal raised $286,440.
- **Did it work?** No. The draws bought a few seasons; the 2008 fall ($14m lost) and falling gifts ended it.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A cultural institution with fixed yearly costs, a building and co-sponsors.
- **How is it not?** It broke on deficits, not on a spending rule or on liquidity; an opera house is far costlier than
  a residency.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d note: co-sponsors are asked for programme money from 2033 because the ten operating
  payments cover a fixed part only; a last-minute appeal is the weakest way to fund a gap. No exhibit.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE.**
- What holds: a spending rule cannot promise a sum. It pays a rate on a smoothed value (rows 1-2), and trustees cut
  below it after losses (row 8). The dedicated reserve pays fixed sums on fixed dates (row 27).
- What changes: "beats" is the wrong frame. The two tools do different jobs: the rule spreads a perpetual fund's
  return across generations (row 2, Yale's "intergenerational neutrality"); the reserve pays a dated, finite list
  (rows 25, 31). For Laura's ten fixed nominal payments the reserve is the right tool; for a residency's open-ended
  life a rule is.
- What changes in timing: cuts come one to three years after the fall, not at once. Harvard -$96m and -$129m in FY2010
  and FY2011 from $1,416m (row 4); Yale -5.0% and -11.0% (row 3); 30% of colleges cut dollars by 21% in FY2009 (row 5);
  49% cut rates in FY2011 (row 7). For Laura: a fall in 2031-32 would reach endowed co-sponsors' grants around
  2033-35, after her facility contribution is paid and while the ten operating payments run (row 34).
- Size of the cut to quote, with source: about one sixth of payouts over two years at Harvard and Yale.

**Claim 2: CHANGE.**
- Harvard: return -27.3% (row 12); operating cash in the risky pool (row 13); swap collateral and about $500m to exit
  (row 14); $2.5bn borrowed (row 15); $3bn of commitments cut (row 16). It did not break.
- Brandeis: -21.9% on investments, full $50m line drawn, $82.8m unfunded, $192.9m locked up, $39.0m to cover
  underwater funds (rows 18-19); art sale authorised, none sold (row 20). It did not break.
- New York City Opera did break, and the cause was a deficit since 2003 paid from capital, with the 2008 fall and
  falling gifts on top (row 23). JEHT closed on fraud, not markets (row 24, unverified).
- Better claim: in 2008-09 the endowed institutions in most trouble had cash calls their assets could not meet on the
  day; returns exposed them. The one that closed spent capital on running costs. Neither is "promised too much" in the
  claim's sense: they owed collateral, capital calls and running costs, not fixed grants.

**Claim 3: CHANGE.**
- The line is real (rows 25, 28, 29, 31): law, theory and curriculum all split perpetual funds from dated payouts.
- It is not either/or (row 30): Dybvig's protected spending puts a safe floor inside a perpetual fund. That is a
  dynamic, rebalanced rule; Laura's reserve is static and cash-flow matched (row 32). Do not borrow Dybvig's or CPPI's
  name for it.
- "The residency after 2042 will need the second" goes. The case ends the ten payments in 2042; what comes back to
  Laura ($75,298 in 1 Jan 2033 money, NUMBERS 8k) is hers (CANON A30, D12). The usable form for 3e: the plan funds ten
  operating years by design; what the residency does after 2042 is for the residency and its co-sponsors. For 3d:
  co-sponsors' yearly programme money from 2033 is the variable part, and endowed co-sponsors' money follows rules
  that cut late (row 34).
