- **Problem class:** funding a fixed, dated promise of money: when the payer must set money aside, at what assumed rate, in what assets, and who owns the money set aside.
  - **Fields that solved it first:** baseball's labour contract (the Basic Agreement's Article XVI on deferred pay, for contracts signed after 31 Dec 1985); US tax law on deferred pay (the IRS model "rabbi trust", 1992; IRC 409A, 2004), which requires the money set aside to stay the payer's; and bankruptcy law, where a player owed deferred pay is an unsecured creditor.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Keep the rule. Drop the superlative and "early". Say what "funded" means in baseball.
  - **The rule (2022-2026 Basic Agreement, Art. XVI, p. 89).** Contracts signed on or after 30 Sep 2002 "must be fully funded by the Club, in an amount equal to the present value of the total deferred compensation obligation, on or before the second July 1 following the championship season in which the deferred compensation is earned." Contracts of 1986-2002 had until "the third January 1". No unfunded allowance for contracts signed from 11 Dec 2011 (before that, $2,000,000).
  - **What "fully funded" means.** A club must hold, every year, "the current present value of the then outstanding deferred payments, discounted by 5% annually". If the prime rate is 7% or more on the preceding 1 November, the two sides "shall meet and confer" on that rate.
  - **In what.** "In such manner as it elects", provided the money is (a) "exclusively for the uses and purposes of satisfying the deferred compensation obligation(s)", (b) "unencumbered assets comprising cash or cash equivalents and/or registered and unrestricted readily marketable securities", and (c) "subject to the claims of the Club's general creditors". Shares are allowed, and the club tops up if they fall (The Athletic). Clubs certify the funding four times a year.
  - **History.** In the 1997-2001 agreement a club had four calendar years and discounted at prime + 1%, rounded: 9% in 2000. The second-1-July deadline came for contracts signed from 30 Sep 2002. The rule has tightened.
  - **Kill** "the league that knows promises best": no source ranks leagues.
  - **Keep, as:** "Baseball, whose clubs earn new money every season, still makes a club set aside the present value of deferred pay within about two years."
  - **Judge's note:** baseball lets a club back deferred pay with shares because ticket and TV money tops up any shortfall. Laura's payments come from the portfolio alone, and her income falls when shares fall (IPS l.9). So she buys the Treasuries that pay.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** The shape is known. "Funded today" is false for baseball, and the ten years are a tax shape.
  - **Ohtani (Dec 2023).** $700m over ten years. "Of each year's salary, $68 million is deferred with no interest, payable in equal installments each July 1 from 2034-43" (AP). Laura's ten are $50,000 each January, 2033-2042. Each Ohtani payment equals 1,360 of Laura's (Keel arithmetic).
  - **Set aside season by season.** For the 2024 season's $68m, the Dodgers set aside about $46m by July 2026 (The Athletic; $68m / 1.05^8 = $46.0m, Keel arithmetic). The 2033 season's share is due by 1 July 2035, a year after the first payment on 1 July 2034. Laura's ten are bought in January 2027 and finished with the first $39,162 of the January 2028 deposit (NUMBERS headline), five years before her first payment.
  - **Why ten.** No state may tax a non-resident's deferred pay paid in "substantially equal periodic payments ... for ... a period of not less than 10 years" (4 U.S.C. 114). Ohtani's ten "appears to be designed specifically to fit" (Smith & Smith, 2024). Scherzer's 14 equal years were built to "shield much of the money from District of Columbia income tax" (AP, 2015).
  - **Keep, as:** "Ten equal yearly payments starting a decade out is a shape baseball knows". Kill "funded today".
  - **Judge's note:** baseball's largest promise is fully set aside only in July 2035, a year after its first payment. Laura's ten are bought before her first deposit is a year old.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The motive is widely told and never documented. Say what is documented.
  - **Documented (AP, 5 Jan 2000).** Salary "deferred for 12-36 years at an 8 percent annual interest rate". "25 equal payments of $1,193,248.20 each July 1 from 2011 to 2035", $29,831,205 in all. The general manager's reason: "a distraction and a problem we needed to address this offseason".
  - **The Madoff motive.**
    - Reported as fact by ESPN (2012), "The $5.9 million went into a Madoff account", with no source given.
    - Called a "presumed back story" by CBS (2013), whose sources said the Mets "wanted to use the money to pursue other players".
    - "The Mets have never really talked about the deal" (Rovell, 2016).
  - **The trustee's complaint (an allegation).** It says Madoff withdrawals paid "payroll, players' deferred compensation, and stadium operations", and "the estimated returns ... were consistently built into the Mets' cash flow projections and budget analyses" (para 793). A team source called the deferred-pay story "inaccurate". The case settled for $162m on 19 Mar 2012, and the trustee withdrew his willful-blindness claims.
  - **Safe line:** "The Mets priced the deferral at 8% when Treasuries paid under 7%" (row 20). It needs no motive.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, with three prices, and dated from January 2028 for Laura.**
  - **The 8% is exact.** $1,193,248.20 is $5.9m grown at 8% for eleven years and then paid out at 8% over 25 years, exact to the cent (Keel arithmetic).
  - **Three prices for one promise on 3 Jan 2000:**
    - $5.9m owed.
    - About $4.95m by baseball's own funding test that year (9%).
    - About $6.9m-$7.4m to buy the 25 payments in Treasuries that day, with the 20-year at 6.94% and the 30-year at 6.61%: 17%-25% more than was owed (Keel arithmetic).
  - **No default.** The Mets have paid every 1 July since 2011, and the club's income covered the gap. It is a story of cost, not of default.
  - **Laura's honest limit.** In 2027 one piece of her ten rests on an assumed return: the interest cash earns while it waits, priced at today's forwards. The extra Treasuries of January 2028 remove it (NUMBERS 8h). So "the opposite of Bonilla" holds from January 2028.
  - **Judge's note:** the promise looked cheapest at the assumed return, $5.9m. The Treasuries that would pay it cost $6.9m-$7.4m. The gap is what an assumed return hides.
- **Claim 5: KILL "safe because set aside". CHANGE to "set aside, not owned".**
  - **The rule.** The money is "subject to the claims of the Club's general creditors". To defer the player's tax, "it is essential that the amounts are not set aside from the employer's creditors for the exclusive benefit of the employee" (IRS). The player holds "mere unsecured contractual rights" (IRS model trust).
  - **Texas Rangers, 2010.** Alex Rodriguez was owed $24,892,006.61 of deferred pay: the largest unsecured creditor, on the creditors' committee. He objected for fear that former players "may not get the millions".
  - **Los Angeles Dodgers, 2011.** Manny Ramirez topped the 40 largest unsecured claims at $20,992,086. The club filed on 27 June, owing $10.5m of deferred pay on 30 June and a deposit of "more than $18 million to prefund deferred compensation" on 1 July.
  - **How they were paid.** Both sales paid creditors in full. The money came from buyers of the franchise, not from a fund the players owned.
  - **Judge's note:** to keep its tax deferral, money set aside for a player must stay the club's, so a player owed deferred pay stands in line with the club's creditors. Laura's ten are hers, held for her and never the broker's (store Q72-008).
- **Where Laura stands:**
  - Baseball funds a promise later, at an assumed rate, in assets of the club's choosing, and the money stays the club's.
  - Laura buys the payments first, in Treasuries she owns, at the rates they pay.
  - Use the parallel for its dissimilarity, never as "the sports world does what Laura does".

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 20 (3a): three prices for Bonilla's one promise.** $5.9m owed, about $5.0m by baseball's own test, about $6.9m-$7.4m in Treasuries. A judge sees in one line why Laura's ten are priced at the Treasuries and not at a hoped-for return. Date Laura's side from January 2028 (row 33).
2. **Row 4 (3a, 2.3): baseball lets shares back a promise because a club has income to top it up.** Laura's payments come from the portfolio alone, and her income falls when shares fall (IPS l.9). This turns the parallel into an argument about her.
3. **Row 10 (3.0, 3a): Ohtani's ten are fully set aside in July 2035, a year after the first payment.** Laura's are bought in January 2027 and finished in January 2028, five years before hers. Certainty has dates.
4. **Rows 5-6 with 26-28 (3a, the conceit): set aside, not owned.** Tax law requires a player's deferred pay to stay the club's. In the Rangers' and Dodgers' bankruptcies, the players owed it were unsecured creditors, paid only because buyers bought the clubs. Laura's ten are hers.
5. **Row 25 (3a, 2.3): the Dodgers filed four days before the 1 July funding date.** A promise paid from future income becomes a cash call in the year income falls. Laura's only payment still to come in is the 2028 deposit, which the IPS already names as a reason to change the plan.

## The five questions for the strongest rows

- **A. Baseball's funding rule (rows 1-7).**
  - **What it is:** a labour-contract rule. A club must hold the present value of deferred pay at 5%, by the second 1 July after the season, in cash or marketable securities, certified four times a year.
  - **Did it work:** every deferred-pay player in the two club bankruptcies found was paid. A-Rod's $24.9m was paid through the Rangers' sale. The Dodgers' plan paid "all creditors" in full. But the funding deadline itself was one of the cash calls that pushed the Dodgers to file (Ingram, 2011).
  - **How Laura is like it:** a fixed, dated promise, set aside long before it is paid.
  - **How she is not:** the club funds later, at an assumed rate, in assets of its choosing, and the money stays its own. Laura buys the payments in Treasuries she owns, at the rates they pay.
  - **What Caplet takes:** one sentence in 3a. Even a business with new income every season must set deferred pay aside; Laura, with none, buys hers first.
- **B. Bonilla (rows 18-24).**
  - **What it is:** $5.9m turned into 25 payments of $1,193,248.20 from 2011 to 2035, exactly 8% a year.
  - **Did it work:** the payments are made, from club income. Against Treasuries the promise cost $1.0m-$1.5m more than the club owed that day.
  - **How Laura is like it:** a long fixed promise made years before the first payment.
  - **How she is not:** her price is the Treasuries' price, not an assumed 8%. No income stands behind her.
  - **What Caplet takes:** the three prices, never the Madoff motive as fact.
- **C. Ohtani (rows 8-12, 15).**
  - **What it is:** ten equal yearly payments of $68m, 2034-2043, agreed in 2023 and set aside season by season to 2035.
  - **Did it work:** too early to say; the first payment is in 2034.
  - **How Laura is like it:** the same shape, almost the same years.
  - **How she is not:** his ten are a tax shape (4 U.S.C. 114) and are set aside later. Hers are the case's commitment and are bought first.
  - **What Caplet takes:** the shape and the dates, with the tax reason said once or not at all.
- **D. The Dodgers' cash call (rows 25-27).**
  - **What it is:** a club that owed $10.5m of deferred pay on 30 June and an $18.7m deposit on 1 July 2011 filed for bankruptcy on 27 June.
  - **Did it work:** creditors were paid in full after a $2bn sale.
  - **How Laura is like it:** her only payment still to come in is the January 2028 deposit.
  - **How she is not:** the ten are bought with money she already holds. Nothing is owed in on a fixed date except that deposit, and the IPS names its loss as a reason to change the plan.
  - **What Caplet takes:** 2.3's point, that one bad year can shrink income and raise calls on the same date, shown in a ballpark.
