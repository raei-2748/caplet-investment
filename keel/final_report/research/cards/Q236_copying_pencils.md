- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A copying pencil was used where a record had to stand but a pen was impractical; what made it a record was that it could not be rubbed out, not that it was ink."
  2. "A figure can look like pencil and act like ink, or look like ink and act like pencil."
  3. "Copying pencils made a second copy from the first (the letter book), so the copy proved the original."
  4. (Our own) "'Indelible' named one hazard, not all: the mark showed any attempt to rub it out, yet faded in light and ran in water; and which mark was which showed only under a test, never by looking."
- **Problem class:** telling a mark that must stand from one that may change when the two look alike on the same page, and deciding which one counts. **Field that solved it first:** American election and records law (ballot, petition and prescription rules, 1890s-1920s, still in force for prescriptions), with paper conservation for how such marks behave and are found out.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The use is right; the reason needs one correction.
  - **Where a pen was impractical: true.** In the First World War Britain bought "thousands of American copying pencils per week" for officers in the field, "where the nonerasable qualities of indelible pencils made them much more convenient than pen and ink". Railways used them so widely they were called "railroad pencils" (Dube 1998, quoting Petroski 1987). Ohio's Supreme Court noted the "extreme difficulties" of circulating petitions "if it were necessary to use pen and ink" (Thrailkill v. Smyth, 1922).
  - **The law asked what the mark does, and called it ink.** Ohio's constitution required petition names "written in ink". Most were in indelible pencil. The court: "We cannot therefore say as a matter of law that an indelible pencil is not ink." Colorado (Baldwin v. Wade, 1911): "The object of the statute in requiring ... ink, was to prevent erasure", and an indelible pencil mark is "practically as enduring and incapable of erasure ... considering the short time the ballots are to be preserved". A federal rule still in force says paper prescriptions "shall be written with ink or indelible pencil" (21 CFR 1306.05(d)).
  - **The correction: "could not be rubbed out" is too strong.** Dry copying-pencil marks erase fairly well. What resists is the stain: "The indelibility conveyed by dry copying pencil markings is due to the dyeing of the fibers of the paper incurred by the rubbing action of mechanical erasure" (Dube 1998). An early study called the name "only relatively correct" (Mitchell 1935). So what made it a record was that an attempt to change it showed. It was not that the mark could never change.
  - **It was made for copying first.** The 1877 patent's "object" was a pencil whose marks "can be copied" in the letter press (Walpuski). Record-keeping was the use that "ultimately predominated" (Dube).
  - **For the conceit:** this is the definition's own shape. Ink is what Treasuries held for her will pay on its date, whatever the figure looks like on the page (V2.1).
  - **Judge's note:** two American state supreme courts asked whether a pencil could be "ink" and answered by what the mark does, one of them timing permanence against how long the record must last.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, both directions, each with a true fact.**
  - **Looks like pencil, acts like ink.** The patent itself: "On dry paper they leave an impression like that of an ordinary lead-pencil, but more permanent, as the marks cannot be erased with rubber, and on moist or wet paper the marks made by them appear as ink" (Walpuski 1877). Conservators still mistake them for graphite ("this deceptive medium", Dube). In the plan: the bottom, said as one end of "about $95,000 to $115,000", reads like a provisional figure. It is owned from January 2028, when the floor is bought with the 2028 deposit (IPS l.15). With every share at zero it is still paid (NUMBERS 4: the floor pays $102,365, stated $95,000).
  - **Looks like ink, acts like pencil.** Pilot's own manual for its FriXion pens: the ink "becomes colorless" at 60°C or more, and erased colour "may reappear" at about -10°C. "Do not use on official documents, addresses, or anything that should not be erased." In the plan: a fund's market value on a broker's statement is printed like a settled figure but moves until the fund pays. On the move day the building's money shows about $111,700, about $3,300 under the $115,000 she signs for. It passes $115,000 about late August 2031. One IBTM line shows about $138,700, which overstates the building's money by $20,000 to $27,000 (NUMBERS 8y). The figure that holds is the count beside it: what those Treasuries and the distributions already paid hand over by mid-December 2032 if they earn nothing more, about $118,600 that day and never under $117,731 (8y; C11).
  - **And the "indelible" pencil itself could fail.** A 1944 British field manual, as transcribed by a collector: "Messages should always be written in pencil, NOT in ink or indelible pencil, which run in the rain" (primary not read).
  - **Judge's note:** the pencil that looked most like graphite was ink in law, and the pen that looks most like ink is sold with its maker's warning not to use it where a record must stand.
- **Claim 3: KILL as stated; two true relations survive as plain facts.**
  - **The copy did not prove the original.** "A letter-press copy of a letter or telegram is merely secondary evidence" (Anglo-American Packing v. Cannon, 1887). A Georgia court rejected a merchant's own copy book "as evidence of the contents of letters which he had written" when nothing was done to produce the originals (Watkins v. Paine, as quoted there).
  - **One act against two.** A carbon made by "the same stroke of the pen" is a duplicate original. "Letterpress copies are produced by an act distinct from and subsequent to the consummation of the legal act" (International Harvester v. Elfstrom, 1907). Courts split: a North Carolina court noted that a letter-press copy "has also been held" a duplicate original (Liberty Chair v. Crawford, 1927).
  - **The letter book was the sender's own record, not a third party's.** Germany's 1861 commercial code required every merchant to keep books showing "die Lage seines Vermögens" (the state of his assets) and, separately, a copy "(Kopie oder Abdruck)" of every business letter sent, entered by date in a copy book (ADHGB Art. 28). Today's HGB § 238(2) still lists "Kopie, Abdruck, Abschrift".
  - **What maps:** co-sponsors get the same two kinds of record: the broker's own statement of the building's money (what she owns) and her signed letter (what she promises) (C11). The conceit's ink is the first, never the second: owned against signed (aha 8, card Q200). For the conceit itself the letter book is colour only, refused.
  - **Judge's note:** commercial law kept "what I have" and "what I wrote" in two different books in 1861; Laura's co-sponsors get one of each, and only the first is ink.
- **Claim 4: KEEP.**
  - **One hazard, not all.** Methyl violet "exhibits poor lightfastness, is vulnerable to oxidation, and is sensitive to pH shifts" (Dube). Aniline is "extremely light sensitive and water-soluble" (PSAP, Illinois). It ran in the rain (the 1944 manual). So "indelible" meant "an eraser shows", nothing more. The conceit's ink is defined against one hazard too: no fall in share prices can change what Treasuries held for her pay on their dates. It never says a statement value cannot move. A 1-point rise the day after the move takes about $1,400 off the statement and nothing off what it pays (8y). It never says an iBond fund's payout is fixed (TNA l.13). This is new evidence for card Q47's guard: never "ink is permanent".
  - **Found out only by a test.** Conservators meet copying pencil as "an unwelcome surprise during a routine wet treatment wherein copying pencil markings were mistaken for standard graphite". The two marks "can exist indistinguishably side-by-side on the same item" (Dube). Section 2's deletion test is the plan's wet treatment: set every share to zero, and the ten payments and $95,000 stand, while the top and her flexibility go (NUMBERS 4).
  - **Judge's note:** a conservator cannot tell the two pencils apart by looking, only by wetting the page; a reader cannot tell the bottom from the top by looking, only by setting the shares to zero.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 6 (Baldwin v. Wade, 1911): permanence judged against how long the record must last.** Serves 2.0 or 2.2, behind "Held to the end, these pay what they say" (IPS l.13). A court read "ink" by its job and timed it to the record's life, which is how the plan judges ink: on each payment date. It is about ballot marks, not signatures, so it does not pull ink toward "signed".
2. **Row 2 (Walpuski's patent): looks like pencil, acts like ink.** Serves 3c as a fact behind "inked in January 2028", or the team's plain lead-in to 3d. The bottom is said as one end of a range, yet it is owned from January 2028 and survives every share at zero (NUMBERS 4). This is the strongest true fact for claim 2's first direction.
3. **Row 16 (FriXion) with NUMBERS 8y: looks like ink, acts like pencil.** Serves 3d's one clause on the statement and Table 3's statement line (C11). A printed market value is not the figure that holds. Showing the count beside the day's value is the answer, and the maker's own warning makes the point without a printing word.
4. **Row 21 (ADHGB 1861 Art. 28): two books, "what I have" and "what I wrote".** Serves the team's lead-in to 3d (plain, no conceit word). It gives owned against signed (aha 8) a 165-year-old legal shape. Co-sponsors receive one record of each kind, and only the statement of holdings is ink.
5. **Row 3 (Dube: found out only by a wet treatment): which mark is which shows only under a test.** Serves 2.0's deletion test as one clause at most. It explains why Section 2 opens on a test rather than on the holdings: the bottom and the top look alike until the shares are set to zero.

## Gaps

- The 1944 Field Service Pocket Book's wording ("run in the rain"; "cancelled only by a new message") was read only in a collector's transcription. The pamphlet is held by The Army Museum, Halifax (Artefacts Canada 2021.22.20); a scan was not found.
- Mitchell (1917, *The Analyst* 42) and Mitchell (1935), Petroski (1987), Bromley and Shore (1939) and Fairbrass (1984) were read only as Dube quotes them.
- No rule was found that named copying or indelible pencil for bank cheques, ledgers or share registers. Nor was one found for US Post Office forms or British statutes. The Indian postal order rule (row 34) is the only money-document rule found.
- No court decision was found on a contract or a cheque in copying pencil, beyond the wills in card Q114 (LaRue v Lee).
- No source was read at source on when manufacturers stopped making copying pencils or why. Medical texts (Mason and Allen 1941; Owen 2008) are cited by Wikipedia only and were not read.
- The 1874 English patent (CAMEO) was not found.
- Nothing was found on copying pencils in Taiwan's or Japan's official records.
- The Italian Interior Ministry's own explanation of why it uses the matita copiativa was read only through press reports (Wired Italia, Domani), so no row rests on it.
