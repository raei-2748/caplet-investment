- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** writing down in advance which events release a long promise and which do not. Commercial law solved it first through its excuse rules: a market change is the very risk a fixed-price contract exists to carry (UCC 2-615 Official Comment 4; Posner in NIPSCO, 1986; England in Tandrin, 2010). Merger lawyers then built the carve-out list. It was in 18% of contracts in 1993 and in nearly all of them by 2005 (Gilson and Schwartz, as quoted by Quinn; Denis and Macias). Delaware's courts tested it in IBP (2001), Hexion (2008), Akorn (2018) and AB Stable (2020-21).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE. The third sentence is the deal world's standard carve-out. The first two sentences are not a MAC; they are close to its opposite.**
   - **How common the carve-out is.**
     - Economy carve-out: "99+%" of 1,293 US deals of $1 billion or more, 2005 to April 2020. Securities-markets carve-out: more than 80% of deals, 2010-2020 (Subramanian & Petrucci, 2021).
     - Nixon Peabody's 2020 survey, 220 deals: economy 92%, industry 89% (as reported by Law360).
     - The carve-outs became standard quickly. In 1993, 18.33% of contracts had any carve-out (Gilson & Schwartz, as quoted). By 2005, 97.7% did, with 7.2 carve-outs on average (Denis & Macias).
   - **What real clauses say.**
     - Akorn's carve-out names "the economy, credit or financial or capital markets ... including changes in interest or exchange rates, monetary policy or inflation".
     - Morgan Stanley and E*TRADE (20 Feb 2020, on EDGAR) carve out changes in "securities, credit, financial, debt or other capital markets" and "any decline, in and of itself, in the market price".
   - **Why the first two sentences are a different kind of clause.** A MAC is a short, vague general trigger ("material adverse effect") followed by a long list of exceptions that a judge applies after the fact. The base wording averaged about 65 words, while the carve-out wording grew from about 220 to over 600 words (Subramanian & Petrucci). l.17 is a closed list of two events anyone can check, plus one carve-out of ten words.
     - Hexion's split fits exactly: MAE clauses are "strange animals, sui generis", against conditions that are "easily ascertainable objective facts". The two reasons are the second kind: a deposit received by 31 March 2028 or not, and a Treasury payment more than five business days late or not (Table 3 rows 1-2).
   - **The line l.17 draws is not a MAC's line.** A MAC splits market-wide risk (the buyer's) from company-specific risk (the seller's; Akorn). A Treasury default is the most market-wide event there is, yet it is on l.17's list. So l.17 does not separate the market from one company. It separates "the plan's own instruments fail to pay" from "the numbers move".
   - **Judge's note:** more than 99 in 100 large merger agreements say that a change in the economy is not a reason to walk away, and l.17's last sentence says it in ten words. The IPS then goes one step stricter. A merger agreement leaves a vague trigger for a judge to read; the IPS names the only two events that count, and both can be checked by anyone from outside.
2. **Claim 2: KEEP, with dated wording.**
   - Hexion (29 Sep 2008): "Many commentators have noted that Delaware courts have never found a material adverse effect to have occurred in the context of a merger agreement. This is not a coincidence." Huntsman's first-half 2008 EBITDA was down 19.9% and forecast to fall 32%, and the court still found no MAE.
   - Akorn (1 Oct 2018, affirmed 7 Dec 2018) was the first. Akorn's 2017 EBITDA fell 86% and its revenue 25%, and serious regulatory failures came to light; the court called it "a dramatic, unexpected, and company-specific downturn".
   - Later courts said no again: Channel Medsystems (2019), AB Stable (2020, affirmed 2021) and Snow Phipps (2021, buyer ordered to close; read only in part). This run found no later Delaware finding of an MAE, but it did not read a complete list of 2022-2026 rulings.
   - Safe wording: "Delaware's courts have let a buyer walk away on a material adverse effect once, in Akorn (2018)", with "as far as published accounts show" if the sentence carries the count past 2021.
   - **Judge's note:** IBP's merger clause had no market carve-out at all ("unqualified by such express exclusions"), and the court still read a short-term fall out of it: "A short-term hiccup in earnings should not suffice". The law kept markets out of exits even before lawyers wrote that down.
3. **Claim 3: KEEP for US and English law. CHANGE for Taiwan, where it is the rule with a narrow exception.**
   - **United States.** UCC 2-615 Official Comment 4: "Neither is a rise or a collapse in the market in itself a justification, for that is exactly the type of business risk which business contracts made at fixed prices are intended to cover." Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 261 cmt b, as a court quotes it: "mere market shifts or financial inability do not usually effect discharge". Posner (NIPSCO, 1986): "If such a gamble fails, the result is not force majeure." New York (Route 6 Outparcels, 2011): the 2008 downturn excused nothing.
   - **England.** Tandrin (2010): "a change in economic / market circumstances ... is not regarded as being a force majeure event", even after a "cataclysmic downward spiral of the world's financial markets".
   - **Taiwan, the rule.** The Supreme Court asks whether the change is a "劇變" (a drastic change) beyond "依契約原有效果足以承受之風險範圍" (the risk the contract's original terms can bear). It held that Tesla's 2019 price cut was within that risk (110台上1696, 2021). In another case a High Court called interest-rate moves "通常可預期之經濟情事" (ordinarily foreseeable economic circumstances); the Supreme Court reversed that judgment on other grounds and left the point unreviewed (113台上1911, 2025).
   - **Taiwan, the exception.** A crash can still be argued as a change of circumstances (情事變更):
     - after rebar fell by half in the 2008 crisis, the Supreme Court sent a case back to test foreseeability (106台上970, as read through 陳聰富, 2025);
     - one arbitral tribunal did apply 情事變更 to a renminbi policy shock in a TRF dispute. Its award was set aside on procedure, not merits (113台上924, 2024).
   - That exception matters for the residency's building contracts in Taiwan, never for the ten payments.
4. **Claim 4: KILL. A carve-out without a do-nothing promise failed in the best-known COVID case.**
   - In AB Stable (Del. Ch. 30 Nov 2020; affirmed 8 Dec 2021, a $5.8 billion sale of fifteen hotels), the pandemic fell inside the carve-out for "natural disasters or calamities", so there was no MAE. The seller still lost. It closed two hotels and cut back the other thirteen, which breached its separate promise to run the business in the ordinary course, and the buyer walked away.
   - The Supreme Court: the MAE standard is "much higher and 'analytically distinct'" from that covenant.
   - The plan already holds the second half. Table 3's "Never" column ("Never: a bond or fund sold early"; "Never: a forced sale"; row 6: carry on at "Any level", act on "None") is the do-nothing promise.
   - 3e should name both halves: what never changes the plan (l.17) and what is never done in a fall (Table 3).
   - **Judge's note:** in the most famous COVID merger case the carve-out held and the seller still lost, because it reacted to the crisis. A market carve-out protects only a party that does not react. The plan pairs l.17 with a table that says what is never done in a fall, so it has both halves.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 3 (3e):** "a fall in the market is not one of them" is the carve-out in "99+%" of $1 billion US deals. It turns l.17's last sentence from the team's opinion into standard practice, with a number a judge can check.
2. **Row 20 (3e and Table 3):** in AB Stable the carve-out held and the seller still lost, because it reacted. This makes the case for the "Never" column, the half of the promise a carve-out cannot keep alone.
3. **Row 17 (3e):** a Treasury default is the most market-wide event there is, yet it is on the list. So l.17's line is "the plan's own instruments fail" against "the numbers move", not a MAC's market-against-company line. A judge who reads l.17 as a MAC gets this backwards.
4. **Row 9 (3e and 3c):** the deal world needs a carve-back because markets can hurt a target unevenly. The IPS needs none because the payments hold no shares: with VT worthless, all ten are paid (NUMBERS 8p).
5. **Rows 26 and 28 (3a):** "Neither is a rise or a collapse in the market in itself a justification" (UCC), and "If such a gamble fails, the result is not force majeure" (Posner). The ten payments are a fixed promise, and the plan holds the instruments that keep it, so it never has a gamble to plead.
