# Q7b. Rules with only two written reasons to change: what makes them credible, and does IPS l.17 meet the standard?

- **Date:** Sun 15 Nov 2026, Sydney (sources read 14-15 Nov 2026).
- **Report parts:** 3e (assumptions, limits, what to watch; Table 3 and "why only these two"), 3a (the commitment), 3d (one clause for co-sponsors), Exhibits (Table 3). Most of it is for the store only.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose. Nothing here is for pasting.
- **Builds on card Q7** (Schelling's commitment, soft before 2031, pension journey plans) and does not repeat it. It closes three of Q7's gaps: the IMF 2020 note's item (iv) ("an effective control mechanism"); Persson and Tabellini (their 1997 NBER survey read, the 1990 book still not); Lohmann (1992), now read through four secondary accounts, the AER text still not read. Store rows used: Q3b-034, Q7-031, Q30-029, Q37-016, H3-077, H6-115, Q15-001, Q15-040, H5-076, H2-052. Card Q34 rows 29 and 34 already hold the two definitions as interpretation, not new triggers; this card does not re-argue them.
- **The text tested.** IPS l.17: "From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve. There are only 2 possible reasons we would change our plan – a missed deposit or a Treasury default. A fall in the market is not one of them." With Table 3 (`keel/final_report/exhibits/tables.md`): reason 1 = "Nothing received by 31 March 2028"; reason 2 = "a payment over five business days late"; columns "Who", "Changes" and "Never"; five more rows to watch that "may change a date or an instrument, never the plan".
- **Claims tested (each to KEEP, KILL or CHANGE):**
  1. "Ulysses contracts (Elster 1979, 2000; psychiatric advance directives and the law on them) are the right family for 'only two reasons to change', but Laura is bound by the assets she buys, not by the sentence." Tested on what makes a precommitment work: the cost of reversal, publicity, specific triggers, and who may invoke them.
  2. "Rules beat discretion when the escape clauses are few, narrow, verifiable and set in advance."
  3. "An escape clause that is too wide or too often used destroys the rule's credibility."
  4. "The plan's two reasons are the only two flows the plan does not own (the 2028 deposit and the Treasury's own payments), and a market fall is excluded; the literature supports excluding market moves from escape clauses."
- **Problem class:** a rule with escape clauses (a commitment that names in advance the only events that release it). **Field that solved it first:** in practice, the classical gold standard's convention of suspending convertibility in a named emergency and returning at the old parity (Giovannini 1993; Bordo and Kydland, card Q7). In theory, monetary economics: Flood and Isard (1989) built the first escape-clause rule, which Persson and Tabellini (1990) named. In design rules, fiscal-rule practice (IMF 2018, 2020). For self-binding by one person, philosophy (Elster 1979) and, since 2008 in Dutch law, psychiatry (self-binding directives, research only here).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The family is right for the rule's self-binding half. "Bound by the assets" is wrong before 2031.
   - Elster's test, read through Hubin (1986), excludes any act after which "the new feasible set includes the old one" (criterion ii). A T-bond or ETF that Laura can sell on any trading day leaves her old options open, less a sale's cost and the day's price (row 27). So before 2031 neither the assets nor the sentence binds in the strict sense.
   - What the Treasuries do is make keeping the plan the default. Held to the end, they pay without any later decision (IPS l.13, CANON A8). Leaving needs an act, and doing nothing carries the plan. This is the inertia mechanism: one firm's 401(k) participation among new hires went from 37% to 86% under a default (Madrian and Shea, row 29).
   - The sentence is a bright line with two exceptions anyone can check. That is Bénabou and Tirole's contingent rule, which holds only "when ... excuses" can be verified (row 28).
   - From the 2031 meetings, co-sponsors make breaking it costly (Schelling's audience, Q7 rows 2-3).
   - The four tests in the brief:
     - **Cost of reversal:** low before 2031 (a sale), reputational after.
     - **Publicity:** private before 2031, public after.
     - **Triggers:** specific (Table 3).
     - **Invoker:** "we" in l.17 (the adviser), with Table 3's "Who" column (Laura for the deposit, Team for the payments).
   - Self-binding directives add two design lessons that the plan already meets in form. Activation and termination criteria are written in detail and "assessed reliably", and the directive "cannot be revoked in the circumstances for which [it is] written". The crash is the circumstance l.17 is written for (rows 30-31).
   - Ulysses and every medical directive stay research only. The report has one conceit (pencil and ink, I114), and medicine imagery is banned (I85) (row 42).
2. **Claim 2: CHANGE (keep the core, add two conditions).** The theory supports few, verifiable triggers set in advance:
   - "a rule with an escape clause always dominates pure discretion and, if supply shocks are sufficiently volatile, it also dominates a simple rule" (Persson and Tabellini 1997 on Flood and Isard 1989, row 7);
   - exits "unambiguously identified both by monetary authorities and by the public" (Giovannini 1993, row 9);
   - triggers "outside government's control and preferably defined in quantitative terms" (IMF 2020, row 2).

   The literature adds two conditions the claim leaves out.
   - **(a) A check or a cost on whoever uses the exit.** Without one, "the policymaker is tempted to treat all situations as extraordinary ones" (Flood and Marion 1997, row 8). Lohmann's override has a cost (row 10). The IMF's item (iv) is "an effective control mechanism" (row 1). Before 2031 Laura has neither, which is Q7's "soft before 2031".
   - **(b) Triggers that do not depend on what people expect.** Otherwise escape clauses can be self-fulfilling and "destabilizing" (Obstfeld 1997, row 13). Laura's two triggers are payments that arrive or do not, so (b) is met.
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.** It is true, but half the story.
   - **Too wide or too often used.** In 2003 the Council held the EDPs for France and Germany "in abeyance". The ECB said this "risks undermining the credibility of the institutional framework", and the Court annulled it (row 17). In 2005 the reform loosened "severe economic downturn" to "any negative growth rate, or even a period of positive but very low growth" (row 18). The defence escape clause, built for "exceptional circumstances outside the control of the Member State", reached 19 activations in about 16 months for a continent-wide, multi-year need (row 20). India's clause allows deviations "as the central government may specify" (row 3).
   - **But no clause at all also failed.** Chile's rule had "no defined escape clauses". Its target was changed in 2008, 2009, 2010 (a "de facto" suspension after the earthquake), 2014 and twice in 2020. Debt went from about 4% of GDP (2007) to about 40% (2023). A clause was added in 2022, applicable only from 2026, "to promote credibility" (row 24). Argentina's 1991 convertibility law had no exit and was rewritten in the 2001 crisis (Ley 25.445), which markets read as "desperate or impractical" (row 25).
   - **And frequent use did not destroy credibility where the trigger asks for an explanation, not an exemption.** The Bank of England has written "over 30" open letters since April 2007. The letters are "a communication tool", not a sanction, and the thresholds "do not define a target range" (row 16).
   - **Narrow clauses used rarely held.** The Swiss clause has been used in three episodes in over two decades (UBS 2008, COVID 2020-21, Ukraine 2022), and needs at least 0.5% of the ceiling and a qualified majority. Even it saw its way back stretched: COVID's CHF 26.3bn shortfall now has until 2035, with 2039 possible (rows 21-22).
   - **Evidence quality:** institutional statements and outcomes, not measured causal effects (gap 6).
4. **Claim 4: KEEP the conclusion, CHANGE the reason.**
   - **The two outside flows.** The two reasons are the only two flows the plan does not own (Q3b-034; SKELETON 3e). The fund issuer, the custodian and the X-date are instruments and dates (Table 3: "a date or an instrument may change, never the plan").
   - **What the literature excludes.** It supports excluding ordinary swings by design:
     - the German court: "mere upswings and downswings as part of a cyclical economic trend" fall outside the emergency clause (row 23);
     - the Swiss debt brake's cyclical factor (row 21);
     - Chile's long-term copper price (row 24);
     - the IMF: predictable variation belongs in the indicator's design, and unpredictable risks belong in escape clauses (row 4).
   - **What it keeps as a trigger.** Every fiscal clause read keeps a severe downturn as a trigger, because keeping a fiscal rule in a deep recession is costly (rows 1, 19, 21; Flood and Isard's logic, row 6).
   - **Why Laura can exclude even a crash.** Keeping her rule in a crash costs the payments and the floor nothing: held to the end they pay (IPS l.11, l.13; A8). Market moves change figures (the bottom until January 2028, the top until the 2031 move, then her flexibility), never the payments (Table 3 row 6).
   - **So the exclusion rests on the plan's design, not on a general rule of the field.** Both reasons are exits for when the plan cannot be carried out, not for when it would be painful to keep (row 33).

**Does IPS l.17 (with Table 3) meet the standard?** On design, mostly yes. Against twelve tests drawn from the rows (Keel inference, row 35): seven met, three partly met, two weak.
- **Met (7):**
  - few, set in advance;
  - events anyone can check;
  - ordinary swings absorbed by the design;
  - triggers that do not depend on expectations;
  - each change limited to what its trigger touches (the "Never" column);
  - a way back for reason 1 (a late deposit runs the same order, card Q34);
  - one exit that co-sponsors can be told in the IPS's own words ("barring a US default").
- **Partly met (3):**
  - outside the holder's control (reason 2 yes; reason 1 is Laura's own deposit, but it closes on 31 March 2028, before anyone relies on a figure);
  - who may invoke ("we", plus Table 3's "Who"; Laura owns the money and can override);
  - protection from widening (frozen for the competition, and CANON A34 for the report, but not in Laura's life).
- **Weak before 2031 (2):** a check on whoever invokes, and a cost of reversal. From 2031 both come from the co-sponsors.
- The report states the two weak tests as a limit in 3e and adds no mechanism (CANON A34).

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Exits for impossibility, not for pain (rows 6, 7, 8, 11, 13, 33)
- **What is it?** Escape-clause theory starts from a rule that is costly to keep in a big shock. Flood and Isard (1989) let discretion in above a threshold, "imposing a cost whenever the central bank chooses to exercise discretion". Persson and Tabellini (1997): such a rule "always dominates pure discretion". Its known failure: "the policymaker is tempted to treat all situations as extraordinary ones" (Flood and Marion 1997), and real-world clauses cannot "precisely define the state that triggers intervention" (Mihov and Sibert 2002).
- **Did it work?** As theory, yes, and it shaped every fiscal clause read (rows 19-23). In practice, clauses defined by vague states were stretched: SGP 2005 and the defence clause (rows 18, 20).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** l.17 is a rule with two named exits, set in advance.
- **How is it not?** Her exits are not for a shock that makes keeping the rule painful. They are for the two events after which the plan cannot be carried out as written: no deposit to complete it, or Treasuries that do not pay. A crash makes keeping the rule cost the payments and the floor nothing (A8). So there is nothing to be tempted by and no vague state to argue over.
- **What Caplet takes:** the theory under SKELETON 3e's existing clause, "the only two events that can remove a layer the payments rest on". No new sentence is needed; this card is the reason a professor would accept it. Part 3e.

### Card 2. Carried, not bound, before 2031 (rows 26, 27, 29, 34)
- **What is it?** Elster's conditions for binding oneself (via Hubin 1986). The act must set up an outside causal process. It must not leave a feasible set that "includes the old one". Elster (2000) lists the devices: remove options, add cost, add delay, insulate from knowledge. Madrian and Shea (2001) show what a default does: 37% to 86% participation.
- **Did it work?** Elster's frame is standard in philosophy and in constitutional theory. Madrian and Shea's result has been repeated widely, though CLEAR rates the first study's causal evidence low.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The Treasuries pay through an outside process, maturity, with no later decision (IPS l.13). Buying them in a calm January is easier than holding firm in a crash.
- **How is it not?** She can sell any trading day, so her options are not narrowed (condition ii fails). Before 2031 the plan uses none of Elster's devices strongly.
- **What Caplet takes:** the note becomes "carried by the assets, named by the sentence, bound from 2031 by her word". In 3a, the plain point is that the payments need no later decision, only the absence of one. Never call the plan a binding or a lock before 2031. Parts 3a, 3e.

### Card 3. A bright line with two checkable exceptions (rows 2, 9, 28, 37)
- **What is it?** Bénabou and Tirole (2004): a contingent personal rule, "persevering except under very adverse circumstances", survives only if excuses can be verified. Bright lines make "lapses more salient". Giovannini (1993) says the same of the gold standard: exits "unambiguously identified both by monetary authorities and by the public". The IMF (2020) asks for triggers "preferably defined in quantitative terms".
- **Did it work?** The gold standard's convention held among the core countries before 1914 (Giovannini; Bordo and Kydland, card Q7). Bénabou and Tirole is theory.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The two reasons are a bank record and a Treasury payment record. Table 3 gives each a number (31 March 2028; five business days). "A fall in the market is not one of them" is the bright line, so a sale in a fall would be a visible lapse.
- **How is it not?** Their agent doubts her own willpower. Nothing in the case says Laura does, and the report must not suggest it. The rule is for co-sponsors who need to rely on her, not a self-control aid.
- **What Caplet takes:** keep both numbers as definitions of l.17's words (card Q34), and keep the five-business-day threshold for the same reason Switzerland keeps its 0.5% floor: a holiday slip is not a default. Exhibits (Table 3), 3e.

### Card 4. Ordinary swings belong in the design, not in the exits (rows 4, 21, 23, 24, 38)
- **What is it?** Fiscal rules remove the business cycle by how they measure:
  - Switzerland's cyclical factor;
  - Chile's long-term copper price;
  - the IMF: "an adequate definition of the fiscal indicators" for predictable variation.
  
  They keep escape clauses for what the design cannot absorb. Germany's court in 2023: "mere upswings and downswings" are outside the emergency clause.
- **Did it work?** Switzerland ran no structural deficit from 2003 to the Bundestag paper of February 2024 except in 2022 (Bundestag WD), with three recognised extraordinary episodes. Chile's design held the cycle out, but the rule's targets were changed six times (row 24).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The range rule is her structural measure. A market move changes the bottom (until January 2028), the top (until the 2031 move), then her flexibility, never the payments (Table 3 row 6). That is why l.17 can say a fall is not a reason.
- **How is it not?** Every fiscal rule still keeps a severe downturn as a trigger. Laura's excludes even a crash, which is right only because her payments do not depend on prices (Card 1).
- **What Caplet takes:** one clause in 3e, "figures move inside the design; the plan does not". Keep the risk in row 38 in view: Table 3 row 6's "Changes" must not read as the market changing the plan. Part 3e.

### Card 5. Table 3 is an activation protocol, plus five open letters (rows 1, 15, 16, 23, 36)
- **What is it?** The IMF's five parts of a good clause (events, authority, way back, control, communication). Germany's causal-link test (change only what the emergency touches). The Bank of England's open letters: thresholds that "do not define a target range" and trigger an explanation, "a communication tool", over 30 since 2007.
- **Did it work?** The open-letter regime survived over 30 letters without being seen as broken; King told MPs in 2011 that Bean had expected letters "almost half the time", and "the question is whether the explanations we give are judged to be reasonable or not". Germany's test voided EUR 60bn of misused credit.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Table 3 names each reason's event, its watcher ("Who"), what it changes and what it "Never" changes. Its other five rows say "a date or an instrument may change, never the plan": they ask the team to look and explain.
- **How is it not?** The Bank's target is hit only on average, so letters are frequent. Laura's payments are owned outright, so her signals should rarely fire. No court checks her "Never" column.
- **What Caplet takes:** in 3e, the sentence before Table 3 can say what the table is (for each reason, who watches, what it changes, what it never changes) without new rules. Keep the look-closer heading word for word, so the five rows never read as more reasons (risk, row 36). Parts 3e, Exhibits.

### Card 6. Too wide, too often, or none at all (rows 3, 17, 18, 20, 22, 24, 25)
- **What is it?** Five paths to lost credibility:
  - a reason not on the list used anyway (SGP 2003, held "in abeyance");
  - the clause widened after a breach (SGP 2005: "any negative growth rate");
  - an emergency clause used for a planned need (19 defence activations);
  - a clause the government defines itself (India);
  - no clause at all, so exits are improvised (Chile's six target changes; Argentina's 2001 rewrite).
- **Did it work?** The narrow, rarely used Swiss clause kept its standing, though its repayment stage was stretched to 2035, possibly 2039. The EU's general clause was opened in March 2020 and closed at the end of 2023 on the record.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** l.17 avoids each failure in form: two named events, no clause the adviser can define later, no planned need dressed as an emergency (a building overrun is her flexibility's job), and exits written before any shock.
- **How is it not?** These are governments under voters and treaties. Laura's text is protected from widening only by the competition's freeze and CANON A34, neither of which reaches her life after 2026 (row 40).
- **What Caplet takes:** for 3e and the store, the claim "a rule with exits named in advance is harder to stretch than one with vague exits or none". Never say Laura's rule "cannot" be widened. Part 3e; store.

### Card 7. The one weak test: no check before 2031 (rows 1, 5, 8, 10, 12, 35, 39, 41)
- **What is it?** Every source read puts a check or a cost on whoever holds the exit. The IMF's item (iv) is "an effective control mechanism". Flood and Marion want a "higher political authority" to impose a cost. Lohmann's override has a price. The IMF warns that "self-imposed sanctions are unlikely to be implemented".
- **Did it work?** Where a check existed (Germany's court, the Swiss qualified majority, the UK's 28-day approval), clauses were kept within bounds or misuse was reversed. Where the rule-holder checked itself (European national rules), compliance was about half.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** From the 2031 meetings co-sponsors are her check: an announced range she cannot quietly lower (Q7 row 3). The exit in her own hands (reason 1) closes on 31 March 2028, before anyone relies on her word. After January 2028 the only exit is a US default, which no one controls.
- **How is it not?** Before 2031 no one outside knows the plan, and selling costs little. This is the honest limit. The report can name it, but CANON A34 forbids adding a mechanism.
- **What Caplet takes:** in 3e, a limit stated plainly: before 2031 the plan holds because it runs by itself, not because anyone enforces it. In 3d, the remaining exit named once in the IPS's own words, "barring a US default". Parts 3e, 3d.

## (c) What would surprise a judge

- The economists' main fear about escape clauses, that whoever holds them calls every bad year "extraordinary", cannot arise here. Neither of Laura's reasons is a judgement: each is a payment that arrives or does not (rows 8, 33).
- Chile's rule, often praised for its credibility, had no escape clause at all. Its target was changed six times, and it gets its first written clause only in 2026, "to promote credibility" (row 24).
- The Bank of England has written over 30 letters since 2007 for missing its target and is not seen as broken. Its thresholds call for an explanation, not an exemption. Table 3's five look-closer rows work the same way (rows 16, 36).
- In 2023 Germany's constitutional court voided EUR 60bn because a crisis clause paid for something the crisis did not cause. Table 3's "Never" column writes that test down in advance (rows 23, 36).
- Before 2031 nothing binds the plan in the philosophers' sense. It holds because doing nothing keeps it running (rows 27, 29, 34).
