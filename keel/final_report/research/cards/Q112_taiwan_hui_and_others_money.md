- **Problem class:** a dated payout that rests on many private promisors each keeping a promise (counterparty and enforcement risk in a mutual-credit chain), against a dated payout that rests on money already owned. **Field that met it first:** development economics of rotating savings and credit associations (Geertz 1962; Ardener 1964; Besley, Coate and Loury 1993; for Taiwan, Levenson and Besley 1996), and civil-law codification of custom (Taiwan's Civil Code, obligations book, section 19-1 「合會」, promulgated 21 Apr 1999), with criminology's victim surveys (Jou 2003) for how often the promises broke.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with numbers and two changes.**
  - **Common, yes.** In a 2003 national sample of 534 adults, 68% had joined a hui at some time (Jou 2003, p. 4). Jou's review gives earlier figures: an estimate of about 85% (Wu 1980), about 70% with hui experience (Lin 1985), and the Taiwan Provincial Government's household savings and loan survey at 51.6% of people in 1985 and 46.8% in 1999 (Jou 2003, p. 3; those primaries not read).
  - **The better-off joined most.** Levenson and Besley (1996), on a national household survey: "participation is highest among high-income households", with a role "in funding durables purchases", and roscas "may be an alternative savings device to the formal financial sector" (abstract). Besley and Levenson (1996) tracked durables in Taiwan 1977-1991 and found roscas lower the cost of saving for them (abstract). The DGBAS household survey's 1977-1992 waves carried the data (Kan 2000).
  - **Saved, and also borrowed.** In 2003 the motives were saving 52%, higher interest 19%, a friend's invitation 15% (Jou 2003, p. 5). But early winners are borrowers who bid interest for the pot, so say "saved and borrowed", not only "saved". The uses named in a 2024 newspaper history column: children's schooling, buying property, starting a business (UDN 報時光).
  - **Payout rested on others: KEEP, and it is the law's own wording.** The legislative reason for art. 709-9: 「合會之基礎，係建立在會首之信用與會員間彼此之誠信上」 ("a hui's foundation rests on the organiser's credit and on the members' good faith toward one another"). Before 1999 a member's claim ran legally against the organiser alone: the Supreme Court held in 1974 (63台上1159) that 「會員與會員間並無法律關係之存在」.
  - **Change "were".** Hui are fewer but not gone. CTS (28 Jun 2016): many born in the 1980s never joined; a Nantou mediation committee saw no hui-default cases in the previous five years. TVBS (1 Jun 2026): a Taoyuan organiser vanished on 26 May 2026; victims estimate about 1,000 people and up to NT$1bn (allegations, under police investigation).
  - **Judge's note:** a hui was not a poor household's last resort in Taiwan: participation was highest among high-income households.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE "in waves"; KEEP the Civil Code with exact dates.**
  - **The Code.** Arts 709-1 to 709-9 (section 19-1, 合會) were promulgated on 21 Apr 1999 and came into force on 5 May 2000 (Civil Code amendment history, entry 10; Lee and Li, 1 May 1999). So "added in 1999, in force in 2000". Jou's "2000" and store H4-051's "codified in 2000" are the in-force year.
  - **Why the law was written.** Its stated aim was to write down custom: 「將民間習慣明文化」 (709-1). It also named abuses: fake bids and invented members were 「相當嚴重」, "quite serious" (709-3). It barred companies so that hui would not become businesses concentrating 「鉅額資金」 ("huge sums"), and it aimed 「遏止倒會之風」, "to curb the tide of hui collapses" (709-2).
  - **Repeated collapses, with official alarm; no year-by-year count found.**
    - The Executive Yuan directed the Justice, Economic Affairs and Finance ministries on 3 Feb and 18 Aug 1983 to devise measures against 倒會 (Jou 2003, p. 4; the directives themselves not read).
    - The Investigation Bureau put the average loss per major collapse at about NT$20m in 1997 and NT$30m in 2002 (Jou 2003, p. 4).
    - Dated cases:
      - Kaohsiung, 1951-52: over half of 180-odd members stopped paying after winning (聯合報, 6 Feb 1952).
      - Pingzhen and Luzhou, 1998: an organiser vanished, and about 300 members gathered (UDN photo captions).
      - Hsinchu, Oct 1998: the organisers had borrowed at 24% a year to keep their hui paying (court judgment of 3 Jun 2000).
    - Say "repeatedly, with a cabinet order in 1983 and a 'tide' named in the 1999 law", never a dated wave.
  - **Guard: the famous chain collapses were in mainland China, not Taiwan.** Leqing 1986, Pingtan 1987 and Fu'an 2004 (more than 10,000 registered victims) are all mainland cases (MBA智库). Never cite them as Taiwan's. Never tie them to Laura, who was born in Wuhan (case p. 1).
  - **Judge's note:** Taiwan's legislature wrote "to curb the tide of hui collapses" into the reasons for its 1999 hui articles. The law could share out a shortfall among the members still unpaid. It could not create the missing money.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE (half KILL).**
  - **What the evidence carries.** Many Taiwanese adults have met, in person, the risk l.11 rules out.
    - 39% of the 2003 joiners had had a hui collapse on them, and in Lin's 1985 survey 40% of participants had (Jou 2003). Keel arithmetic: 68% × 39% ≈ 27% of all adults in 2003, and 70% × 40% = 28% in 1985, so about 1 in 4.
    - The losses were concentrated: 60% of collapses fell on 10% of people. More than half of victims let it drop, and 6% went to law (Jou 2003, p. 4).
    - Taiwan's own law defines the hui by exactly that dependence (row 3).
  - **What it does not carry.** "Likely to hear it as the line that sets Laura's promise apart" is untested. Five reasons:
    - (a) No source reports how any Taiwanese funder reads a founder's operating pledge.
    - (b) Co-sponsors are foundations, companies and agencies. Art. 709-2 bars legal persons from hui, so any hui memory is an officer's own.
    - (c) Younger officers mostly never joined (CTS 2016; Penghu District Court 2021).
    - (d) Jou's sample was 534 people, with a 44% response rate and urban-weighted.
    - (e) The "anybody else's money" half of l.11 restates the case's own rule (case l.91-92: teams "may not rely on co-sponsors, grants, program fees, or other outside funding"). So it sets the ten apart from the facility, not Caplet apart from other teams.
  - **Where l.11 goes further than the case.** It also rules out any private promisor's solvency (an insurer, a bank, a fund sponsor), and that is the hui's risk exactly (row 41).
  - **Never** generalise about "Taiwanese culture" or "how Taiwanese hear" anything.
  - **Judge's note:** of l.11's two exclusions, "a share price" is Caplet's own choice; "anybody else's money" is the case's rule, widened by Caplet to every private promisor. A hui is that wider risk, in Taiwan's own legal words.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 3 (3a, 3.0).** Taiwan's own Civil Code says a hui "rests on the organiser's credit and on the members' good faith toward one another". That is the dependence l.11 rules out, in the words of the country where the residency will stand.
2. **Row 37 (3a, aha).** In a hui, the member paid last earns the most and is owed by the most people. In the ladder, the 2042 payment was bought in 2027-28 from the same single issuer as 2033. Waiting is paid for time, never for trusting more people. Keep NUMBERS 2's guard.
3. **Row 11 with row 41 (3a or 3.0, one clause).** About 1 in 4 Taiwanese adults surveyed in 1985 and 2003 had had a hui collapse on them (Keel arithmetic on Lin 1985 and Jou 2003). Give the figure and the law's words, never a claim about "how Taiwanese hear" anything.
4. **Row 15 (store; 3d background).** In Taiwan the better-off joined hui most (Levenson and Besley 1996). The people who sit on funders' boards are the likeliest to know the risk first-hand. This is the only peer-reviewed support for claim 3, and it is 1977-1991 data.
5. **Row 40 with row 36 (the conceit, 3a).** A hui sheet is a dated list of payouts written all in pencil. Laura's ten are the same list in ink. Retire the store's "a hui whose organiser is the US Treasury", which inverts l.11.
