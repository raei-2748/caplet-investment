## Claims tested

1. **"Income from writing, illustration, speaking and licensing falls with share markets, with a lag of about a
   year."** Verdict: **CHANGE.** It fell in both recent recessions, but on two different clocks. 2009 fits the lag:
   arts sole proprietors reported 11.6% less in receipts and 15.0% less profit, the year US shares rose 25.94%.
   2020 does not fit: the closest Census series fell 12.4% the same year shares rose 18.02%. The national accounts
   show no fall at all in 2008-10. Authors' incomes have also slid for 15 years while shares rose: a trend of their
   own, not a share-market link.
2. **"For a fixed-dollar liability, the matching asset is the default-free bond of the same date, so adding issuers
   would add risk to the payments. The portfolio's diversification job is to diversify Laura. Conventional
   diversification lives in VT."** Verdict: **KEEP**, with the professional words and two honest qualifiers.
   Leibowitz (1986): a dedicated bond portfolio is "a least-risk asset" for "nominal-dollar liabilities". CFA
   curriculum: a matched zero-coupon book has "no price risk, no reinvestment risk ... although credit risk remains".
   Qualifier 1: the plan holds coupon bonds and funds, not zeros, so reinvestment risk remains. Qualifier 2: the funds
   pay their net asset value. VT, read at the source: 10,185 stocks for 0.06%.
3. **"For most workers income is bond-like; for a self-employed author, illustrator and speaker it is equity-like."**
   Verdict: **CHANGE** to "less like a bond and more tied to the business cycle". The first half is right: the stock
   part of a career averages under 20% for college workers (Huggett & Kaplan). The second half has no measured figure
   for authors. The theory, read at the source, gives the direction and the size of the effect: in Bodie, Merton &
   Samuelson's own example, risky earnings cut the share of shares in the financial portfolio from 73% to 23%.
4. **The post-submission test (Q1b: "2009 supports it, 2020 does not").** It stands, now on stronger and wider data.
   2009 support now rests on tax returns (IRS) and unemployment (NEA, Q1b row 21). The national accounts (BEA) disagree
   for 2009. 2020 fails on every source.

## (b) Cards: the six strongest ideas

### Card 1. The least-risk asset for a dated promise (rows 29, 34, 39)
- **What is it?** Pension practice since Leibowitz (1986): when the money owed is fixed in dollars and dated, the
  "least-risk asset" is a portfolio whose cash arrives on those dates ("dedication", now "cash flow matching" in the
  CFA curriculum). For a zero-coupon match the curriculum says there is "no price risk, no reinvestment risk ...
  although credit risk remains".
- **Did it work?** It became standard: the CFA Level III curriculum teaches it as the simplest liability-driven
  strategy, with "essentially no underlying assumptions", unlike duration matching.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Ten fixed nominal $50,000 payments on known dates (case p.3), no outside money,
  "a high degree of certainty". The one risk a match keeps is credit risk, so the right issuer is the one with the
  least of it: the FINAL's "barring a US default".
- **How is it not?** Zero-coupon Treasuries (STRIPS) are banned this year. The plan's coupon bonds and iBond funds
  leave some reinvestment risk (instrument duration 7.76 years against 9.94, NUMBERS 1). The funds also pay net asset
  value, not par (NUMBERS 3).
- **What Caplet takes.** Section 2.3's central sentence, with its source: for a dated, fixed-dollar promise the
  textbook asset is the default-free bond of the same date. Spreading across issuers adds the one risk a match keeps.
  Say "matched by date" and "concentrated by issuer on purpose"; never "diversified by date".

### Card 2. A risky career means fewer shares, in numbers (rows 23, 24, 25, 27)
- **What is it?** Bodie, Merton & Samuelson's Table 1 (1992). Same wealth, same appetite for risk. If the career is
  safe, 73% of the financial portfolio goes into shares. If the labour is fixed, 60%. If half the career is risky,
  23%. Viceira (2001) adds that a positive link can push a worker's shares "below the level of retired investors".
  Bodie, Treussard & Willen (2007): people with risky careers, "such as Samuelson's businessmen", may best "start out
  early in life with no stock market exposure".
- **Did it work?** Real business owners behave this way: Heaton & Lucas (2000) find that households with "high and
  variable business income hold less wealth in stocks".
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her income is a business: advances, speaking, licences (case p.2). The IPS calls
  it "a large holding of shares ... you cannot sell". The book is 89.55% Treasuries and 8.84% VT at cost.
- **How is it not?** These are models of a whole life's saving, with a target risk level. Laura's plan has a second,
  separate reason for Treasuries (the ten dated payments), so the career is not the only driver of the 89.55%.
- **What Caplet takes.** The quantified hedge argument the co-sponsor judge asked for, framed as published
  arithmetic: "in the textbook example, a risky career cuts the share of shares from 73% to 23%". It is not a
  measured beta for Laura.

### Card 3. The tax returns say 2009; the national accounts say no (rows 5-8)
- **What is it?** Two US sources disagree about the same downturn. IRS tax returns: for 2009, arts sole proprietors
  had 11.6% less in receipts and 15.0% less profit, with the same number of returns. 2010 rebounded (+7.0%, profits
  +17.4%). BEA's satellite account: real value added for independent artists and writers rose 6.0% in 2008 and 3.0%
  in 2009. Census nonemployer receipts slowed from +9.4% to +1.8% in 2008.
- **Did it work?** As a test of the career idea, partly. On tax data the dip came the year after the crash, while
  shares were already up 25.94%. That is the IPS's "later and smaller" exactly. On BEA it never came.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The case that matters is a crash the year before a deposit. A 2027 crash would put
  this kind of dip on the 2028 deposit. The plan does not count on that deposit for the ten: the 2027 deposit buys
  them first.
- **How is it not?** The series pool performers with writers and illustrators. One recession is one data point. The
  two sources measure different populations.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3e and the reflection: "on the tax data, 2009 fits; the national accounts do not show
  it". Quote both. Never quote BEA as support.

### Card 4. 2020: the career fell while shares rose (rows 9, 10, 16, 17)
- **What is it?** Census receipts for independent artists, writers and performers fell 12.4% in 2020 (BEA: -18% to
  -25%, by vintage), then rose 24.9% in 2021. US shares returned 18.02% in 2020.
- **Did it work?** As a test of "falls with shares, a year later", no. The fall came with the lockdown, in the same
  months as the share crash, and lasted after shares recovered. Most of it was people stopping work (-9.9% of
  businesses), not lower pay per business (-2.8%).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her events and speaking stopped first (Q1's Authors Guild row: 46.3% lost
  speaking income). Licences dipped and recovered by 2021.
- **How is it not?** A pandemic is not a business cycle, and policy support propped up asset prices.
- **What Caplet takes.** The IPS's "during that same period" holds for the downturn, not for the calendar year. The
  report says "in the same downturn, not always in the same months", which is the Q1b CHANGE, now confirmed on three
  sources.

### Card 5. Where the diversification actually is (rows 37, 38, 28)
- **What is it?** VT, read on its 30 Sep 2026 fact sheet: 10,185 stocks for a 0.06% fee. US 62.4%, Japan 6.0%,
  Taiwan 3.5%. Top ten holdings 23.5%, technology 34.2%.
- **Did it work?** It is the broadest single holding WInS offered, which is why Caplet chose it on 5 Oct over a Taiwan
  fund and gold (EXPERIENCE_RECORD).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Merton: risk analysis "must take into account human capital ... and its
  correlation with other assets", so do not "double up". Laura's career is one concentrated holding. The book
  answers it twice: Treasuries for every promised dollar, and for the unpromised dollars one fund of about 10,000
  companies, not a sector bet.
- **How is it not?** VT is itself 62.4% US and 34.2% technology. It diversifies across companies and countries, not
  away from a world recession. In a recession it falls with the career (Q24 joint stress).
- **What Caplet takes.** One line for 2.3: the book's conventional diversification is VT, quoted with its date. The
  sheet's own "approximately 8,000 holdings" sentence is never quoted.

### Card 6. The long slide: a risk that has nothing to do with shares (rows 11-13)
- **What is it?** Authors Guild: full-time median writing income fell from $25,000 to $17,500 (-30%) between the
  2009 and 2015 surveys, while US shares more than doubled. UK: median writing earnings fell 60% in real terms,
  2005-2021. Only 19% of UK authors lived from writing alone in 2022.
- **Did it work?** As evidence for "career = shares", it cuts the other way: over five or more years authors' pay and
  shares went in opposite directions. As evidence that a writing career is a risky, concentrated holding, it is
  strong.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her income comes from the same industry, and about half of authors' writing
  income now comes from outside books (Authors Guild 2023), as hers does: speaking, licences, teaching (case p.1-2).
- **How is it not?** She is a bestselling author whose books are "read in classrooms around the world" (case p.1),
  the top tail, not the median.
- **What Caplet takes.** A better reason than correlation for the IPS's rule: whatever her career does, the promised
  money must not depend on it. Use in 2.3 and 3e as a second, independent reason. It also keeps the report honest:
  the long-run link with shares is not supported for authors.
