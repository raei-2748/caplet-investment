- **Problem class:** notice and cure periods for promised money: how far ahead a payer is warned, how long after the due date "late" becomes "default", and what is watched in between. **Field that solved it first:** insurance and lending law (the statutory premium grace period; the bond indenture's cure period), then pension funding law (ERISA and the Pension Protection Act's presumptions), private equity (ILPA's capital-call standards) and university gift offices (pledge reminder ladders).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** A month is enough to confirm money already earned. It is not enough to raise money that is not there. And the plan does not need it for the payments.
  - **Against practice, a month is generous.** Wed 1 Dec 2027 to the first trading day, Mon 3 Jan 2028 (NUMBERS 5), is 33 calendar days and 22 trading days (Keel arithmetic). That is about twice ILPA's floor of "at least ten (10) Business Days" for a capital call. It also beats a life-insurance premium notice, sent "at least fifteen and not more than forty-five days" ahead (NY Insurance Law 3211). A pledge reminder goes "a month before a payment is due" (Utah State).
  - **Raising the money takes longer.** The average purchase loan took 36.8 days from application to closing in March 2026 (ICE). A house sale took about 65 days from listing to cash: 35 days on the market (NAR, October 2026) plus contracts that "typically closed in 30 days" (NAR, September 2026) (Keel arithmetic). So a 1 December confirmation is only worth something if it rests on money already earned. The case says it will be: "earnings from publishing advances, speaking engagements, licensing, and other entrepreneurial ventures."
  - **The warning is the 2027 look, not the date.** ILPA wants more than 10 business days when one call is "20-25% or higher" of what an investor still owes. Laura's 2028 deposit is 100% of what she still owes (Keel arithmetic: $150,000 of $150,000), so the earlier look matters more. ILPA pairs its notice with "estimates of quarterly projections on capital calls", and US pension law has underfunded sponsors pay in four quarterly instalments (26 U.S.C. 430(j)). Q15's draft row has the same two speeds: watch 2027's earnings each quarter, then confirm in December.
  - **Judge's note:** a capital call needs notice because the fund must pay a seller on the day. Nothing in Laura's plan is paid from the 2028 money until January 2033, and the ladder survives a 98.4% smaller deposit (NUMBERS 5). The month's warning protects what she says in 2031 and what the deposit buys, not the ten payments.
- **Claim 2: KEEP.** 31 March 2028 is 90 days after 1 January 2028, because 2028 is a leap year (Keel arithmetic).
  - **It sits on banking's own line.** ECB Banking Supervision calls a loan non-performing "if more than 90 days have passed without the borrower paying the agreed instalments".
  - **Gift offices act at the same point.** SFSU considers a write-off for "a payment that is over 90 days overdue". At three months past due a person, not a letter, takes over (Utah State; a 2014 pledge policy for pledges over $10,000).
  - **It is longer than the grace given where money is needed sooner.** A defaulting fund investor has "[ five (5) ] Business Days" (ILPA model LPA). The PBGC waives a missed pension contribution notice if it is paid "by the 30th day". ERISA 101(d) tells members "before the 60th day". A bond's interest is in default after 30 days and its principal on the day.
  - **It is far shorter than write-off clocks.** Penn writes off small pledges "one year after its maturity date". Cal Poly Pomona waits "over two years". Those are book entries. Laura's line decides what the 2028 money buys and when a range can be stated.
  - **Timing.** It also falls about when her late-2027 royalties arrive (Q12 row 8).
  - **Judge's note:** the 31 March line is not a guess. It is the 90-days-past-due line European bank supervisors use to call a loan non-performing.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, as a reason to look closer, never a reason to change the plan.**
  - **Pension law treats silence as the worst case.** With no funding certificate "before the first day of the 10th month", the plan is "conclusively presumed to be less than 60 percent" funded (26 U.S.C. 436(h)(2)). There is an earlier 4th-month step as well (436(h)(3)).
  - **Supervisors warn that late signals come late.** The ECB says some banks lean on "late indicators such as insolvency or bankruptcy and days past due, which are not good at identifying early distress". SAMA lists "reluctance or unwillingness to respond to various communications" as an early signal.
  - **Gift offices keep a silent file open.** Reminders stop only "upon payment or when the donor responds ... that the pledge will not be paid" (Lewis & Clark).
  - **For Laura,** no word by 1 December starts a closer look. Only 31 March settles anything, because the IPS names two reasons and no others (IPS l.17; CANON A34).
  - **Judge's note:** pension law presumes the worst when a certificate is missing. Table 3 should not copy it: a missing confirmation is a question, and only the money, or 31 March, is an answer.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE.** Committed institutions almost never miss a call. That is because missing one is ruinous, and Laura's promise to herself carries no such penalty.
  - **Institutions.** Capital-call defaults are "extremely rare events for institutional LPs" (Fitch). Only two well-known cases exist, both fraud (PitchBook). There were "very few cases" even in 2009 (BCG and IESE).
  - **Why they pay.** ILPA's model lets a manager sell a defaulter's stake at "[ 50 ] %" or have it "forfeit up to 100%". In 2008-2009, investors sold stakes at discounts of "40% to 70%" rather than default (Fitch, citing Cogent).
  - **Individuals.** The rate is "slightly higher" for "high net-worth individuals" (Fitch), and "no systematic data on actual LP defaults exists" (Maurin, Robinson and Strömberg).
  - **People's written gifts.** These lose about 2% to 15% (allowances in six audited statements, Keel arithmetic). In a survey of charities the mean allowance is 5.4% of the goal and the median 2% (NRC 2011).
  - **For Laura.** The deposit stays a stated assumption (Q37 row 16), not a base rate. The likelier bad case is a smaller deposit: in 2009, investors were expected to negotiate cuts of "5 to 15" percent. The plan's order absorbs that, and "most" still goes to the floor fund down to a 49.0% cut (NUMBERS 5).
  - **Judge's note:** investors almost never miss capital calls because a miss can cost them their whole stake. Laura's promise to herself has no such penalty, which is why the report states it as an assumption rather than leaning on the base rate.
- **A correction for the next round.** ERISA 101(d) (29 U.S.C. 1021(d)) is the notice of a missed contribution, 60 days. The "funding-based limitation" notice is 101(j), "within 30 days".

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 30 (Table 3 row 1; 3e).** 31 March 2028 is 90 days after 1 January, and 90 days past due is where European bank supervisors call a loan non-performing. A judge sees a standard line, not a guess.
2. **Row 44 with row 1 (3e; Table 3).** The month's notice is about twice ILPA's floor for a capital call. Yet unlike a fund, the plan pays no one from this money until 2033, so the warning protects the 2031 range and what the money buys, not the payments. That reverses the obvious reading.
3. **Row 3 (3e; Table 3).** ILPA asks for extra notice when one call is 20-25% or more of what an investor still owes. Laura's deposit is 100%, which is why the real watch is 2027's earnings, quarter by quarter, and not the December date.
4. **Rows 36 and 38 (Table 2's note "deposits on time"; 3e).** A loan takes about 37 days and a house sale about 65. A one-month confirmation can confirm money already earned but cannot raise it, so the assumption rests where the case puts it, on 2027's earnings.
5. **Row 23 with row 17 (Table 3; 3e).** Pension law presumes the worst when a certificate is missing, while gift offices keep a silent file open until money or a clear no arrives. Table 3 follows the gift office: no word by 1 December is a question and never a reason (IPS l.17).
