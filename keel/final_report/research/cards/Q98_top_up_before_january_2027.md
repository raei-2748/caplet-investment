## Verdicts in one screen

**What sets the top-up (Keel arithmetic, MODEL).** The ten cost $300.5 more for each basis point lower (register: $300). By tenor of the 6 Nov par curve, the 10-year yield carries 66.8% of that, the 20-year 24.0% and the 7-year 16.6%. The 1- to 5-year tenors carry -7.3% together, and bills and the 30-year nothing. So the top-up is a bet on the 7- to 20-year part of the curve, not on the Fed's rate. Every history figure below re-sizes the ten exactly on the 6 Nov curve moved by each window's real change at every tenor. Windows overlap, so each figure is a share of windows, never a probability. Since 1962 there are 16,164 windows of 56 days (6 Nov to 1 Jan), about 400 that do not overlap.

  - **The same eight weeks every year.** From the close on or before 6 Nov to the first January close, 1962-2025, the top-up would have been $0 in 25 of 64 years. It would have been over $10,000 in 20 and over $20,000 in 8 (1970, 1980, 1985, 1991, 2000, 2008, 2018, 2023). It passed $38,282 once, in 2008. The median is $3,215.
  - **One week on, it was $0.** At the 13 Nov 2026 close (treasury.gov, read at source), the 10-year was 5.15% (+0.29) and the 20-year 5.52% (+0.23).   - **Judge's note:** the register's $2,388 is a 6 Nov price, not a cost. A quarter-point fall makes it $10,000, and one week later it was $0. January's prices decide it.
- **Claim 2: KEEP for the large top-ups; CHANGE the mechanism and the timing.**
  - **Large top-ups cluster in recessions.** 10.9% of windows start in an NBER recession. They hold 70.2% of the windows with a top-up over $38,282, and 81.4% of those where "most" fails. Starting in a recession, the top-up passed $38,282 in 10.1% of windows, against 0.61% with no recession within 14 months. For $20,000 the figures are 18.0% and 6.5%; for $10,000, 35.5% and 22.1% (Keel arithmetic, MODEL).
  - **But counted by episode, most large falls had no recession.** Of 15 episodes over $38,282 since 1962, 5 started in a recession and 6 had one within 14 months. Since 1990, 2 of 5 did (2008; January 2020, weeks before COVID). The other three (1998, 2011, 2023) came without one. Recessions are long, so they add many overlapping windows.
  - **The mechanism.** Short yields fell in every recession since 1969: the 1-year by 0.81 to 6.74 points from peak to trough. The yields that price the ten mostly did not. On the ten's own key rates the peak-to-trough change was -1.10 (1969-70), +1.32 (1973-75), -0.33 (1980), -3.82 (1981-82), -0.25 (1990-91), -0.07 (2001), -0.34 (2007-09) and -0.46 (2020). Yet every recession since 1969 held an eight-week fall that would have taken the top-up past $20,000. The largest inside each was $38,120, $21,043, $97,019, $103,893, $30,205, $22,411, $65,608 and $20,496.
  - **The timing.** The ten are bought in January 2027; the deposit comes in January 2028, after a year of her income. A 2-point fall during 2027 adds $7,065 to the first part of the deposit; the same fall before January 2027 adds $57,034 (NUMBERS 5; Keel subtraction). So the double hit is mostly closed the day the ten are bought. What remains is a slump whose bond-market fall lands in November-December 2026 and whose income loss lands in 2027. These same weeks were like that in 2000 ($31,225), 2007 ($16,360) and 2008 ($44,993).
  - **Judge's note:** the yield a recession reliably pushes down, the 1-year, carries none of the ten's cost. The yields that do often end a recession almost where they began, but every recession since 1969 had an eight-week plunge in them.
- **Claim 3: KEEP the order; CHANGE "nothing changes".**
  - **The order holds.** In every 56-day window since 1962, the full $150,000 covered the top-up and the extra Treasuries. Lower rates make each own rung bigger, so less is missing at zero interest.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 12 (3a or 3e area (2)): the same eight weeks of 2008.** On today's curve the top-up would have been about $45,000 and the shares about $1,300, with the payments whole and the floor bought. It is a real event that shows the IPS's order holding in the worst November-December on record. A judge sees the design tested, not asserted.
2. **Rows 24-25 (3e area (2), claim 2): what a recession does to the yields that price the ten.** The 1-year falls in every recession and carries none of the ten's cost. The 7- to 20-year yields often end a recession almost where they began, yet every recession since 1969 held an eight-week plunge worth over $20,000 of top-up. A smart reader has the mechanism half right; this is the aha.
3. **Row 26 (3e area (2)): buying in January 2027 closes most of the double hit.** A 2-point fall after the purchase adds about $7,000; before it, about $57,000. The rate risk is mostly gone a year before the deposit that her career puts at risk. This is why the order of the IPS (buy first, complete later) matters.
4. **Row 11 (Table 3 row 6 note, or 3e): the same weeks every year since 1962.** These weeks would have cost more than $10,000 of the 2028 deposit in 20 of 64 years and more than $20,000 in 8. It replaces "small" with a dated, checkable scale.
5. **Rows 28-31 (3e area (2), one clause): the price lock.** Pension buy-ins call this gap the time between quote and transaction and lock it with a portfolio of gilts. Laura cannot lock (no money until January 2027, no derivatives), so IPS l.13's completion from her own second deposit does the job of a balancing premium. Borrowing the field's name shows the team knows where its problem was solved first.

## Gaps

- **FRED at source:** only DGS10 was re-read at fred.stlouisfed.org (Sep-Nov 2026; Nov 2008-Jan 2009; Oct 2023-Jan 2024; nine values match the repo copies exactly). The other tenors (DGS6MO-DGS30) are the repo's team download (to 25 Sep 2026), not re-read; 2026 comes from treasury.gov files and the 9-13 Nov TextView rows.
- **Filled tenors:** the 20-year has no data 1 Jan 1987-30 Sep 1993 (filled from the 10- and 30-year changes in proportion to maturity); the 7-year starts July 1969, the 2-year June 1976, the 6-month September 1981. The filled tenors carry 24.0% (20-year) and 16.6% (7-year) of the weight in those years.
- **Non-parallel extra Treasuries:** the top-up is exact for every window, but the extra Treasuries are read off a 0.05-point parallel grid at the window's parallel-equivalent fall; a twist could move them a little.
- **The fund rungs:** the engine models IBTM-IBTR as par bonds at their average maturity; the real funds' prices in a fall were not checked.
- **The joint risk with a smaller deposit:** her income is not observed, so history cannot say how much smaller the deposit would be after a large fall. NUMBERS 5's deposit table covers the arithmetic.
- **Price-lock figures:** no source read gives how far buy-in premiums move between quote and deal, how long the gap usually is, or how US pension risk transfer deals handle it. The SOA's 2022 pension risk transfer report (search hit) was not opened.
- **Recession dating:** NBER lists no peak after February 2020; recent windows' flags are provisional (row 27).
- **Where money left from the first deposit waits in 2027** (row 20): not in NUMBERS or the IPS; the lead should decide the words.
