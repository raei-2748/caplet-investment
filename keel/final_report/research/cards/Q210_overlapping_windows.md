## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Hansen and Hodrick had fifteen quarters; Taiwan has seventeen two-year spans

- (a) In 1980 two economists testing whether forward exchange rates predicted spot rates had 198 weekly observations of 13-week forecasts, about 15 separate quarters; they used every overlapping week anyway, corrected their standard errors for the overlap, and worked out in a footnote that the overlap made their data worth "approximately 30" separate quarters, twice what they had, never 198.
- (b) Laura's Taiwan test has the same shape: 405 two-year windows that start a month apart, so each shares 23 of its 24 months with the next, and they hold only 16 or 17 separate two-year spans; by Hansen and Hodrick's own yardstick (twice) or Richardson and Smith's (about one and a half times) they are worth somewhere between 17 and about 35 separate spans, so the 405 can describe the past but cannot carry "1 in 100", and "1 in 20" is close to the edge of what they can carry.
- (c) It breaks in a way that makes the honest figure smaller still: Hansen and Hodrick estimated an average, where overlap helps most, while a 1-in-20 rise is a rare event, and the 20 to 22 windows above 28% come from just four episodes (1991-93, 2002-05, 2006-08 and 2019-21), so the honest count for the tail is four events in 35 years, not 20 windows.
- **Decision it serves:** none changes; it sets how 3e states the one limit history shows on her flexibility (the half of the shares promised to no one, IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3e's Taiwan sentence and Table 2's note.
- **Source:** Hansen and Hodrick (1980), pp. 832-833 and the footnote to table 4; Richardson and Smith (1991), section on the benefit of overlapping data; rows 1-3 and 8.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to the finance judge (Hansen-Hodrick standard errors are textbook); the footnote's "worth about 30" and the four episodes are not, and they let the report answer attack 7 with the field's own founding paper.

### Idea 2. A nuclear regulator needs 59 independent runs; history gives Laura 17, which is why no promise rests on it

- (a) When reactor engineers must show that a temperature stays under a safety limit in 95% of accidents with 95% confidence, they use Wilks's 1941 result: run the simulation 59 times, take the worst run, and the worst of 59 independent runs lies above the true 95th percentile 95 times in 100, because 1 - 0.95^59 is about 0.95.
- (b) Taiwan's record gives about 17 separate two-year spans, and the worst of 17 lies above the true 1-in-20 point only about 58 times in 100 (1 - 0.95^17, Keel arithmetic), so "28% at 1 in 20" is roughly a coin toss as an upper bound; the plan can live with that only because no promise rests on it: the ten payments and the figure Laura states are owned in Treasuries, and the Taiwan history touches only her flexibility, which is promised to no one and which the case asks the team not to size as a contingency fund (CANON A31).
- (c) It breaks where the engineers have a choice Laura does not: they can run the code 59 times or 124 times, but history cannot be run again, so where a figure must be certain the plan buys it (ink, in the report's conceit) and where only history is available it says what happened, not what will; the nuclear papers draw the same line, calling Wilks's bound best "for demonstrating compliance" with a limit "rather than estimation".
- **Decision it serves:** the split in IPS l.15: the stated figure is moved into Treasuries before 2031, while half the shares stay unpromised for overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short.
- **Report part:** 3e (the limit and why it is tolerable), one clause.
- **Source:** Porter (2019), section 2 and table 1; Hong and Woo (2012); Jyrkama and Pandey (2017); rows 10-11.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge will not know the 59-run rule, and it turns a weakness (thin history) into the reason for the plan's shape: what cannot be rerun must be owned.

### Idea 3. The hydrologist's rule: an observed extreme is stated with a range, and as a yearly chance, never as a "40-year event"

- (a) When the US Geological Survey is asked how rare a flood was, its 2012 rule says that putting a probability on an observed flood "is associated with more uncertainty" than the usual reverse question, so every such estimate "must be accompanied" by a range, worked from the flood's rank and the record's length: "the largest flood in 47 years" is somewhere between 0.4% and 4% a year.
- (b) The same formula (which reproduces the memo's own figures) puts the worst of about 17 separate two-year spans at somewhere between 1 in 10 and 1 in 94 per span, and the second worst between 1 in 6 and 1 in 23 (66.7% ranges, Keel arithmetic); a resampling of Taiwan's own months gives the 1-in-20 two-year rise a 90% range of about 18% to 36% around the record's 28.0%, so 3e may say "four times since 1991 the same building became 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years", and must not say "1 in 20" as if it were a measured chance.
- (c) It breaks because hydrologists keep one peak per year so that their observations are independent, and they assume a river's climate holds still, while Taiwan's windows overlap by design and its building costs have a trend and a 2021-22 break in the link with the dollar (card Q131 row 31); the British flood guidance adds the plain-reader lesson: give non-specialists the yearly chance, because they hear "a 100-year flood" as a schedule, so the report never turns "1 in 20 two-year stretches" into "a 40-year event".
- **Decision it serves:** how her flexibility's limit is stated to co-sponsors' readers and judges; nothing in the plan moves.
- **Report part:** 3e and Table 3 row 7's note.
- **Source:** USGS Office of Surface Water (2012), Technical Memorandum 2013.01; England et al. (2018/2019), Bulletin 17C, p. 5; Environment Agency (2023), p. 14; rows 12-17.
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche to finance; the aha is that a public agency already wrote the honest sentence for an observed extreme, and it is the professor's sentence.

### Idea 4. Correlations of running sums: salmon scientists' correction, and the "regime flip" that disappears month by month

- (a) Fisheries scientists who correlate smoothed ocean indices with salmon numbers learned that smoothing makes neighbouring years alike, so a correlation looks far stronger than its sample can support; they shrink the sample to an "effective" size from both series' autocorrelations before testing (Pyper and Peterman's modified Chelton method), and Valkanov showed the same for overlapping returns in finance, where the usual t-statistics "do not converge".
- (b) Applied to the store's +0.42 (two-year joint move against world shares since 2001, 286 overlapping windows), the effective sample is about 24, the 95% band runs from about 0.0 to 0.7, the twelve-span versions range from -0.12 to +0.64, and a share series with no link to Taiwan at all produces correlations between -0.41 and +0.42 (95%), so the observed figure sits on the edge of noise; month by month, with no overlap, 309 months give a cleaner answer: a stronger Taiwan dollar came with rising world shares (+0.31, band 0.20 to 0.40) while building costs moved on their own (+0.09, band -0.02 to 0.20).
- (c) The break teaches the most: the register's "-0.39 before 2001, +0.32 since", which it called "a regime, not a law", sits inside the noise band for two-year windows (about ±0.6 for the earlier 183 windows), and month by month the currency's link with US shares was positive in both eras (+0.15 to 2000, +0.33 since); what monthly figures cannot see is timing, the Taiwan dollar's rebound after a crash while shares are still low (Mar 2009 to Mar 2011, NUMBERS 8j), which is a dated episode to name, not a correlation to print.
- **Decision it serves:** 3e's statement of how the half kept and Taiwan's costs move together (IPS l.15's second use, "a stronger Taiwan dollar").
- **Report part:** 3e, one sentence; Table 2's note if any correlation is printed.
- **Source:** Pyper and Peterman (1998); Barker et al. (2016), supplement S1; Afyouni, Smith and Nichols (2019); Valkanov (1999/2003); Boudoukh, Richardson and Whitelaw (2005/2008); Richardson (1993); rows 19-26.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha, because it is checkable and reverses a register claim: the sign flip that made the link look like a regime is a property of two-year windows, not of Taiwan.

### Idea 5. A dated fact can always be printed; a frequency of days, or a model's 1 in 2,900, cannot

- (a) "On no day between June 2007 and August 2026 did $300,000 buy all ten payments" is a statement about history, and it is false: on Thu 19 Oct 2023 the ten cost $299,293 on the register's engine (NUMBERS 8e); the true sentence names the stretches, which since 2000 were Jan 2000 to Jul 2002, May-Jun 2004, Apr-Aug 2006, Jun-Jul 2007, that one day in 2023, and from 10 Sep 2026 (Keel arithmetic on the register's engine).
- (b) A count of days is exact, but "731 of 6,716 days since 2000 (10.9%)" is not a chance, because yields drift for years, so a day tells you little that the day before did not; most of the 731 come from the single 2000-02 stretch, and the honest unit is the stretch, of which there were five before 2026.
- (c) The model's figure fails the other way: "70 of 200,000 futures, about 1 in 2,900" for the 2028 deposit's "most" rule is precise as arithmetic (about 54 to 86 at 95%), but the register's own row shows the house model draws the triggering fall (1.3 points before 1 January 2027) in 0.02% of futures where history from today's level of yields shows 0.20% in three episodes (Aug 1998, Sep 2001, Dec 2007 to Jan 2008), so the model's tail is about ten times thinner than history, and a 1-in-2,900 is a fact about the model.
- **Decision it serves:** the 2027 Treasury purchase (IPS l.13) and the 2028 completion; the history line in 2.1.
- **Report part:** 2.1 or 3e (the dated stretches); 3e (state the condition for "most", never its model odds).
- **Source:** NUMBERS 8e and section 5 "When 'most' cannot hold" (d); rows 27-28.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree in principle; the corrections are what a judge would catch.
