- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Laura's $1.11 for each $1 is dear against what insurers charge to take a fixed pension promise off a sponsor's books."
  2. "A sponsor that keeps the promise itself (hibernation, cash-flow matching) holds a buffer above the matched value; about 11% is large."

- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** the price of handing a fixed promise to a guarantor, against keeping it yourself. Life-annuity pricing first measured it ("money's worth" studies, Mitchell et al. 1999, carried on by Koijen and Yogo 2015 and Poterba and Solomon 2025); the UK bulk annuity market and the Pension Protection Fund's buy-out basis price it as a yield over gilts; US pension risk transfer prices it as a share of the accounting liability (Milliman's index); US structured-settlement law records it payment by payment in court. Insurance economics names the alternative: self-insurance (Ehrlich and Becker 1972).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Dear on the sticker, cheap in expectation; the gap is who keeps the spread and who carries the risk.**
  - **US bulk price.** Retirees "can be annuitized for an estimated 99.9% of accounting liabilities" (competitive quote), 102.9% at the average insurer, 31 Aug 2026 (Milliman Pension Buyout Index, nine insurers). The liability is valued on an AA corporate curve.
  - **Put on Laura's yardstick.** The Treasury's own pension curve (HQM, AAA to A bonds) stood at 5.86% at 10 years in Sep 2026, against a 10-year Treasury zero rate of about 4.98% that month: about 0.9 points (Keel arithmetic). Valued 0.9 points above the Treasury curve, the ten are worth $275,881 against $301,544 (91.5%). A buy-out at 99.9% to 102.9% of that is about **$0.91 to $0.94 per Treasury dollar** (Keel arithmetic; rough, because HQM includes A-rated bonds, so an AA curve would sit a little lower and the figure a little higher).
  - **UK bulk price.** The Pension Protection Fund's proxy for "insurance company buy-out pricing terms", set after talks with six bulk annuity providers (July 2026): the gilt curve plus 0.6% for pensioners, plus 0.3% for members not yet retired. On Laura's ten that is $284,152 to $292,702, **$0.94 to $0.97 per Treasury dollar** (Keel arithmetic; her payments start in six years, like the not-yet-retired, so $0.97 is the nearer case).
  - **Retail price.** A person buying fixed payments for a set term paid on average 7.0% (10-year, quotes from 1989) and 4.4% (15-year, from 1998) above their Treasury value (Koijen and Yogo, Table 2): **$1.04 to $1.07**. In May 2024, 10-year term annuities yielded 4.06% against 4.58% on Treasuries (Poterba and Solomon).
  - **Laura's price.** $444,545 owned against $399,089 that the payments are worth on 1 Jan 2033, **$1.11** (NUMBERS 8h). At today's forward rates the extra Treasuries come back worth $45,456 in 2033 money; carried at forwards they cost $45,487, so certainty costs **$31 of fund fees** (NUMBERS 8h). In the median of 585 historical windows $43,469 comes back (NUMBERS 8k).
  - **Which way it falls.** Up front Laura pays 14-20 cents more than a bulk insurer and 4-7 cents more than a retail buyer. In expectation at today's rates she pays about $1.00, less than either, because the insurer's price is paid away in every world and her extra 11 cents is a deposit. The insurer is cheaper up front because it backs the promise with corporate bonds and private loans (Hymans 2026) and keeps what they earn above the pensions; Laura backs hers with Treasuries alone.
  - **Judge's note:** an insurer would take Laura's ten for about 91 to 97 cents per Treasury dollar, but only by backing them with corporate debt. She pays $1.11 to back them with Treasuries alone, and at today's rates all but $31 of the extra comes back.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Large for a pension, ordinary for an insurer, and sized by a test rather than chosen.**
  - **Pensions, on a Treasury yardstick.** A US plan enters hibernation at 105%-110% of a liability valued at corporate yields (card Q68 row 1): about 96%-101% of Treasury value (× 0.915, Keel arithmetic). A UK scheme 100% funded on a low dependency basis (gilts + 0.5%) holds about 95% (card Q68 row 36). Boots kept 5% (Q68 row 10). Laura's 111.4% is larger than each.
  - **Insurers.** A US insurer's reserve for term-certain payout annuities, pension risk transfer and structured settlements is, from 2026 (mandatory 2029), the average of its worst 30% of scenarios (CTE70), discounted at what its own assets are projected to earn (NAIC VM-22; WTW). Before VM-22, 53% of US PRT insurers reserved deferred lives at best estimate and 6% at "worst case" (WTW 2024 survey). Capital comes on top: UK annuity writers target 140%-190% of their solvency capital requirement (Hymans 2026).
  - **Why Laura's is larger.** Pensions spend their coupons as they arrive (Q68 row 24); $93,941 of Laura's interest waits up to six years (NUMBERS 2). The 11% is what one test needs (all ten paid if cash waiting earns nothing), not a rule of thumb.
  - **Judge's note:** 11% looks heavy beside a pension's 5%-10%, until both are measured in Treasuries: a hibernating plan's 105%-110% on corporate yields is about 96%-101% on Laura's yardstick.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with the numbers. The market for fixed payments is a ladder of prices, and the Treasury price is its fair middle.**
  - **Lottery: the Treasury price, no spread.** The cash value "is the amount of money required to be in the jackpot prize pool ... to fund the estimated jackpot annuity prize" (Powerball); the annuity factor is "determined by the best total securities price obtained through a competitive bid of qualified, preapproved brokers" (61 Pa. Code § 871.9).
  - **Insurer: cheaper, because the buyer takes its credit.** One $125,000 payment due 1 Oct 2010 was worth $95,395 at the 3.6% federal rate; Safeco quoted $82,409 for a comparable annuity (86%, about 5.6% a year, Keel arithmetic); a factoring company offered $39,000 (41%), and the court refused it (Ballos, NY Sup. Ct. 2003).
  - **Factoring: the seller's price.** Payees selling fixed payments received 54.0%-59.9% of their federal-rate value (California, 2000-2003), at mean effective rates of 19.5%-20.9%. Even lottery payments, backed by a lottery's own Treasuries, sold at "about two points over comparable Treasurys" (2002 law review quoted in Ballos).
  - **Judge's note:** one fixed payment had three prices: $95,395 at the federal rate, $82,409 from an insurer, $39,000 from a buyer of the payment. Laura is the buyer of certainty, and she buys at the Treasury price.
- **Claim 4: KEEP the plan; CHANGE how the answer is argued. The honest critique stands on its first half only.**
  - **The critique:** "An insurer would have taken the ten for less, and a smaller margin would have stated a bigger range." True on the sticker (claim 1), and true for the range: the standard moves $33,150 of floor payout to the payments' safety, and the central range is $95,000-$115,000 instead of $125,000-$155,000 with no margin (NUMBERS 4).
  - **Best answer 1: both cheaper routes break the IPS's own sentence.** An insurer makes the payments depend on "anybody else's money" (IPS l.11). The smaller margin of Mon 9 Nov leaves the ten $22,192 short if cash waiting earns nothing, and the only money left would be the shares (NUMBERS 2), so payments would "depend on a share price" (l.11).
  - **Best answer 2: it is a deposit that refunds itself.** At forwards all but $31 returns; in the median historical window $43,469 of $45,456; in the 1-in-20 low window $18,053; nothing only in the zero-interest world it was sized for (NUMBERS 8h, 8k). An insurer's markup returns nothing in any world.
  - **Best answer 3: it buys freedom from every future rate,** and the market charges a lot for that freedom: interest-rate risk management is "at least half of the average life annuity markups or eight percentage points" (Verani and Yu, 1989-2019).
  - **Best answer 4: insurer certainty was cheapest when insurers were weakest.** Between November 2008 and February 2009, insurers sold 30-year term annuities 16% and life annuities at 60 19% below Treasury value, because reserve rules let them book "far less than a dollar of reserve per dollar of future insurance liability" (Koijen and Yogo).
  - **Kill nothing.** The evidence does not show the plan over-reserved against its own standard. It shows the price is paid by the range co-sponsors hear in 2031, not by Laura's wealth.
  - **Judge's note:** the 11 cents behaves like a premium that refunds itself in proportion to how kind rates turn out to be. Every price an insurer quotes is paid away.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 10 (with row 3):** 3a and 3b. The like-for-like answer a finance judge expects: about 91-97 cents with an insurer, $1.11 for Laura, about $1.00 in expectation. It turns "dear" into "who keeps the spread".
2. **Row 28:** 3a or 3e. "A premium that refunds itself" is the one image that makes the extra Treasuries feel like her money, not a cost, with real history behind each number.
3. **Row 30:** 3a. Both cheaper routes to certainty break IPS l.11, so the report evaluates the IPS's sentence instead of repeating it: the margin is what that sentence costs.
4. **Row 13:** 3e or 2.3. Certainty bought from insurers was cheapest in November 2008, when they were weakest. It reverses a smart reader's intuition that a lower price means a better deal.
5. **Row 16:** 3a. The market charges annuity buyers about 8 points, never returned, for interest-rate risk alone. Laura's 11% covers the same risk and mostly comes back.
