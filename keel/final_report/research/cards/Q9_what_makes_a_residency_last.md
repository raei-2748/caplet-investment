## Verdicts on the four claims

1. **"Residencies and small arts groups fail from running costs and thin reserves more than from the cost of the
   building." CHANGE.** The first half holds; the comparison is off. The best-known closure, Black Mountain College,
   had no endowment, raised money year by year, built cheaply with its own hands and was ended by debts and unpaid
   salaries (rows 1-3). Set in Stone found that new buildings rarely closed the groups that built them; they added
   running costs that some could not carry, and the groups cut programmes and staff (rows 4-7). Reserves, margins and
   grant dependence predict closure in the academic work (rows 14-15). Better claim: small arts bodies get into trouble
   when fixed running costs outrun unreliable yearly income and there is little or no reserve; a building hurts mainly
   by adding running costs, not through the construction bill.
2. **"A dedicated, multi-year operating reserve is what lets a residency survive bad years." CHANGE.** The residencies
   that have lasted a century (Yaddo, MacDowell) did it on perpetual endowments, mostly in shares, plus founders and
   boards who kept raising money (rows 19-22). Those endowments fall in bad years (MacDowell's fell 15% in 2022) and a
   market fall nearly ended Yaddo before it opened (row 20). Multi-year reserves are rare: the typical US arts group
   holds about 1.5 months (row 13). Better claim: residencies last when their running money does not depend on each
   year's fundraising; the famous ones used endowments that move with markets, while Laura's ten bought payments give
   the same protection for one decade without market risk, and end in 2042.
3. **"Building projects routinely overrun, so money promised to no one is prudent." KEEP, with one condition.**
   Overruns are routine for buildings (Flyvbjerg: about 60% on average; rows 24-25) and in Taiwan's own arts buildings
   (Weiwuying +28.64% and more than 7 years late; Taichung's theatre 37 to 58 months; two music centres extended by
   about 5 and 11 years; rows 26-28). Condition: unpromised money protects only if it stays apart (NFF: capital not
   held apart is spent on deficits, row 10). In Caplet's plan it sits in shares, so it is likely to be smallest when
   a co-sponsor falls short (row 30, Clash: yes, a risk to note).
4. **How Taiwan's residencies are run and funded (answer, plus a CHANGE to any line implying stable public money).**
   The main ones are public: Taipei's two villages are run by a city-backed foundation and were 71% and 86% government
   funded in 2018 (row 32), on public land that can be taken back (Taipei Artist Village moved out in 2023 for urban
   renewal, row 31), with Ministry grants that are yearly and reviewed (store H9-116). Private ones lean on a founder
   (Bamboo Curtain, store H9-117). The national grant-maker's endowment never reached its legal target and its income
   fell 41% in one year (rows 33-34). Sponsors fall short even for a national theatre (row 29).

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. Black Mountain College: an arts community that died of running costs, not its building
- **What is it?** The most famous US experimental arts college (1933-1957). It had no endowment and believed it could
  live "on a year-by-year basis"; it built its own simple Studies Building when the Gropius and Breuer design proved
  unaffordable (rows 1-3).
- **Did it work?** As art, yes; as a body, no. From 1949 it lived on loans secured on its land; debts and unpaid
  salaries ended it, and courts stopped classes in spring 1957 while enrolment fell to 9 (row 2).
- **How is Laura like it?** A founder-led creative community that needs money beyond its founder's (case p.3:
  co-sponsors, grants, program fees).
- **How is she not?** Her ten payments are bought before the first is due; a residency has no fee-paying students to
  lose; nobody borrows against the facility in Caplet's plan.
- **What Caplet takes:** evidence for changing claim 1, and a clean example of the failure path the ladder closes:
  running costs met year to year, then by borrowing.

### Card 2. Reserves before rooms: what Set in Stone and the funders learned
- **What is it?** Set in Stone (more than 700 US arts buildings) plus the funders' own lessons: NFF and Helicon,
  Grantmakers in the Arts, NFF for Kresge (rows 4-11).
- **Did it work?** Buildings rarely closed the groups that built them, but many left running costs "beyond the
  capacity" of their owners; groups cut programmes and staff (Taubman, Spertus). Raising reserves after opening is
  "often an exercise in futility"; funders now put reserves inside the campaign (COCA added $3.9M).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her money goes to running costs and a building, and the case puts the running money first
  ("before making the first operating payment or contributing to the facility", case p.3).
- **How is she not?** She gives money; she does not own or run the building. The facility's cost "has not been
  determined" (case p.4), and the residency is small.
- **What Caplet takes:** the order of the plan (ladder first, then floor and shares) is the order this field
  recommends. For co-sponsors: the part of a budget donors fund last is the part Laura has already bought.

### Card 3. How Yaddo and MacDowell have lasted a century
- **What is it?** Two of the oldest US residencies: Yaddo (corporation 1900, guests from 1926) and MacDowell (1907)
  (rows 19-22).
- **Did it work?** Yes, on large endowments mostly in shares (Yaddo $39.3M, 65% shares, paying about 57% of costs)
  and on founders who kept raising money (Marian MacDowell's recitals until 1956). Markets also hurt them: the Trasks'
  investments fell in 1910 and the mansion was closed in 1916 to save the endowment; MacDowell's endowment fell 15% in
  2022 while it ran a $3.4M gap before investment return.
- **How is Laura like it?** She is the founder whose name and continuing work will draw co-sponsors.
- **How is she not?** Her money is dated (ten payments, 2033-2042), not perpetual, and none of it is in shares; she
  will not need to raise the running money year by year.
- **What Caplet takes:** changes claim 2. The IPS line that a market fall is not a reason to change the plan has a
  real history behind it: the residencies that last have been hurt by exactly that. Use the case's term "operating
  reserve" with a plain gloss for funders (store H9-114).

### Card 4. One founder, ten years: the private museum evidence
- **What is it?** Velthuis and Gera's study of private art museums (76 closed, 446 open), the UK Mapping Museums
  study, and two founder-led residencies already in the store (Bamboo Curtain H9-117, Hedgebrook H9-118) (rows 16-17).
- **Did it work?** Closed private museums had lasted a median of 10 years; closures were "usually related to financial
  difficulties, rather than the founder's death"; staying open needs the founder's continuing money "or the
  establishment of a large endowment". UK museums are most likely to close in their first 10 years.
- **How is Laura like it?** A sole founder whose own money and reputation carry the project at the start.
- **How is she not?** These museums lacked what her plan provides: their founders' money arrived year by year, hers
  for the first decade is bought in 2027. They were also far bigger.
- **What Caplet takes:** the ten payments cover the decade in which founder-led institutions most often fail, and do
  it without relying on Laura's later income. A note, not a change: what happens after 2042 is outside the case's
  ten payments.

### Card 5. Overruns in Taiwan's own arts buildings
- **What is it?** Primary Taiwan government records on Weiwuying, the Taichung theater and two pop music centres, with
  the Taipei Performing Arts Center already in the store (H9-097), set against Flyvbjerg's about 60% average for
  buildings (rows 24-28).
- **Did it work?** Every one opened, but late: Weiwuying +28.64% and more than 7 years late, censured by the Control
  Yuan, with contractor bills still paid from reserve funds in 2020; Taichung 37 to 58 months, three budget changes
  and 274 days won in arbitration; the music centres extended by 11 and 5 years.
- **How is Laura like it?** A Taiwan arts building, built through contractors, with costs that move after the first
  budget; the IPS names "building costs that overrun".
- **How is she not?** These are national projects under public tendering rules; Laura contributes a fixed dollar
  amount, not the whole budget, and the project bears the overrun (store H7-086).
- **What Caplet takes:** keep claim 3, with Taiwan's own primary records as the local example a judge cannot wave
  away. Correct store H7-086's Weiwuying dates (first plan 2005-2009, not "due 2014").

### Card 6. Money promised to no one: what makes it work, and the risk in where it sits
- **What is it?** The IPS keeps half of the shares' value "promised to no one" for overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar
  or a co-sponsor falling short; NFF and Set in Stone say what makes such money useful (rows 5, 10, 30).
- **Did it work?** In the arts field, capital not held apart was spent on running deficits; groups that could shrink
  their programme came through. Flexibility worked when it was named and kept apart.
- **How is Laura like it?** The kept half is named, with three stated uses, and is not part of what she promises.
- **How is she not?** It sits in VT. The IPS's own first paragraph says that in a recession "shares fall and
  co-sponsors cut", so the kept half may be smallest when a co-sponsor falls short (Clash: yes). Building-cost
  inflation tends to come in strong years, when shares are more likely up, which partly offsets this (Keel inference,
  not tested).
- **What Caplet takes:** a risk to state plainly in the Final Report, not a change: the payments and the floor do not
  depend on the kept half; only the extra room to absorb surprises moves with markets.

### Card 7. How Taiwan's residencies are run and funded
- **What is it?** Taipei Artist Village and Treasure Hill (run by the city-backed Taipei Culture Foundation), the
  Ministry of Culture's yearly village subsidy (store H9-116), the National Culture and Arts Foundation, and the
  private Bamboo Curtain Studio (store H9-117) (rows 31-34).
- **Did it work?** The public villages have run for more than 20 years on city money (71% and 86% in 2018), but
  Taipei Artist Village lost its site to urban renewal in 2023. The national arts fund never raised its NT$4bn private
  share, and its income fell 41% in one year when returns dropped. Bamboo Curtain stopped in 2021, the year its founder
  died (store; cause not confirmed).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her residency would sit in a field where public grants are yearly, reviewed and cuttable,
  and where co-sponsors include public bodies with annual budgets.
- **How is she not?** Hers is private and founder-funded, closer to Bamboo Curtain than to the city villages, and its
  first ten years of her running money do not depend on any budget vote.
- **What Caplet takes:** in Taiwan a grant or a sponsor's pledge is a hope, not money in hand (Taichung's theatre got
  58.74% of its sponsorship target; NCAF's private share never came). Laura's prepaid payments are the steady part of
  a residency's money, which is the case for the plan's order.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **America's best-known residency nearly died before its first guest because its founders' investments fell.** The
   Trasks' investments "depreciated" in 1910, "jeopardizing Yaddo's future"; Katrina Trask closed the mansion in 1916
   "to conserve what was left of the endowment", and the first guests came in 1926, 26 years after the founding
   corporation (row 20). It is a century-old version of the risk Caplet's plan keeps away from the payments.
2. **The most famous arts-community failure was not about a building.** Black Mountain College built its own simple
   building, but had no endowment, lived year to year and was shut by courts over loans and unpaid salaries (rows 1-3).
   Set in Stone, the big study of US arts buildings, found few closures after building, and many deficits (row 4).
3. **Taiwan's own national arts fund never raised its private half, and its income fell 41% in a year.** The private
   NT$4bn promised in the 1990s never came; the fund is still under 70% of its legal target (rows 33-34). The national
   theatre in Taichung raised 58.74% of its sponsorship target (row 29).
4. **Founder-led cultural institutions most often close within about ten years, mostly for money.** That is the decade
   Laura's ten payments cover (rows 16-17).
