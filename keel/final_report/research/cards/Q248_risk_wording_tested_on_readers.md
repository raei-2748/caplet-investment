# Q248. Risk and limit wording tested on real readers: what made a limit understood on one reading, and what did not

- **Date:** Wed 25 Nov 2026, evening, Sydney. Every row marked VERIFIED was read at source on Wed 25 Nov 2026 (Sydney).
- **Report parts:** 3e (about 200 words, failure first: each promise, what would break it, what drives that, one figure, who covers it) and Table 3. Also 3c where a row bears on how chances are said ("in about 91 of 100 simulated futures"), 3f where a row bears on "about 1 in 31 against none", and the make-up clause in 3d's letter.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Who the readers were.** Every reader study below tested real people: retail investors and members of the public in EU, US, UK, Dutch and Australian regulators' tests, crowd panels, students or jurors in published experiments. None is a simulated reader. None tested funders, gift officers or competition judges, so no row is evidence about co-sponsors or judges; rows say so where it matters.
- **Not repeated:** the FCA's own tests, Feddersen et al. 2020 and Delias et al. 2022 (Q130-027 to Q130-029); Durney et al. 2021, linking each risk to the line it affects (Q130-035); "no test separates outcome first from cause first" (Q130-031, now partly answered by row 11); PRIIPs 2015's simple scale 74.2% against 58.3% (Q60-041), net-only figures and costs (Q105-029), detail in one table (Q105-030), scenarios read as forecasts (Q105-032, Q105-033); the EC's 2020 test, "out of 10" +4-11 points and the guarantee missed by more than half (Q60-042); the dilution effect (Q105-028); van der Bles et al. 2020 (Q28-026, Q37-026) and Gustafson and Rice 2020 (Q18-022); Gaertig and Simmons 2018 and the confidence heuristic (Q18-021, Q235-020, Q235-021); natural frequencies (H9-140, Q14a-015); refutational two-sided messages (Q149-027) and stealing thunder in performance reviews (Q80-020); the Bank of England's tested summaries (Q60-015 to Q60-018).
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. A limit is understood best as one condition and one consequence in one sentence, saying who covers the loss.
  2. A figure framed as a scenario ("if X happens, the top falls by about $Y") is understood better than a probability.
  3. Frequencies ("1 in 20") are understood better than percentages by lay readers of investment documents.
  4. A short list of the risks that matter is read and recalled better than a full list (disclosure overload).
  5. A document that states its own limits plainly is trusted more, not less.
- **Problem class:** writing a limit so that a lay reader understands on one reading what can fall short, by how much, and who bears it. **Field that solved it first:** health risk communication (drug labels, the drug facts box, number formats; the Making Numbers Meaningful reviews sum it up), then financial regulators' consumer testing, which began at scale with the EU's test of the UCITS key investor information (IFF Research and YouGov, 2009) and went on with the SEC's section 917 study (2012), PRIIPs (2015), ASIC and the Dutch AFM (2019) and the FCA (2018-2022).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. The shape holds, but tested readers fail on three things the claim leaves out.**
  - **What supports the shape.** Linking each risk to what it affects changed retail judgments where a plain list did not (stored, Q130-035). The FCA's tested risk summary gives each risk one numbered consequence with its conditions as bullets underneath (row 7). No test isolates "one per sentence" against two per sentence.
  - **What readers got wrong.** What is guaranteed and what is not: "difficult for participants to grasp" (row 1). Words set up in another section: readers do not link sections, and one unknown term sinks the whole section (rows 4, 5). Acronyms for the protector: the FCA left FSCS and FOS out of its warning for that reason (row 6). "Low risk" read as "no risk" (rows 2, 24).
  - **So.** Each limit sentence says what holds, what can fall short and by how much, and who makes it up, in words set up inside 3e. No "warning" frame; it was read as lock-in (row 12).
  - **Judge's note:** the regulators' hardest case is Laura's case: a guarantee with a limit. Readers of guaranteed products could not say "what was guaranteed and what was not" (row 1). 3e's top sentence is exactly that sentence, said plainly. This is a checker's rule, not an outside parallel (3e already has the gilt crisis).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. A scenario helps only with its money figure and a plain word on how likely it is.**
  - **Without chances, readers invent them.** Shown scenarios with no chances attached, readers "often wrongly assumed likelihoods"; shown chances, they still made mistakes (row 20).
  - **Scenarios are read as forecasts.** That held even under a bold "not predictions" line (row 21), and any indicative figure is read as definitive (row 23).
  - **Money beats percentages.** Readers preferred cash figures to percentages "by a wide margin" in seven countries (row 22).
  - **The gap.** No test compares "if X, the top falls by about $Y" with a probability for the same limit.
  - **So.** Write "if X, about $Y, made up by Z". For the hard case, add one dated fact from history, or say no chance is attached. Never give a percentage alone.
- **Claim 3: KILL as stated. Frequencies are not reliably better than percentages; which frequency matters more.**
  - **Percentages do as well.** Percentages and "N in 100" did about equally well (57% and 55% right), and both beat "1 in N" (45%), across 16,133 people (row 30). The 2025 systematic review finds few, mixed findings on recall and arithmetic, apart from a shared denominator helping (row 32).
  - **"1 in N" inflates.** It makes a risk feel larger (13 moderate- or high-credibility findings; row 29). It also makes the gap between two risks feel larger, and more so at low risk (row 31).
  - **Threshold frequencies cause errors.** "x years in every 20" produced more inconsistent choices than chance-of-a-range wording among Australian retirement savers (rows 26, 27). Readers who understood "1 in 10 below this" did not reliably flip it to "9 in 10 above" (row 33).
  - **Already stored.** Natural frequencies help with Bayesian sums, and "out of 10" helped by 4-11 points in the EU's 2020 test. Neither is this claim's test.
  - **So.** Use one denominator per part ("in about 91 of 100") and say which side is meant. Never "x years in every 20". "1 in 31" in 3f makes the first plan's risk sound larger than "about 3 in 100" would; that is the lead's call (row 34).
- **Claim 4: KEEP, with two limits.**
  - **For it.** Generic warnings "detract from the relevant risks" (row 39). Boilerplate dulls readers to the facts that matter, and highlighting rescues novices only (row 19). Readers use few items: a third of choices used none, a quarter used three (row 35). Generic risk text, listed or in a block, did no more than no text (row 40).
  - **Against it.** Shorter alone did not improve choices (a key facts sheet 41%, the full disclosure 41%; row 36). "'Simplification' often amounts to simplification of language, rather than concepts" (row 37). A warning recited by 80% of a country changed no behaviour (row 15). Recall is not use.
  - **So.** Three limits and one hard case, each specific and each with its reason. No generic line such as "markets can fall".
- **Claim 5: CHANGE. Plain, specific limits stated by the plan's own authors raise trust; generic ones do not, and the gain fades.**
  - **For it.** Hedged science news made scientists and journalists more trustworthy, more so when the scientists themselves gave the limits (N = 601; row 43). Volunteering a damaging fact first cut its impact through credibility (row 44). Forthcoming bad news raised credibility, in the short term only (row 45). Generic risk text left readers more surprised when the risk came and cost credibility. Specific text raised perceived risk a little (8.35 against 7.73) but softened the fall afterwards (0.83 against 1.34; row 46).
  - **Against it.** Disclaimers are read as cover ("I'd kind of just gloss over it"; row 14). Disclosure can raise trust without improving judgement (row 48), and readable bad news lands harder (row 47).
  - **So.** Say each limit in the team's own voice, specifically, with its answer. A judge reads once, so the short-term gain is the one that counts.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 1, 3 and 6: what is guaranteed, what is not, and who covers the gap, in plain words set up in 3e.** Tested readers grasped guarantees with limits least. They read bond funds as guaranteed. The FCA dropped the protectors' acronyms from its warning. So 3e's top sentence names the late-2032 Treasury fund in words, says it can pay a little less than its Treasuries, and says her flexibility makes it up under the letter.
2. **Rows 20-23: a scenario with its money figure and a dated fact.** Without chances, readers invent them. Scenarios are read as forecasts, and cash beats percentages. So "a rise of about 25% in the cost of her share" comes with its condition and the two dated slumps that were followed by a dearer building. It is never left as a bare percentage.
3. **Rows 26-33: one denominator, the side meant, never "years in every 20".** "1 in N" inflates a risk and the gap between two risks. Threshold frequencies breed errors, and readers do not flip a chance. This bears on 3c's "91 of 100" (keep), 3e's four episodes (dated events, never a rate) and 3f's "1 in 31" (the lead's call, row 34).
4. **Row 24: scope the closing line.** Readers stretched "low risk" to "untouched by any crisis". So the closing line says what no fall in share prices can change: the ten payments and the bottom from January 2028, and the top once moved.
5. **Rows 43-46: limits in the team's own voice, specific, with their answers.** Self-stated limits raised trust, and stolen thunder cut the damage. Specific risk text protected credibility when the risk came, while generic text did not. The gain fades (Mercer), but a judge reads once.

## Gaps

- No test isolates "one consequence per sentence" against two per sentence, or a limit with its owner named against the same limit without.
- No test compares a scenario ("if X, about $Y") with a probability for the same limit.
- No reader study in this card used funders, gift officers or finance judges; every reader was a retail investor, a member of the public, a student or a crowd panellist.
