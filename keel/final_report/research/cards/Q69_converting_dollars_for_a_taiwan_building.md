- **Problem class:** a capital budget stated in one currency before any contract exists, paid later in another currency, by a funder that may not use derivatives.
- **Field that solved it first:**
  - Corporate and public treasury: a budget rate, then cover in steps as the bill becomes certain.
  - For bodies barred from derivatives, US federal appropriations law:
    - the Defense Department's Foreign Currency Fluctuations account (authorised FY1979; Pub. L. 96-38, 25 Jul 1979, per the 10 USC 2779 source table);
    - the State Department's Buying Power Maintenance Account (22 USC 2696(b), as amended in 1982).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Institutions fix the rate in steps as the bill becomes certain, and at the latest at the contract. Laura's 2031 figure is an approval-stage number, so most of the exposure is properly left open.**
  - **Skellerup (NZ manufacturer), capital items over NZD 250,000:** "50% hedged when the purchase of the item is formally approved" and "100% hedged when the supply contract/procurement order is signed".
  - **NZ Treasury, rules for government departments, ¶25:** "The point at which a transaction exposure is considered certain to arise is the point at which the exposure should be covered. The latest point ... is at the contract stage". Earlier cover (¶26) needs the department to "specify the currencies, amounts, and timing".
  - **Waikato University:** hedges 80-100% of confirmed or contracted amounts, and only 0-50% of unconfirmed amounts 0-12 months out.
  - **Marinus Link (Chatham, for the regulator AER):** small hedges before notice to proceed, then a hedge "from NTP to project completion". It warns of "Loss of flexibility ... changes in cash flow timing and amount".
  - **What this means for Laura.** In 2031 there is no NT$ contract, no known NT$ amount and no fixed date, so on the NZ Treasury test the exposure is not yet certain.
  - **Judge's note:** a judge may expect a careful funder to lock the rate the day she names the figure. Treasurers do the opposite: they lock only what is contracted.
- **Claim 2: KILL for this plan. Converting in 2031 is outside the frozen plan, it costs a sure interest gap, and it buys NT$ for a bill that does not yet exist.**
  - **Outside the plan.** In 2031 Laura's money is in Treasuries maturing late 2032 (IPS l.15: the half moved "into Treasuries" before the meetings). Converting early means one of two things:
    - selling those Treasuries early, which is not one of the IPS's two reasons; or
    - a forward contract. Derivatives are not permitted in Laura's plan, and the US Defense Department faces the same bar (Gardner et al. 2017: "prohibited by law").
  - **The cost.** Holding NT$ instead of Treasuries gives up the gap between the two countries' interest rates:
    - US Treasury 2-year 4.46% (5 Nov 2026 curve).
    - Taiwan's five leading banks: 2-year 0.78% (CBC daily page, 12 Nov 2026), or a 1.70% posted 1-year rate at the end of 2025 (CBC Annual Report 2025).
    - The gap is about 2.8-3.7 points a year. On $115,000 for about two years, that is about $6,300-$8,500 (Keel arithmetic at Nov 2026 rates; an illustration, not a plan figure).
    - That is 55-74% of the $11,500 a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar would need (NUMBERS 8j). Converting early pays most of the shock for certain in order to remove a shock that may never come.
  - **What it would remove.** The two-year move from the meetings to the gift: 1-in-20 +13.7%, median +0.1%, 10% or more against her in 11.0% of windows since 1983 (NUMBERS 8j).
  - **Judge's note:** converting early does not make the currency risk free. It turns a possible cost into a certain one of about half the size, paid in lost interest.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Converting bill by bill is safer than converting everything at the end, but riskier than converting on receipt whatever is already contracted. The choice belongs to whoever receives the dollars.**
  - **The comparison.** FRED EXTAUS monthly, Oct 1983-Oct 2026, 505 twelve-month windows, all measured against converting on the day the dollars arrive (Keel arithmetic). The figures are the extra US dollars needed for a fixed NT$ budget:

    | Since | Spread over 12 monthly bills, 1-in-20 / worst | All converted at month 11, 1-in-20 | Spread over 12 bills, more than 5% | All at month 11, more than 5% |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Oct 1983 | +5.7% / +13.0% | +10.1% | 7.3% of windows | 22.2% of windows |
    | Jan 2001 | +4.5% / +7.1% | +7.8% | 3.7% of windows | 17.8% of windows |

  - **Waiting is not cheaper on average.** The median 12-month move is +0.1% since 1983 and 0.0% since 2001.
  - **The same advice from treasurers.** Monash buys and holds the currency once a payment is within six months. Newcastle buys "as soon as practicable, once the required amount is known".
  - **Judge's note:** "pay as you go" sounds cautious, but every month a dollar waits unconverted is a month of currency risk. The safe moment is the day the NT$ bill is known.
- **Claim 4: KILL as a general rule. The recipient carries the risk by default. A few funders top up at their own discretion, and a funder that fixes the grant in the spender's currency carries it in full.**
  - **The default: grants fixed in US dollars or pounds, recipient bears the loss.**
    - Gavi: "grantees take on the exchange rate risks".
    - Global Fund: "any currency exchange losses are to be borne by the implementer", and budgets must not include "any contingency reserve for exchange rate variations".
    - Wellcome: the organisation covers the extra cost if it chose the currency, and the currency cannot be changed after the award.
    - Science for Africa Foundation, UK Aid Match and GlobalGiving: the same rule.
  - **Discretionary top-up.** JRS keeps its grants in US dollars, "only to be exchanged into local currency as necessary". For significant losses it may add funds "on a case-by-case base and at the discretion of JRS".
  - **Fixed in the spender's currency.** Fulbright Taiwan pays "in NTD", so the funder holds the move.
  - **Laura** states US dollars and keeps her half as a discretionary backer (IPS l.15), which puts her with JRS: generous, but not a guarantor.
  - **Judge's note:** the currency a gift is written in decides who carries the move. Laura chooses that currency in 2031, and it is the one decision that cannot be changed afterwards.
- **Claim 5: KILL as a blocker. Conversions above NT$500,000 need a declaration form, and the US$10 million annual limit is about 87 times the top (Keel arithmetic). One line matters: a non-resident individual converting more than US$100,000 at once needs CBC approval.**
  - **The declaration.** Any NT$ conversion of NT$500,000 or more (about US$15,300 at 32.768, Keel arithmetic) is declared (Regulations, Art. 2).
  - **The annual limits.** Since 1 Nov 2024 the limit is US$10 million a year for associations and individuals, and US$100 million for companies (CBC press release (113) No. 180).
  - **Foreign currency held without converting.** Flows that do not involve NT$ conversion are 「完全自由」 (completely free).
  - **Non-residents.** A non-resident individual (no residence certificate valid a year or more, Art. 3) converts up to US$100,000 per remittance directly at a bank (Art. 4). Above that it needs documents and CBC approval (Art. 6), which takes about a week (Business Insider Taiwan).
  - **Where Laura's figure sits.** Her range of $95,000-$115,000 straddles that US$100,000 line. The case does not say where she resides.
  - Facts only, not legal advice.
  - **Judge's note:** the rule that touches this gift is not a cap on money leaving or entering Taiwan. It is a US$100,000 line on a single conversion by a foreigner, which is another reason the residency, not Laura, should convert.

## Gaps

- **The Council on Foundations resource** "Addressing the Impact of Currency Fluctuations on International Grantmaking" is members-only; only its description was read (UNVERIFIED).
- **Other US foundations.** No published currency policy was found for Ford, Mellon, Hewlett, Packard or Mott. The Mott and Hewlett grantee FAQs were read and say nothing on currency.
- **Taiwan Foundation for Democracy.** Its grants page was read and shows no currency terms.
- **A building abroad.** No university or museum building abroad with a published conversion policy (when the builder's currency was bought) was found.
- **How Taiwan classifies the gift.** The CBC's remittance-nature codes (匯款性質細分類) for a foreign individual's donation to a Taiwan foundation were not read. Nor was whether a gift fits Art. 6's approval purposes. Both are legal questions, out of scope.
- **Taiwan deposit rates.** The tiers are unclear: the CBC daily page (0.77%, 1-year) against the posted 1-year rate (1.70%, end-2025) and banks' large-deposit tiers. The rate a residency would actually earn on NT$ was not pinned down.
- **The State Department's overseas building office.** Its own construction currency practice, beyond the Buying Power Maintenance Account, was not read.
- **Seen only in search results, not read:** Canada's defence department currency studies (DRDC 2013; Bucar et al. 2003) and the CASRAI guide on currency risk in research grants.
- **Daily data.** Daily NT$ data were not used for rows 35-37. Monthly averages understate the swings.
