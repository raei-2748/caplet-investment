## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Savage's own test for a small world that tells the truth fails for anyone who holds tickets in the draw, and IPS l.9 says Laura does.**
(a) Savage wrote that a small world can be trusted when its draw has nothing to do with what its prizes are worth, like a lottery drawn from a drum, which for an ordinary person is "utterly irrelevant to his life, except through the rules of the lottery itself", "unless the person himself (or perhaps some other person or organization with whom he had some degree of contact) held tickets in the lottery".
(b) IPS l.9 is the finding that Laura already holds tickets in the share market, because in a recession her advances and fees come later and smaller while shares fall and co-sponsors cut their budgets, so a plan that paid her promises from shares would fall short in the very years her dollars are worth most to her, while a payment owned in Treasuries held to their dates (directly or through a fund that holds them to its end) is met in every market state and so needs no forecast of markets at all (Keel's reading of §5.5).
(c) The test breaks at the dollar, because Savage insists that even a cash prize is no "sure consequence" (his Jones would rather own a car "if he feared extensive inflation"), so what $50,000 does for the residency stays in the large world, and the case itself draws the border there by fixing each payment at $50,000 in dollars, which is why the building's cost, priced in Taiwan dollars and materials, gets a stated dollar contribution plus room (her flexibility), never a promise to cover the cost itself.
- **Decision:** IPS l.9's counterbalance and l.11's step (promised money into Treasuries, unpromised money into shares); IPS l.15 (the building met with flexibility). It supports l.9's reason for dropping the first plan and never replaces it (CANON A3).
- **Report part:** 2.3 (one clause beside the career line), 3a.
- **Source:** Savage (1954/1972), §5.5 pp. 83-90 and §5.6; IPS l.9; card Q148 row 14 (the tie is a lag: the career's bad years follow shares' by about a year, which still ties the draw to the prize over a plan that runs from 2027 to 2033).
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha.** A finance judge knows the career-as-shares idea (pedigree), and a decision theorist knows small worlds, but almost nobody has read the clause in which Savage himself, in 1954, named the ticket-holder as the person a small world misleads, who is exactly the person IPS l.9 describes.

**2. Savage's two proverbs belong to one act, so the plan is a chain of small worlds, one built on each dated step.**
(a) Savage did not give "Look before you leap" and "You can cross that bridge when you come to it" to two different problems: crossing a bridge when you reach it, he wrote, means "artificially confining attention to so small a world that the 'Look before you leap' principle can be applied there", and looking is worth it only "in so far as the looking is not unreasonably time-consuming and otherwise expensive".
(b) Laura's plan reaches three bridges on three dated days and builds a small world on each, ten dates and ten sums in January 2027, the rest of the ten and the bottom of her range in January 2028, and the top before the 2031 meetings when half the shares move into the same late-2032 fund (the move), and on each of those days looking is cheap because the next promise's price is on the screen, while looking a year earlier is dear: a top named 12 months before the move would be more than the move buys in 10.6% of simulated futures (NUMBERS 4).
(c) The parallel breaks twice, because Savage's small worlds are drawn in the mind and can be redrawn while Laura's are bought and are never sold to redraw them (IPS l.17's two reasons are the only exits), and because one part of her money is never put into any small world, her flexibility, whose bridges (an overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor short) are crossed only when she comes to them, the one place the plan follows the second proverb alone.
- **What this corrects:** Q168 idea 3 gave "look" to the payments and "bridge" to the building. Savage gives both to every step. The prompt's picture of "the payments and the stated top decided early" holds for the payments and the bottom only: the top is decided as late as the plan allows, on the day its small world can be built, and what is fixed early is the rule, never the figure (NUMBERS 4, "When each end is fixed").
- **Decision:** the three dated steps (GOLDMINE 27); why nothing is stated before the move (NUMBERS 4); her flexibility (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3.0, 3c; `CONCEIT.md` (each inking is the day a small world is built).
- **Source:** Savage (1954/1972), §2.5 pp. 16-17; §5.5 pp. 82-83; NUMBERS 4.
- **Pedigree or aha:** a niche correction more than an aha; its job is to stop the report from saying the plan "decides everything early", which a judge who knows Savage would catch.

**3. Each writer draws the border by a different test, and the plan's test is none of theirs: it makes each promise indifferent to which surprise comes.**
(a) Savage draws the border by judgment, Binmore by foresight ("You are in a small world if it is feasible always to look before you leap"), Gilboa, Postlewaite and Schmeidler by evidence (the trouble starts where "there are no agreed-upon methods for assigning probabilities"), and Kay by a plan's ability to survive what nobody foresaw (a sound plan is "robust and resilient").
(b) By Binmore's test Laura's whole case is a large world, since "the worlds of macroeconomics and high finance most certainly don't fall into this category", and the plan does not dispute it: nobody foresaw every surprise for her, but for each payment, and later for each end of the range, she owns Treasuries (directly or through a fund that holds them to its end) that meet it whichever surprise comes, so each promise is safe from surprise by indifference, not by Binmore's foresight.
(c) It breaks where the indifference is not spread out, because the late-2032 fund carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, about a quarter of her plan from January 2028 (NUMBERS 8w), so one notice from one fund touches three promises at once, the one place the plan falls short of Kay's "modularity and redundancy"; the issuer's promise and the funds' costs sit at the same edge (Q168 row 32).
- **Decision:** IPS l.11 (its one named exception); Table 3 row 4 (the fund watch); 3e's line on one fund carrying three promises.
- **Report part:** 3a (one clause), 3e.
- **Source:** Binmore (2007), section 1; Binmore (2009), ch. 1, p. 2; Gilboa, Postlewaite & Schmeidler (2008), pp. 173-174; Kay (2020); NUMBERS 8w.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha** for a judge who knows decision theory: Binmore's large world usually ends any talk of certainty in finance; the plan grants it and still keeps the promised money out of its reach.

**4. The best companion: Gilboa, Maccheroni, Marinacci and Schmeidler's two kinds of rational choice are the plan's two pots, and the register checks it.**
(a) In 2010 these four split a rational choice into two kinds, an "objective" one the decision maker "can convince others" is right because it comes out better under "each and every" reasonable forecast, and a "subjective" one she "cannot be convinced" is wrong, and when the first kind cannot settle a choice their rule called Caution settles it "by opting for the sure thing".
(b) Laura's promises are made to others, so the plan holds them to the first kind, and her flexibility is hers alone, so it needs only the second, which predicts something checkable in the register: before the move the fifteen forecasting houses' central paths point to two different tops (thirteen to $115,000, GMO and Ninety One to $110,000), after the move the top is what the move bought, one figure whatever forecast a co-sponsor holds, and her flexibility, never stated, runs from about $13,000 on GMO's path to about $22,700 on BlackRock's (NUMBERS 8p).
(c) It breaks where the authors themselves warn that "The Caution axiom is rather extreme", since it can pick a sure thing over an act that is better under almost every forecast, and the plan answers by using Caution only for promised money, keeping half the shares promised to no one and pricing its step once (aha row 10); the agreement also runs only across forecasts of markets, never across beliefs about the issuer, which IPS l.11 names once.
- **Decision:** "Laura states a figure only once it is inked" (CONCEIT); 3c's confidence; 3d's language (a holding co-sponsors can be shown, not a forecast they must trust).
- **Report part:** 3c, 3d, 3b.
- **Source:** Gilboa, Maccheroni, Marinacci & Schmeidler (2008 working paper; 2010); NUMBERS 8p.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha.** Decision theorists know the paper as theory; a client's plan whose stated and unstated money divide along its line, with the register showing the stated part the same under every house's forecast and the unstated part not, is new.

## What a judge who knows decision theory would find new

1. **IPS l.9 is Savage's exception.** Savage's working test for a trustworthy small world (a lottery irrelevant to your life) carries its own exception, the ticket-holder; a writer whose income falls in the same downturn as shares is that person, so a market model cannot value her promises paid from shares, and the plan stops asking it to.
2. **Safe from surprise without foresight.** Binmore's small world is made safe by foreseeing every surprise; the plan's promises are made safe by not depending on which surprise comes. That lets Savage-style planning work inside what Binmore calls a large world, for the promised money only.
3. **A calendar of small worlds.** Savage's small world is timeless and built once; the plan builds three, each on the day its price can be read, and leaves one pot (her flexibility) in no small world at all.
4. **Two kinds of rationality, two pots.** The plan states only what passes the "convince others" test under every forecast and keeps unstated what passes only the "cannot be shown wrong" test; the register's fifteen-house panel shows the split (NUMBERS 8p).
