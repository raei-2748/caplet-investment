## Verdicts in one screen

1. **CHANGE: "only under conditions" is right, but the conditions are timing, safety and facts, and the averages are
   already in Q91.** The meta-analyses (Q91 rows 8-9) say task conflict does not help on average. What this card adds
   is when it helps. In 51 three-person MBA teams over a semester, the high performers' task conflict peaked in the
   middle (2.33 against 1.70 early and 1.63 late), while the low performers' rose at the deadline (3.39). A snapshot
   taken at one moment showed every kind of conflict going with lower performance (r -.11 to -.27), so the pattern over
   time was what separated the two groups (Jehn & Mannix 2001). In 117 student project teams, task conflict went with
   better results only under high psychological safety (Bradley et al. 2012). In 48 top teams, openness raised
   task-focused conflict and, with mutuality, lowered personal conflict (Amason & Sapienza 1997). In 12 top teams,
   working from facts went with low interpersonal conflict ("The absence of conflict is not harmony, it's apathy";
   Eisenhardt, Kahwajy & Bourgeois 1997). Relationship conflict hurting is KEPT: the high performers' relationship
   conflict stayed low throughout.
   *Judge's note:* "conflict is good" and "conflict is bad" are both wrong without a date. The research turns on when
   the argument happened, so 3f's dates (lock 1 Oct, objection 5 Oct, orders 7-8 Oct) carry the point.
2. **CHANGE: the claim is untested as worded, and "groupthink" should be dropped as a frame.** No study found
   compares decision quality with the same dissent placed before and after a commitment. The nearest evidence:
   - Voice before a decision was judged fairer than voice after it, and voice after it fairer than none (3.70, 3.15 and
     2.43 on a fairness scale, 179 undergraduates; Lind, Kanfer & Earley 1990). That measures fairness, not quality.
   - Groups given a unanimity rule stayed with a failing course more often than majority-rule groups (Kameda &
     Sugimori 1993).
   - Learning everyone's preference at the start made groups worse at finding the best option (four experiments;
     Mojzisch & Schulz-Hardt 2010).
   - Groups with a norm of criticism chose the best option 67% of the time against 22% with a norm of consensus, and
     83% against 42% in a second study, with no effect on people deciding alone (Postmes, Spears & Cihangir 2001).

   Janis's full groupthink model has "largely equivocal support" (Turner & Pratkanis 1998) and no "convincing support"
   (Aldag & Fuller 1993), so "we avoided groupthink" would rest 3f on a contested model.
   *Judge's note:* the objection came after the team had locked its strategy and before any money moved. In the
   research that is the last point at which voice still changes something: it is late enough to test a real plan and
   early enough to change what is bought.
3. **KEEP, with sources; the exact population is a gap.** Investment clubs have a measured record. Across 166 clubs,
   the average returned 14.1% a year against 17.9% for the index and 16.4% for the average individual, and 60% of
   clubs trailed the index (Barber & Odean 2000). Clubs bought "good reason" stocks more often than individuals (46.5%
   of their buys were in the most-admired tenth against 33.0%), and in a lab, groups chose them 90% of the time
   against 64% for individuals (Barber, Heath & Odean 2003). Each extra work or school tie in a club went with 8%
   better returns, and friends were "less apt" to debate (Harrington, as reported by Brown University). Student teams
   are the standard sample: MBA teams (Jehn & Mannix), 117 student teams (Bradley), undergraduates (Postmes; Lind), and
   school pupils, whose talk Mercer sorts into disputational, cumulative ("uncritical agreement", which "often arises
   when groups are organised on the basis of friendship") and exploratory. Exploratory talk can be taught (Wegerif,
   Mercer & Dawes 1999). No study was found of student investment-competition teams.
   *Judge's note (the aha):* groups favour the option with the easiest reason to give, and "own the whole market" is the
   easiest reason in investing. Darren's word "copout" names exactly the bias the research predicts. What turns keeping
   VT into a decision rather than a default is a test fixed before the result, and a rival that came within a
   thousandth of it (0.8991 against 0.90, 30 Sep model). The reason in Trading Note 3 does not do that job.
4. **CHANGE: the copout did not change the decision; it tested it.** The record says "We decided, in the end, to keep
   the one world fund (VT)", so "a disagreement that changed our decision" would be false. Wharton's word is
   "influenced", and Q91 row 21 shows that dissent improves decisions even when it loses. Five findings separate a
   credible account from a staged one:
   - **Answer the objection.** A message that refutes the other side beats a one-sided one (r .077), and one that
     names the other side without answering it does worse (r -.049; O'Keefe 1999). So Darren's case goes in at full
     strength and is answered.
   - **Use details a judge can check.** True accounts carry more of them: g 0.42 for the count and 0.49 for the share
     (Palena et al. 2021). The effect shrinks when the teller has an incentive to look credible (Verschuere et al.
     2021), so the details that count are ones a judge can check in documents already in hand.
   - **Avoid the conversion story.** The staged form is "penance, conversion and commitment" (Macfarlane & Gourlay
     2009, as summarised at Huddersfield), and self-criticism can be self-praise in disguise (Q80 row 23).
   - **Admit something that costs.** The admission that earns trust is the one that costs something: the decision was
     close (the statement-against-interest rule; Williamson v. United States).
   - **Do not claim unanimity.** "We decided, in the end" claims none, and the best teams in Eisenhardt's study
     "resolve issues without forcing consensus".

   *Judge's note:* a reflection that admits the rival came within a thousandth of the bar is stronger than one that
   says the team learned humility. The first can be checked; the second costs nothing.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 20 (groups favour the easy reason; 90% against 64%) for 3f, moment C.** This is the aha. "Own the whole market"
   is the easiest reason in investing, so Darren's "copout" is the objection the research itself would raise. 3f
   answers it with a test fixed before the result and a rival within a thousandth. Trading Note 3's reason does not do
   that job.
2. **Row 16 (a norm of criticism: 67% against 22%, 83% against 42%) with row 12 (voice before a decision) for 3f and
   PROCESS.** Together they make the case for telling a recorded objection at all, and for giving its dates: it came
   after the lock and before the money.
3. **Row 27 (answer the objection: r .077 against r -.049) for 3f.** It gives the craft rule behind SKELETON's "put so
   its holder would accept it". Darren's case goes in at full strength and gets an answer. Naming it in passing is
   worse than leaving it out.
4. **Rows 28-29 (checkable details: g 0.42-0.49, weaker when the teller wants to look credible) for 3f.** Each detail
   in the moment should be one a judge can check in documents already in hand: Trading Note 3, the IPS, the dates.
5. **Row 23 (exploratory, cumulative and disputational talk) with row 10 (four alternatives, not a duel) for 3f.** These
   rows show how to keep the word "copout" without Darren reading as the fool. The word comes with his proposal and
   the other options raised, which is exploratory talk, not name-calling.

## Gaps

- Jehn's original conflict-type papers (1995 onward) were not read at source. They are known here only through Jehn &
  Mannix's citations.
- Amason (1996): the correlations were not read. JSTOR lists the article as free, but it was not fetched this run.
- Korsgaard, Schweiger & Sapienza (1995): the full text was not reached. Whether decision quality differed by procedure
  appears only in a secondary summary, which was not used.
- Macfarlane & Gourlay (2009): the full text is paywalled. Its "tips" (eat humble pie; revelation brings conversion;
  toe the line) were seen only in a student blog, so they are not used.
- McGarr & Ó Gallchóir (2020): the full text timed out at the University of Limerick repository. The spectrum of
  self-enhancement techniques is still unread (Q80 row 23 has the abstract).
- Maloney et al. (2013): the full text was not read. The "20% completely honest" figure is second-hand.
- Harrington (2001), Research in the Sociology of Organizations: not reached. The 8% figure is from Brown University's
  release.
- De Dreu & Weingart's "23% of studies positive" is reported by the BPS Digest only. It needs a check in the
  meta-analysis.
- No study was found of disagreement in student investment-competition teams or high-school finance teams. The nearest
  are investment clubs and MBA or undergraduate project teams.
- No experiment was found that compares decision quality with the same dissent placed before and after a commitment.
  Lind measures fairness, and Kameda & Sugimori measure entrapment.
- A next round could look for research on how assessors rate a reflection that names a teammate against one that says
  "a teammate". Nothing was found this run.
