- **Problem class:** issue only against what is held: rules that let someone make a promise other people will plan and
  spend against only when the asset that pays it is already held (cover, backing and collateral rules). **Field that
  solved it first:** public banking and note issue, from the Bank of Amsterdam's "100 percent reserves" charter (lending
  from 1615) to Peel's Bank Charter Act (1844); the same rule was later written for currency boards (Hanke and
  Schuler), for public budgets (the Antideficiency Act, from 1870) and for payment firms (the UK's EMR 2011).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Laura's rule is stricter than Peel's, and it uses the asset Peel did not count as cover.**
  - **Peel's rule.** Up to £14 million of notes could go out "upon securities" (£11 million the Government owed the Bank
    plus £3 million such as Exchequer Bills); every note beyond that needed bullion (Peel's 1844 Commons speech; Hansard HL 12 Jul
    1844). The uncovered "fiduciary issue" grew by order and by statute: £14,475,000 from December 1855, £1,575 million
    by 1954.
  - **Three suspensions by letter.** Each crisis (1847, 1857, 1866) was met by a Chancellor's letter. Only 1857 breached
    the limit, by a peak of £928,000 reaching the public, and Parliament then passed an Indemnity Bill.
  - **Laura's rule.** No figure is stated beyond what she owns, no one can write her a letter, and nothing can forgive
    her afterwards. Her only exits are the two the IPS names (l.17).
  - **Why her cover differs from Peel's.** A note promised gold, so government paper was not cover. Laura promises dollars
    on dates, so a Treasury that pays dollars on that date is the cover.
  - **Judge's note:** in 1844, government bonds were the part of the note issue that was NOT backed. For Laura they are
    the backing, because what counts as cover depends on what is promised.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Payment on demand caused the strain (card Q3b), but the rules failed in three other ways.**
  - **The boundary.** The 1844 Act covered notes, and the panics came through deposits: the 100% rule "was evaded by
    recourse to checking deposits" (Fisher).
  - **The substance.**
    - Argentina let up to a third of its "backing" be its own government's dollar bonds "valued at market prices".
    - The Bank of Amsterdam swore its vault was full while lending to the East India Company from 1615. When its accounts
      were published in 1795, its money fell to nearly 30% below par.
  - **The checking.** UK e-money firms that failed in 2018-2023 had an "average shortfall of 65%" despite a
    safeguarding law.
  - **Where cover was genuine, covered every promise and was checked, holders lost nothing.** National bank notes,
    backed by US bonds, 1863-1913 ("noteholders suffered no losses"). The Issue Department in 1847, 1857 and 1866: only
    in 1857 did any note go out beyond the limit (a peak of £928,000, down to £15,000 within ten days), and Parliament
    made it lawful afterwards. Hong Kong in 1997-98.
  - **Judge's note:** Adam Smith wrote that "no point of faith is better established" than Amsterdam's full vault. The
    bank had been lending from it since 1615, over 150 years before he wrote. Backing is shown, not sworn, which is why
    Table 1 lists every Treasury by CUSIP.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. The margin is standard; Laura's is small for a reason.**
  - **Standard margins.**
    - Orthodox currency boards hold "100 per cent or slightly more", with a surplus historically up to about 10%, as "a
      cushion against losses in the securities" (Hanke and Schuler).
    - Hong Kong keeps 105%-112.5%.
    - National bank notes were issued at 90% of the bonds' value (100% from 1900).
  - **Why Laura's is smaller.** A board marks its cover to market: in September 2026 Hong Kong's backing lost HK$10,385m
    on revaluation. Laura measures each end by what the fund's own holdings pay if nothing more earns interest, worked
    out on the trade day. So her 0.5% covers costs beyond the 0.07% fee (about 0.27% shown by the five ended Treasury
    iBond funds), not price falls (NUMBERS 0).
  - **Hong Kong's upside matches hers.** Above 112.5%, assets move out to its investment portfolio. What the funds pay
    above Laura's stated top (about $8,476 centrally) joins her flexibility.
  - **Its downside does not.** Hong Kong's lower trigger refills the backing from that portfolio. Laura's shares never
    refill a payment (deletion test).
  - **Judge's note:** Hong Kong moves backing above 112.5% into bonds and shares, as Laura's excess joins her
    flexibility. But Hong Kong's shares also stand behind its backing, and Laura's never do.
- **Claim 4: KILL as conceit turns; KEEP as evidence and one bridge sentence at most.**
  - **Near domain.** Money to money fails Caplet's "strikingly dissimilar" test.
  - **A second mapping.** The parallels bring their own mapping (backing and issue) beside pencil and ink, the switched
    mapping card Q42 found cancels the gain.
  - **The one usable bridge.** A co-sponsor plans against Laura's stated figure as a holder spends a banknote. So if it is
    used, ink stays on Laura's side (the owned Treasuries), stating stays "stating", and the report never calls a
    banknote "ink", never says "printing money" and never puns on "issue" (CONCEIT guard).
  - **Judge's note:** the scale contrast, not the domain, is what is striking. The rule that has held the Hong Kong
    dollar since 1983 (HK$2.33 trillion of backing) is the rule behind a $95,000 bottom.
- **Claim 5: KILL.** The Antideficiency Act is what shuts the US government when appropriations lapse (since the 1980
  opinions; 20 shutdowns of a full day or more FY1977-FY2019; 43 days in 2025). But interest on the public debt is a
  permanent appropriation (31 U.S.C. 1305(2)), and "the Treasury can continue to pay interest on U.S. Treasury debt on
  time". Principal rests on the pledge in 31 U.S.C. 3123(a) and on borrowing, which is the debt-limit question already
  in the store. A shutdown is not reason 2 and needs no Table 3 row.
  - **Judge's note:** the law that shuts the US government every few years for spending money it does not have is
    written so that it cannot stop the Treasury interest Laura owns.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 14, the vault exemption** (3c). Backing attaches when a promise leaves the issuer, as US and UK note law both
   say. It explains in one sentence why the top is stated only after the 2031 move, and why the half promised to no one
   never needs owning.
2. **Row 18, Hong Kong's 105%-112.5% band** (3c, 3b). The excess above Hong Kong's band goes to its investment portfolio,
   as Laura's excess above the top joins her flexibility. The disanalogy (Hong Kong's shares refill its backing, and
   Laura's never refill a payment) sharpens the deletion test.
3. **Row 2, Peel's fiduciary issue** (2.0, 3.0). In 1844 government debt was the uncovered part of the note issue. For
   Laura it is the cover, because she promises dollars on dates. It turns "why Treasuries?" into "what is promised
   decides what counts as cover".
4. **Row 26, Adam Smith and the Bank of Amsterdam** (2.5, Table 1, 3e). Adam Smith took on faith a full vault that the bank had
   been lending from for over 150 years. It is the reason Table 1 shows every holding instead of asking to be
   trusted.
5. **Row 35, shutdowns and Treasury interest** (3e, Table 3). The strictest US law against unfunded promises cannot stop
   Treasury interest, so a shutdown is not reason 2 and needs no row. It does a job Wharton asks for (what to monitor and
   what not to).
