- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** the order in time of a fixed promise and the money that pays it (a timing mismatch between a dated liability and a risky asset), and the slow lowering of a promise nobody can enforce (time inconsistency). Finance solved the first with cash-flow dedication (a bond maturing before each payment) and law with security and sureties; behavioural economics named the second (precommitment, cards Q7, Q7b). Literature has staged both failures since about 1596, with the causes laid out step by step, which is why it can be read as a casebook.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**Idea 1. The same sentence in the wrong order.**
(a) In Shakespeare, Austen, Dickens, George Eliot, Ibsen, Trollope and Balzac, someone promises a fixed sum first and expects the money to arrive later, and every ruin happens in the gap between those two dates.
(b) Laura's plan writes the sentence the other way round: it buys each promise and only then states it, on three dated steps (January 2027, January 2028, January 2031), so the gap where all these ruins happen is almost empty.
(c) The parallel breaks because most of these characters needed the money they promised, while Laura gives money she will already have; the break teaches that the case's calendar (both deposits arrive before anyone relies on a figure) is what makes the right order possible, and that the team's first plan threw that calendar away by leaving the ten to be bought on 1 January 2033 (NUMBERS 8s).
- *The check it predicts, and it holds:* if every ruin lives in the gap, the plan's only weak point should be its only gap. NUMBERS 8u: the ten are "most of each owned from January 2027, all ten from January 2028", and "a missed deposit is in the payments' set only". That one gap is exactly what IPS l.17 names as a reason to change ("a missed deposit"); the other reason (a Treasury default) is the payer itself.
- *The gap in each text:* Antonio seals a three-month bond and says "My ships come home a month before the day" (1.3); John Dashwood promises "every thing in his power" and lowers it before paying a penny (S&S ch. 1-2); Micawber hands over "my I.O.U." (DC ch. 36); Fred Vincy renews his bill with Caleb Garth's signature, "having ample funds at disposal in his own hopefulness" (Middlemarch ch. 23); Nora signs, then pays by copying at night (A Doll's House, Act I); Melmotte receives the Pickering deeds "on the authority of a mere note" (WWLN ch. 69); Birotteau buys land "by promises of sale under private seal, until we are masters of the whole property" (César Birotteau); Timon's "promises fly so beyond his state" (1.2).
- **Decision served:** IPS l.15 ("whatever figure you choose is money you already own") and the three dated purchases (GOLDMINE 27). **Source:** the eight texts quoted above, each read at source (rows 1-2); NUMBERS 8s, 8u. **Part:** 2.2 or the opening of 3b; 3f for the first plan. **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows cash-flow matching; nobody has shown that literature's financial ruins share one structure and that the IPS's two reasons are that structure's two remaining weak points.

**Idea 2. The money usually existed; it came on the wrong day.**
(a) In Act 5 Portia tells Antonio that "three of your argosies / Are richly come to harbour suddenly", after his bond was forfeit; Birotteau's uncle sees that "later you may borrow on the lands about the Madeleine", but the notes fall due "the 15th"; Lopakhin's plan to lease the land for villas would earn "at least twenty-five thousand roubles a year", yet the orchard is sold on 22 August.
(b) The danger to a dated promise is often not that the venture is worthless but that its value is not cash on that date, and that was the shape of the first plan: shares were expected to be more than enough, but all ten payments had to be bought on one date, 1 January 2033 (NUMBERS 8s), just as Antonio expected "thrice three times the value of this bond" with one month to spare.
(c) The parallel breaks because shares can also lose value for good, as Fred Vincy's horse does when it goes lame (Middlemarch ch. 24); the break teaches that a Treasury maturing just before each payment (IPS l.13) answers both failures at once, because it fixes the sum and the date together.
- **Decision served:** ten Treasuries, each maturing just before its payment; the standard "paid even if cash waiting earns nothing" (NUMBERS 8u). **Source:** Merchant of Venice 1.3, 5.1; César Birotteau (Pillerault); The Cherry Orchard Act I (West); Middlemarch ch. 24; NUMBERS 8s. **Part:** 3a; 3f (the first plan, told beside its one turn "the first plan's payments were in pencil"). **Pedigree or aha:** "illiquid, not insolvent" is pedigree to a finance judge; Act 5 is the aha, since nearly everyone remembers the pound of flesh and almost no one remembers that the ships came home.

**Idea 3. A promise to your own cause has no Shylock.**
(a) John Dashwood promises his dying father to help his stepmother and sisters, plans "a thousand pounds a-piece", and in one talk with his wife lowers it to five hundred, then "A hundred a year", then "A present of fifty pounds, now and then", then to "sending them presents of fish and game".
(b) Nobody could make John pay, because his promise had no figure and no creditor, and Laura's ten payments are the same kind of promise: they are owed to her own residency, which will never sue her, so the only thing that holds them is that she buys them in January 2027, before any later year can reason them down.
(c) The parallel breaks because John is talked down by greed, while Laura's later reasons would be good ones (a recession, a building that costs more); the break teaches that good reasons are the greater danger, which is why IPS l.17 lists only two reasons to change and adds "A fall in the market is not one of them".
- *A second lesson in the same chapter:* Fanny's mother paid annuities to old servants out of her income, and "Her income was not her own, she said, with such perpetual claims on it". Laura's ten are paid for out of the two deposits (2027 and 2028), so no claim sits on her income as a writer after 2028.
- *In the register's own verbs (NUMBERS 8u):* John never "broke" his promise; he called fifty pounds now and then "amply discharging my promise". He lowered a figure he had never stated, and lowering is honest only while nobody counts on the figure. Mrs Dashwood was already counting. Laura lowers only pencil figures nobody has been told.
- **Decision served:** buying the ten in January 2027; the short list of reasons to change. **Source:** Sense and Sensibility ch. 1-2; NUMBERS 8u; IPS l.17. **Part:** 3a, 3b. **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Precommitment is pedigree (cards Q7, Q7b); the new part is who enforces a promise to your own project (no one, so the purchase must), and that the erosion comes in reasonable steps, each defensible.

**Idea 4. The patron whose promises flew beyond his state.**
(a) Timon of Athens opens with a Poet and a Painter waiting on a rich patron's "Magic of bounty"; his steward sees that "His promises fly so beyond his state / That what he speaks is all in debt", and when the bills come Timon says "I am wealthy in my friends", and his friends answer "in a joint and corporate voice / That now they are at fall, want treasure".
(b) Laura is the writer and the artist of that first scene, and with the residency she becomes the patron, so the play shows the two mistakes her plan refuses: promising beyond what she owns (IPS l.15), and counting friends' money as hers when their budgets fall in the same bad year as everything else (IPS l.9 and l.11).
(c) The parallel breaks because Timon gives on impulse and borrows to give, while Laura borrows nothing; the break teaches that co-sponsors are not a lender of last resort but part of the same risk as her career, which is why her half kept, promised to no one, and not a friend, covers a co-sponsor falling short (IPS l.15).
- **Decision served:** the career as shares she cannot sell, leaning the portfolio the other way; no payment on anybody else's money; the half kept. **Source:** Timon of Athens 1.1, 1.2, 2.2 (Folger). **Part:** 3d (co-sponsors), 3a (career). **Pedigree or aha:** aha; Timon is rarely read, and the Poet-and-Painter opening makes the client the play's first character and its last mistake.

**Idea 5. Completing the sum: Fred Vincy's horse against the 2028 deposit.**
(a) Fred owes a bill of £160 that Caleb Garth has signed; he puts £80 safely with his mother, then takes it back to swap horses because "he meant to make the sum complete", the horse goes lame five days before the bill is due, and the Garths pay with "the ninety-two pounds that I have put by for Alfred's premium".
(b) Laura's plan may also need completing, and IPS l.13 does it the opposite way: if yields fall, "we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit", before any of that deposit goes to shares, so safe money is never put back into a venture to finish a promise.
(c) The parallel breaks because Fred's debt was owed to a creditor and Laura's top-up comes from her own next deposit; the break teaches who pays when a pencil figure fails: not the promiser but whoever relied on it with their own earmarked money, which is the co-sponsors' position in 2031 ("her figure is their target", SD-1).
- *Fred's model:* the night before the swap, his sum showed "the deficit temporarily thrown on Mr. Garth would at the utmost be twenty-five pounds". It left out the horse's temper. The first plan's model missed in 3.2% of simulated markets (NUMBERS 8s); the IPS took "one step further" (l.11) rather than trust a reassuring sum.
- **Decision served:** the order of the 2028 deposit (complete the ten, then the late-2032 fund, then VT); stated figures owned because others plan on them. **Source:** Middlemarch ch. 23-24; IPS l.13-14; NUMBERS 8s. **Part:** 2.x (the 2028 deposit), 3d. **Pedigree or aha:** aha for a finance judge; Fred is well known to readers of Eliot, never to readers of investment policy.

**Idea 6. Two tests a co-sponsor could run on any stated figure: Micawber's and Krogstad's.**
(a) Micawber hands Traddles "my I.O.U. for forty-one, ten, eleven and a half", and David is sure "this was quite the same to Mr. Micawber as paying the money"; in A Doll's House Krogstad notices that Nora's father "signed this bond three days after his death".
(b) The two scenes are two tests: Micawber's asks whether a figure is owned or only signed, and Krogstad's asks whether the backing existed on the date the figure was written; every figure Laura states passes both, because it is owned in Treasuries before the day she states it (the bottom from January 2028, the top after the move of 1 January 2031, NUMBERS 8r and 8u).
(c) The parallel breaks because Micawber is a debtor and Nora a forger, and Laura is neither; the break teaches why the definition of ink needs the word "owned" (readers hear "inked" as "signed", card Q42): left alone, the word would carry Micawber's meaning.
- **Decision served:** the rule that a figure is stated only once owned; the definition of ink at first use. **Source:** David Copperfield ch. 36; A Doll's House Act I (Sharp); card Q42; NUMBERS 8r, 8u. **Part:** 2.0 (support for the definition, no new turn), 3d's reserves. **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for Micawber's rule on income and expenditure (textbook); the I.O.U. scene and Krogstad's date are niche.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 1, the same sentence in the wrong order (rows 1-2), for 2.2 or the opening of 3b.** It turns a reading list into a finding a judge can check: every ruin happens in the gap between promise and money, and the plan's only gap is the one IPS l.17 already names.
2. **Idea 2, the ships came home (rows 4-5), for 3a and 3f.** Every judge knows the pound of flesh and almost none remembers Act 5; Antonio was nine times covered in value and one month short in time, which is the first plan's risk on 1 January 2033 in one picture.
3. **Idea 3, a promise to your own cause has no Shylock (rows 9-11), for 3a and 3b.** It explains why Laura must buy the ten rather than intend them: nobody will enforce a promise to her own residency, and Austen shows such promises fall in reasonable steps, which is why the IPS keeps only two reasons to change.
