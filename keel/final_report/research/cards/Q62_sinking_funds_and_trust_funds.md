- **Problem class:** setting aside, ahead of time, a pool that its own owner controls, to pay a fixed schedule of future sums.
- **Field that solved it first:**
  - British public finance. Robert Hamilton (1813) showed that only a surplus of income over spending is a real sinking fund; Parliament accepted it in 1828-29.
  - Government accounting (consolidation: OMB, Treasury) later put the same test into the books.
  - Municipal finance answered the raids by replacing sinking funds with serial bonds.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP the core, CHANGE the frame.** The difference is not the paper. It is who holds the paper and who owes it.
  - **For the owner as a whole, an own-debt reserve is empty.**
    - OMB (FY2000) said the trust funds' balances are available "only in a bookkeeping sense". They are "claims on the Treasury that, when redeemed, will have to be financed by raising taxes, borrowing from the public, or reducing benefits".
    - Treasury's Financial Report (FY2025, Note 22) says those Treasuries "do not represent an asset or a liability" for the government as a whole.
    - New York City's sinking funds held 91% own-city bonds in 1940 (Lent, 1955). In 1932 the city simply cancelled $774 million of its own bonds held in them: the reserve and the debt disappeared together.
  - **For the holder, the same paper is real.**
    - Social Security's special issues "can be redeemed at any time at face value" (SSA).
    - OMB (FY2025) calls them "legal claims on the Treasury, similar to Treasury securities issued to the public".
    - When the debt limit forces Treasury to redeem the federal employees' funds early, the law makes them whole. In 2025 the G Fund got back $185.5bn of principal plus $4.4bn of interest.
  - **Where Laura stands.** She stands where the trust fund stands (a holder), not where the government stands (the issuer). So her reserve is real for her.
  - **The limit.** No statute makes her whole if she spends it herself.
  - **Judge's note:** the US's own retirement reserve is, in Treasury's words, not an asset of the government as a whole. Laura's reserve holds the same kind of paper, but it is an asset for her, because she is not the one who owes it.
- **Claim 2: KEEP the borrowing; CHANGE "failed" to "circular".** Pitt's fund was not empty.
  - **What it held.** On 1 Feb 1813 the commissioners held £210.5m of the £812.0m funded debt (Hamilton, 1813), about 26% (Keel arithmetic).
  - **Why that was circular.** The stock was the nation's own debt, bought in wartime with new loans.
  - **What it cost.** Hamilton puts the "Loss to the public by the sinking fund" at £12,585,580, and above £20m with compound interest.
  - **Peace did not fix it.** Hume told the Commons in 1828 that Britain had "borrowed one hundred and six millions since 1816, to keep up the Sinking-fund".
  - **Pitt's wall.** It stopped raids, but it kept the fund "inviolate" while the country borrowed.
  - **Judge's note:** Pitt's legal wall protected the reserve's form and destroyed its substance. A wall cannot make a circular reserve real.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. There are two different failures, and they hit different reserves.**
  - **Own-debt reserves fail by circularity.** Cancellation (New York City, 1932); refilling from general revenue (the Highway Trust Fund: $275bn since 2008); suspension in a squeeze (the debt-limit funds).
  - **Real reserves in other people's assets fail by their owner's choice.**
    - Ireland's National Pensions Reserve Fund was globally invested and legally closed to withdrawals before 2025. Its owner changed the law and directed €20.7bn of it into two Irish banks in 2009-2011.
    - Norway drew NOK 302bn in 2020, above its 3% path, and stayed within its rule.
  - **Laura.** Her structure (US Treasuries, money already owned, no borrowing) removes the first failure. Only her rule guards against the second.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE.**
  - **No one else can raid it.** She owns it outright, it is no one's IOU to itself, and no lender or co-owner can draw on it.
  - **Laura can.** The IPS is not a trust (card Q3b row 33).
  - **Her guard is a rule.** The IPS's own line: "There are only 2 possible reasons we would change our plan – a missed deposit or a Treasury default. A fall in the market is not one of them."
  - **History says when that rule matters.** The raids came in crises: Walpole in 1733, New York's cities in the 1930s, Ireland in 2009-11, Norway's above-path draw in 2020.
- **Claim 5: KILL for corporate bonds.**
  - **Whose protection it is.** A corporate sinking fund is a lender's covenant, not the borrower's savings. Failing to pay into it "entitles the bondholders to the same legal rights as default in payments of interest" (Woelfel). It is an event of default (FDIC; IRS).
  - **For Laura.** She has no lender standing over her reserve, so her two-reason rule does the covenant's job.
- **The four ways history's reserves broke, set against Laura's plan (Keel synthesis, rows 40-41).**
  1. Circularity (own debt). Removed by structure: she holds the US's debt, not her own.
  2. Kept up by borrowing. Removed by structure: no borrowing. The one flow not yet owned, the 2028 deposit, is reason 1.
  3. The reserve's issuer failing to pay. This is reason 2, a Treasury default.
  4. The owner's own raid. Answered only by the rule's last sentence.
  - **Judge's note:** the IPS's two reasons and its "not one of them" line answer the three failures her structure cannot rule out on its own, one by one.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 40 (four failures, four answers).** Serves 3a and 3e. It shows a judge that the IPS's two reasons and "a fall in the market is not one of them" answer, one by one, the failures her structure cannot rule out, so they are not arbitrary.
2. **Row 15 with row 18 (New York cancels $774m; Minneapolis's "debt-creating instead of a debt-paying scheme").** Serves 3a. It is a concrete aha: a reserve in your own paper vanishes when you cancel it, and a court said so in 1895.
3. **Rows 26-27 (Treasury: trust fund Treasuries "do not represent an asset" for the government; OMB: "legal claims on the Treasury" for the holder).** Serves 3a, and 3d as one line. It is the official source that makes the aha precise: Laura stands where the holder stands.
4. **Row 37 (Ireland's €20.7bn).** Serves 3e. It is the honest limit: real assets in other people's debt were still spent by their owner in a crisis, so the report says what guards Laura's reserve (her rule) instead of claiming it cannot be raided.
5. **Row 22 (serial bonds replaced sinking funds after the 1930s).** Serves 2.2 and Table 1. Laura's ten Treasuries are the holder's side of a serial issue: one slice each January, with no pot left waiting to be raided.

## Gaps

- **GAO.** Its own statement on whether the trust funds are an asset of the government as a whole was seen only as quoted by Heritage (secondary). The GAO report itself was not read.
- **CBO.** Its own wording on the Social Security trust fund as an asset was not found. Only CBO's Highway Trust Fund work was read.
- **The 1829 Act.** The text of 10 Geo. IV c.27 was not read. Rows 6-7 rest on Hansard's account of it.
- **Walpole's 1733 sum.** Hamilton's extract says L.500,000 and is cut off after it; a secondary biography says five millions. The full page of the Inquiry needs reading.
- **Municipal defaults of the 1930s.** How much of the sinking funds' other-municipality bonds defaulted was not found. Hempel's NBER study of municipal defaults is the likely source.
- **Ireland's 2009 Act.** The law that let the Minister direct the NPRF (the Investment of the NPRF and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2009) was not read. Row 37 rests on the annual reports.
- **Norway's domestic fund.** The Government Pension Fund Norway (Folketrygdfondet) holds Norwegian assets. It was not read; it would test whether Norway keeps any reserve in its own country's paper.
- **Kelly v. Minneapolis.** The reporter citation was not confirmed; the case was read on CourtListener only.
- **Other public pension raids.** Puerto Rico, Detroit and Illinois were not read at source.
