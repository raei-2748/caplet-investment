## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim (a): CHANGE. Right question, but the sources do not say what the claim needs, and they say something better.**
  - **What Kahneman and Klein (2009) set down.** Skilled intuition needs "an environment of high validity" ("stable relationships between objectively identifiable cues and subsequent events"), plus "adequate opportunities for learning the environment (prolonged practice and feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal)" (p. 524). Their own examples: "To a good approximation, predictions of the future value of individual stocks and long-term forecasts of political events are made in a zero-validity environment" (p. 524). So by the paper's own text a team of six cannot add value by picking shares in the way the claim hopes: the paper names it as the standard case where skill cannot develop.
  - **Where Darren was right, in the paper's words.** (1) Skill is real where the conditions hold: "we have more reason to trust the intuition of an experienced fireground commander ... or the intuitions of a nurse about an infant, than to trust the intuitions of a trader about a stock" (p. 517). (2) Professionals can be good at part of the job: "financial analysts may be skilled at evaluating the likely commercial success of a firm, but this skill does not extend to the judgment of whether the stock of that firm is underpriced" (fractionation of expertise). (3) Picking is not worthless in a low-validity world: "weak regularities ... can sometimes support the development of algorithms that do better than chance", and algorithms beat people when "validity is so low that human difficulties in detecting weak regularities and in maintaining consistency of judgment are critical" (p. 524). VT is the algorithm in that sentence, a fixed rule that holds the whole market; the picker is the human whose edge the paper puts at risk.
  - **Why the conditions do not hold for a top Laura must state three years ahead (Keel inference on the paper's list).** Regular environment: no, a share's value three years out is the zero-validity case. Prolonged practice: no, the team makes the 2028 purchase once and the 2031 statement once. Rapid, unequivocal feedback: no, the answer arrives in 2031 and the payments run to 2042; the paper says finance professionals' feedback on long-term judgments is "delayed, sparse, and ambiguous" (p. 523). The team did meet the conditions in one place: choosing which Treasury matches which January (printed cash flows, a quoted price, ten repetitions). That is the only picking in the plan, and it is the regular, fast-feedback kind (Keel inference; compare Q175 idea 3).
  - **Judge's note:** a judge who expects "index funds because efficient markets" gets something else: the authors who defended expert intuition and the one who attacked it jointly name stock prices as the textbook case where nobody can learn the skill, and name an algorithm as the best remaining tool. Darren's charge, said fairly, becomes "is a fixed rule a copout?" and the paper answers that where validity is low the rule is what works.
- **Claim (b): CHANGE.** Keep the protocol as method; kill "is what makes a disagreement productive" as a claim about outcomes, and kill any suggestion the team named evidence in advance.
  - **What the protocol asks (Mellers, Hertwig and Kahneman, 2001, Table 1, rule 2):** "The participants should seek to identify results that would change their mind, at least to some extent, and should explicitly anticipate their interpretations of outcomes that would be inconsistent with their theoretical expectations. These predictions should be recorded by the arbiter to prevent future disagreements about remembered interpretations." Rule 4: "Accept in advance that the initial study will be inconclusive."
  - **What the inventor says it achieves.** Not changed minds: "We can expect that even successful collaborations will end with few minds changing and we can expect adversaries to renege on their commitments to critical experiments", because of the "15 IQ point benefit" (once the result is known, people find an extra wrinkle that rescues their theory). The gains he names are new facts accepted by all, narrowed differences, "considerable mutual respect" (2003, p. 729), and that "the adversaries ended up on friendlier terms than they started" (Edge lecture). Large 2020s projects say the same: "Rather than producing a clear 'winner', the most common outcome was a deeper understanding of the problem space" (Reflections, 2025).
  - **Could the copout argument have been a test both sides accept?** As a question of conditions, yes, and rows 3-9 show it can be answered from the literature on one reading. As a contest of outcomes, no: the one fact that would decide it (does a picked top beat a world-fund top by 2031?) is one three-year observation. Keel arithmetic on the Berkshire bet's own table (Berkshire, 2017, 2016 letter): in the first year of the bet, 2008, all five funds beat the index (-16.5% to -30.1% against -37.0%); over nine years the funds gained 2.9% to 62.8% against the index's 85.4%. A one-year or three-year read would have shown either answer. So any "test" before 2031 settles nothing about skill and must change nothing in the plan.
  - **What could still be run before 2031 without changing the plan:** nothing that decides VT, because the plan is frozen and Table 3 is symptoms and IPS l.17's two reasons (card Q223: no new rule or trigger). The only honest run is a desk test already done here (the three conditions against the plan's two halves, rows 3-9), which needs no data. A paper log of Darren's basket against VT, kept off WInS, would be an experiment in learning, not a decision rule, and one three-year window cannot tell skill from luck.
  - **Judge's note:** the man who invented the protocol predicts adversaries will go back on their pre-commitments, and designs it with an arbiter keeping the record because of that. "Naming evidence in advance" is the start of the method, not its guarantee.
- **Claim (c): KEEP, with two changes.** The collaboration literature does say to report the other side at full strength, and gives forms for doing it. Change one: the literature's phrase is "engaging each other's strongest arguments" (Clark et al., 2022, abstract), not "steelmanning". Change two: the report cannot put words in Darren's mouth. Only what the record holds is his ("a copout"; pick stocks and ETFs; EWT and GLD raised); the strongest published form of the argument (Keynes's record; Protégé Partners' own case; Grossman and Stiglitz, all in Q175) is introduced as "the strongest version of that argument", never as what he said.
  - **Forms the literature gives:** (1) say first what both sides agree on, then give the disagreements one at a time (Ariely, Kahneman and Loewenstein joint comment, described in Kahneman, 2003, p. 729); (2) set out each side's origin and stance before the answer (Kahneman and Klein, 2009, "Two Perspectives"); (3) after the common result, each side's own interpretation in its own paragraph, then "Both adversarial collaboration teams agree ..." (Martel et al., 2024); (4) the length of each side's separate discussion fixed in advance (Mellers et al., Table 1, rule 5).
  - **What goes wrong without it.** "The feature that makes most critiques intellectually useless is a focus on the weakest argument of the adversary ... refute the weakest argument in that caricature, and declare the total destruction of the adversary's position" (Kahneman, Edge lecture). And "hardly anyone ever admits an error or acknowledges learning anything from the other" (Kahneman, 2003, p. 729).
  - **Judge's note:** the paper that gave the field its method records the authors' "sharpest differences" as "emotional rather than intellectual" (Kahneman and Klein, 2009, p. 518): one admires experts, one enjoys their comeuppance. A team that says its disagreement was about what it should feel safe doing, not about facts, is saying something the literature's best case also says.
- **Claim (d): KEEP, with the dates fixed.** See rows 29-32. The bet began 1 Jan 2008 (agreed 19 Dec 2007); each side bought $500,000 face of zero-coupon Treasuries for $318,250; "late in 2012" both sides agreed to sell them (at 95.7 per 100 of face, a yield of 0.88%) and buy 11,200 Berkshire B shares; Buffett "agreed to make up any shortfall" if the shares sold at year-end 2017 produced less than $1 million; the charity received $2,222,279 (January 2018). The letters give no month for the switch, so "November 2012" in Q175 row 19 is not confirmed and must read "late in 2012". The bonds were strips, which Wharton bars for Laura this year: the parallel is the act, never the instrument.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 7 (an algorithm wins when validity is low; VT is the rule).** 3f or 2.3, one clause. It answers "copout" in the authors' own words: where prices already hold what is knowable, the consistent rule is the best tool, and what looked like declining to choose was choosing the rule.
2. **Row 1 with rows 3 and 6 (the two conditions, and why three years ahead fails them).** 3f, one sentence of "what we understand now": conditions, not conviction. The team can say the argument turned from "are we brave enough" to "could anyone learn this skill in three years", which is the only reading a judge can check.
3. **Row 5 (fractionation: skilled at the firm, not at the price).** 3f, Darren's case at its strongest: analysts and pickers really do know firms; the gap is the price. It is the fairest form of his charge and leads straight to the team's own picks of ten Treasuries, where the conditions hold.
4. **Row 22 with row 13 (the 15 IQ point benefit; a fair test is written before the result).** 3f's method, or Table 3's note: why the plan's two reasons to change are fixed before the first dollar. A judge who knows the protocol sees why that is not stubbornness.
5. **Rows 29-30 with row 32 (Buffett's bet: prize in Treasuries, the switch "late in 2012" only with a guarantor, year one against nine years).** 3c, one clause, never 3f. The exact dates and the guarantee make the parallel true; the table shows a three-year window cannot settle a picking argument.
