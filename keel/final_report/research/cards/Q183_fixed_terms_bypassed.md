## How the two-exit rule was bypassed, from the sources (notes)

- **The rule.** Polling days every five years (s.1); an early election only on a motion backed by two-thirds of all
  seats or a no-confidence vote not reversed in 14 days (s.2); "Parliament cannot otherwise be dissolved" (s.3(2)).
  Two things sat outside the list: the Prime Minister could move polling day up to "two months later" with both
  Houses' approval (s.1(5)-(7)), and "This Act does not affect Her Majesty's power to prorogue Parliament" (s.6(1)).
- **Autumn 2019.** The Act's early-election motion won a simple majority but fell short of two-thirds on 4 September
  (298 to 56), 9 September (293 to 46) and 28 October (299 to 70; JC note 39). A five-week prorogation was advised in late August; on 24 September the Supreme Court held the
  advice "unlawful, void and of no effect" (Miller, press summary).
- **The bypass.** On 29 October the Commons passed a two-section Bill naming 12 December, after rejecting Labour's
  amendment for 9 December by 315 to 295; third reading 438 to 20 (Hansard, Division 16). The Lords took every stage on
  30 October and "unanimously approved" the Bill "with no substantive debate" (JC para 49); Royal Assent on 31 October. The Act did
  not amend the 2011 Act: 12 December "is to be treated as a polling day appointed under section 2(7)" (s.1(2)), so the
  bypass ran through the old Act's own machinery.
- **The repeal.** The 2022 Act repealed the 2011 Act (s.1), revived the old prerogative "as if the Fixed-term Parliaments
  Act 2011 had never been enacted" (s.2(1)), barred courts from questioning it (s.3), kept one fixed end, a Parliament
  "dissolves at the beginning of the day that is the fifth anniversary of the day on which it first met" (s.4), and
  repealed the 2019 Act (Schedule para 32).

## What the reviewers concluded (Joint Committee, 24 March 2021; notes)

- The Act "very clearly fulfilled its immediate political purpose": the 2010-15 Parliament and the Coalition both lasted
  the full term (para 35); its purpose was to let the Coalition form, since the smaller party "wanted an absolutely clear
  commitment that there would be no snap election" (para 31, Lord O'Donnell).
- It aimed only at a "default expectation" of a full term (para 41).
- The two-thirds bar "lacks credibility given that, in 2019, it was overridden by bespoke primary legislation"
  (Summary); a supermajority cannot be entrenched "where Parliament remains legally sovereign" (para 48); "in practice it
  did not do so" (para 50); "No Parliament can bind its successor" (para 29).
- The override did not beat the bar on numbers: the 2019 Act had "the support of 440 MPs (six more than would have been
  required" for two-thirds); "The only substantive difference" from the motion refused on 28 October was that the Bill named polling
  day, "removing the Prime Minister's discretion to recommend the date" (para 39).
- Naming some confidence motions in statute "had the effect of undermining a shared understanding" of the rest (para
  76); the 14-day period "serves no useful purpose" (para 77).
- Their repair, had repeal not been settled: a simple majority, any early-election motion "to stipulate when polling day
  would take place", and no statutory no-confidence route (para 80); "the relevant legislation should not contain
  supermajority provisions" (para 50).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. The 2019 bypass was about a date, not a vote count

- (a) On 28 October 2019 an early-election motion under the Act won 299 votes to 70, far short of the 434 its two-thirds
  door needed; the next day the Commons passed a two-section Act naming 12 December by 438 votes to 20, more than
  two-thirds, and the reviewers found the only real difference was that the new Act named the day, "removing the Prime
  Minister's discretion to recommend the date".
- (b) Laura's plan leaves no promised date in anyone's hands: each payment rests on a Treasury or Treasury fund whose end
  date is in its own terms, and the three steps fall on the first trading day of January 2027, 2028 and 2031, so a
  co-sponsor knows from the start the day after which the top Laura names is owned.
- (c) It breaks because the MPs who held out in 2019 feared what the Prime Minister would do with the date, while nobody
  fears Laura's, and moving her 2031 step to April or July would change no figure she states; the break teaches that her
  dates matter to co-sponsors as the day they can start counting, which is why the report names January 2031 and not only
  the IPS's "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031".
- **Decision it serves:** the three dated steps (GOLDMINE 27); the move on the first trading day of 2031 (NUMBERS 8r:
  1 April or 1 July still states $115,000, the bottom unchanged); Table 3 row 5 (a trade day may move "day by day", never
  past the 2031 meetings). **Part:** 3c ("two inkings, two dates"), 2.2.
- **Source:** Joint Committee (2021) para 39 and note 39; Hansard, HC 29 Oct 2019, Division 16; Early Parliamentary
  General Election Act 2019 s.1; UK Parliament news, 29 Oct 2019.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The usual telling, the parent card's included, is that a simple majority beat a
  two-thirds rule; the record shows the two-thirds was met the next day once the date was written into the law, a
  finding no finance judge will know and one that maps onto the plan's own dates.

### 2. The one exit in Laura's own hands

- (a) Both of the Act's exits were votes of the House it bound, and the reviewers described one door for when "there
  will sometimes be a broad consensus that an early election is needed" and one for when no government can keep the
  House's confidence; the first was used in 2017 and gone around in 2019, while the second never ended a Parliament.
- (b) Laura's two reasons split the same way, a Treasury default being something nobody in the plan can choose and a
  missed deposit being her own act from earnings a recession makes "later and smaller", so a downturn could come in
  through the deposit door; what limits the harm is what lies behind that door, which is only money not yet bought: the
  2033 payment's last $3,016 at today's rates, the extra Treasuries, the bottom and the top.
- (c) It breaks because the Act's "want" door stayed open for the whole five years, while Table 3 reads a missed deposit as
  nothing received by 31 March 2028, three years before Laura names a range; that date is Keel's reading of the IPS, not
  the IPS's own words, so the report gives it only as the way the table recognises a missed deposit.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.17's first reason; the order of the 2028 deposit (IPS l.13, then l.15); Table 3 row 1.
  **Part:** 3e.
- **Source:** Joint Committee (2021) paras 42 and 62; case l.43-45; IPS l.9, l.17; NUMBERS 5; CANON A34, D9.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree with a correction. The structure (a "want" exit and a "cannot" exit) is new to the
  store and sharpens Q7b row 33, which holds for reason 2 and only in part for reason 1; the report needs it mainly to
  say the deposit reason correctly.

### 3. What can be voted away, and what cannot

- (a) "No Parliament can bind its successor," the reviewers wrote, and the record shows it: the House the Act bound went
  around it in 2019, and in 2022 Parliament repealed it and revived the old power "as if the Fixed-term Parliaments Act
  2011 had never been enacted".
- (b) Caplet's letter binds no better, since Laura and Caplet can rewrite the two reasons any day without anyone else's
  vote, but a rewrite of the letter cannot change what the Treasuries she holds will pay, because the US Treasury wrote
  those amounts, not her; so every figure she states is bound by owning, and only the steps not yet taken are bound by
  the letter alone.
- (c) It breaks because owning does not stop Laura selling, which is an act on the money rather than a vote on the words,
  and the plan names no reason to sell; and unlike the Act, whose every date stayed a rule until polling day, each of
  Laura's rules is spent on its date and replaced by a Treasury, so a rewrite can reach only steps not yet taken, and she
  states no figure before the step that buys it.
- **Decision it serves:** state a figure only after the step that buys it (IPS l.15: "so whatever figure you choose is
  money you already own"); held to the end (l.13, CANON A8); the two reasons carry no stated figure (l.17). **Part:**
  3e ("the rule is not what keeps the figures"), 3c.
- **Source:** Joint Committee (2021) para 29 and Summary; Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 s.2(1); IPS
  l.13, l.15, l.17; NUMBERS 4 and 8r.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Laura's letter is less entrenched than a British statute, and that costs her nothing, because
  by the time anyone relies on one of her figures the letter that announced it has been replaced by a Treasury whose
  payment someone else wrote. Q165 idea 1 holds "keeping a held Treasury takes no act"; this adds who can change what,
  and when.

### 4. The best companion: America's debt limit, the same kind of rule seen from the other side

- (a) The US Congress caps its own borrowing by law, and in the Treasury's words it "has always acted when called upon to
  raise the debt limit", 78 times since 1960, each time by its own new act.
- (b) Once the 2028 deposit is in, a Treasury default is the plan's only reason left to change, and the debt limit is
  the road by which the US has come nearest one, so the same weakness that undid Britain's Act (a rule its makers set
  aside when it bites) is the strength behind Laura's last exit; it predicts something checkable, that the next
  standoff, whose deadline is projected for about August 2027 to May 2028, ends the same way, and Table 3 already watches
  that deadline.
- (c) It breaks because a habit is not a law: the standoffs since 2011 cost extra borrowing and the plan rests no payment
  on Congress's habit, since each payment's Treasury pays out before its date; the break teaches that the debt limit's
  record explains why the second reason is remote, never why a payment is safe.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.17's second reason; Table 3 row 3 (a debt-limit deadline within a month of a cash date:
  "Never: a sale ahead of it"). **Part:** 3e (one clause), Table 3's note.
- **Source:** U.S. Department of the Treasury, Debt Limit page; store Q15-024 (thirteen impasses 2011-2025, all ended by
  suspension or increase), Q30-025 (the X-date window, about Aug 2027 to May 2028), H3-013 (each payment's money arrives
  before its date).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha in the pairing. Every finance judge knows the debt limit; almost none has set it beside a
  failed self-binding law to see that the rule's weakness is what keeps Treasuries paying.
