## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. There are two forms, not one, and much of the field outside the US has spent fifteen years (2008-2023) moving away from the single cause.**
  - **The US form: the outcome is the subject, the cause the predicate, in one to four sentences.** "The probable cause of this accident was the ingestion of large birds into each engine" (US Airways 1549, 35 words). Amtrak 188: three sentences, 81 words: the cause; "Contributing to the accident was the lack of a positive train control system"; "Contributing to the severity of the injuries were ...". San Bruno (a gas pipeline, NTSB/PAR-11/01): four sentences, 195 words, the cause alone 100 (Keel count). The NTSB prints it twice: in the Executive Summary (p. xii), before the evidence, and again at 3.2 (p. 127), after the list of findings at 3.1.
  - **The international form is a list, placed after the evidence.** ICAO's Final Report format puts "Conclusions" third, after "Factual information" and "Analysis"; its synopsis is chronological, "concluding with a brief résumé of the circumstances"; and "States may use either 'causes' or 'contributing factors', or both" (Annex 13, 13th ed., 2024, Appendix 1). The UK AAIB's Leicester report lists 3 causal and 4 contributory factors. Canada's TSB listed 23 causes and contributing factors, 19 findings as to risk and 9 other findings for one runaway train (Field, BC, 2019), opening "an accident rarely results from a single cause".
  - **The move away from "cause".** Australia (2008): "Assessing a single 'probable cause' can be potentially misleading or simplistic". IFALPA (2023) asks ICAO to remove "cause" altogether, because the causes section is the part "the media and public tend to read", giving "the (false) impression that the occurrence was the result of a few easily-avoided errors"; six states (Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Israel, New Zealand, Uruguay) never use it or have stopped.
  - **"What would have prevented it" sits in the definition, not always in the sentence.** Annex 13: contributing factors are what "if eliminated, avoided or absent, would have reduced the probability of the accident ... or mitigated the severity". The NTSB names the missing defence inside a contributing sentence ("the lack of a positive train control system"; San Bruno's pressure-test exemptions, "which likely would have detected the installation defects") and keeps its fixes for a separate recommendations section.
  - **Finance writes the US form.** "Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) failed because of a textbook case of mismanagement by the bank", then three one-line causes (Barr, 28 Apr 2023: four sentences, 55 words, Keel count). The FDIC split the trigger from the condition: "The primary cause of SBNY's failure was illiquidity precipitated by contagion ... However, the root cause of SBNY's failure was poor management."
  - **What every form shares:** the outcome and its cause in the same sentence, joined by a fixed linking phrase ("the probable cause of ... was", "Contributing to ... was", the VA's "led to" and "increased the likelihood that", the TSB's "If ..., increasing the risk of"); and a stated place for what was ruled out (San Bruno: "the following were not factors in this accident: seismic activity, corrosion, direct third-party damage, or drug use") and for what held (US Airways 1549: "Contributing to the survivability of the accident was ...").
  - **Judge's note:** much of aviation and rail investigation moved away from the single cause because accidents in complex systems rarely have one. Laura's promises are the rare case where the honest list is short, because they rest on Treasuries she owns: a US default is in every promise's failure set, a missed deposit in the payments' only, and no fall in any price in any (NUMBERS 8u). The short form is earned here, and 3e can say why in one clause.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Specific outcome statements beat generic labels; "first" is not isolated by any test found; "one number" is not supported by these sources; and "retain" was not measured.**
  - **Outcome-led, specific warnings help.** The FCA's warning "You could lose all of your money invested in this product." then "This is a high-risk investment ..." beat "Capital at risk" for every risky product (log-odds +0.74 to +1.70 in wave 1; Keel arithmetic: odds of a correct reading about 2 to 5.5 times; Feddersen et al., 2020). Its 2022 experiment (6,618 and 4,008 adults): salient, informative warnings raised comprehension 6-10 percentage points (9-17%), and "changes in the content of the risk warnings are a key driver" (Delias et al., 2022).
  - **Order was never tested on its own.** The 2022 "Basic Info" warning led with the class ("This is a high-risk investment. You could lose all your ..."), the 2020 one with the outcome; both worked, and "No single individual treatment is more effective than the others" (FCA, 2022). Wogalter et al. (1987) found a warning "placed before procedural instructions is more likely to lead to compliance": that is the warning before the steps, not the outcome before the cause.
  - **Where answer-first lost.** Narratives beat the inverted pyramid for comprehension of complex health science among 461 lay adults, with lower effort, but readers with little interest may stop before the facts arrive (Ameseder et al., 2025). And a two-page SEC risk summary at the front did not change retail investors' risk judgments (p = 0.456, N = 100), while tying each risk to the figure it affects did (p = 0.007; Durney et al., 2021).
  - **The cost of an outcome warning:** it can spread. The 2020 warning led some readers to link shares ISAs with "losing more than their deposit"; the 2022 warnings made readers miss the weaker warning on the stocks page, and a third of control readers (33-36%) saw no warning at all.
  - **Judge's note:** a summary of risks at the front of a filing changed nothing for retail investors; linking each risk to the number it moves did. So the order that matters is promise by promise, not risks first.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Build the limits around how each promise fails; keep the case's five areas named, in the case's order, for the assumptions.**
  - **Structure beats a list.** The same facts were recalled better as comparison, problem/solution or causation than as "a collection of descriptions about a topic" (Meyer & Freedle, 1984, two studies). Tying each risk to what it touches gave "greater causal coherence" (Durney et al., 2021). The supervisors' two-step form (card Q109) and NUMBERS 8u are causal structures; five areas read one after another are a collection.
  - **But the case's areas must stay visible.** The case asks teams to "identify and explain their assumptions about investment performance, the timing of cash flows, outside funding, the effect of inflation on portfolio projections and facility costs, and the financial flexibility Laura should preserve" (case l.146-148), and Wharton to "Clearly identify important assumptions, uncertainties, and limitations". Signals steer what is kept: with half the topics signalled, "unsignaled content was recalled more poorly" than with none signalled (Lorch & Lorch, 1996). A judge checking the case's list must find all five named; numbered signals aided free recall of ten target sentences (1986, N = 120).
  - **Two lists that sound alike get merged.** Kleimann's 2018 readers were "unclear if differences in phrasing signaled a significant difference or merely a difference in phrasing", drew "a false parallel", and "simply took information from the first bullet they read". The IPS's two reasons to change and the ways a promise breaks (NUMBERS 8u, "The two lists") need one clause that says they differ, and 8u's three verbs need a gloss at first use, or a reader hears only wording.
  - **The case's order costs nothing.** Its list has no causal order of its own (it is a checklist), so keeping it for the assumptions spends no clarity, while the failure sentences carry the structure.
  - **Judge's note:** the research does not say "re-order the case's list"; it says "do not let a list carry the argument". The assumptions can stay the case's checklist while the limits become three cause-and-promise sentences.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 35 (3e, Table 3):** a two-page summary of risks at the front of a filing changed nothing for retail investors (p = 0.456); tying each risk to the figure it moves did (p = 0.007). It is the tested case for building 3e's limits promise by promise (NUMBERS 8u) instead of as a list.
2. **Rows 12 and 16 with row 6 (3e):** much of aviation and rail investigation moved away from the single cause because a complex accident "rarely results from a single cause" (TSB); Laura's promises are the rare case where the honest failure list is short, because they rest on owned Treasuries. The judge's aha: the short form is earned, and 3e can say why.
3. **Row 3 (3e):** investigators name what they ruled out ("the following were not factors in this accident"). One clause can rule out any fall in prices from every promise's failure set (IPS l.17, tested by NUMBERS 8u), the suspect a judge expects.
4. **Rows 14-15 (Table 3's note, Table 3):** Canada's TSB defines a finding as to risk as "not ... a factor in this occurrence but could have adverse consequences in future", written "If ..., increasing the risk of ...": a real precedent, in one line, for what a Table 3 watch is and how its row reads.
5. **Rows 38 and 36 (3e):** readers merge lists that sound alike and keep what is signalled. 3e names the case's five areas as signals, and one clause tells the two lists apart, or a judge hears "only two things can break the plan".
