## Verdicts in one screen

**Claim 1: CHANGE. The result holds in the lab. In real teams, disagreement on its own does not help.**

- **What was found:**
  - In a laboratory study, both debate methods "led to higher quality recommendations and assumptions than consensus".
  - Dialectical inquiry also beat devil's advocacy on "the quality of assumptions brought to the surface".
  - But consensus groups "expressed more satisfaction and desire to continue to work with their groups and greater acceptance of their groups' decisions" (Schweiger, Sandberg & Ragan 1986, abstract).
  - "Fast-advancing middle managers" over several sessions gave the same result: "significantly higher quality decisions", "lower acceptance", and "no differences between dialectical inquiry and devil's advocacy groups". Practice improved quality and cut the time to decide (Schweiger, Sandberg & Rechner 1989).
- **With whom, and how big:**
  - Every test is a written case analysed in a lab, with the groups' recommendations rated by judges.
  - The groups were part-time students (30 groups of four, a figure taken from a review table, not the paper) and middle managers.
  - Neither abstract gives an effect size (UNVERIFIED; the full texts are paywalled).
- **Schwenk 1990 is not about consensus:**
  - His meta-analysis compares both methods with an "expert" approach: one recommendation and no conflict.
  - "DA is more effective than the E approach in general."
  - Dialectical inquiry's advantage was "not demonstrated" on ill-structured tasks, the kind Laura's plan is.
  - "Neither the DA nor the DI was shown to be a more effective method."
- **Against:**
  - "High-consensus groups generally had higher performance" (Schwenk & Cosier 1993).
  - An executive game found satisfaction higher with debate, "directly opposed to Schweiger et al's findings" (Chanin & Simko 1991).
- **In real teams:**
  - Conflict over the task correlates with team performance at ρ = -.23 (De Dreu & Weingart 2003; 25 studies) and ρ = -.07 (de Wit, Greer & Jehn 2012; 92 studies). Here ρ is a correlation corrected for measurement error; below zero means more conflict went with worse performance.
  - The link is less negative, or positive, in three cases: when the task conflict is kept apart from personal conflict, in top management teams, and when the outcome measured is the quality of the decision (de Wit).
- **The claim as changed:**
  - In laboratory case studies, structured debate gave better-rated recommendations than seeking agreement, at a cost in members' satisfaction.
  - In real teams, disagreement on its own is neutral to harmful.
  - It helps when it is about the task, is kept off the person, and is settled by a test.
- **Judge's note:** the famous result is a lab result. In real teams, disagreement alone goes with slightly worse performance (ρ -.07 to -.23). What made the lab method work, and what the three moments carry, is a test that settles the argument.

**Claim 2: CHANGE. It holds best with working adults. No moment used a devil's advocate.**

- **Nemeth, Brown & Rogers 2001: the sizes.**
  - Number of solutions: 2.76 with an authentic minority, against 1.86 with a consistent devil's advocate and 1.84 with no dissenter.
  - Rated quality alone was "not statistically significant (F(2, 52) = 1.28)".
  - A measure combining quantity and quality was "marginally significant" (p < .06), with planned contrasts favouring authentic dissent.
  - The groups were female undergraduates chatting by computer with a scripted dissenter.
  - So the claim holds for how many ideas a group produces, and only weakly for how good they are.
- **The stronger test, with working adults:** 201 employees and managers in three-person groups. "Heterogeneity was more effective in preventing a confirmatory information-seeking bias than devil's advocacy was" (Schulz-Hardt, Jochims & Frey 2002). A confirmatory information-seeking bias means looking only for evidence that supports the choice already made.
- **An assigned critic is not useless:**
  - It beat the expert approach (Schwenk 1990).
  - The best result combined real disagreement with a structure: escalation of commitment was "reduced in heterogeneous groups that used the devil's advocacy procedure" (Greitemeyer, Schulz-Hardt & Frey 2009).
  - Tone matters. Face-to-face groups with a "carping" critic "reached the lowest solution quality, yet reached decision consensus in the fewest voting rounds" (Valacich & Schwenk 1995).
- **Which kind of challenge each moment was** (RANKING_5; BLIND_POOL_3 E1, E3, E4):
  - "A copout" (Mon 5 Oct) was authentic minority dissent inside the team: a teammate's own belief, not a role.
  - "Too timid" (28-29 Sep) was an unassigned outside critique. Readers asked only to rank six anonymous plans raised it on their own. Who the readers were is never said in the report.
  - The knife edge (28-29 Sep) had no dissenter. The challenge was the team's own headline set against every close of 2026.
  - So 3f never says "devil's advocate".
- **The find that matters most for 3f:**
  - The task was a "hidden profile": the facts that point to the best choice are spread among the members, so a group finds it only by pooling what each knows.
  - Groups whose members began with different wrong choices solved it 27% of the time, against 7% for groups that agreed on a wrong one, and 62% when one member was right from the start.
  - How much the members disagreed made no difference (Schulz-Hardt et al. 2006; the rates are from Schulz-Hardt & Mojzisch 2012).
- **Judge's note:** the copout's proposal (pick stocks and ETFs) lost, and that is where dissent pays. Dissent improves a decision even when the dissenter turns out wrong, because it forces the discussion that finds the reasons.

**Claim 3: SPLIT. "Premortem" is dropped for every moment. "The bar before the numbers" is kept, and it is the stronger half.**

- **The premortem evidence is small and measures confidence.** A premortem means imagining the plan has already failed and asking why.
  - Students: 178 students in groups. Confidence in a plan fell 25.0 points after a premortem, 14.0 after listing pros and cons, and 12.4 after listing cons only (Veinott, Klein & Wiggins 2010).
  - Managers and engineers: premortem teams found "better quality risks ... and ... more black swan risks" than brainstorming teams (Gallop, Willy & Bischoff 2016, abstract).
  - Army cadets: 52 cadets had "fewer fouls" (Peabody 2017). Done alone, a premortem "produced the least change in confidence".
  - Game teams: confidence rose from 72.8 to 83.9 once fixes were written (Roose et al. 2023).
  - No study found here tests whether plans actually succeed more often.
- **No moment was a premortem.** The knife edge comes closest, but it looked up a real failure instead of imagining one: the ten payments cost more than $300,000 on 173 of 185 closes. The completion rule was written before any order. Call it a test.
- **The bar before the numbers is well supported, from three fields:**
  - Hiring:
    - People who committed to hiring criteria before learning an applicant's gender showed no bias.
    - Those who did not "redefined the criteria for success" to fit the applicant they preferred.
    - And "perceiving one's judgments as objective and free of bias predicted greater gender bias" (Uhlmann & Cohen 2005).
  - Medicine: large heart and lung trials found a benefit in 17 of 30 (57%) before 2000 and 2 of 25 (8%) after (Kaplan & Irvin 2015). From 2000 the trials had to declare their main outcome in advance. This is an association, not a proven cause.
  - Health tests: people told of bad test news retested 52% of the time, against 18% of those told good news (Ditto & Lopez 1992).
- **Which moment shows it:** "a copout".
  - The bar was written Wed 30 Sep, before any number was run and five days before the objection on Mon 5 Oct.
  - The bar: switch only if an alternative raised the bad-case 2033 gift by $2,000, or narrowed its spread by a tenth, on every run.
  - The closest rival stopped at 0.8991 against the 0.90 bar, passing about four runs in five (30 Sep model).
- **Judge's note** (Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984, experiment 1, people who already supported the policy):
  - Telling people to be "as objective and unbiased as possible" made their bias larger. They rated the study that agreed with them 2.4 points better than the one that did not, against 1.6 with no instruction.
  - A procedure cut the gap to 0.3. The procedure was to ask whether they would rate the study the same "had exactly the same study produced results on the other side".
  - "We stayed open-minded" is the first instruction. A bar written five days before the objection is the second.

**Claim 4: CHANGE. Keep the idea, and add the bar and the luck.**

- **The claim as changed:** each moment ends on what changed, or what was tested and kept. The test is judged against a bar set before the result, with one line on what was luck.
- **One finding per moment, one line each:**
  - **The knife edge: motivated scepticism, reversed** (Ditto & Lopez 1992). People demand several doctors for bad news and one for good. The team took its good news ($7,736 of room on 25 Sep, register) to every close of 2026, where the ten cost more than $300,000 on 173 of 185. So the headline was dropped and the completion rule written. Filling near the year's cheapest close was timing, not skill (NUMBERS 8b).
  - **Too timid: consider the opposite** (Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984). The team did not resolve to be open-minded. It built the readers' plan (floors from $150,000 down to $50,000) and priced it. Each $25,000 less floor added about $2,000-3,000 in the middle and cost about $9,000-10,000 in a bad market (29 Sep model). The floor stayed.
  - **"A copout": criteria fixed first** (Uhlmann & Cohen 2005), **and dissent that pays even when it loses** (Schulz-Hardt et al. 2006). The bar came first, so 0.8991 against 0.90 counted as a fail, never as a reason to move the bar. The world fund stayed.
- **Judge's note:** a reviewer's checklist asks, "Were there dissenting opinions within the recommending team? If so, were they explored adequately?" It also warns that "an absence of dissent in a team addressing a complex problem should sound an alarm" (Kahneman, Lovallo & Sibony 2011). 3f answers both questions with dates.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 34 (Kaplan & Irvin: 57% to 8%). Serves 3f, the copout.** It is the largest measured effect of setting the bar before the numbers, from a field a judge trusts. It makes a five-day gap between the bar and the objection look like method, not luck.
2. **Row 35 (Lord, Lepper & Preston: "be unbiased" made the bias larger, the procedure cut it to 0.3). Serves 3f, all three moments, and the writers' brief for 27 Nov.** It says why the reflection shows tests rather than claiming open minds: an aha about the reflection itself.
3. **Row 21 (Schulz-Hardt et al.: dissent helps even when the dissenter is wrong, 27% against 7%). Serves 3f, the copout.** It turns a lost objection into evidence that the team decided well, without making the teammate the fool.
4. **Row 32 (Ditto & Lopez: several doctors for bad news, one for good). Serves 3f, the knife edge.** The team did the opposite of the bias with good news. The image is vivid and plain enough for a sentence.
5. **Rows 8-9 (task conflict alone: ρ -.23 and -.07). Serves 3f's closing paragraph.** It says why the disagreements are judged by their tests and not praised for existing. This is where a smart reader has it backwards.

## Gaps

- **Effect sizes.** Schwenk (1990) gives none in the abstract, and the ScienceDirect full text is paywalled. Schweiger, Sandberg & Ragan (1986): the full text is behind the AOM paywall, so the sample (30 groups of four part-time students) is second-hand, from an unattributed review table.
- **Gallop, Willy & Bischoff (2016):** the full text is paywalled. The sample of 101 managers and engineers is second-hand.
- **The premortem:** no study was found that tests whether plans that used one succeed more often. The search covered ISCRAM, HFES, a Michigan Tech thesis, a GWU thesis and the Journal of Enterprise Transformation.
- **Nemeth, Brown & Rogers (2001):** the unit counts were not settled. The table's N of 17, 21 and 19 per condition does not obviously match the methods section. The JASP companion paper (Nemeth, Connell et al. 2001) was read as an abstract only.
