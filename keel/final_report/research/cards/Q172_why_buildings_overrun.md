## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Not even an Act of Parliament could hold a building's cost; Laura's figure holds because it is money, not a cost

- (a) In April 1960 the New South Wales Parliament wrote into law that the Opera House, estimated at £4,880,000, "shall not, under any circumstances, be exceeded by more than ten per centum", then amended that section four times and paid $102 million, about nine and a half times the legal limit.
- (b) A promise about what a building will cost is a promise about other people's prices, which even a statute could not keep, while Laura's figure is a promise about Treasuries she already holds, so the plan never promises what the residency will cost, only what she will give.
- (c) It breaks on scale and on who was promising (a government limiting spending on its own landmark, not one donor giving to a small residency), and the break teaches that a rise in the building's cost does not vanish when her figure holds: it moves onto the co-sponsors' share and onto her flexibility, which is why both exist.
- **Decision it serves:** the range stated only once owned (IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own").
- **Report part:** 3c (why a figure is stated only once owned); 3e (the limit: the plan secures money, not the building).
- **Source:** Sydney Opera House Act 1960 No 29, s 2(4) as made (assented 19 April 1960) and in its last form ("eighty-five million dollars"; s 2 amended 1963, 1965, 1966, 1969); final cost $102 million "excluding cost for the organ and car park" (Museums of History NSW); £3.5m given as $7m (BBC, 2025), so two dollars a pound. Keel arithmetic: £4,880,000 is $9,760,000; ten per cent more is $10,736,000; $102,000,000 / $10,736,000 = 9.5.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Judges know the Opera House overran; almost none know that Parliament had promised in law it would not, "under any circumstances". That one clause shows the difference between promising a cost and promising money.

### Idea 2. Every famous overrun was paid by someone who could keep paying; Laura cannot, so she states only what she holds and keeps part unpromised

- (a) Sydney paid for its Opera House with 496 lotteries that sold 86.7 million tickets from 1957 until 1986, thirteen years after the Queen opened it; Scotland paid for its Parliament building out of a parliament's budget, where the auditors found "the client always pays for risk"; and San Francisco's former mayor Willie Brown defended a $300 million overrun with "Start digging a hole and make it so big, there's no alternative to coming up with the money to fill it in."
- (b) Each overrun could be paid only because the payer's money kept coming, while Laura's portfolio gets two deposits, the last in January 2028, and her career income, the IPS says, comes "later and smaller" in the same recessions that hurt shares and co-sponsors, so she does the two things a payer with a limit can do: she names only money she owns, and she keeps half the shares promised to no one for the ordinary overrun.
- (c) It breaks because in a small residency the payer who cannot walk away from a half-dug hole is usually the founder (Taiwan's Ministry of Culture tells grantees they "may not ask for more", SC-4), so a building started short of money will pull on her flexibility, and the plan can keep that money hers only by its words ("promised to no one"; the co-sponsors' pledges fall away together unless all are made by December 2032), never stop her choosing to pour it in.
- **Decision it serves:** her flexibility (IPS l.15); the range stated only once owned; 3d's December 2032 condition.
- **Report part:** 3b (why half is kept); 3e (what it cannot do).
- **Source:** Muhlen-Schulte (2024), NSW Treasury; Sydney Opera House Act 1960, s 4 (Treasurer's advances repaid from the lottery account); Audit Scotland (2004), para 2.6; Brown (2013) as quoted by Flyvbjerg (2021) and by Akst (2023); IPS l.9.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Finance judges know the famous overruns, not who paid for them. The pattern (every overrun that got paid had a payer whose money kept coming) turns Caplet's closing line into evidence: Laura's money stops, so her promises can take no risk.

### Idea 3. Ignorance may start the building, but never the money

- (a) In 1967 Albert Hirschman argued that projects get started because planners underestimate the difficulties, and get rescued because people also underestimate their own creativity, quoting the historian John Sawyer: "Had the total investment required been accurately and objectively known at the beginning, the project would not have been begun."
- (b) Laura's plan takes Hirschman's side for the building and the opposite side for her money: the case says the facility's total cost "has not been determined", so the residency may well be begun before anyone knows it, but every figure Laura names is exact, because an architect's creativity can redesign a building to fit a budget and nobody's creativity can make a Treasury pay more than it says.
- (c) It breaks on the evidence: when Flyvbjerg tested the idea on 2,062 projects, the rescue Hirschman described (benefits running further over than costs) turned up in about one project in five, and in the 24 buildings costs came in 36% over estimate while benefits came in at 99%, so not even the building can count on creativity to pay for a cost rise, which is the work her flexibility does.
- **Decision it serves:** the range stated only once owned; her flexibility.
- **Report part:** 3c; 3b.
- **Source:** Hirschman (1967); Flyvbjerg and Sunstein (2016), Table 1; Flyvbjerg (2021).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha for a finance judge. Hirschman is a development economist's classic and Flyvbjerg's test is recent. The split (ignorance allowed where creativity can repay it, refused where it cannot) is new.

### Idea 4. The overlooked reason to state a figure early: a figure that cannot fall is a budget the building can be designed to

- (a) Manufacturers using target costing fix the price first and then design the product to it, and construction's version, target value design, fixes the allowable cost before design starts; on 12 US projects studied by the method's developers, the final cost came in on average 15% below market cost.
- (b) An owned figure named in 2031 gives the residency's architect two years' notice of money that cannot shrink, which is the Opera House's order reversed (there Utzon said in 1962 that "costs should be a secondary consideration"), and it puts the hard number where Flyvbjerg says changes are cheapest, in planning, before anything is built.
- (c) It breaks because target value design needs the whole budget fixed and a team contracted to share savings, while Laura's figure is one part of a total whose other parts wait on co-sponsors, and the 15% is the developers' own result on projects they chose; the break teaches that her figure is the part of the design budget that cannot move, which carries SD-1's "her figure is their target" from the funders to the architect.
- **Decision it serves:** the 2031 timing; stating only owned figures.
- **Report part:** 3d (what an owned figure lets the residency do); 3c.
- **Source:** Zimina, Ballard and Pasquire (2012), abstract; Muhlen-Schulte (2024) for Utzon; Hole and Flyvbjerg (n.d.) for "think slow, act fast".
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Target costing is known to management accountants, but the store's case for stating early is all about the co-sponsors (SD-1, SD-3). This adds what an owned figure does for the building itself.

### Idea 5. "The client always pays for risk": Holyrood shows what her flexibility is for, and what it can never cover

- (a) Scotland's Parliament building was estimated at £90 million in January 1998, agreed at £195 million in April 2000 and forecast at £431 million in May 2004, and the auditors found that early cost reports had left out "contingency, fees, furniture and fittings, and VAT", that the floor area had grown 47%, and that "whatever construction method is adopted the client always pays for risk".
- (b) In a residency she founds, Laura's side is the client, and funders cap their grants and send any overrun home (SC-4), so an ordinary overrun lands on her; that is the first use IPS l.15 names for her flexibility, and her flexibility plus anything above the top covers a rise of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of a building budget set on the top, if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed (NUMBERS 8u).
- (c) It breaks on scale, because Holyrood's cost rose nearly fivefold from its first full estimate and a parliament could keep paying, so the honest limit is that her flexibility meets an ordinary overrun (about the 60th to 75th percentile of small buildings, card Q5d) and a Holyrood would have to be met by changing the building's size, its funders or its timetable: the plan keeps her promises safe from a Holyrood, not the building.
- **Decision it serves:** her flexibility; 3e's limits.
- **Report part:** 3b; 3e.
- **Source:** Audit Scotland (2004), Exhibits 1-2, paras 2.6, 9, 11, 16. Keel arithmetic: £431m / £90m = 4.8.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for a British reader, worth one clause. "The client always pays" is the line that explains why the founder's side, not the funders', needs the flexibility.

### Idea 6. Close the window: Laura's own figure is exposed for no time at all; the co-sponsors' pledges for two years

- (a) Flyvbjerg's rule "think slow, act fast" says plan slowly and build quickly, because "a fast production schedule closes the window during which things can go wrong": the Empire State Building took less than two years and finished 17% under budget, while the Opera House, expected to take four years, took fourteen.
- (b) The case leaves two years between naming the contribution (2031) and paying it (2033), and the plan closes that window for Laura's own figure by buying the Treasuries before she names it (the move on 1 January 2031, NUMBERS 8r), so what stays exposed in the window is building prices, the Taiwan dollar and other people's pledges, the three uses IPS l.15 names for her flexibility.
- (c) It breaks because the building's own window (design, tender, construction) has no date, is not Laura's to shorten and may open only after 2033, so the break teaches that her flexibility waits on a window the plan cannot date, and Table 3 can watch the building's timetable as well as the markets.
- **Decision it serves:** the 2031 timing; the move; her flexibility.
- **Report part:** 3c; 3e; Table 3.
- **Source:** Hole and Flyvbjerg (n.d.); Sydney Opera House (n.d.).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree. "Think slow, act fast" is the motto of a 2023 business bestseller. It earns one clause in 3c, where it names the window the move closes.

### What the history says, in one paragraph (for the lead, not prose)

**How much flexibility:** the famous overruns were all paid by payers whose money kept coming; Laura's does not, so no reserve she could hold would cover a Holyrood or an Opera House, and none should try. What the evidence supports is a reserve for the ordinary overrun on a small building (card Q5d puts a fifth to a quarter at about the 60th to 75th percentile), held back from every promise, with anything larger met by the building changing (its size, its funders, its timetable), never by her promise changing. **Stating a figure early:** every famous early figure that failed was an estimate of a cost (the Opera House's £4,880,000 written into law; Holyrood's £90 million); Laura's early figure is a count of money she holds, so it cannot overrun, it closes her own two-year window, and it gives the architect a number to design to. Its one risk is the one the IPS already names: the building's other money stays exposed, and her flexibility is the only part of her portfolio that can meet it.

## Best three for the report

1. **Row 1 with row 2 (idea 1), 3c.** An Act of Parliament promised the Opera House's cost would not be exceeded by more than ten per cent "under any circumstances", and the building cost nine and a half times the limit. A judge who knows the overrun has never heard the promise, and the contrast shows in one sentence why Laura promises money, never a cost.
2. **Row 19 (idea 2), 3b and 3e.** Every famous overrun was paid by someone whose money kept coming: a lottery for 29 years, a parliament, a city's taxpayers. Laura's money stops in January 2028 and her income shrinks in the same downturns, so she names only what she owns and keeps half the shares unpromised. That turns the IPS's closing line from a slogan into a finding.
3. **Rows 13-16 with row 27 (ideas 3 and 4), 3c and 3d.** Hirschman said ignorance gets projects started; Flyvbjerg found it rescues them about once in five. The plan allows ignorance where creativity can repay it (the design) and refuses it where nothing can (the money), and an owned figure named early becomes the one part of the building's budget the architect can design to. That is a reason to state early that the store's fundraising evidence never gave.
