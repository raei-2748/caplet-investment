## Verdicts on the four claims

1. **"A strong case states the committed money first, then the gap, then what each share buys": CHANGE.** The written
   case for support, in every professional guide read, starts with the mission and the need, not the money: AFP's
   questions open with "Who is the organization, and what does it do? Why does it exist?" and its component list ends
   with "Statement of needs" and "Gift range chart" (row 1); The Fund Raising School calls a money-first case ("We need a
   new building. We're trying to raise one million dollars") "not a convincing case expression" (row 2); Campbell and
   Company say the six elements need no fixed order and "you can even lead with the appeal" (row 3). But the public
   ANNOUNCEMENT of a campaign does lead with money already committed: MacDowell 2010 ("more than $10 million has
   already been raised" toward $13 million, row 5), Banff Centre 2007 ("publicly launched with a $10 million dollar
   donation", row 6), Artists Repertory Theatre 2020 ("approximately 65% of funds in hand", row 12). What each share
   buys is real and specific in residency texts (named residencies, studios, buildings: rows 10, 11). "The gap" is the
   weak part for Laura: the case says teams are "not expected to ... determine the project's total funding gap" (case
   p.4). Keep: committed money as proof, early and in numbers. Change: lead the wider case with the residency's
   purpose; Laura's part is the "committed money" section inside it, and the gap stays unsized.
2. **"Funders want an operating reserve or endowment named before they fund a building": CHANGE (true of expert
   funders, not of practice).** Kresge required capitalization plans and board-approved building-reserve policies with
   every facility grant and says a campaign should include "transitional operating funding" (rows 22, 23); GIA says
   reserves "should be prerequisites for entering into a significant capital project" (row 25); Eckerd asks for "a
   financial, staffing and management plan for operation of a new facility" (row 28). Against: Set in Stone found
   buildings "left ... large annual operating expenses that were sometimes beyond the capacity" of their owners (row 26),
   GIA says organisations have "little external incentive" to plan for health and post-opening reserve drives are
   "often an exercise in futility" (row 25), Ragdale built first and started an operating endowment afterwards (row 21),
   and Kresge's own guide records the fear that a reserve makes an organisation look as if it has "less need" (row 22).
   The accurate claim: the best-informed funders want operating money planned before the building because most
   projects do not plan it. Laura's ten payments are exactly that money (case p.3: "reliable support for its early
   operations"). The case also says teams need not size "a separate contingency fund or endowment" (case p.3).
3. **"Gift charts are steep: top gift about 10-20% of the goal; a few gifts give most of the money": KEEP, widen the
   range.** The rules of thumb run from "10 percent or more" (The Fund Raising School, 2003) to "at least 20%", often
   "20% or 25%" (Capital Campaign Pro), with smaller campaigns needing bigger top gifts (row 30). Data agree: in 171
   educational campaigns (CASE, 1997-98) the top 10 percent of donors gave 80 percent and the top 1 percent gave 57
   percent (row 31); Capital Campaign Pro's benchmark puts the top 20 gifts at about 72 percent of the goal (row 31);
   Yaddo's real leadership list gives at least 30 percent from three gifts and at least 50 percent from seven (row 8,
   Keel arithmetic on band minimums).
4. **Surprising and fitting Laura: found.** (a) CASE's counting rules already split firm money from revocable or
   conditional money and tell campaigns to announce them as separate goals (row 32): Laura's owned floor and
   market-dependent top are the same split. (b) Feasibility studies hand boards a goal RANGE and call it "a conditional
   forecast ... not a guarantee", with "conditions attached to the upper end" (row 33). (c) Set in Stone: theatres' real
   budgets rose about 92 percent, but the public saw about 19 percent because managers chose when to announce (row 26):
   naming a number late is how credibility is kept, which is the FINAL's 2031 move. (d) The closest real precedent to
   Laura is Fogo Island Arts: a woman who made money elsewhere returned home, put her own capital in first, and two
   governments then added $5 million each "after some persuasion" (row 14). (e) In Taiwan, the pioneer founder-funded
   residency, Bamboo Curtain Studio, ran for 26 years mostly on its founder's resources and earned income, became a
   nonprofit in 2011 to accept sponsorship, and ended operations in 2021 (row 15): operating money is the hard part.

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. The order real texts use (claim 1)
- **What is it?** Two kinds of text. The written case for support opens with mission and need and puts the money near
  the end (AFP's questions and components, row 1; The Fund Raising School's warning against "We need a new building",
  row 2; Campbell's free order, row 3). The public announcement opens with money already committed (MacDowell's "more
  than $10 million has already been raised", row 5; Banff's launch "with a $10 million dollar donation", row 6).
- **Did it work?** MacDowell, Banff and Skowhegan all reached or passed their goals (rows 5, 6, 11); Banff "exceeds its
  original goal, raising $128 million". Real phrasing patterns for committed money: "already been raised toward",
  "raised and pledged", "in hand", "lead investment", "made possible by", "has majority support from", "to kickstart
  the project"; for uncertain money: "up to", "once each threshold is met", "a total match of" (rows 9, 11-14).
- **How is Laura like it?** The case asks for "part of Laura's fundraising materials describing this potential
  contribution" (case p.4): her part IS the committed-money section, so leading that part with what she owns fits the
  announcement pattern.
- **How is she not?** No goal, no gap and no naming prices: the facility cost is undetermined and teams are not asked
  to size the gap (case p.4). Her money is a range, not a single gift.
- **What Caplet takes:** describe the residency's purpose first in the wider materials; in Laura's section, state what
  is owned before what may come, in the vocabulary co-sponsors already use for committed and conditional money.
  Claim 1's "then the gap" is dropped for Laura.

### Card 2. Money in hand before the public number, and when to say it
- **What is it?** Campaigns go public only with most money secured: AFP 60-80% (row 34), Capital Campaign Pro at least
  65%, Kresge 20-50% before applying (row 27), Truist 60% (row 28); MacDowell went public with at least 77% in hand
  (row 5). CCS told a parish to set its formal goal only "after the receipt of the first 15 commitments" (row 33).
- **Did it work?** Set in Stone shows the cost of the opposite: theatres' budgets rose about 92%, yet the public saw
  about 19% because managers chose "when to announce their budgets publicly" (row 26). Credibility depends on the timing
  of the first public number.
- **How is Laura like it?** The FINAL moves the promised half of the shares into Treasuries "Before you meet co-sponsors
  in 2031, ... so whatever figure you choose is money you already own". Her quiet phase is 2028-2030 (store H4-042).
- **How is she not?** A campaign can still raise or cut a working goal in private; Laura's range, once said, is meant to
  hold (case p.3 on credibility).
- **What Caplet takes:** the FINAL's 2031 move is the fundraising profession's own rule taken to its limit: at the first
  meeting, 100% of the named figure is owned. This is the cleanest outside proof for MOVE2031.

### Card 3. Operations are the money nobody raises (claim 2)
- **What is it?** Expert funders want operating money and reserves planned before the building: Kresge's reserve
  policies and "transitional operating funding" (row 22), NFF's finding that grantees did not plan operating costs
  (row 23), "Capital is episodic; revenue is forever" (row 24), GIA's "prerequisites" (row 25), Eckerd's operating plan
  (row 28).
- **Did it work?** Mostly it did not happen in practice: buildings left owners with operating costs "beyond the
  capacity" (row 26); raising reserves after opening is "often an exercise in futility" (row 25); Ragdale built first and
  started its operating endowment after (row 21); Bamboo Curtain Studio in Taiwan ran 26 years on its founder's money and
  earned income and closed in 2021 (row 15).
- **How is Laura like it?** The case funds "reliable support for its early operations" from her portfolio alone and puts
  the reserve before the facility ("before making the first operating payment or contributing to the facility", case
  p.3). The FINAL buys all ten payments in January 2027, years before any building money.
- **How is she not?** Ten payments, not a permanent endowment; the case says teams need not size "a separate contingency
  fund or endowment" (case p.3).
- **What Caplet takes:** to a co-sponsor, Laura's ten payments answer the question expert funders ask first and most
  projects cannot answer. GIA's "three to five years" to stabilise after opening is inside her ten-year commitment.

### Card 4. Steep gift charts, measured (claim 3)
- **What is it?** A few gifts carry a campaign: rules of thumb put the top gift at 10% to 25% of the goal (row 30); data
  put the top 10% of donors at 80% and the top 1% at 57% of proceeds (CASE 1997-98) and the top 20 gifts at about 72%
  of the goal (Capital Campaign Pro) (row 31).
- **Did it work?** A real residency list matches: Yaddo's three $1,000,000+ gifts are at least 30% of $10M and its top
  seven at least 50% (row 8, Keel arithmetic on band minimums).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her contribution is the first and probably the largest figure co-sponsors hear, so it sets
  the scale ("The ten largest gifts set the standard for the entire campaign", Fund Raising School; row 29).
- **How is she not?** Charts are built from many donors' capacity; Laura's is one balance sheet. The case does not ask
  for a campaign size (case p.4), so any size implied by her range stays illustration (store H4-044).
- **What Caplet takes:** a lead figure that later falls lowers every tier below it (store H4-043); this is the
  fundraising reason the FINAL keeps the bottom in Treasuries.

### Card 5. Firm money and uncertain money are counted, and announced, separately
- **What is it?** CASE's reporting standards exclude conditional pledges until the conditions are expected to be met
  and tell institutions "to develop and announce separate goals for future commitments and outright gifts" because
  revocable intentions carry a "relatively lower degree of donative obligation" (row 32). Feasibility studies give
  boards a range with "conditions attached to the upper end" (row 33).
- **Did it work?** It is the standard for university campaign reporting worldwide (Global Reporting Standards,
  updated 2024); the 1999 Chronicle article notes CASE's planned-gift rule existed so as not to "misrepresent to donors
  and the public the amount of money actually raised".
- **How is Laura like it?** The IPS: "markets will be able to increase or decrease the top of your planned band ... but
  the bottom will remain constant". Her floor is the outright part; her top, until the 2031 move, is the uncertain part.
- **How is she not?** Her top depends on markets, not on a donor's will or an institution's action; after the move it
  is owned too.
- **What Caplet takes:** the floor-and-top structure is not a student invention; it is how the profession already
  keeps faith with donors. CANON caution: "owned" is not "fixed to the dollar" (IBTM pays its NAV).

### Card 6. Founders who go first
- **What is it?** Real residencies anchored by a founder's own money: Fogo Island Arts (Cobb's own capital, then two
  governments at $5 million each, row 14); Bamboo Curtain Studio (Margaret Shiu's own resources, row 15); Grand View Art
  Village (one private foundation, row 16); Akademie Schloss Solitude (a state founder pays the operations, row 19).
- **Did it work?** Fogo Island's Inn "broke even" in year four (HBS case) and governments joined "after some
  persuasion"; Bamboo Curtain lasted 26 years but became a nonprofit only in 2011, to accept sponsorship, and ended
  operations in 2021.
- **How is Laura like it?** A founder whose wealth was made elsewhere (her books) returns money to a place she cares
  about (case p.2-3), commits first, and asks co-sponsors to join.
- **How is she not?** Cobb committed tens of millions and runs a business that funds operations; Shiu funded year by
  year. Laura commits a bounded range and ten fixed payments, and states them in advance.
- **What Caplet takes:** two real lessons in one pair: a founder's first money draws public partners (Fogo), and
  founder money without secured operations runs out (Bamboo Curtain). The FINAL secures operations first.

### Card 7. Overruns eat reserves, and reserves can look like less need
- **What is it?** More than 80% of US cultural buildings ran over budget (row 26); even well-planned reserves are hard
  "to protect ... from covering cost overruns, fundraising shortfalls" (row 25). Kresge notes a reserve is "intent
  rather than a legal restriction" and should carry a drawdown policy, and that reserves can create "the appearance
  that the organization has more money, and therefore less need" (row 22).
- **Did it work?** NFF found facility reserves gave grantees room to take programme risks, but a few smaller grantees'
  matches drained money they needed for operations (row 23).
- **How is Laura like it?** The FINAL names three uses for the kept half: "building costs that overrun, a stronger
  Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short". The first is the likeliest by the data.
- **How is she not?** Her payments are not in the same pool: they sit in Treasuries, so an overrun can only reach the
  kept half and her gift, never the operating money.
- **What Caplet takes:** the kept half is a real-world reserve with a stated drawdown purpose. RISK (Clash, row 22):
  shown too prominently to co-sponsors, it may read as "Laura will cover any shortfall" and lower their giving (store
  H4-065, Varian). Note the risk; the IPS stays as it is.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **The fundraising profession already uses Caplet's floor-and-top split.** CASE's standards tell campaigns to announce
   firm and revocable money as separate goals, and exclude conditional pledges until their conditions are expected to be
   met (row 32); feasibility studies hand boards a range with "conditions attached to the upper end" (row 33). Most teams
   will present a range as a statistics result; few will show that co-sponsors' own development offices think this way.
2. **The 2031 move matches how credible campaigns time their first public number.** Theatres' real budget rises
   averaged about 92%, but the public saw about 19% because managers chose when to announce (Set in Stone, row 26);
   modern campaigns go public at 60-80% secured (row 34). Laura goes public at 100% owned.
3. **Operating money is what real residencies lack, and Laura brings it first.** NFF, Kresge and GIA all say campaigns
   forget operations (rows 22-25); Ragdale raised its operating endowment only after its building (row 21); Taiwan's
   pioneer residency, Bamboo Curtain Studio, ran on its founder's money and closed its operations in 2021 (row 15).
   Laura's ten payments are bought in January 2027, six years before the residency opens.
4. **There is a real Laura.** Fogo Island Arts: a woman who made her money elsewhere came home, committed her own
   capital first, and two governments joined at $5 million each "after some persuasion" (row 14).
5. **A real residency's donor list is as steep as the textbook.** Yaddo: at least 30% of a $10M campaign from three gifts
   (row 8). The first figure Laura names will set the scale for everyone after her.
