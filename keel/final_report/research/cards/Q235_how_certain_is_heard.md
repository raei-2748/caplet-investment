- **Problem class:** a person with a stake states a future amount and wants it heard exactly and believed.
- **Field that solved it first:** securities custody rules. The SEC's 2009 amendments moved who sends the statement, from the interested adviser to the custodian. Psychology found the "against her own interest" effect first: Walster, Aronson & Abrahams (1966). Psycholinguistics found the cost of the word "certain" only recently: Teigen & Juanchich (2024).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **"Saying 'certain' makes a donor suspicious": CHANGE.** It depends on who is the subject of the word.
   - **"I am certain"** makes the speaker sound less sure. In Teigen & Juanchich's (2024) Study 1, 58.0% judged the plain speaker more confident.
   - **An expert's impersonal "it is certain"** about a future event raised trust: 69% against 31% for the plain statement. Switching the same sentence to "I am certain" cut that to 42% (Study 3).
   - **"Guaranteed"** on a future money figure bought nothing: "Kenneth's income is guaranteed to be £5,000 next month" scored 2.21 against 2.36 for the plain sentence, not significant (Study 4).
   - **Regulators teach the public that "guaranteed" is a warning sign**, but only beside high returns with little risk (Investor.gov; FINRA). Laura's pairing is the reverse: a low, set sum from Treasuries.
   - **A known stake costs sincerity** once the motive is plain (Campbell & Kirmani 2000). It costs nothing when the evidence is one a listener can check (Isaac & Grayson 2017).
   - So the word is not the danger. The danger is putting it in her mouth and on a dollar figure.
   - **Judge's note:** saying you are certain makes you sound less certain. A plain statement was read as exact by 63-65% of readers, the same statement with "I am certain" by 21%.
2. **"A number beside the word is always clearer": CHANGE.** The clearest form is the number with no certainty word at all.
   - Adding "certain" to a figure moves listeners from "exactly" to "at least". In Study 7, 60.9% read the plain "200 will be saved" as exactly 200, against 40.4% with "certain". The "at least" reading rose from 8.7% to 38.5%.
   - Q71 holds the point that numbers beat words. What is new here: a word added to a number makes the number less exact.
   - **Judge's note:** "certain" beside $115,000 is heard as "at least $115,000". That is the one phrase the report already bans, and the reason is now measured.
3. **"Showing what holds the money beats saying how sure": KEEP, with three conditions.**
   - **(a) The proof must come from the holder, not from the person with a stake.** Madoff-style frauds "covered up the misuse by distributing false account statements" (SEC 2009). The 2009 rule made custodians send statements to clients directly, and about 190 firms that sent their own had to stop.
   - **(b) One look shows the money on one day only.** It does not show debts against the money, or what happens to it afterwards. Proof-of-reserve reports "are not audits" and give a view "at a particular moment in time" (PCAOB 2023), and Mazars stopped making them because of how "the public" read them. Laura's plan has its own answer: two looks (the 2031 meetings and before December 2032), no borrowing against the holdings, and the signed letter for what comes after.
   - **(c) The listener must know the check exists.** 72% of buyers who did not use escrow gave lack of awareness as a reason (Pavlou & Gefen 2004).
   - Supporting evidence: federal cost-share rules count a match only if it is "verifiable in the recipient's or subrecipient's records" (2 CFR 200.306). Disclosing a stake does not do the job on its own (Cain et al. 2005).
   - **Judge's note:** Madoff's clients did get statements. The 2009 fix changed who sends the statement, not what it says.
4. **"Listeners plan on the bottom of a range": CHANGE.** Which end they plan on depends on the word.
   - "Certain" pulls listeners to the bottom and turns it into a floor: "at least" (Q71 rows 1-3; Q159-026). Investors read a company's range at its top (Q35 row 13).
   - What is new here: if Laura adds "I am certain" to "$95,000 to $115,000", both figures turn into floors. The top is then heard as "at least $115,000" and the bottom as "at least $95,000, maybe more".
   - **Fix:** say one figure she signs (the top), and give the bottom as its history ("held for me since January 2028"), with no certainty word on either figure. This is Branch 5's form.
5. **"A founder with a stake is discounted whatever she says": CHANGE.**
   - Sincerity cues are discounted when the motive is plain (Campbell & Kirmani 2000). But people under-correct for a known stake (Cain et al. 2005).
   - Listeners are more persuaded when a speaker goes against the bias they expected. Eagly, Wood & Chaiken (1978) found this, and that such speakers are "rated ... as more unbiased". Walster et al. (1966) found the same for arguing against one's own interest.
   - Laura's "my own $115,000 comes first either way", and her keeping the half promised to no one out of the figure, are exactly such disconfirmations.
   - The real risk is not suspicion on the day. It is the backlash if the figure later proves wrong: Tenney et al. 2007 and 2019, Vullioud et al. 2017. A held figure removes that risk.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 3, with row 37 (3d; WORDS).** The word "certain" beside $115,000 is measured to produce "at least $115,000", the one phrase the report bans. It turns a style rule into evidence.
2. **Rows 22-24 (3d).** Madoff's clients had statements, and the 2009 fix was who sends them. This is why co-sponsors see the broker's own statement, not one Laura prints herself. It also gives the statement in Branch 5 an outside reason in one clause.
3. **Row 4 (3d).** "It is certain" from an expert raised trust; "I am certain" lowered it (69% against 42%). This is the evidence for Branch 5's one sentence of confidence with the Treasuries, not Laura, as its subject.
4. **Row 26, with row 36 (3d, 3e).** A proof of reserves shows assets on one day, not debts or what happens next. The plan's answer: two looks, no borrowing against the holdings, and the signed letter. This makes "owned versus signed" a three-part answer to a known failure.
5. **Row 33 (3d or 3c).** Sellers take about 11% less for money already in hand, against about 3% that a model of the risk explains. This is the market price of "already held" over "promised on a condition", and it explains why co-sponsors count the held top.

## What this means for Laura's words (notes, not prose)

- **Say the figures plainly.** Give each figure, its date and what holds it. Put no "I am certain", "surely" or "guaranteed" on a dollar figure. A plain figure is heard as that figure; a figure with "certain" is heard as "around" or "at least" (rows 2-3).
- **If she says how sure she is, make the Treasuries the subject, never "I".**
  - The shape is: Treasuries held for her pay this amount on this date.
  - The late-2032 fund's payout is never called fixed. The 0.5% margin is why the stated figure sits below what its Treasuries pay.
  - The IPS's own words fit this shape: the bottom is what she can promise "without doubt", "built on the ever-reliable Treasuries" (IPS l.15). Row 4.
- **Offer the check before anyone asks, and from the holder.**
  - Use the broker's own statement, sent by the broker if it can be arranged (Gaps).
  - Offer it at the 2031 meetings and again before the December 2032 payout, and "whenever you ask". Rows 22-25 and 32.
- **Say what the statement shows and what the letter adds.**
  - The statement shows the money is held today.
  - The signed letter says it will be given.
  - Neither stands in for the other. Rows 26-27 and 36.
- **Lead with what runs against her own interest.**
  - Use "my own $115,000 comes first either way".
  - The half promised to no one stays hers and is not offered.
  - Rows 13-14.
- **Let co-sponsors decide after the meeting, in writing.** That way she never needs to press in the room. Row 16.
- **Get every small fact right.** That means the fund's name at first mention, "about 15 December 2032", the figure and its dates. One small slip costs the big claim (row 17).
- **Keep the odds for the judges.** The 91 and 57 in 100 futures belong in 3c. In 2031 nothing is left to estimate, so Laura's words carry no odds (row 8; Branch 4).
- **None of this softens the report's own voice.** The report may still call Treasuries guaranteed, locked, set in stone or risk-free (Books 3, 5, 19). The evidence is about a person with a stake saying "I am certain" about a figure, not about the strength of the asset.

## Gaps

- **Research on donors themselves.**
  - No study was found on how donors, foundations or arts co-sponsors hear "certain" or "guaranteed" from a founder or a donor. Every finding comes from lab vignettes, advice, sales, auctions or housing.
  - No study tested a checkable mechanism against a stated probability head to head in giving.
- **Wording in Taiwan.** Nothing on Mandarin certainty markers (一定, 保證, 確定) or on how Taiwanese foundations hear them. This matters if Laura's words are translated.
- **"Fully funded".** How lay readers hear it: no at-source study was found.
- **Papers read only in abstract.**
