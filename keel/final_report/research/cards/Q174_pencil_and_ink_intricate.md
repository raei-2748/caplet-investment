## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. The Supreme Court ruled that a pencil with an eraser on the end is not an invention, because each part "continues to perform its own duty, and nothing else"

(a) In 1875 the US Supreme Court struck down the patent on the pencil with an eraser fixed in its end, because the rubber and the graphite do nothing for each other: "There must be a new result produced by their union; otherwise it is only an aggregation of separate elements."
(b) That ruling describes exactly why Laura's plan is safe. The Treasuries pay the ten payments and the bottom whatever VT does, so if VT were worth nothing at the move, every payment would still be paid and the range would be one figure, the bottom, about $95,000 (NUMBERS section 4's deletion test). Portfolio insurance in 1987 was the opposite kind of plan, a true "combination": a selling rule tied the shares to the floor, and when prices gapped the floor broke (store H2-071, H3-037, H4-058). So a plan that fails as an invention succeeds as a promise.
(c) It breaks on the court's next sentence. The law wants "more than a change of form, or juxtaposition of parts, or ... of the order in which they are used", yet order is the whole of Laura's plan: promised money is bought first, and only money "you haven't promised yet" goes into shares (IPS l.11). There is also one place where a part does work for another, the 2031 move, when half of VT's value becomes Treasuries. But the move is fixed to a date, not to a price, so it never sells into a fall. The break teaches the report's two claims in one: the parts never interact, and the order is everything.

- Decision: buy-and-hold, with no price trigger (IPS l.11, l.17 "A fall in the market is not one of them"); the move fixed to the first trading day of 2031; the deletion test.
- Report part: 2.3 (why the parts do not need each other), or 3c (the deletion test's one clause). One outside name per part, so use it only where no other name sits.
- Source: Reckendorfer v. Faber, 92 U.S. 347 (1876), read at LII and in the US Reports scan (Library of Congress); the patent was Lipman's, dated 30 Mar 1858.
- Pedigree or aha: AHA. A finance judge knows 1987 and buy-and-hold, but not that the Supreme Court ruled on the pencil-and-eraser in terms that separate the two. It also makes a prediction we can check: delete one part and the other's job is unchanged, which NUMBERS section 4 confirms to the cent.

### 2. Comics used to be lettered before inking, so the words got their room before the art was finished

(a) When comics were hand-lettered, "a letterer's work would be completed after pencils, but before inks" (Blambot), and older artists wrote in every word first "to show where the lettering should go, and make sure they left enough room" (Klein). The "Marvel method" drew the art first and wrote the words afterwards, and Klein says it "torpedoed that idea".
(b) Laura's plan letters first. The ten payments are bought in January 2027. The 2028 deposit then completes them if needed, buys the extra Treasuries and the late-2032 fund (the bottom), and only what is left goes to VT (IPS l.13, l.15). The team's first plan used the Marvel method: shares first, with all ten payments to be bought on one date, 1 Jan 2033, out of whatever room the shares had left. In the 27 Sep model that fell short in about 1 in 31 markets (NUMBERS 8s; CANON A3). IPS l.9 is the hinge between the two methods: "So, naturally, that was our plan – until we stopped".
(c) It breaks in two places, and both teach. First, a letterer only reserves room in pencil (Klein places balloons "with an erasable light blue pencil"), and the old method "worked when artists remembered to leave room". Laura's room is not remembered, it is bought: the January 2027 purchase is ink on the day, never a reservation. Second, in comics everything is inked in the end, words and art. In Laura's plan her flexibility is never inked, and that is by design.

- Decision: the order of the two deposits (IPS l.11, l.13, l.15); why the allocation is a rule, not a target mix (SKELETON 2.2); the first plan dropped (3f).
- Report part: 2.2 as a fact behind the working word (one clause, no staged image). 3f's existing turn "the first plan's payments were in pencil" gains its reason: the words came last. In 3f, as a fact only, with no outside name (panel 4's cap).
- Source: Blambot (n.d.); Klein (n.d., Balloon placement; 2023, Sam and Joe Rosen part 3); Evanier (n.d.): "letterers receive the art in the pencil stage".
- Pedigree or aha: AHA, from her own trade. No finance judge knows the lettering order, and it gives the plan's order a cause a 16-year-old can see: the words need their room before anything permanent goes down.

### 3. Pencil and India ink are the same carbon; what differs is how it is held to the page

(a) Graphite is "a rare pure form of carbon" (Cumbria County History Trust), and India ink is carbon black in a binder (CONCEIT A13). A pencil's graphite stays on paper only through "a very small attraction between molecules", while ink "is carried by liquid into the fibers" (Popular Science, quoting two chemists).
(b) Ink in Laura's plan is not a different kind of money. It is the same dollars, held a different way: bound to a Treasury and a date. That is why VT is owned but still pencil (the carbon is on the page but not bound), and why the 2031 move inks the top without creating a cent. It rebinds half of VT's value to dates. The UK Electoral Commission makes the same point about ballots: pen or pencil does not matter, because "pen marks can be crossed out" too, and "What is key is that the integrity of the process ... is maintained". What makes a mark last is custody, not the medium.
(c) It breaks on time. Carbon ink lasts with no end date, while Laura's ink is bound to a date and is spent on that date: it is ink made to be used up. And her binding is a rule kept, not chemistry: the Treasuries stay held to their dates because the IPS allows only two reasons to change (l.17). So a stated figure rests on a choice, not on physics, and the report never says it "cannot move".

- Decision: the definition (2.0); VT owned but pencil; the 2031 move (IPS l.15).
- Report part: 2.0 at first use, as the fact behind "owned in Treasuries that will pay it on its date". This is the one CONCEIT line it sharpens: "owned" alone is not ink, "owned and bound to a date" is.
- Source: Cumbria County History Trust (n.d.); Popular Science (Weber, 20 May 2026); Electoral Commission (2026), Polling Station Handbook; Full Fact (6 May 2026) quoting the Commission's 2016 FOI reply.
- Pedigree or aha: AHA for a general reader, KNOWN to a chemist. It explains the conceit's hardest case, VT, rather than decorating the easy ones.

### 4. A memoirist's promise is not "I, the undersigned" but "I swear to tell the truth", and narrowing the oath is proof of honesty

(a) Philippe Lejeune, the theorist of autobiography, says a memoir's pact with its reader is a truth claim that can "submit to a test of verification". Its formula is not "I, the undersigned" but "I swear to tell the truth", and to restrict the oath to what one can know is "a supplementary proof of honesty" (On Autobiography, 1989, p. 22).
(b) That is the conceit's guard ("ink is never signed") given a reason from Laura's own genre. Ink is not a signature, it is a figure anyone can check against what she owns: Table 1 lists the bonds by CUSIP. And the narrowness is the proof. She names only the inked range and never states her flexibility, so the figure she does state is believable. Her first comic, the case says, was "originally intended as a response to misinformation" (case p.1).
(c) It breaks where Lejeune himself draws a line. A memoir's pact "can be ... badly kept" without losing its value, but "this is not the case for historical and journalistic texts". In 2031 Laura speaks to co-sponsors under the journalist's pact, not the memoirist's: memory may blur a scene, but a stated figure may not blur. The break tells 3d which standard her words must meet.

- Decision: Laura states a figure only once it is inked; her flexibility is never stated (IPS l.15); the words for co-sponsors.
- Report part: 3d, one clause behind "her side of the sum is exact". Or 2.0, against hearing "inked" as "signed". Never both, and never in Laura's own words to co-sponsors (CONCEIT).
- Source: Lejeune (1989), pp. 22-23, read in the English PDF and checked against the French original (1975).
- Pedigree or aha: AHA. It comes from the theory of the genre she writes in, so the conceit stops being a metaphor imposed on her and becomes her trade's own rule.

### 5. A monk scraped Archimedes off the page in 1229, and the iron in the old ink was read by X-ray in 2006

(a) The scribe Johannes Myronas scraped Archimedes' text off parchment to write a prayer book, finished on 14 Apr 1229 in Jerusalem. He "failed to scrape all of the ink from the parchment fibers", and in 2006 X-rays at Stanford read the old text from a signal that "can come only from iron atoms" (SLAC).
(b) Ink that has been stated stays in the record even after someone tries to remove it. Once Laura names a figure in 2031, a co-sponsor's gift office books it as "committed" (card Q147), and taking it back leaves a trace. So the plan states nothing before it is inked, and the range is stated only after the move.
(c) It breaks on value. In the palimpsest the scraped text was the treasure (Christie's, 28 Oct 1998, $2 million). For Laura, a scraped figure would be a broken promise found later. Pencil leaves traces too (idea rows on pentimenti), so the report never says pencil vanishes. The guard is to not state pencil at all, not that pencil disappears.

- Decision: a stated figure never comes off the page; nothing is stated before the 2031 move.
- Report part: 3d or 3c, as a reserve. It overlaps with 3b's "ink only goes one way", so use only one of them per part.
- Source: SLAC (2006 press release; X-ray imaging page); Walters Art Museum (2005; exhibition page).
- Pedigree or aha: KNOWN to well-read judges as a story; the iron-atom detail and the mapping are new. A reserve, not one of the three.

### 6. A tattoo outlasts every cell that holds its ink, because the ink is passed from cell to cell

(a) A 2018 mouse study found tattoo ink sits in skin cells that die and are replaced, and the pigment goes through "successive cycles of capture–release–recapture without any tattoo vanishing". Its persistence "likely relies on macrophage renewal rather than on macrophage longevity" (Baranska et al., 2018).
(b) Her Treasuries last the same way: held for her through a chain of holders, not by any one holder living forever. When Lehman's broker failed, 110,000 customer accounts moved to solvent brokers "over the next ten days", and all were made whole (store Q2a-022). Her claim is "not a claim to a specific identifiable thing" (UCC 8-503, comment 2), just as the ink is not tied to one cell.
(c) It breaks on gaps and on species. The relay takes time (MF Global's retail accounts moved after 39 days, store Q72-017), and the study is in mice (Science News quotes a dermatologist's caution). So it supports 3e's point exactly: the risk above the insurance limit is delay, not loss.

- Decision: custody at one broker (3e's limits); "owned" as the law's word.
- Report part: 3e, one clause at most. Never "identified", never "in her name".
- Source: Baranska et al. (2018), Journal of Experimental Medicine; Inserm (2018) press release; Science News (2018).
- Pedigree or aha: NICHE. A reserve for 3e; strong only if 3e has room for a picture.
