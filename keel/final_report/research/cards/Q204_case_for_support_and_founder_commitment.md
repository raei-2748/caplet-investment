## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. The fundraisers' own dictionary has one word for money that comes first and another for money that sets the size, and Laura's figure is only the first kind.**
(a) AFP's Fundraising Dictionary calls a donation that "demonstrates a commitment to a campaign and provides momentum at the outset before external solicitations are undertaken" an advance gift (also "nucleus gift"), and it keeps a separate entry, the leadership or "pace-setting" gift, for one that "sets a standard for all subsequent gifts" on the gift range table, which is a projection of gifts by size "so as to achieve a particular fundraising goal".
(b) Laura's figure fits the first entry exactly, because Treasuries held for her will pay it before the 2031 meetings and announced before anyone else is asked; it cannot claim the second, because a standard for later gifts needs a goal, and the case says the facility's cost "has not been determined" and teams should not estimate it (case l.164-166).
(c) The break is in the word itself: by the usual rule that a lead gift is 10-20% of the goal, calling $115,000 "the lead gift" tells a co-sponsor the campaign is about $575,000 to $1.15 million (Keel arithmetic), a size nobody has set, and four real campaigns read today put their lead gift at 17-33% of the goal (Keel arithmetic, rows 6); so the report should say what the dictionary's first entry says in plain words ("the first money committed, before anyone else is asked") and measure the figure against her means, which AFP's "proportional giving" names as the second yardstick beside the goal.
- **Decision:** what Laura's facility contribution is called in 3d (WORDS already bans "gift" for her money; this gives the reason the campaign term would mislead as well).
- **Report part:** 3d (one clause: first committed, measured against her means: $115,000 is about a quarter of the $450,000 she deposits, Keel arithmetic).
- **Source:** AFP (2003), entries "advance gift", "leadership gift", "pace-setting", "nucleus fund", "gift range table", "proportional giving"; Overture (2024), The Hill Arts (2025), Midland Center for the Arts (2026), Arkansas Arts Center (2019) for the shares.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha** for a finance judge and useful to a campaign director: it settles attack 12 with the profession's own definitions, and it matches the rule the report already keeps for Taiwan ("the founder pays first", never "the largest share").

**2. Campaigns already count the opening years' running money as part of what a building costs, and Laura has bought that line for ten years before anyone is asked.**
(a) Artists Repertory Theatre's 2025 budget to finish its building, $11,504,266, includes "$500,000 operating seed funds for first two years once open"; the Arkansas Arts Center's $128 million campaign "will also provide transition and opening support"; and CASE's campaign guidance counts "unrestricted operating support" and "building construction" in one comprehensive total, while it calls a campaign for one building a "special purpose" campaign.
(b) So Laura's ten payments of $50,000 (2033-2042), bought in Treasuries in January 2027 and completed in January 2028, are not a separate kindness beside the building: they fill the line that campaigns plan for and, by Grantmakers in the Arts' account, usually fail to raise after opening (store Q11b-025); her words should therefore show both parts, $50,000 a year for ten years towards running costs and $95,000-$115,000 for the building, and then the sum once, "about $615,000 over ten years" (Keel arithmetic), because a co-sponsor's office counts a building-only campaign differently from a comprehensive one, and forecasts shown in parts are believed more than one total (store Q159-020).
(c) The parallel breaks on scale, and the break answers attack 10: Artists Rep's $500,000 covers two years of a theatre with a budget in the millions, while Laura's $500,000 is $50,000 a year, a contribution to the residency's running costs and not the running costs, so the report keeps INTENT's "a base, not the residency's whole budget"; and the $615,000 is a campaign's face-value sum of dollars paid from 2033 to 2042 (CASE counts pledges "at face value"), not money worth $615,000 today, since the ten payments cost roughly $300,000 in January 2027 (IPS l.13).
- **Decision:** how 3d shows her whole commitment (the case's "reliable support for its early operations", l.83, is hers; the building is shared, l.84-86).
- **Report part:** 3d's opening (the two parts, then the sum once); 3.0 (the ten years as the base).
- **Source:** Artists Repertory Theatre (n.d., budget updated fall 2025); Arkansas Arts Center (2019); CASE and Marts&Lundy (2021); AFP (2003), "cumulative giving"; store Q11b-025, Q159-020, Q18-006.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha**: the judges will expect a founder's running money to be described as generosity; describing it as the line a campaign budget already carries, filled in advance for ten years, is the stronger and truer claim.

**3. In real announcements the institution leads with money, but the founder's own words lead with why, then her figure, then the others.**
(a) When Overture Center launched a $30 million campaign in 2024, the press release opened on the $10 million gift, but the founder's own words ran in a fixed order: "my vision was to create a world-class arts destination", then "With my $10 million pledge", then "the additional support of other donors"; The Hill Arts' 2025 release does the same, a headline on its $5 million lead gift and a founder speaking of "care and concern for an important piece of community infrastructure".
(b) The case asks the team to draft "part of Laura's fundraising materials describing this potential contribution" (case l.123-124), and that part is a founder speaking, so it should take the founder's order: one or two sentences on why the residency exists (from the case's own facts: her work on identity, belonging and storytelling; a place where "artists, writers, designers, entrepreneurs, and educators" live and work, l.77-80), then the ten years she has already bought, then the figure, then what she asks of others; Taiwan's national arts foundation reads in the same order, since its 2026 operating-grant form asks first for the 「緣起」 (origin) and puts the finance plan third (row 19), and affluent US donors name "believing in the mission of the organization" as the top reason they always give (58%, row 21).
(c) The parallel breaks on length and authorship: Frautschi's two sentences sat inside a release the institution wrote, and AFP's "fundraising tripod" splits a programme into "the case, leadership, and sources of support", so the residency's full case for support is the residency's to write, and the team's part is the leadership leg, which means purpose in one or two sentences and no more, written as language for Laura from the case's facts and never as invented quotations.
- **Decision:** the order of Laura's 2031 words (case l.123-124; Wharton's Creativity criterion asks that co-sponsor communication be clear and credible).
- **Report part:** 3d (the passage's first sentence is purpose; Q22's checklist then runs unchanged).
- **Source:** Overture Center for the Arts (2024); The Hill Arts (2025); 財團法人國家文化藝術基金會 (2026); 國立傳統藝術中心 (2022); Bank of America (2021, 2025); AFP (2003), "fundraising tripod".
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to a campaign director (who asked for it), and it earns its place for Client Knowledge: it uses Laura's own story where the plan's other lines are all financial.

**4. Bankers who fund big projects solved "committed first, paid last" long ago: they accept equity paid at the end when something solid stands behind it.**
(a) The World Bank's PPP guidance says project sponsors "will want to pay in their equity investment as late as possible ... even wholly back-ended", that lenders prefer it first, and that lenders "may want a bank or third-party payment guarantee on equity payments to be made later"; Gatti's textbook adds that equity paid last is used "only when sponsors are of the highest creditworthiness" and with "a backup guarantee in the form of a letter of credit".
(b) Laura's facility contribution is equity paid last of exactly this kind: she commits it at the 2031 meetings and it is paid in January 2033 from a Treasury fund that pays out in mid-December 2032 (NUMBERS, about 15 December), and what stands behind the late payment is not a bank's promise or a writer's income but the Treasury fund itself, so "paid last" is no weakness once it is said with its backing (card Q200 adds the signed letter that makes it countable).
(c) The parallel breaks twice, and both breaks teach: a letter of credit is payable to the lender, while Laura's fund is held for her, which is why Q200's signed letter is still needed; and project lenders also ask sponsors for "contingent equity ... to cover construction or other price overruns", which is exactly what her flexibility is not, because the IPS promises it "to no one" (l.15), so a co-sponsor should count her stated figure and nothing for overruns.
- **Decision:** how 3d answers "her money is paid last" (attack 11's timing point) and why the half kept back is never offered (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** store, with one clause in 3d if needed ("paid in January 2033, from Treasuries she already owns"); 3b for the contingent-equity contrast.
- **Source:** World Bank PPP Legal Resource Center (n.d.), "Sources of financing and intercreditor agreement"; Gatti (2008), ch. 6.7.1, p. 186.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to a project-finance judge; **aha** to a campaign reader, for whom "first committed, paid last" looked like a flaw the plan had not seen.

**5. A campaign needs the founder's figure when its quiet planning starts, and the plan supplies the bottom three years early and the top in time.**
(a) AFP defines a campaign's "initial phase" as "the quiet, non-public period during a campaign in which the study is conducted, the case is developed", CASE counts the quiet phase inside the campaign, and the study's interviews ask prospects for "an estimate of the gift" (store Q22-034), so the planners need the founder's own number before the quiet phase opens.
(b) The bottom of Laura's range, $95,000 on today's figures, is paid by Treasuries held for her from January 2028 and never changes after that, so she can tell the people planning the building and the campaign "at least $95,000" from then without breaking the rule that she states only what Treasuries held for her will pay; the top is bought by the 2031 move in January 2031, before the case's first co-sponsor meetings in 2031 (case l.109), so the whole range is in ink when a quiet phase could open.
(c) The break is the audience: an "at least $95,000" said early to co-sponsors would anchor them on the bottom (store Q147-037), and a later $115,000 would earn little extra trust because beating a promise earns less than keeping it (store H7-063), so the early bottom is for her own planners only, and FACTS_PLAIN's "Laura states no figure before the 2031 move" should read "states no top", since the 11-in-100 reason it gives is about a top stated early.
- **Decision:** when Laura states each end of the range (IPS l.15: "the bottom will remain constant"; "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries").
- **Report part:** 3d (one clause: the bottom is owned from January 2028 and can guide the residency's planning; the top is stated after the move); FACTS_PLAIN line on "no figure".
- **Source:** AFP (2003), "initial phase"; CASE and Marts&Lundy (2021); store Q22-034, Q147-037, H7-063; case l.109.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree; it earns its place because it turns attack 13 from a contradiction into a timetable that already fits the case.
