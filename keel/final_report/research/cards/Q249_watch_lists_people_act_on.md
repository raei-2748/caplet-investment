- **Problem class:** graded early warning and escalation. The question is what to read and how often, at what level to look closer, at what level to act, what the act is, and who does it by when. **Fields that solved it first:**

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
  - **What the fields do.** Two levels are used where the measure drifts by degrees and a person reads it by hand: NEWS2's score bands, the EBA's traffic lights, a loan's lock-up above its default level, Westgard's 2s warning before the reject rules.
  - **The look level is itself a small act,** never the big one:
    - read more often (NEWS2: hourly at 5-6)
    - inspect with the other rules (Westgard: "It doesn't mean stop, it means look carefully before proceeding")
    - keep cash in the project (the lock-up)
    - hire a consultant though "not an Event of Default" (Paideia 2026)
  - **Its price.** Every extra level costs firings. Treating a single red 3 as a full trigger "would increase workload by 40%" for 3% more detection (RCP 2017). NEWS2 trebled one trust's escalations (Forster et al. 2022). Out of hours, only 6.5% of NEWS2 scores of 5 or more were acted on (Gonem et al. 2024).
  - **Westgard's own advice.** Drop the warning level when a single rule already detects enough.
  - **Table 3 already has two levels per row** ("Carry on while" and "Act when").
    - Rows that can drift (3, 7): two levels fit.
    - Rows that are events (1, 2): the look stage is a date before the event, and it needs a named small act. Row 1's "Confirmed by 1 Dec 2027" has none (Q223-028).
    - Row 2's "Act when" puts a look (a debt-limit deadline within a month) beside an act (over five business days late).
  - **Judge's note:** in loan documents the higher level is not a warning light. It already does something small (cash stays in, a consultant is hired), so the lower level never arrives as a surprise.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with a limit.**
  - **Every field names who acts and how fast:**
    - NEWS2: who responds at each level; a 5-6 is assessed "within 60 minutes"; a 7+ "within 30 minutes", with a senior review "within 60 minutes" if there is no improvement (RCP 2020)
    - EBA: the internal escalation, then notice to the supervisor within "one additional business day"
    - BCBS: "what actions to take at what time, who can take them", "the designation of alternates for key roles" and each source's "lead time"
    - BATA: coverage computed "within ten Business Days" of each fiscal year
  - **Two gaps in Table 3.**
    - It gives the trigger's date but not the act's deadline in rows 4 (swap funds by when?) and 6.
    - Rows 1, 6 and 7 name Laura with no alternate. Wharton's own instruction names two watchers: "Laura or her asset management team".
  - **The limit.** Naming is not enough. One hospital fully complied with its written protocol in 12 of 144 serious events (8%), and escalated appropriately in only 41% of cases at score 6 or more (Petersen et al. 2017, citing their 2014 audit).
  - **Judge's note:** what makes a named owner act is how cheap the act is. Laura's acts on the two reasons are holds, the cheapest act there is.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The fields do not set dates against levels. They read a level on a set date:
  - a loan's "Calculation Date" (the last day of the second quarter and of the year), "tested semi-annually"
  - BATA's ten business days into each fiscal year
  - the EBA's values "at least on a monthly basis, even if the values for the indicators have not changed"
  - NEWS2's minimum reading every 12 hours even at a score of 0
  - **The memory evidence.** A date with no cue is the weak form: 10 of 19 people (53%) phoned within 10 minutes of a set time, against 16 of 20 (80%) who phoned after a text message arrived (Kvavilashvili & Fisher 2007).
  - **For Table 3.** Its dates hold best where a document arrives on them: the broker's statement (at least quarterly, Q230-036), a fund's notice by 31 October, the January trade confirmations, the 2031 meetings. Row 7 has no date (Q222-039) and can ride the quarterly statement.
  - **Judge's note:** a date on its own is a promise to remember. A date that arrives with a statement is a reminder.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE (split by stage).**
  - **Act on symptoms:**
    - NEWS2 reads the body's signs for "all causes of acute clinical deterioration"
    - a loan acts on cash cover
    - Fitch sets its break-evens to "tangent a default on cash payment (not on covenanted default triggers)"
  - **Look at causes only when the cure needs lead time.**
    - BCBS's early warnings are mostly causes ("rapid asset growth", "growing concentrations", "increases in currency mismatches"). They are paired with "the lead time needed to tap additional funds".
    - The EBA's complaint is lateness: indicators "often ... triggered too late".
  - **Laura's case.** Her answers to the two reasons need no lead time: state no range, fill the ten first, hold and wait. A symptom (nothing received by 31 March 2028; a payment more than five business days late) is early enough.
  - **Where her answer does need days, Table 3 already looks early.** The fund notice by 31 October comes about 45 days before a fund's end "on or about" 15 December (Keel arithmetic). The 2031 trade waits "never past the first 2031 meeting".
  - **Judge's note:** banks watch causes because their cures take months. Laura's cure for either of her two reasons is to hold, so she can wait for the symptom. That is why two rows are enough to change the plan.
