- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "0.15% a year is a fair, cautious charge for the short-term Treasury ETF where every waiting dollar sits" (NUMBERS 0, 2).
  2. "A short-term Treasury ETF holds bills and its price cannot fall" (the reading a judge brings to TNA l.5's words).
  3. "In a stress, short-term Treasury ETFs are where money runs to, and they kept their value in 2008 and March 2020."
  4. "Bill ETFs, like money funds, steered clear of bills maturing near the 2023 X-date."
  5. "A closure, merger or index change of the waiting fund would strand the money."
- **Problem class:** choosing and pricing a cash sweep: which vehicle holds money between a known receipt and a known payment, and what it costs against the bill rate. **Field that solved it first:** escrow and corporate cash management, where rating agencies admit only top-rated stable money funds for idle cash (card Q54 rows 4 and 17), and index design, where a maturity band fixes what a fund may hold (Bloomberg, ICE and Solactive bill indexes read below).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, at the top of the range; CHANGE what is measured.**
  - Fees read at the issuers' pages and prospectuses (11-16 Nov 2026 unless stated): VBIL 0.06%, CLIP 0.07%, TBLL 0.08%, SGOV 0.09%, GBIL 0.12% net (0.14% gross), BIL 0.1352%, BILS 0.1353%, and 0.15% for SHV, TBIL, TFLO, USFR and SHY (rows 1-4). No Treasury bill or short Treasury ETF read charges more than 0.15%. The register's 0.15% is the dearest fee on the shelf, not a middle.
  - What the standard really charges is the gap between what the fund earns and what bills earn. BIL trailed its own index by 0.12 to 0.15 points a year over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years and since 2007: about its fee, nothing hidden (row 5). USFR, a floating-rate Treasury fund on the 2025-26 WInS list, trailed its index by 0.17 to 0.20 points a year, more than its 0.15% fee (row 6).
  - At zero interest the fee costs the plan about $1,427 ($2,604 left after 2042 without it, $1,177 with it, NUMBERS 2): about $95 per 0.01 point (Keel arithmetic, straight line). With no fund costs beyond the iBond fee, all ten are paid even if waiting money loses 0.28% a year net (NUMBERS 2). With the full 0.5% margin used, 0.15% is exactly what is covered (rows 11-12).
  - Fees move: SGOV's 0.12% fee was waived to 0.07% (2020 prospectus) and to 0.03% (2021 prospectus, through 30 Jun 2022); it is 0.09% today (row 10).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** "Short-term" spans two different things. A 0-3 month bill fund (SGOV, effective duration 0.11 years) returned +0.02% in 2021 and +1.58% in 2022. A 1-3 year Treasury fund with the same 0.15% fee (SHY, 1.81 years, about 16 times as rate-sensitive) returned -0.73% and -3.90% (iShares fact sheets; rows 13-14). On $50,000 waiting through a year like 2022 that is about $1,950 (Keel arithmetic), more than the $1,177 left after 2042 at zero interest when no fund cost beyond the iBond fee is used, and the standard has nothing spare once the full 0.5% is used (NUMBERS 2). SHY fell 0.29% in one day, 10 Nov 2026: about two years of the 0.15% fee. The register's "0 to 3 months" (NUMBERS 0) is the load-bearing phrase; the 2025-26 WInS list held both kinds (row 15).
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with primary numbers.**
  - 2008: BIL returned +1.77% (its index the same); its lowest quarter was Q4 2008, +0.23%, the quarter bills reached zero (2009 summary prospectus; row 16).
  - March 2020: BIL took $8.66bn of new money in the month, second only to SPY among all US ETFs; SHV took $3.10bn (ETFGI; row 18). BIL returned +0.39% in 2020, and no quarter through Sep 2021 was worse than -0.03% (2021 summary prospectus; row 19).
  - Bill ETFs' own price against net asset value in March 2020 was not found (Gaps); short Treasury funds went to a premium (card Q45 row 12).
  - **Judge's note:** in the worst fortnight for markets in a decade, the second-largest flow into any US ETF went into a 1-3 month Treasury-bill fund. The place Laura's waiting money sits is where frightened money goes, not where it leaves from.
- **Claim 4: KILL for index bill ETFs.**
  - On 31 May 2023, two business days before the deal passed the Senate, SGOV held $1.65bn (16.3% of net assets) in bills maturing 6, 8 and 13 June and 31.0% in bills maturing in June (its SEC Form N-PORT; row 21; Keel arithmetic on the filing's lines).
  - At the same time "By all reports, government money market funds have been avoiding T-Bills that mature in June" (TCW). An index fund follows its index's maturity band; it does not dodge an X-date.
  - The cost to Laura of such a bill paid late is small: at 1979's loss (about 0.10% of the late bills, card Q78 row 41) on a 16% share, about 0.016% of waiting money, $8 on $50,000 (Keel arithmetic; row 23).
  - **Judge's note:** a reader may assume the "safest" fund dodged the 2023 X-date bills as money funds did. The largest bill ETF held one sixth of its money in exactly those bills.
- **Claim 5: CHANGE.** Changes are common and harmless; closures are rare and pay cash.
  - Invesco Treasury Collateral ETF became Invesco Short Term Treasury ETF (CLTL to TBLL) on 25 Aug 2023, "No other changes were made to the Fund" (row 26).
  - TBIL changed its index and its name (row 27). SHV changed its index twice and was renamed iShares 0-1 Year Treasury Bond ETF on 10 Dec 2025 (row 28).
  - PIMCO's 1-3 Year US Treasury Index ETF (TUZ, 0.15%) closed on 12 Nov 2019 with about $51m (secondary pages; row 30). Card Q78 row 40 has REX's laddered bill fund closing this month. The giants (SGOV $117.3bn, BIL $49.3bn) have not.
  - So Table 3 row 4 should name the waiting fund by what it holds and costs, not by its name or ticker (row 34).
