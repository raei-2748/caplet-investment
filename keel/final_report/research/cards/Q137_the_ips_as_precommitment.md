- **Claims tested:**

  2. "Evaluating an IPS's implementation means checking the trades against the written rules and the reasons, not the returns."
  3. "Only two reasons to change (IPS l.17) is unusually few."
- **Problem class:** a written policy as a precommitment and as an audit standard: what the document is for, and how conduct is judged against it afterwards. **Field that solved it first:** institutional investment consulting in the 1970s (Ellis's articles in the Financial Analysts Journal, 1975 and 1979), then trust law and fiduciary practice (judging conduct by process, not results; Tibble 2015 on monitoring), with public-funds law (Texas, 1993 and 1995) and UK pensions law (1995) turning the written statement into something an auditor can check.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE. The "main job" holds as the profession's own ranking; "more than choosing assets" fails; and for Laura most of that job is done by the bonds, not the paper.**
   - **Ellis's books (not read at source) rank it first.** The wording differs by edition: "The principal reason you should articulate your long-term investment policies explicitly and in writing is to protect your portfolio from yourself" (5th edition, as quoted in a review and a 2010 forum post), against "to protect the portfolio from ad hoc revisions of sound long-term policy, and to help them hold to long-term policy when short-term exigencies are most distressing" (a 2021 printing, p. 59, as quoted by Allodium 2024). CONFLICTING (row 5).
   - **Read at source, in his own voice:** "all of us are always our own worst enemies" (2023 podcast). The CFA Institute (2010) ranks the same job first, with a hedge: "**Perhaps most importantly**, the IPS serves as a policy guide that can offer an objective course of action to be followed during periods of market disruption". Card Q7 row 20 quoted this sentence without its first three words.
   - **The 1975 article is about something else.** "The Loser's Game" argues that trading costs and active managers lose to the market. It is not about a written policy calming the investor. Its advice is "Know your policies very well and play according to them all the time", then "Make fewer and perhaps better investment decisions" (rows 1-3).
   - **The 1979 article gives the written policy a different first job:** deciding who decides. "60 per cent of the companies surveyed ... delegate key policy decisions to investment managers", so policy should be written down as "a basis for continuing, responsible communication between money manager and client" (row 4).
   - **The builders give an IPS three jobs (row 18):**
     - hold to policy in bad markets;
     - say who decides, and keep the people who come later on the same plan (Ellis's ten-year and "successor committee" tests, fi360's "competent third party", Trone's continuity);
     - be the record that conduct is judged against (fi360's "paper trail"; Texas 2256.006(b)).
   - **"More than choosing assets" fails.** For Ellis the policy is the asset mix and staying with it, so choosing and holding are one job (row 19).
   - **For Laura, job one is carried mostly by the instruments.** IPS l.13 says so itself: "Held to the end, these pay what they say – no price fluctuations for us to fear." The paper's calming job is needed only where a price can tempt a change: VT before the 2031 move. That is exactly what l.17's last sentence addresses. Her IPS chiefly does jobs two and three.
   - **Judge's note:** the founder of the practice said the written policy's job is to protect the portfolio from its owner. In Laura's plan the bonds do that for every promised dollar, so the IPS is left with his other test: a plan a stranger could carry out and check ten years later "serene in the confidence that it would all have been done just the way it should have been done".
2. **Claim 2: KEEP, with one change. The checks are about adherence, but returns are still judged against the objective, and the ladder's objective is dates and amounts.**
   - **The law and real audits judge adherence, not returns.**
     - Texas judges public investing on "whether the investment decision was consistent with the written investment policy", across the whole portfolio, not one holding (2256.006(b)). It requires an audit of "adherence to the entity's established investment policies" every year or two (2256.005(m)/(n)).
     - Real audits test exactly that. The Texas Water Development Board (Dec 2025) tested "investment transactions and holdings for adherence to strategies, restrictions, controls and objectives" and reported "no discrepancies were noted". TxDOT (2022) checked approvals, conflict statements, training and reports. Neither tested returns (rows 20-23).
     - fi360: "fiduciary liability is not determined by investment performance, but in whether a prudent process was followed". CEFEX audits advisers' files against a written standard built on ISO 19011 (row 30).
   - **The change.** fi360 still compares performance "against an appropriate benchmark and evaluated in the context of portfolio objectives" (4.1.1). Monitoring never ends at the purchase: Tibble (2015, unanimous) calls it a "continuing duty". For the ladder the objective is each payment covered on its date, so the yardstick is dates and amounts (Q17 rows 18-19), and the watch list is Table 3.
   - **What such a review would check in Caplet's case** (rows 31-32, from NUMBERS 8m):
     - each holding against its job: 10 of 10 payments each have a holding that pays before it;
     - only Treasuries and VT held, and no promised money in shares (l.11);
     - every Treasury held at its target units, and VT bought last from the cash left;
     - 11 of 200 trades and $200 of commissions; nothing sold, and no trade after 8 Oct;
     - a written reason for each trade (15 notes on 11 trades);
     - the two planned steps checked by date and order, not by what the market did.
     - No review rule is added (CANON A34).
   - **Judge's note:** a state auditor reviews a public portfolio without once asking how much it made. It checks the holdings and trades against the written policy, and the finding is "no discrepancies were noted". Section 2.5 can be written in that form.
   - **A second aha for 2.0 and 2.3 (row 24).** Texas law ranks what public money must weigh "in order of importance": suitability to what the money must pay comes first and diversification fifth, with yield last. Ten Treasuries matched to ten dates are not "under-diversified" for promised money by that standard.
3. **Claim 3: KEEP "unusually few", but for a different reason than expected. The market carve-out is the profession's boilerplate; the rare word is "only".**
   - **The carve-out is near-universal.** Six of the seven institutional IPSs read at source carry a version of "It is not expected that the investment policy will change frequently. In particular, short-term changes in the financial markets should not require an adjustment": TRSL, SCERA, FIU, CSULB Alumni Center, Akron and Stockton. UNE's does not. The wording repeats almost word for word across documents written by different institutions, so it is template language (row 33).
   - **Every one of the seven keeps an open door. Examples:**
     - "The Policy may be modified, in whole or in part, by the Board at any point in time" (TRSL);
     - "reviewed periodically and amended, if appropriate" (Stockton);
     - "Other presently unknown significant structural issues not currently anticipated by the IPS" (Akron);
     - a manager "will recommend changes ... at any time when the manager views any part of this statement to be at variance with overall market and economic conditions" (SCERA).
   - **The doors get used.** SCERA lists 12 revisions from 21 Feb 2019 to 30 Jul 2026, and FIU's IPS reached its ninth draft (rows 34-36).
   - **Stockton names the very trigger l.17 rules out:** "Extended periods of negative returns or greater-than-market volatility will be cause for review of the strategies employed by the OCIO" (row 38).
   - **What Caplet's two reasons gain:**
     - a list anyone can check;
     - nothing to decide at a market low;
     - no allocation bands and no manager reviews, because there is no mix to rebalance and no manager chosen for skill.
   - **What they give up** (row 41):
     - the catch-all for events no one foresaw;
     - a calendar review;
     - a board's open power to amend.
   - **Laura keeps her legal power to change anyway** (Q7: the commitment is soft before 2031), so the closed list binds the reasons the plan gives, not her rights.
   - **The IPS is silent on a change in Laura's own goals.** Most real change clauses are about that (UNE, Akron). The report notes the silence (the building not happening is card Q77) and never fills it with a rule (A34).
   - **Judge's note:** "A fall in the market is not one of them" is the most ordinary sentence in the IPS world, said in ten plain words instead of about thirty. What no other IPS read says is "only".

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 33 with row 34 (3e).** "A fall in the market is not one of them" is the profession's most ordinary sentence; the rare word is "only". This reverses what a judge would expect and evaluates l.17 without adding to it.
2. **Row 38 (3e).** Stockton's IPS makes "extended periods of negative returns" a reason for review, which is the exact trigger l.17 excludes. The reason it is excluded (no manager chosen for skill; bonds that pay the same after a fall) is the plan's design in one contrast.
3. **Row 22 with rows 21 and 31 (2.5).** A state auditor judges a portfolio on adherence to its written policy and reports "no discrepancies were noted", with no test of returns. Section 2.5 can take that form: each criterion from 8m, the test, the result.
4. **Row 24 (2.0 or 2.3).** Texas public-money law ranks matching the money to what it must pay first and diversification fifth. It answers "why so few holdings?" with a standard from outside finance theory.
5. **Row 18 with row 9 (2.0 or 3.0).** The founder's job for an IPS (protect the portfolio from its owner) is done by the bonds for every promised dollar. What the IPS still does is Ellis's ten-year test: a plan a stranger could carry out and check later.
