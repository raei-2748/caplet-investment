- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** choosing how sure is sure enough before promising money to someone who will rely on it. Prudential regulators answered with a percentage over one year plus a named refill (Basel 2005; Solvency II 2009; the PPF's funding strategy, 2010); US law for fixed sums owed on fixed dates answered with a method, naming what may back the payment (IRC 130 for structured settlements, 1982; the escrow "without reinvestment" in bond refundings).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Every number below 100 has someone behind it.**
- (a) An insurer, a bank and Britain's pension lifeboat each accept a small chance of falling short, and each names who pays when that chance comes up: the insurer's owners must refill it within six months, the bank's capital takes the loss first, and the lifeboat can raise its levy on other pension schemes or trim pensioners' increases.
- (b) Laura has no one in that seat, because the case says the ten payments may not rely on "co-sponsors, grants, program fees, or other outside funding" and no money reaches her portfolio after January 2028; so a percentage borrowed from any of these regimes would quietly borrow a backer she does not have, and that, not a threshold, is why the IPS kept the payments off "anybody else's money" (l.11).
- (c) Where it breaks: for twelve months Laura does have a backer, her own 2028 deposit, and it is exactly the backer IPS l.9 distrusts, since a writer's advances shrink in the years shares fall; the break teaches that the plan's only reliance on anyone lasts one year, and the deposit's first dollars go to the payments.
- Decision: IPS l.11's "one step further" (ten payments in Treasuries, nothing promised resting on shares or on others). Part: 3a reserve, one clause; 3f only as the reason no anchor is named. Sources: Directive 2009/138/EC Arts 138-139; BCBS (2005) s.5.1; PPF (2010; 2015); Q97-012; Rev. Proc. 2016-42; case l.64-65, l.91-92.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha.** A finance judge knows each regime; the common thread, and that the case's own "no outside funding" rule is what makes every one of them inapplicable, is not in any of them.

**2. A one-year level multiplied up is the wrong ruler, and the regulator's own ruler is harsher.**
- (a) The professor's anchor turns an insurer's 99.5% for one year into about 97.0% for six years by multiplying, which only works if the insurer starts every year refilled, as the law makes it do within six months.
- (b) Measured the insurer's own way, one year at a time, the first plan fails at once: three-quarters of $300,000 in shares would draw a capital charge of about $88,000 against about $7,700 of room on the 25 September curve, and in about a third of the 27 September model's markets its money would already be worth less than the ten payments at the end of 2027.
- (c) The break is the lesson: the first plan's 96.8 in 100 counts on six years of share returns to repair early losses, which no one-year regulator credits, so "just below 97%" is an artefact of the conversion; an insurer's real test would have called the gap tens of thousands of dollars in the first year, not 0.2 points over six.
- Decision: dropping the first plan; naming no outside threshold. Part: none in the report (it is the answer to the professor if asked; store). Sources: Directive Arts 138, 139, 144; Delegated Reg. 2015/35 Arts 168-169; NUMBERS 8s; Keel arithmetic on the 27 Sep model.
- **Pedigree or aha: pedigree for the finance professor (he will know compounding assumes refills), aha for the other three readers.** Its job is to stop the report adopting the professor's first remedy.

**3. The models straddle every line, so a line decides nothing.**
- (a) The same first plan scores 96.8 in 100 in the team's 27 September model, 95.0 on the register's current model, 95.9 when its safe part is a bond fund, and 98.6 on US share history since 1871.
- (b) The insurer's compounded 97.0 and the US tax code's 95 both sit inside that spread, so whether the first plan "passes" depends on which model a reader picks, and a threshold that a modelling choice can flip cannot settle anything.
- (c) It would break if one model were plainly right, but each leaves something out (rate moves before 2033, fees, a deposit that shrinks); what every test agrees on is the gap between some misses and none, which is why the report gives the count and its basis, never a pass mark.
- Decision: 3f gives a count (about 1 in 31), never a comparison with a standard. Part: 3f (governs wording); store. Sources: NUMBERS 8s; Q120-025; Rev. Rul. 77-374 via Rev. Proc. 2016-42.
- **Pedigree or aha: neither; a methods point.** It shows why "chosen after the fact" is a fair charge against any anchor.

**4. The 96.8 was measured in a world where the deposit always comes.**
- (a) The 27 September model handed the first plan the full $150,000 in January 2028 whatever shares had done in 2027, so its 96.8 in 100 describes a world where a writer's income never falls with the market.
- (b) That is the world the team stopped believing in that same day (IPS l.9: in a recession the advances "come later and smaller, and during that same period, shares fall"); in the model's own runs, after a year in which shares fell, the first plan missed a payment about 1 time in 17 with the full deposit and about 1 time in 4 with half of it.
- (c) Where it breaks: nobody has measured how far Laura's own income falls with shares, so 1 in 4 is a slice of a model, not her forecast; but the direction is enough to show that the team's reason needs no threshold, because the 96.8 was computed on the very assumption the reason rejects.
- Decision: dropping the first plan for IPS l.9's reason (CANON A3). Part: 3f (the deposit case at the centre, as the plain reader asks; one figure in prose); the conditional slice to the numbers owner for NUMBERS 8s. Sources: `strategy_mc.py` (27 Sep model, imported unchanged; its 6,453 and 27,386 misses reproduce); NUMBERS 8s, 8u; FINAL l.9.
- **Pedigree or aha: aha.** It turns the team's reason from a story into a measured mechanism with the team's own model, and it shows the attacked concession ("96.8 would already pass") was conceding something the team never believed.

**5. Where law governs a fixed sum on a fixed date, it names a method, not a percentage.**
- (a) When US law says how a fixed sum owed on a fixed date must be backed (a structured settlement for an injured person, an escrow that repays a city's old bonds), it names a method, US government obligations timed to the payments, and writes no percentage at all.
- (b) Where law does write a percentage (90% for oil reserves, about 90% for a judge's "beyond reasonable doubt", 99.5% for an insurer's whole year), it is measuring something uncertain by nature that no method can settle; Laura's ten payments are the first kind, so the honest outside anchor for them is a method, which her plan meets and the first plan fails outright.
- (c) It breaks because the method rules were built for other jobs (a tax exclusion, a bond refunding, a UK pension's end state that still allows gilts plus 0.5% and calls itself "not no dependency"), so they are pedigree for 3a's standard, one clause at most, and never a pass mark for 3f.
- Decision: the escrow standard behind IPS l.11's step (NUMBERS 2). Part: 3a, a clause at most. Sources: Q2b-020 (IRC 130, read in full by card Q2b); Q20-022; Q7-022; Q2a-027; Q120-015; Q120-001.
- **Pedigree or aha: pedigree.** Both fixed-income readers know dedication and defeasance; its use here is to classify, not to impress.
