**The problem in register figures (NUMBERS headline, 4, 8h).** Central range $95,000 to $115,000; contribution $115,000.
At the same rates about $75,000 returns to Laura: the reserve's overflow (the payments' backstop) $45,456 in 1 Jan 2033

**Claims tested.**
1. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE: "Promising only what is certain and keeping the rest back reads as design, not holding back:
   breaking a promise costs trust far more than exceeding it earns." (the lead's prior; tested hardest)
2. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE (the objection at its strongest): "Lead-gift theory says the size of the lead gift signals the
   project's quality, so a founder who holds back about $75,000 lowers what others give."
3. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE: "Accounting and gift-reporting practice already put Laura's stated range inside the countable
   pledge and the rest outside it, legitimately."

**Problem class and the fields that solved it.** Calibrating a public commitment when a lead gift is read as a signal.
Social psychology measured how promises are judged (broken, kept, exceeded). Accounting and finance measured the reward
to meeting or beating a stated figure and the cost of managing it down (earnings guidance). Public economics tested
lead gifts in the field and in the lab (announcement, size, sequence, signalling). Nonprofit accounting and CASE
reporting rules decide what counts as a pledge (already in Q18 and Q11b).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
  - The asymmetry holds for promises, and it is large:
    - real promises: broken 8.32, kept 9.88, exceeded 9.77 on an 11-point scale (d .74 against .06). Delivering 15
      tables instead of the promised 10 earned nothing;
    - recalled promises: exceeding gained only 13.3% of what breaking cost;
    - concert seats: any shortfall, however small, 7.97 down to 2.05; better seats than promised 7.20, slightly worse
      than exact.
  - Not supported: "keeping the rest back reads as design". No study shows that, and two lines of evidence push the other
    way:
    - markets do reward a beat: about 3.2% a quarter over a miss with the same forecast error (Bartov et al.);
    - a figure known to be set low reads as sandbagging (the co-sponsor judge). Talking expectations down bought firms
      nothing ("counterproductive", Downing & Sharpe); Bartov finds the premium only "somewhat smaller" when it was
      managed.
  - Changed claim: never miss a stated figure; giving more than stated buys almost no trust; so held-back money must be
    justified by its job, named and sized, never by "exceeding wins trust".
  - Sized for Laura: the cap's $8,476 excess, if given, would buy about nothing (d .06). Kept, it costs nothing in trust.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** The objection survives in part: at about $30,000, not $75,000, and mainly if the money is left
  unnamed.
  - Announcing a credible lead gift does most of the work:
    - mean gift €74.3 to €132 (+78%) and total raised +65%, with no change in response (Huck & Rasul);
    - +71% raised on the same money, announced against unannounced (Rondeau & List);
    - leading by example raised contributions 50%, but only when followers could not judge quality themselves (Potters
      et al.).
  - Size matters too, on weaker evidence:
    - a €12,000 lead against €60,000: response 1.3-1.5% against 3.5%, mean gift €102-€113 against €132; a different
      year and recipients, "tentative" (Huck, Rasul & Shephard);
    - seed money from 10% to 67% of the goal: about sixfold giving (Q11a).
  - What makes the signal credible is the leader's sacrifice (Andreoni). A leader who can give again later does not
    free-ride (Vesterlund). No field test compares a held-back top-up with giving everything at once.
  - Keel sums on the register:
    - $45,456 of the $75,279 (60%) is the payments' backstop, the price of their certainty, not facility money;
    - the facility-side money held back is $29,823: 20.6% of the $144,823 on the facility side, 5.1% of everything she
      holds on 1 Jan 2033;
    - she promises 87.2% of everything ($399,089 of payments at forwards plus $115,000);
    - in nominal terms, $615,000 promised from $450,000 deposited.
- **Claim 3: KEEP.** Nothing new was needed at the source. Q18 (rows 1-7) and Q11b (row 32) already hold the rules:
  - an intention is not a promise (ASU 2018-08);
  - undocumented pledges are not counted;
  - CASE advises separate goals for revocable money.
  - Only the range is pledged. The excess, the half kept and the backstop sit outside it, legitimately. A later gift
    from them counts when it is documented, as a new gift.
- **IPS l.15 ("half ... promised to no one").** Its words bar promising the kept half, not giving it later. Its three
  named uses are all project uses. Two limits:
  - a default destination "to the facility" would turn it into a promise, against l.15 and CANON A30;
  - the backstop is operating reserve (l.17) until the payments no longer need it.

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. A promise kept counts, a promise exceeded does not (rows 1-4, 29)**
- What is it? Four sets of experiments on promises: real ones (puzzle tables), recalled ones, hypothetical ones and a
  concert pilot. Breaking costs a lot; exceeding earns about nothing. A promise is valued above an expectation met.
- Did it work? Yes:
  - real promises: d .74 for breaking, .06 for exceeding;
  - recalled promises: exceeding gained 13.3% of what breaking cost;
  - any worse concert seat: 7.97 down to 2.05, whatever the size of the shortfall.
  - No direct replication was found, and Crossref shows no update notice. Pilot samples are students.
- How is Laura's case like it? The 2031 range is a public promise. The cap means she gives the top ($115,000 in the
  median) and never more. The $8,476 excess goes to her own money.
- How is it not?
  - Co-sponsors are institutions, not puzzle partners, and the stakes are larger.
  - Funders also judge what a gift reveals about the project (rows 17-21), not only whether a promise was kept.
- What Caplet takes: 3b note: the cap gives up no trust (exceeding earns about d .06). 3d: never show a modelled payout
  above the top as an expectation (row 3). Exhibit 3's "excess" column can read "kept by Laura".

**Card 2. It is the fact of a miss, not its size (rows 4, 9, 10)**
- What is it? Two fields found the same shape:
  - growth stocks lose 10-15% on a small earnings miss, "the fact ... rather than its magnitude";
  - concert buyers marked any worse seat down to 2.05 "regardless of how much worse".
  - And firms that talked expectations down gained nothing ("counterproductive").
- Did it work? These are field data on firm returns over many years (Skinner & Sloan; Downing & Sharpe) and one pilot.
- How is Laura's case like it? Both stated ends must be owned to the dollar: the 0.5% deduction and the rounding down
  (NUMBERS 4). Choosing the bottom to be beaten would buy nothing and invite the sandbagging reading (the co-sponsor
  judge).
- How is it not? Stock prices aggregate many traders. Laura's co-sponsors are few, and they talk to her.
- What Caplet takes: 3c and 3d already state the rule behind each end; this card supports saying the bottom comes from
  a rule, not from caution.

**Card 3. Announcement and credibility first, size second (rows 12-16, 21)**
- What is it? Field experiments on lead gifts:
  - announcing a lead gift: +65% to +78% raised (Huck & Rasul);
  - the same money announced against unannounced: +71% (Rondeau & List);
  - a smaller lead in a later year: lower response, 1.3-1.5% against 3.5% (Huck, Rasul & Shephard, tentative);
  - in the lab, leading by example works only when followers cannot judge quality themselves (+50%; Potters et al.).
- Did it work? The announcement effects are randomised and large. The size effect is real in List & Lucking-Reiley's
  sixfold result, but it is measured against a stated goal. The Huck, Rasul & Shephard comparison is not randomised.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her owned, dated $115,000 announced in 2031 is the lead. The kept money, unannounced,
  adds nothing to the lead effect either way (row 13).
- How is it not?
  - Small-donor mailings, not institutional co-sponsors.
  - Her goal, the facility's cost, is out of scope (case l.106-107), so the "share of the goal" that drives the size
    effect cannot be computed.
- What Caplet takes: 3d leads with what is committed and owned (as Q19 and Q22 already plan). The size worry is answered
  by the sacrifice in card 4, not by giving the kept money away.

**Card 4. The sacrifice is the whole commitment, and most of the $75,000 is not facility money (rows 19, 25-28)**
- What is it? Andreoni: a lead gift persuades because of the leader's sacrifice; it must be "unusually large".
- Did it work? It is theory, consistent with the field results in card 3.
- How is Laura's case like it? Laura's sacrifice to the project is:
  - the ten operating payments ($500,000 nominal; $399,089 at forwards on 1 Jan 2033, NUMBERS 8h);
  - plus $115,000;
  - together 87.2% of all she holds on 1 Jan 2033 (Keel sum).
  - Of the $75,279 that returns, $45,456 (60%) is the payments' backstop: the price of "sufficient without
    reinvestment", spent on the payments in a low-rate world ($20,606 if rates are 3 points lower; $1,414 at zero).
  - The facility-side money she keeps is $29,823: 20.6% of the $144,823 on the facility side.
- How is it not?
  - Co-sponsors may weigh only the facility gift, since operations are not their ask.
  - The backstop's return is a forward-rate figure, not a forecast.
- What Caplet takes: 3b splits what returns into two named parts with sizes (see (c)). 3d's first line is the operating
  commitment (Q22 already plans this). Suggest adding the 87.2%, 60/40 and 20.6% figures to NUMBERS before any use.

**Card 5. Giving again later is allowed in theory and used in practice (rows 18, 22, 23)**
- What is it?
  - Theory: Vesterlund shows that a leader who can give again does not free-ride.
  - Practice: practitioners use closing challenges from early lead donors (JGA).
  - A real case: a lead donor who stated "more than $113 million" ended at $260 million (Q18 row 32).
- Did it work? There is no field test of hold-back-then-top-up against all-at-once. The evidence is theory, practice
  and one case.
- How is Laura's case like it? D12 already makes any later gift from the kept money her separate choice, outside the
  range. A closing challenge in 2033-2034 is one form it could take.
- How is it not? A match is a condition co-sponsors cannot count until it is met (Q18 row 7). Laura's later money is
  small and depends on markets.
- What Caplet takes: one line of design in 3b: what returns is Laura's, and any later gift is a new, announced decision.
  This is not promised in 2031 (l.15).

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE.**
- What survives: the promise asymmetry, in four experiments.
  - Real promises: kept 9.88, broken 8.32 (-1.56), exceeded 9.77 (-0.11); d .74 against .06.
  - Recalled promises: the exceeding gain was 13.3% of the breaking loss.
  - Expectations differ: exceeding them is rewarded (d 1.04). Exceeding a promise is not (d .14).
  - Pilot: any worse seat 2.05 against 7.97 for exact; better seats 7.20.
  - Political pledges agree (Naurin et al.; abstract only).
  - The case's own words are one-sided the same way (case l.114).
- What breaks:
  - "Keeping the rest back reads as design, not holding back": no source supports it. It holds only if the held-back
    money has a named job and size (the co-sponsor judge: "Named and sized, it is prudence. Unnamed, it is a reason to
    wonder").
  - The general claim that exceeding never pays is false in markets. A beat earns about 3.2% a quarter over a miss with
    the same forecast error, and per unit the reward exceeds the penalty (Bartov et al.). That is a repeated game in
    prices, unlike a one-off public promise.
  - A low-set figure is read as sandbagging (the judge). Firms gained nothing by talking expectations down (Downing &
    Sharpe). Markets still pay most of the premium when the beat was managed (Bartov et al.), so this penalty is
    social, not a market fact.
- Changed claim, sized:
  - A miss on a stated figure costs about 7 times (recalled promises) to 14 times (real promises, in rating points) what a beat earns.
  - So both stated ends must be owned (already the rule; NUMBERS 4).
  - Giving the $8,476 excess would buy about nothing.
  - What Laura keeps must be justified by its job: the payments' certainty, and the flexibility the case asks for (case
    l.102-103, l.145). Never justify it by "under-promise and over-deliver".
- One new risk: showing co-sponsors a modelled payout above the top turns it into an expectation (row 3). The capped
  gift would then read as a shortfall. Q19 card 5's "the modelled payout is stated" needs this caution for 3d.

**Claim 2: CHANGE.** The objection survives at about $30,000, and only if that money is unnamed.
- Announcement and credibility dominate:
  - +78% mean gift and +65% total from announcing a lead (Huck & Rasul);
  - +71% from announcing the same money (Rondeau & List);
  - +50% from leading by example only under asymmetric information, none under full information (Potters et al.).
- Size has some evidence:
  - about sixfold from 10% to 67% seed (Q11a);
  - response 3.5% against 1.3-1.5% for a lead a fifth the size, in a later year, not randomised (Huck, Rasul &
    Shephard).
  - The authors read it as "tentative" support for the value of the gift, not the donor's identity, as the quality
    signal.
- A second, later challenge: no randomised test found. Theory says the ability to give again removes the leader's
  free-ride (Vesterlund 2003). Practitioners use closing challenges (JGA). One real lead donor stated low and gave far
  more (Q18 row 32).
- Founders who state a conservative gift and give more later: only that case was found, with no measure of others'
  response. That is a gap.
- Applied to Laura (Keel sums on NUMBERS 4 and 8h):
  - $45,456 of the $75,279 is the payments' backstop. It is operating money until the payments no longer need it, so it
    is not withheld from the building.
  - Facility-side money kept: $29,823 (20.6% of $144,823; 25.9% of the contribution; 5.1% of everything).
  - Promised: 87.2% of everything she holds on 1 Jan 2033.
  - The sacrifice behind the signal (Andreoni) is the operating commitment plus the gift.
- Where the objection still bites:
  - co-sponsors who weigh only the facility gift;
  - the practitioners' "top gift too low" rule (Q11b row 29);
  - Kresge's "less need" (Q11b row 22);
  - the judge's "anchor low".

**Claim 3: KEEP, from Q18 and Q11b.** Nothing new was read at the source; the rules are already verified there.
- FASB ASU 2018-08: an intention to give is not a promise (Q18 rows 1-3).
- CASE counts documented pledges at face value and not undocumented ones (Q18 row 6). It advises separate goals for
  revocable money (Q11b row 32).
- So only the stated range is countable. The excess, the half kept and the backstop sit outside it, legitimately.
- A later gift from them is counted when it is documented, as a new gift. This matches CANON D12 word for word in
  substance.

  - (i) the payments' backstop: about $45,000 in 1 Jan 2033 money at today's forwards. It is the price of the payments'

**Risk to record if the objection survives.**

**Does IPS l.15 limit what Laura can later give?**

  - "Half of the value of the shares will be promised to no one" bars a promise in advance, and CANON A30 bars counting

  - The three named uses are project uses ("building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor
    falling short"). Spending the kept money on them later is inside the IPS's own words.
  - A later gift is not a change of plan under l.17's "only 2 possible reasons". It is Laura's separate choice (D12).

  - (i) No default destination "to the facility". That would be a promise by another name, against l.15 and A30.

  - (ii) The backstop is part of "the operating reserve" (l.17). It can be freed only as the payments stop needing it.
