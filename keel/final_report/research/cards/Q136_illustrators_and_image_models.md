## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP "measurably"; CHANGE "fallen" to "fallen where work is short, commissioned and generic, and still falling".**
  - **Measured on platforms, not only surveyed.**
    - Demirci, Hannane and Zhu: job posts for graphic design and 3D modelling fell 17.01% within a year of the image models (July 2022), against a control group of jobs AI cannot do. Graphic design fell 18.47%, 3D modelling 15.52%. The data are 1,388,711 job posts from July 2021 to July 2023 (rows 1-2).
    - Hui, Reshef and Zhou, on Upwork: image freelancers (designers, image editors, artists) lost 3.7% of monthly jobs and 9.4% of income in the 2023 version. Later versions give 2.1% and 5.2%, with other specifications from 1.9%/4.8% to 4.3%/17.0%. Every version is negative (rows 3-5).
    - Goldberg and Lam, on a stock-image marketplace: after labelled AI images were allowed (December 2022), non-AI production fell 15%, active non-AI artists 29% and non-AI sales 28%, while total sales rose 82% (rows 6-7).
  - **Surveyed.**
    - Society of Authors (UK), January 2024, 787 replies: 26% of illustrators had already lost work to generative AI, and 37% said their income had "decreased in value" (row 13).
    - Association of Illustrators (UK): those who know they lost work to AI rose from 32% (2025, 5,000 replies) to 46% (2026, 549 replies). More than half said their illustration income fell in the last 12 months, 22% by more than half; the AOI says AI "is not the only reason" (rows 14-17).
    - Freelance League of Japan, Sep-Oct 2025, 24,991 replies (54.2% illustrators, 15.0% manga artists): 12.0% said AI had cut their income (9.3% by 10-50%, 2.7% by more than half), 40.9% saw little change and 30.7% did not know (rows 18-19).
  - **The counter-case.** On China's leading art-commission platform, a leaked anime-image model (October 2022) cut prices 64%, but orders rose 121% and revenue 56%; artists registered before the leak won 97.41% of orders afterwards (rows 9-10).
  - **Still falling.** Demirci et al.'s effect was 12.90% to November 2022 and 17.01% over the full year ("With a longer post-period, we observe a bigger impact"). Hui and Reshef (2025): the declines "continue to grow". The AOI's share rose 14 points in a year (rows 2, 5, 14).
  - **Judge's note:** in Japan 88.6% of the creators asked call AI a serious threat to their living, but 12.0% say it has cut their income so far (Keel arithmetic: about 7 to 1). Quote loss questions, never fear questions, as evidence of the shock.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Less exposed in what rests on her own name, story and copyright; about as exposed in commissioned work. No study isolates author-illustrators (Gaps).**
  - **For.**
    - The US Copyright Office cut the AI pages out of a graphic novel's copyright: in *Zarya of the Dawn* the text and the "selection, coordination, and arrangement" are protected; the Midjourney images "are not the product of human authorship" (row 22).
    - The D.C. Circuit held in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (18 Mar 2025) that the Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being" (row 23).
    - The Authors Guild's model contract has the publisher agree "not to use AI-generated images, artwork, design, and other visual elements for the book cover or interior artwork without Author's prior express approval" (row 24). A commissioned illustrator has no such clause to bargain for.
    - The AOI found the least pressure in children's books: 42% saw clients use AI instead of commissioning (the lowest of 18 areas), against 64% in concept art (row 16).
    - Where cheap images arrived, the top of each market held: Mihuashi's commercial orders stayed roughly flat (row 10); higher-capability designers moved to harder contests (row 11); remaining non-AI artists moved into niches (row 7); Getty's own 2008 memo said high-end marketing and "publishing (e.g., textbooks)" were not hit by the last shock (row 33).
  - **Against.**
    - In the AOI's "comics and graphic novels" area, 56% saw AI used instead of commissioning, above children's books (row 16). Those are illustrators hired for comics, not author-illustrators selling their own books.
    - Hui et al.: past success did not protect; above-median earners in image work lost a further 7% of jobs and 14% of income (row 4).
    - 78% of UK illustrators expect lower future income (row 13).
  - **Better wording:** "Commissioned illustration has lost work to image models. The part of an author-illustrator's income that rests on her own books (advances, royalties, her name) has a moat the law built: a machine's pictures have no copyright."
  - **Judge's note:** copyright now draws the line the IPS's metaphor needs. The pages a graphic novelist draws are hers to license. The pictures a model makes belong to no one, so nobody can sell them as she can sell hers.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, sharpened. It argues for the order of purchase, not for fewer shares.**
  - **One door.** No money goes in or out before 2033 except the two deposits (case p.2; Q1-047). A permanent fall in her income after January 2028 never touches the portfolio.
  - **What the door can take.** At today's rates the 2028 deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller (73.9%) and all ten payments would still be paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 8u). The payments need the deposit's first $39,162, 26.1% of it (NUMBERS 5). Even at zero rates in January 2028 the first slice is $60,393, so the standard survives a deposit 59.7% smaller whatever 2027 brings (8u (d)).
  - **What a smaller deposit lowers.** The facility contribution, never the payments: a $112,500 deposit gives a stated bottom of $65,000; a $75,000 deposit buys no VT and gives one figure, $40,000 (NUMBERS 5). Nothing is stated before it is owned (IPS l.15), so a smaller deposit lowers a figure not yet stated and breaks nothing (8u's three verbs).
  - **Not a reason to change.** IPS l.17 names a missed deposit and a Treasury default; a smaller deposit runs the same order (CANON D9). Only a deposit that never comes changes the plan (the ten $57,455 short at zero interest, 8u (b)).
  - **Milder for this plan than a recession.** A recession tends to bring falling yields, which raise what the deposit must first complete (Q1-048). A 2-point fall during 2027 cuts the deposit cut the standard survives from 73.9% to 69.1% (NUMBERS 5). An AI shock that comes in a share boom brings no such fall (Keel inference), so the 73.9% stands.
  - **Why not fewer shares.** The AI shock came while the S&P 500 returned 26.06%, 24.88% and 17.78% in 2023-2025 (85.4% compounded, Keel arithmetic). It is the career's own risk, not its market risk. Theory says such risk shrinks the tilt toward shares but does not reverse it (Q1-004); what it argues for is owning promised money before it is needed, which IPS l.11 and l.13 already do.
  - **Judge's note:** the IPS's "giant stock" has two risks, the market's and its own. The plan meets both the same way: the payments are bought with money she already has, before either can bite.
- **Claim 4 (added): KILL "2009 was only cyclical". Riskier in degree, not in kind.**
  - **Photography, 1930s-1960s.** *Vogue* reported in 1936 that photographic covers sold better (Illustration History). In summer 1962 the *Saturday Evening Post*'s editors "decided to use mostly portraits or photographs on the covers"; Rockwell's last *Post* cover ran in December 1963 (Norman Rockwell Museum) (row 31).
  - **Microstock and the web, 2006-2009.** iStockphoto licensed images "for as little as one dollar"; Getty bought it on 9 Feb 2006 for $50.0 million (Getty 10-K, 8-K). Getty's traditional creative stills revenue fell 8.3% in 2007 ($611.5 million to $560.8 million), and its traditional unit sales fell from 0.35 million (Q1 2006) to an estimated 0.28 million (Q1 2008), down 20% (Keel arithmetic). The S&P 500 returned 5.48% in 2007 (rows 32-33).
  - **What is new now.** Speed and reach. Image models reached graphic design, 3D modelling, concept art, games and book covers within a year, and the measured effect is still growing (rows 2, 5, 14, 16).
  - **Judge's note:** a 2009 illustrator also faced a structural shock, and it too began in a rising share market (2007). The pattern repeats: the trade's own shocks do not wait for a recession.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 21 (shares rose 85% while illustrators' losses rose), with row 28.** Part 2.3. The "giant stock" has its own risk as well as the market's, and image models are the own-risk kind: the opposite timing from IPS l.9's recession. A judge expects "AI is bad for illustrators, so hold fewer shares"; the evidence says "own the promises first".
2. **Rows 29 and 15 together.** Part 3e area (2). In a trade where about one respondent in five lost more than half a year's illustration income (AOI 2026), the ten payments survive a deposit about three-quarters smaller at today's rates (NUMBERS 8u). It shows the size of the plan's room in the client's own industry's terms, with no claim about her income.
3. **Row 32 (microstock, 2007).** Part 3.0 or 2.3. A 2009 illustrator also faced a structural shock, and it too began in a rising share market. It tells the judge the risk is old and recurring, so the plan was built for its kind, not for one headline.
4. **Row 22 (Zarya of the Dawn).** Part 2.3. Copyright splits a graphic novel: the human pages are property, the Midjourney pages belong to no one. It gives "a stock she can't sell" a precise edge: the part of her career that cannot be sold is also the part a machine cannot own.
5. **Row 35 (milder for this plan than a recession).** Part 3e area (2). A recession hits the deposit and raises what it must first complete (falling yields); an AI shock in a boom does only the first. It turns a scary headline into one figure that does not move (73.9% at today's rates).
