- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** telling a client or the public a dollar figure about the future without passing a forecast off as a fact, or a fact off as a forecast. Solved by securities regulation (US broker rules since NASD days, the SEC's projections policy in Regulation S-K Item 10(b), the Advisers Act marketing rule of 2020), professional ethics (CFA Institute Standards I(C) and V(B)), performance-reporting standards (GIPS 2020), the UK's conduct rules (COBS 4.6 and 13) and the EU's investor summary (PRIIPs KID, rebuilt from 1 Jan 2023 after its scenarios misled).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: KILL for owned ends; KEEP only for today's figures.** Every rule read defines a projection as an estimate of future performance: FINRA, "an estimate of the future performance of an investment or investment strategy, which is often based on historical data and assumptions" (as quoted in SEC Release 34-106675, fn 3); the SEC, "Projected returns reflect an investment adviser's performance estimate" (2021 adopting release); FINRA's FAQ D.6, a return on holdings is a projection when "its valuation [has] to be estimated". An end that is owned in Treasuries maturing before 15 Dec 2032 and counted at what its holdings pay if nothing more earns interest, after the fund fee and the waiting ETF's fee, less 0.5% (NUMBERS 0, 4), estimates no future performance. The EU's investor summary has the right form for it: "Where a minimum return is guaranteed that minimum return shall be stated in monetary amounts" (Delegated Regulation 2021/2268, Annex V). So: today's "about $95,000 to $115,000" is a projection (MODEL, on the 6 Nov 2026 curve); the bottom becomes owned in January 2028 and the top at the 2031 move, and from then on "projection" is the wrong word, as is "estimate".
   - Judge's note: the professional line is not "calculated or not calculated" but "owned or estimated". CFA's Example 5 (Dox) shows the trap from the other side: a calculation from drill samples is "a quantitative opinion, not a fact". A count of what is owned is a fact.
2. **Claim 2: KILL.** The rules permit projections to a known client with their basis shown, and they say nothing to hedge about owned money. The SEC approved on 15 Oct 2026 (Release 34-106675) FINRA's new Rule 2210(d)(1)(F)(iv): a broker may now project performance if it has written procedures making the projection "relevant to the likely financial situation and investment objectives of the intended audience" and gives "sufficient information" on "the criteria used and assumptions made" and "the risks and limitations"; plus a record of the source (2210(b)(4)(A)(iv)). The same three tests have bound advisers since 2022 (17 C.F.R. 275.206(4)-1(d)(6)). What the rules forbid is projections "for a mass audience or general circulation". A plan for one named client is the case the rules allow; Laura's words to co-sponsors are the case they warn about.
   - Judge's note: the regulators drew their line exactly where Caplet drew its own: models for the person whose situation is known (Laura, and the judge reading the plan); only owned figures for the wider audience (co-sponsors, 3d). It is the conceit's non-photo blue (CONCEIT 3c), arrived at by the SEC and FINRA.
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.** The SEC's adopting release: "projections of general market performance or economic conditions ... are not targeted or projected performance returns". J.P. Morgan's 7.5% for world shares (2027 LTCMA, estimates as of 30 Sep 2026) is a market forecast and an assumption; J.P. Morgan itself calls it a forecast ("a 50bps upgrade to global equity forecasts, to 7.5%") and marks the document "NOT FOR RETAIL DISTRIBUTION". What becomes a projection is the top of Laura's range before the move, built on it. Label the 7.5% as J.P. Morgan's forecast for world shares, with its date; label the top as "we modelled". And note what it moves: from 4% to 10% the stated top stays $115,000 (NUMBERS 4).
4. **Claim 4: CHANGE.** The UK rule for any figure about the future: it must "not be based on and does not refer to simulated past performance" and must rest on "reasonable assumptions supported by objective data" (COBS 4.6.7). GIPS asks a firm to say whether theoretical results come from "the retroactive or prospective application of a model" and never to "link actual performance to historical theoretical performance" (1.A.27, 4.C.48). So the replays stay named as what would have happened ("US stocks from July 1929"), each with its window, as the EU's template does ("This type of scenario occurred for an investment between [dates]"). The confidence Laura states after the move rests on ownership, not on any replay (NUMBERS 4: "certain" after the move, by construction).
5. **Claim 5: KILL.** CFA Standard I(C) bars guaranteeing returns on volatile investments but "does not prohibit ... products that have guarantees built into the structure of the product itself". Its own example calls bank deposits "guaranteed" "appropriate" within the insured limit. Its other example sets the limit: interest-only mortgage securities sold as "guaranteed by the US government" broke the standard, because "Although the underlying mortgages are guaranteed, neither the investor's investment nor the interest stream on the IOs is guaranteed". So "guaranteed" belongs to each Treasury held to its date (Book 19), never to an iBond fund's payout, which is its net asset value.
   - Judge's note: EU law states a guaranteed minimum "not taking into account the situation where the ... party bound to make ... payments ... is not able to pay" (2021/2268, Annex IV point 4); the payer's default sits in its own section. That is Book 19's rule, written by a regulator: the condition is said once, apart from the figure (IPS l.11 already said it).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 32, the EU's form for a guaranteed minimum** (3c). A regulator states a guaranteed minimum as a money amount, gives its one condition, and keeps the payer's default out of that line. It gives Book 19 the weight of law and shows a judge that "certain" after the move is a standard form, not bravado.
2. **Row 3, the audience line** (3c, 3d). FINRA and the SEC allow projections for an audience whose situation is known and forbid them for general circulation. That is exactly Caplet's split: models for Laura and the judge, owned figures for co-sponsors. A judge who expected "no projections" finds that the plan's rule is the regulators' rule.
3. **Row 20, the CFA's interest-only example** (2.1, 3a, 3c). A wrapper around guaranteed paper is not itself guaranteed. That is the exact line between a Treasury and an iBond fund, and it shows why the stated ends are counted from holdings at zero interest, less 0.5%, and never called fixed.
4. **Row 15, the CFA's Dox example, with row 37's tense rule** (3c). The same $95,000 is a projection today and an owned figure from January 2028. The sentence "Laura's range is ..." written today is the textbook violation; "on today's market rates the rule gives about ..." is the fix.
5. **Rows 26-28, the UK's statutory projection form** (3c, Table 2). Rounded down, no more than three significant figures, explanation beside the figure, "might" in the heading, and a contractual minimum that replaces the model rate. Caplet's range rule already has this form; the report can say so in a note if a judge asks why the ends are round.
