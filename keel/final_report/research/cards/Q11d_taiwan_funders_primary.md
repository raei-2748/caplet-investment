**Claims tested**
1. Keep, kill or change: "Taiwan's public arts funders (the Ministry of Culture, local cultural affairs bureaus, NCAF)
   fund residencies' programmes and operations year by year and rarely pay for a building; when they do fund a space it
   comes with conditions (matching money, an operating plan, a contract term)."
2. Keep, kill or change: "Taiwan's corporate and family foundations, the likeliest private co-sponsors, are funded year
   by year by their parent companies and must spend a set share each year."
3. Keep, kill or change: "A Taiwanese co-sponsor reads a founder's gift in New Taiwan dollars and expects it in
   writing."

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** Co-financing between partners with different commitment horizons: one
party can fix money years ahead (Laura's owned floor), the others can only commit one budget year at a time. Public
finance and project finance solved it with the matching-grant contract (fixed share, fixed year, overruns on the
owner) and, in Taiwan, with concession contracts under the Act for Promotion of Private Participation in
Infrastructure Projects (促參法: BOT and ROT), which fix the site, the operator, the term and the hand-back while the
money comes from private gifts and the running costs are carried year by year. Nonprofit law adds the payout and
single-grant rules that cap what a foundation can give.

| 1 | Public funders pay year by year and rarely for a building | **CHANGE (mostly keep)** | Programme money is yearly, budget-bound and often paid after delivery: the MoC's rooting grants (cap NT$5m a case a year) approve multi-year work "year by year" and exclude capital; NCAF pays "in principle" within 14 working days after the project ends and all amounts are in NT$. But central government DOES fund buildings, through city and county governments only, with matching (MoC share capped at 35% to 90% by the local fiscal tier), a NT$3m capital cap inside a NT$10m venue grant, work finished by 10 December of the same year, and "no request for extra subsidy" when costs overrun. A private museum can apply directly but is capped at 50% and needs at least 4 years' right to the site and an operating plan with years. So: "rarely pays for a building" is right for NCAF and for private applicants, wrong for cities. |
| 2 | Corporate and family foundations are funded yearly by the parent and must spend a set share | **CHANGE** | They DO hold an endowment, but a small, locked one: NT$10m at both the Taishin and Fubon arts foundations, NT$50m at Shin Kong Security's, and NT$30m is the national minimum for new culture foundations since 2019. The endowment cannot be spent without the authority's permission. The real money is the parent's yearly gift: Taishin Bank gave its foundation NT$30m in 2025, three times its endowment. The "set share" is real: at least 60% of each year's income must be spent (tax-exemption condition), with a 4-year use plan for any larger surplus. New, and the biggest limit: one grantee may get at most 10% of a foundation's spending that year, unless named in the charter, paid from a donor-designated fund, at or below NT$1m (national culture foundations), or approved. Budgets are filed every January. So a foundation can state multi-year intent, but each year's cheque depends on that year's parent gift and filed budget. |
| 3 | A Taiwanese co-sponsor reads a founder's gift in NT$ and expects it in writing | **CHANGE** | NT$ is the default unit: Taiwanese texts write "13億" or "480萬元" with no currency at all, and NCAF states "all amounts in NT$". But a foreign donor's gift in US dollars was accepted and published as such: Cloud Gate's own page names "a gift of US$ 5 million from the Alphawood Foundation Chicago". "In writing" holds, but the writing that mattered was a public contract, not a pledge letter: Cloud Gate's 2009 BOT contract (self-funded, 40 years plus 10, free hand-back) and Chimei's building gift to Tainan followed by a 50-year ROT with yearly rent. ## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Taiwan's public arts money is yearly, budget-bound and often paid after the work (rows 1-8, 12)
- **What is it?** The MoC's rooting grants approve multi-year work year by year, cut to the legislature's budget, and
  exclude capital and basic running costs. NCAF pays after the project ends, within that year's budget. Capital money
  for venues exists, but only through city and county governments, with matching, a NT$3m capital cap inside a NT$10m
  grant, completion by 10 December, and overruns on the venue.
- **Did it work?** As a control system, yes: the rules are clear and enforced (double funding barred, other funders
  listed, assets kept in use). As a source of steady money, no: NCAF itself ran deficits and lives partly on market
  returns (row 12; Q9 row 34).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her residency would apply for programme money from exactly these bodies, and the
  case asks her to plan for co-sponsors who may fall short (IPS l.15).
- **How is it not?** A private residency cannot use the city-only venue grant; the museum route caps a private
  applicant at 50%.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d: ask co-sponsors for programme money by year, never for a matching capital promise of the
  same length as Laura's. 3e: the assumption below. No number beyond NUMBERS 8j's NT$ figures.

### Card 2. A Taiwanese corporate foundation is a small locked core plus the parent's yearly cheque (rows 13-22)
- **What is it?** Registered endowments of NT$10m (Taishin, Fubon) to NT$50m (Shin Kong Security), locked by the
  Foundations Act and the charter. The spending money is the parent's gift that year: Taishin Bank gave NT$30m in 2025.
  At least 60% of each year's income must be spent; one grantee may get at most 10% of a year's spending unless an
  exception applies; budgets are filed each January.
- **Did it work?** For steady mid-size programmes, yes (Taishin's arts award has run for years). For large one-off
  gifts, the 10% rule and the yearly budget make it slow and conditional.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** These are the likeliest private co-sponsors in Taiwan (Q4b row 30, now upgraded to
  primary sources).
- **How is it not?** Laura's money is hers and already owned; theirs is a board's yearly decision about a company's
  money.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d: a co-sponsor can state intent for several years (fixed sums over fixed years, as NCAF's
  corporate partners did: NT$800,000 a year for 3 years, Q22 row 16) but cannot promise the later cheques. 3e: list
  co-sponsors' filed January budgets as a monitoring item.

### Card 3. In Taiwan the written promise around a building gift was a public contract (rows 25-27)
- **What is it?** Cloud Gate's 2009 BOT contract: self-funded build and run, 40 years plus 10, free hand-back. Chimei:
  building handed to Tainan in 2012, then a 50-year ROT with yearly rent.
- **Did it work?** Both buildings opened (2015) and run today, but both founders then carried large yearly running
  costs (Cloud Gate about NT$15m a year before staff; Chimei about NT$300m with tickets covering half).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A residency facility in Taiwan will sit on a site someone owns, on a term someone
  sets, with someone running it.
- **How is it not?** Laura gives a contribution, not a whole building; the case leaves the facility's cost and Taiwan
  legal questions out of scope.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d: one clause naming that the residency's own owner, site and operating plan sit with the
  residency (as Q22 item 11 already says for governance), so the co-sponsor sees Laura's money as capital. No legal
  detail.

### Card 4. NT$ is the funder's unit, but a US$ gift is normal if the NT$ figure is shown and dated (rows 4, 25, 28, 32)
- **What is it?** Taiwanese texts omit the currency for NT$ ("13億", "480萬元"); NCAF states every amount is in NT$.
  Cloud Gate published a foreign gift in US$.

## (c) Verdicts

### Claim 1: CHANGE (mostly keep)
- **Programmes and operations year by year: KEEP.** MoC rooting grants: 逐年核定, cut to the legislature's budget,
  no capital or basic running costs (rows 1-2). NCAF: paid after the project, within the year's budget; its longest
  space-related grant is three one-year platform grants with yearly review, excluding equipment, rent and utilities
  (rows 3, 5). The MoC residency operating subsidy (Q22 row 11, cap NT$3m) is also annual.
- **"Rarely pays for a building": CHANGE.** True for NCAF and for private applicants. Not true for cities: central
  capital money reaches venues through local governments (rows 7-8). The plain version: Taiwan's national government
  pays for buildings through cities, not through private founders, and only with matching, an operating plan, a site
  right of at least 4 years and the owner carrying overruns.
- **Conditions: KEEP, with sizes.** Matching (MoC share 35% to 90% by fiscal tier; private applicants at most 50%), an
  operating plan with years, at least 4 years' site right, at least 200 open days, other funders listed, no double
  public money, completion within the year, and overruns not topped up (rows 6-8; Q22 rows 11-14). Five years of
  operating reports after a culture-circle building are required by the special budget (LY evaluation of 31 Oct 2025,
  search excerpt only, UNVERIFIED; not in the table).
- **Operating models: add.** Taiwan's public spaces run on fixed-term commissions (Pier-2: 3 years, then 2, then taken
  back by the city; Taipei: a city foundation on commission). They fail when subsidy stops (row 9).

### Claim 2: CHANGE
- **"Funded year by year by the parent": KEEP as the main channel.** Taishin Bank's NT$30m in 2025 is three times the
  foundation's NT$10m endowment (rows 19-20).
- **But they do hold endowments: CHANGE.** Small and locked: NT$10m (Taishin, Fubon), NT$50m (Shin Kong Security);
  NT$30m is the minimum for new national culture foundations; drawing the endowment needs permission (rows 13, 15,
  19-22). This answers Q4b's open question.
- **"Must spend a set share": KEEP with the number.** At least 60% of each year's fund income and receipts (tax
  standard, since 2013; 70% before), unless the surplus is NT$500,000 or less or an approved 4-year use plan exists
  (row 17). This is a tax-exemption condition, not a Foundations Act rule.
- **Add the rule that matters most for the ask: the 10% single-grantee cap** (Art. 21(2)), with its four exceptions,
  including the NT$1m threshold for national culture foundations (rows 14-15).
- **Can they commit years ahead?** Only as board intent: plans and budgets are filed each January (row 16), the cheque
  depends on the parent's gift that year (row 20), and NCAF's own records show pledges slipping a year (Q22 row 16).
  Fixed sums over fixed years (NT$800,000 a year for 3 years) are the documented form (Q22 row 16).
- **Corporate patrons cut in downturns: new support for CANON B4.** Chimei dropped a planned second museum after 2008;
  Chang Foundation's museum lost its space and money in 2004 with its parent (rows 23-24). One firm each; enough for a
  dated example, not a rate.

### Claim 3: CHANGE
- **NT$: KEEP as the default.** Taiwanese sources state gifts in NT$, often with no symbol at all (rows 25, 28); NCAF
  says every amount is in NT$ (row 4).
- **But not required: CHANGE.** A foreign donor's US$ gift was taken and published in US$ (row 28). So a US$ range is
  acceptable if the reader can see the NT$ size at a dated rate.
- **"In writing": KEEP, but name the right writing.** The documents found were public contracts with the city or
  county (BOT 40+10 years; ROT 50 years with rent) and published donor lists, not founder pledge letters (rows 25-27).
  No founder pledge text was found (as Q11b also found).
- **No range-with-confidence precedent found** (row 31). Gift sizes in public retellings drift (Chimei NT$1.3bn to
  NT$3bn; Cloud Gate 4,115 or 4,155 gifts), which argues for stating the rule, as NUMBERS 4 already does.
