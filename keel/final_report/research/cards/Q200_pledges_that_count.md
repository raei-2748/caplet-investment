- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** telling apart a giver who can pay from a giver who will pay, when the money is promised now and paid later. Gift offices solved it first with two separate tools: a signed document for "will" (CASE's counting rule) and a doubtful-pledge allowance for "can" (accounting); building committees, who could not live with the allowance's guess, added a third: cash collected before a contract is awarded. Taiwan's Civil Code (1929, amended 1999) answers the same question from the giver's side: a promise to give binds only when the right has moved, and even then not if the giver's own means collapse.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. A gift office checks "will" with a signature and only guesses "can", and the pledges that failed in 2008-10 failed on "can".**
(a) A college counts a pledge once it holds a signed document giving at least the amount, the purpose and the payment period (CASE, as quoted by Taylor, 2021), and then, because it cannot see the giver's future means, it books an allowance for the share that will not be paid; Lehman Brothers' $10 million pledge to Spelman College, announced in 2007, turned into $3 million when the bank collapsed in 2008, and the Chronicle of Philanthropy found that colleges "very rarely, if ever, take legal action" over unpaid pledges and that "No national statistics are kept" on them.
(b) So the judges are right that ownership proves "can" and not "will", and Laura must sign something; but the plan has already done the harder half, because her figure is paid by a Treasury fund held for her that pays out in December 2032, so her "can" is a fact a co-sponsor can check, not a guess an allowance has to price; the signed letter makes her figure countable, and the Treasuries make it payable.
(c) The parallel breaks on "will": nothing in the plan stops Laura selling the fund after the meeting, just as nothing stopped a donor in 2008 from changing his mind, so a signature still matters; what the break teaches is the order of the two proofs, since in every failed pledge read here the promise came first and the money was expected later, while in Laura's case the money exists before the promise.
- **Decision:** the written form of Laura's 2031 figure (IPS l.15, "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"); what the report claims for ownership.
- **Report part:** 3d (one sentence: owned is why it will be paid, signed is why it is counted); replaces SD-6's title "Her word takes the Treasury's credit".
- **Source:** Taylor (2021), p. 2, quoting the CASE Global Reporting Standards (1st ed., 2021); Chronicle of Philanthropy (2010, 8 Oct); store H7-070.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha** for a finance judge, who knows credit and counterparty risk but rarely sees that a charity's two tools for a pledge measure two different risks, and that the one law and practice cannot fix (a giver's means turning) is the one Laura's plan removes first.

**2. The institutions that live on pledges do not build on them: they collect first and build second, which is Laura's rule seen from the other side of the table.**
(a) The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill "must collect 100% of the budgeted amount in cash before the construction project is awarded" (85% in cash by exception); DePauw asks for "firm, written funding commitments for 100% of the estimated total cost" and 75% received; Villanova asks for 75% in documented pledges and 50% in cash; and Middle Tennessee State, after it was left owing about $1 million on 54 Steinway pianos bought against a ten-year pledge (card Q90 row 19), now "recommends having 50 percent of a gift before moving ahead with purchases or construction".
(b) Spelman came through Lehman's collapse with no debt because of the same rule ("We don't spend money unless we have it"), and Laura's plan is that rule applied by the giver instead of the builder: she owns the money before she states the figure (IPS l.15), and a residency that follows Spelman's rule can plan on her figure from 2031 and spend it from January 2033.
(c) The parallel breaks on timing, and the break is useful: under any of these rules her figure stays a pledge, not cash, until the late-2032 fund pays out, so a residency that keeps them awards no building contract on her share before January 2033, which is exactly when the case says she contributes (reserve first, then the facility, case l.94 and l.101); "first money" is therefore true only as first committed, never as first cash.
- **Decision:** the timing of the facility contribution (case l.101-103: after the operating reserve, in 2033) and the claim "first money in a field of last money" (SD-3).
- **Report part:** 3d (the parallel plain reader 12 asks for, in place of the comfort letter); 3b (the residency's own rule is what meets a lost co-sponsor, not her flexibility).
- **Source:** UNC Capital Gift Funding Policy (2020, rev. 2023); DePauw University (2010, rev. 2017); Villanova University (n.d.); Chronicle of Philanthropy (2010).
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha** for most finance judges (the rules are buried in university policy manuals); pedigree for a campaign director, who would recognise the rule and approve of Laura following it from the giver's side.

**3. A capital gift agreement has three parts the gift office needs and three clauses the building needs, and Laura's letter needs all six.**
(a) The gift office needs a signed amount, a purpose and a payment date (CASE; CSUSB: "date, amount pledged, usage/purpose detail, and payment schedule"); the building's templates then add a reliance clause ("the Charity is relying on this commitment to either commence or continue construction"), a changed-purpose clause (Texas A&M: if the purpose becomes "impracticable" or "unnecessary or unwise", the receiver "may designate another purpose", with the donor's intent "taken into regard"), and a default clause that removes the donor's name if payments stop (UT System, UAB).
(b) Laura's letter can carry every part with no strain: one figure (the report's call is $115,000 on today's figures, ASK_HUMANS 19 Nov), the residency's facility, January 2033, the source (a US Treasury fund held for her that pays out in December 2032, $95,000 of it held since January 2028 and the rest since January 2031), no condition on her side (card Q147), and a clause saying that if the building is late the money waits in Treasuries until it is drawn, and if the facility does not go ahead unspent money comes back (card Q147: a return clause keeps the figure countable; card Q77).
(c) The templates break in one place: they are written by an institution to protect itself from a donor, and Laura is the founder of the body that will receive her gift, so until the residency has its own board she would in effect be writing to herself; the break tells the report to address the letter to the residency (its legal form is out of scope, case l.169) and to name the purpose, because a purpose the founder can quietly change is what a co-sponsor fears most.

**4. Taiwan's gift law gives a giver three ways out, and only one of them is closed by a notary.**
(a) Under the Civil Code a gift is a contract the other side accepts (art. 406); until the right has moved, the giver may revoke it, unless it was notarized or made "for the discharge of a moral obligation" (art. 408); after it has been agreed, a giver whose "economic conditions have changed" so that paying "would seriously affect his means of livelihood" may refuse to pay, and the article names no notarized exception (art. 418); after transfer, a gift can be taken back only for a personal wrong by the receiver, such as a deliberate crime against the giver's family, within a year (arts. 416-417).
(b) So the judges' reading is right as law: paper, even notarized paper, does not make a Taiwanese gift promise final, and only transfer comes close; but art. 418's usual trigger is a giver who promised out of income and then lost it, and Laura's figure is paid from a fund bought before she speaks, while the case puts her living costs "outside the portfolio" (case l.61-64), so the plan removes the situation the article was written for, though not the right itself.
(c) The parallel breaks on practice: the Taiwanese gift forms read for cards Q83 and Q113 are signed forms and agreements (amount, use, naming, payment), with no seal field and no notary, and one school issues a receipt only once cash arrives; so the attack's suggestion that "a notarized pledge is the form a Taiwanese funder relies on" is not what the forms show, and the report, which gives no legal advice (case l.169), should name a signed letter and say nothing about notaries.
- **Decision:** whether 3d mentions notarization or Taiwanese law (it should not); whether the plan needs a step beyond owning and signing (it does not: the next step is payment, January 2033).
- **Report part:** store only; at most one clause in 3e ("Taiwan's law and the residency's legal form are outside the case").
- **Source:** Ministry of Justice (n.d.-a, n.d.-b), Civil Code arts. 406, 408, 412, 416-419, Chinese and English; cards Q83 rows 10-12 and 26, Q113-031, Q55-037.
- **Pedigree or aha:** store only. True and niche, but it is law, which the case puts out of scope; its job is to stop the report from either ignoring art. 418 or giving advice about it.

**5. The case asks Laura's figure to do three jobs, and each needs a different proof.**
(a) The case says a meaningful personal contribution "may signal that the residency is financially viable, demonstrate Laura's commitment to the project, and make potential co-sponsors more willing to contribute" (case l.110-112).
(b) Read with a gift officer's eyes, those are three separate proofs: viability is shown by money that exists (her figure owned in a Treasury fund), commitment by a signed document (CASE: oral pledges "should not be reported in campaign totals", Case Western), and willingness in others by being first in line, which the last-money funders read in cards Q11c rows 19 and 21 count only once it is "confirmed in writing" or "committed"; the plan as written supplies the first, the letter supplies the second, and the two together make the third true.
(c) The parallel breaks against the case's next sentence: "Rather than promising one exact amount, she wants to communicate a credible range" (case l.115), so a signed single figure looks like the thing Laura said she did not want; the answer lies in the case's own reason, "In 2031, however, the value of her portfolio in 2033 remains uncertain" (l.112-113), which the plan removes before the meetings, so the report still recommends the range and its confidence (3c), and the letter carries "whatever figure you choose" within it (IPS l.15), the IPS's "one step further" (l.11) applied to the building.
- **Decision:** what Laura states in 2031 and in what form (IPS l.15); the order of 3c (range and confidence) and 3d (her figure in writing).
- **Report part:** 3d's opening (one sentence mapping the case's three jobs to owned, signed and first); 3c's bridge to 3d.
- **Source:** case l.109-117; Case Western Reserve University (n.d.); UMass Amherst (n.d.); Q11c-019, Q11c-021.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 1 with idea 5 (rows Q200-001, -008, -025, -030), for 3d.** "Owned is why it will be paid; signed is why it is counted": the case asks her figure to show viability, commitment and others' willingness, and a gift officer reads those as three proofs (money that exists, a signed document, first in line). Lehman's signed pledge to Spelman paid $3 million of $10 million, so paper alone never was the protection; the plan supplies the proof that pledges usually lack.
2. **Idea 2 (rows Q200-006, -007, -009 to -012, -031), for 3d in place of the comfort letter.** The institutions that live on pledges do not build on them: UNC collects 100% in cash before awarding a contract, and Middle Tennessee changed its rule after its pianos. Laura's "buy first, state second" is the same rule from the giver's side, and the break (her figure is cash only in January 2033) matches the case's own order.
3. **Idea 3 (rows Q200-005, -013 to -018), for 3d's lead-in.** The six parts of a capital gift agreement give the report a concrete written form: one figure, the facility, January 2033, the Treasury fund that pays it, no condition on her side, and a clause for a late or cancelled building. This turns "the written form funders count" (SKELETON 3d) into something a co-sponsor could sign against.

**Verdicts on the attacks:**
- **Campaign director 2: stands, with one part changed.** Owning proves "can", not "will", and CASE counts only "a signed document detailing at least the amount, purpose and payment period" (row 1, verified as quoted by Taylor). Arts. 408 and 418 read as the judge says (rows 20-21). Changed: notarization is not the Taiwanese practice found (row 24), and Taiwanese law stays out of the report. The report should drop SD-6's "takes the Treasury's credit", name a signed pledge letter with the six parts of row 18, and say what the Treasuries prove: the money exists today and pays out in December 2032.
- **Campaign director 11: stands; the claim changes.** Last-money funders and building rules count documents and cash, not intentions (rows 9-12; Q11c-019, -021). Say "first committed, in writing, in 2031, and paid in January 2033", never "first money" alone, and never "first cash".
- **Campaign director 15: stands.** CASE's split is about what the donor still controls, not price; both ends are owned at the meetings. Drop Q11b-032's mapping and use CASE only for the documentation rule (row 28).
- **Campaign director 22: stands.** Klein goes in 3b only, as the reason the flexibility is never announced. IPS l.15's "promised to no one" forbids offering it to co-sponsors (row 29).
- **Plain reader 12: stands.** Say it plainly: owned means the money exists and will pay on its date; a signed letter means it will be given and can be counted. The comfort letter leaves 3d, and the replacement is idea 2's parallel, where holding money first changed what institutions did (row 31).
