- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** when an average over many possible outcomes describes what happens to one person over time. Probability met it first (Huygens 1657, the expectation; Daniel Bernoulli 1738, utility, and the first worked example of spreading cargo across ships); information theory answered it for a gambler who reinvests (Kelly 1956); statistical physics named it (ergodic theory; Peters 2019, Peters and Gell-Mann 2016); decision theory replied that its own tools already cover one-time choices (Doctor, Wakker and Wang 2020); experiments then tested whether people change their caution when their wealth multiplies (Meder et al. 2021; Vanhoyweghen et al. 2022; Skjold et al. 2024).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Kelly's weekly dollar: the average is the right guide only when every bet is paid with fresh money from outside, and Laura's case takes that money away.**
(a) In 1956 Kelly showed that a gambler who keeps re-staking his whole pot should not chase the highest average, but in his last paragraph he added the exception: if the gambler's wife "allowed him to bet one dollar each week but not to reinvest his winnings", then "He should then maximize his expectation", because a new dollar arrives every week whatever happened to the last one; Peters and Gell-Mann (2016) give the physicist's version, that an average speaks for one person only when gains and losses simply add up like this, or when he belongs to "a large resource-sharing group mimicking a statistical ensemble".
(b) Laura has no weekly dollar, because the case says that apart from her two deposits "she will neither add to nor withdraw from the portfolio before 2033" and bars "co-sponsors, grants, program fees, or other outside funding" from the payments; so all ten payments come out of one pot, whatever six years of returns have made of it, and that is how the first plan could beat the Treasury plan on average and still leave a payment short in 1 simulated future in 31, with every shortfall fixed on one day, 1 January 2033, and never made up, since no money arrives after January 2028 (NUMBERS 8s).
(c) The parallel breaks at her career, which is a weekly dollar of a kind (advances and fees keep coming), but the case keeps it away from the payments, and IPS l.9 says it shrinks in the same recession that shrinks shares, so even if it were allowed it would be smallest in exactly the futures where the pot fell short; the lesson is that a clause in the case, not Laura's temperament, is what makes the average a poor guide for her.
- **Decision:** IPS l.11 (no payment depends on a share price when it falls due); the ten Treasuries bought from the first deposit in January 2027 and completed from the 2028 deposit's first dollars (IPS l.13).
- **Report part:** 3a (one clause on why an average across simulated futures was not enough); 3f (what the check that priced l.11's step was measuring).
- **Source:** Kelly (1956), p. 926; Peters and Gell-Mann (2016), §III.A and the box "Huygens' weakness"; case l.63-65, l.91-92; NUMBERS 8s.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha.** A finance judge knows Kelly's rule for a gambler who reinvests; almost nobody remembers the paragraph in which Kelly says a gambler refilled from outside every week should chase the average after all, and nobody will have seen that condition switched off by one sentence of a client's case.

**2. Bernoulli's ships: one merchant collects the average only by spreading his cargo over many ships that sink one at a time, and shares sink together.**
(a) In the same 1738 paper that invented utility, Bernoulli's merchant Sempronius has 4,000 ducats at home and 8,000 ducats of goods abroad, one ship in ten is lost at sea, and his prospects are worth 6,751 ducats to him if the goods sail on one ship, 7,033 on two, and more with every further split, though they "will never rise in value above 7200 ducats", which is exactly the average (nine-tenths of 8,000; Keel arithmetic reproduces his figures to the ducat); spreading across many independent ships is how one man turns the average into his own outcome, the eighteenth-century form of Peters's "pooling and sharing among many entities".
(b) VT's thousands of companies look like Sempronius's many ships, but in a recession they go down together, and IPS l.9 adds that Laura's advances and her co-sponsors' budgets go down in the same storm, so splitting the payment money across thousands of companies would not bring it to its average; the register shows the storm, since in US history since 1871 the first plan's design fails in 25 of 1,758 starting months, and every one of the 25 is a purchase made between October 1927 and October 1929 (NUMBERS 8s), one storm met by everyone who sailed in those two years rather than 25 ships lost at random.
(c) So Caplet puts the ten payments on no ship: a Treasury held to its date is, in Bernoulli's own words for Caius's goods, cargo already "in Petersburg", and the break teaches the sharpest correction of the ergodicity slogan, which is that for market risk the average is not taken across many investors (every investor lives through the same market history) but across possible histories, of which the world will have only one, shared by Laura, her publishers and her co-sponsors.
- **Decision:** IPS l.9 and l.11 together: why owning thousands of companies is not enough for promised money; the ten Treasuries.
- **Report part:** 3a; 2.3's career line (one clause); `CONCEIT.md`: "cargo already landed" is a dissimilar parallel for ink to test, never a second conceit and never in tables.
- **Source:** Bernoulli (1738/1954), §15 pp. 29-30, §16 pp. 30-31; Peters (2019), p. 1218; Peters and Gell-Mann (2016), note 27; NUMBERS 8s.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha.** Every judge knows "diversify"; few know that diversification's first worked example sits in Bernoulli's 1738 paper, and fewer that the same example shows exactly where diversification stops: at risks that do not sink one by one.

**3. Peters's two averages are already printed in Laura's numbers, and where the plan forecasts at all it uses the one that describes a single future.**
(a) Peters's coin (heads +50%, tails -40%) makes the average across all players grow 5% a round while the typical player shrinks about 5% a round (Keel arithmetic: the square root of 1.5 times 0.6 is 0.949), and a milder version of the same gap is printed in every forecaster's table: J.P. Morgan's 2027 row for world shares reads 8.94% for the average year and 7.50% for the compound rate, the rate at which money in the middle future actually grows, the 1.44 points between them being the drag that ups and downs put on anything that compounds (Peters credits the inequality to Euclid, *Elements* Book V, Proposition 25).
(b) The gap appears in Laura's own register as the price of "none": in the 27 Sep model the first plan left $35,305 more than the Treasury-first plan on average across 200,000 simulated futures, but only about $19,000 more in the middle future, and less in 44 futures in 100 (on today's engine Caplet's plan gives up about $65,000 in the middle future; NUMBERS 8s), because a few futures in which shares soared pull the average up, and Laura will live in one future, most probably not one of those.
(c) Where the plan does forecast, for the top of her range before the move, it takes J.P. Morgan's 7.5% as the middle future's growth ("VT median growth 7.5% a year", NUMBERS section 0), which is the choice Peters would make, but the register's test shows how little the quarrel matters to her: at 9%, about the average year's rate, the top before rounding rises only from $117,142 to $117,972 and the stated top stays $115,000 (NUMBERS 4), because half the shares carry a forecast for three years at most and Treasuries carry everything else.
- **Decision:** the 7.5% in Table 2's note and 3e; aha row 10's price ("1 in 31 against none", never $19,000 alone); NUMBERS 4's own sentence, "the 7.5% is not doing the work; the Treasuries are".
- **Report part:** 3f (the price of l.11's step said two ways, on average and in the middle future); 3e (what the 7.5% is).
- **Source:** Peters (2019), pp. 1216 and 1220; CANON B15 (the J.P. Morgan row read at source 9 Nov); NUMBERS section 0, 4, 8s; Keel arithmetic.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for the gap itself (volatility drag is known to every quantitative judge); **aha** for the reader who sees that the price of Laura's certainty is almost twice as large on average as in the middle future, which is the ergodicity gap in her own numbers.

**4. Both sides of the ergodicity quarrel fail at ten dated promises, which is why the plan scores the payments by neither and owns them instead.**
(a) The critics win the central point: Doctor, Wakker and Wang reply that expected utility needs no parallel universes, because "Decisions can be single-person and one-time. Then averaging over persons or time plays no role", and Peters and Gell-Mann concede the mirror image, that their time averages give "little insight" into a bet made once (their example is a coin toss after which the player drops dead), since "Our methods are not developed for such a-typical cases".
(b) That corrects the store (card Q168 idea 2(b)): 1 in 31 was not a statement about copies of Laura but, if the model was right, her own chance; and the critics' best objection to Peters then becomes the plan's reason, because they write that "we do not just maximize our entire wealth at the end of our life, but intermediate consumption patterns virtually always play a role" and that "For nonquantitative outcomes, growth rates cannot even be defined", while Laura owes ten fixed sums on ten Januarys (money spent on dates, not wealth at the end) and risks, if one goes unpaid, her credibility with co-sponsors (case l.114-115), which no growth rate can measure.
(c) So the plan steps outside the quarrel rather than winning it: a growth rate cannot see a date, and an expected utility can price a broken promise only by guessing how bad it would feel, so Caplet buys, for each payment, a Treasury that pays it in every future, and from January 2028 the number of misses is none in all 200,000 simulated futures and in every starting month from 1962 to 2010 (NUMBERS 2, 8s), where the average future, the middle future and Laura's own future are the same number; the averages are kept for money nobody has been promised.
- **Decision:** IPS l.11's "one step further", a structural definition of certainty, not a probability (CANON A4); IPS l.17 (a fall in the market is not a reason to change).
- **Report part:** 3a; 3f (why 1 in 31 against none is a real choice and not a modelling trick).
- **Source:** Doctor, Wakker and Wang (2020), Supplementary Information §1 and §3; Peters and Gell-Mann (2016), §III; case l.114-115; NUMBERS 2, 8s.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha.** A judge who has followed the 2020 exchange expects a student team to side with Peters, the exciting physicist; the report instead takes the economists' strongest objection to Peters, dated spending that no single growth rate captures, and shows it is the reason for the ladder.

**5. Circumstance, not temperament: Bernoulli in 1738, Peters in 2019 and Caplet's IPS make the same turn.**
(a) Bernoulli began the theory of risk by separating price from worth, "The price of the item is dependent only on the thing itself and is equal for everyone", while its utility "is dependent on the particular circumstances of the person making the estimate", and 281 years later Peters made the same move in one line, "Perhaps people aren't so different, but their circumstances are"; Copenhagen's experiment (which Peters reported in 2019, published in 2021) and that group's pre-registered replication (2024) found the same people growing more cautious when their wealth multiplied than when it merely added up, and a study by another group (2022) found that time pressure changed people's choices only when wealth multiplied.
(b) IPS l.9 makes this turn in its first two sentences: it grants Laura "your taste for thoughtful risk and 6 years to wait", admits shares were "naturally" the plan, and then changes the plan not because her taste changed but because the team "considered the actual nature of a writer's income", a circumstance that makes VT, which costs Laura exactly what it costs anyone, worth less to her as payment money than to an investor whose income does not fall when shares do.
(c) The parallel breaks on which circumstance counts, since Bernoulli's is how much a person already owns and Peters's is whether wealth adds or multiplies, while Laura's is that her income is tied to the market, a circumstance neither of them names (Savage's ticket-holder, card Q182, comes closest); and the critics' reply that temperament is real too (the 2024 replication found differences between people that persisted in both settings) is answered by the plan itself, which keeps her taste for risk alive in the half of the shares promised to no one (IPS l.15).
- **Decision:** IPS l.9's turn, the reason the first plan was dropped (CANON A3: supported here, never replaced); IPS l.15 (her flexibility, the place her temperament keeps).
- **Report part:** 3f (the team's thinking changing: from how brave Laura is to what her income does); 2.3 (one clause beside the career line).
- **Source:** Bernoulli (1738/1954), §3 p. 24; Peters (2019), p. 1218; Vanhoyweghen et al. (2022); Skjold et al. (2024); Doctor, Wakker and Wang (2020), Supplementary Information appendix.
- **Pedigree or aha:** **aha** for 3f. "We changed our minds when we stopped asking how brave Laura is and started asking what her income does" is a reflection judges will not have read before, and it has two and a half centuries of pedigree behind it.
