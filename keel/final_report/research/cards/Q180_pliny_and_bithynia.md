## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Trajan's rule for Prusa's bath: the running money comes before the building

- **The parallel.** Pliny proposed a new public bath for Prusa. Trajan agreed only if it needed no new tax and left the city no less for its "necessary expenses" in the years ahead (10.24; Firth: "their contributions for necessary expenses shall not show any falling off").
- **What it shows.** This is the order of Laura's plan in one Roman sentence. The payments she has promised for every year come first, and her building figure comes only from what is left once they are bought.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Trajan's condition was a promise that Prusa had to keep in its future budgets.
  - The bath's own money was partly money owed to the city by private persons, which Pliny had only "begun to claim" (10.23).
  - Laura's plan meets Trajan's condition before any building figure exists, because the ten payments are already held in Treasuries. So it does not promise to protect the running money: it has already bought it.
- **Decision it serves:** the order in which the plan buys things (IPS l.11-15). The ten payments come first, the extra Treasuries next, and only then the building.
- **Report part:** 3.0 or 3a, one clause, or the team's lead-in sentence before 3d.
- **Source:** Pliny, Letters 10.23-24 (Latin; Firth; Loeb).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. Classicists know the letters, but no finance judge will have seen an emperor's one-line rule that puts running costs ahead of a new building. The companion below (Pliny's own will) makes it a pair.

### Idea 2. Nicaea read whole: the city went first by spending, and the money sank with the theatre

- **The parallel.**
  - Nicaea spent more than ten million sesterces on its theatre "so at least I am told, for the accounts have not been made out" (Firth).
  - The theatre then sank, either because the ground was soft or because the stone was weak. The parts that private donors had promised were all put off: a basilica around it and a colonnade above the seats.
  - Pliny could only ask whether to finish it, change it or pull it down. He warned against spending more "in our anxiety to save the money already disbursed" (10.39).
- **What it shows.** The difference that matters is how each goes first.
  - Nicaea went first by spending.
  - Laura goes first by owning. Her figure is fixed in 2031, but her money stays in Treasuries until the building can take it in 2033. If the building is late, her money waits in short-term Treasuries (SD-7).
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Nicaea's donors promised named parts, which could stand only on the theatre.
  - Co-sponsors pledge toward one total for the building. So their pledges wait on the total being promised by December 2032, not on Laura's money being spent first. This is one more reason why 3d says "one total".
  - Her money still cannot firm up soft ground (Q167 row 25).
- **Decision it serves:** Laura's figure goes first and is held, not spent, until the building takes it (3d, SD-3, SD-7). It also gives 3e one limit.
- **Report part:** 3d, as SD-3's evidence clause, if the comfort letter leaves room. Otherwise the store.
- **Source:** Pliny, Letters 10.39-10.40 (Latin; Firth; Loeb).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. The sentence about saving money already spent is the oldest statement of the sunk-cost error found in the store, and it is made about a building that several parties paid for.

### Idea 3. The book's pattern: three failures, and the order Pliny reached later

- **The parallel.**
  - Read in order, the letters show three failures again and again:
    - Cities spent before they checked: Nicaea's theatre, Nicomedia's two abandoned aqueducts, and a bath at Claudiopolis dug into a hollow.
    - Cities counted money before it was collected: the money owed to Prusa by private persons, and the entry fees at Claudiopolis still to be "demanded".
    - Cities took promises for parts of a building before its core stood: Nicaea.
  - In a later letter Pliny has the soft ground at Sinope tested "at small expense" ("modico impendio", Keel's translation) before he asks to build an aqueduct there. Trajan agrees only "provided that it can bear the expense alone" (10.90-91; Firth).
- **What it shows.** Laura's plan takes the money half of Pliny's later order: no figure is named until the Treasuries that pay it are held for her.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - The other two failures belong to the building. Testing the ground and keeping the accounts are the residency's work, so 3e should say that owned money removes only the money reason a building stalls.
  - Pliny could make those who owed a city pay. Laura can make no co-sponsor pay, which is why their pledges never enter her figure.
- **Decision it serves:** no figure is stated before the move (NUMBERS 4). The pattern also shows which risks are hers and which are the building's (3e).
- **Report part:** 3e, one clause, and 3c, at most a half-clause. Mostly the store.
- **Source:** Pliny, Letters 10.17A-18, 10.23, 10.37-41, 10.49, 10.90-91 and 10.98-99 (Latin).
- **Pedigree or aha:** a medium aha. The order "test, gather the money, then build" is plain sense, but seeing a governor reach it letter by letter is new.

### Idea 4. "For the sake of which they were promised": how far "unconditional" reaches in Laura's words

- **The parallel.** Trajan made Nicaea's donors build their parts only once the theatre was finished, because they had promised them "propter quod" (for the sake of) the theatre. In other words, he read a building's purpose as an unwritten condition on every promise made toward it (10.40).
- **What it shows.**
  - Laura's plan writes out the co-sponsors' condition: if the pledges are not all made by December 2032, none of them has to pay.
  - It puts no condition of markets, or of anybody else's money, on her own figure. Round 9's spoken sentence says so for the co-sponsors: "my own $115,000 comes first either way".
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Her figure is also given for the sake of a building, so Trajan's reading applies to her too.
  - So 3d should keep "unconditional" to markets and to the co-sponsors. It must never use words that a judge could hear as "paid even if nothing is built". SKELETON leaves that case open (3d's row: the building late past January 2033, or never built).
- **Decision it serves:** the words of 3d's ask ("her figure first and unconditional") and of Laura's spoken sentence.
- **Report part:** 3d. This is a check on the wording, not a new sentence.
- **Source:** Pliny, Letters 10.40 (Latin; Loeb).
- **Pedigree or aha:** not an aha. It is a precision fix for a gap that a careful judge could otherwise catch.

### Best companion: Pliny's own gifts, the same rule from the giver's side

- **The fact.** An inscription records Pliny's own gifts to his home town, Comum (CIL V 5262, in Radice's translation).
  - By his will he left public baths, "an additional 300,000 sesterces for furnishing them", and the "interest on 200,000 for the upkeep".
  - He also gave a library, with "100,000 for the upkeep of the library".
- **Why it is the best companion.**
  - As governor, Pliny saw Bithynia's buildings stall on money that had been spent before the work was checked, or counted before it was collected.
  - As a giver, he attached the running money to his own buildings.
  - So one man shows Trajan's order from both sides. Laura's plan does the same for the residency's running costs, and it shares only the building with co-sponsors.
- **Where it breaks.** Pliny's upkeep money was the interest on a capital sum, which makes it an endowment. Laura's ten payments spend their principal and end in 2042, so her reserve is never called an endowment. Pliny also paid for his whole building alone.
- **Order of events:** the dates of the gifts were not checked in this run (Gaps). So the claim is only that one man stood on both sides of the rule, never that Bithynia taught it to him.

## Best three for the report

1. **Trajan's rule for Prusa's bath, with Pliny's own gifts** (rows 1, 24 and 25; idea 1 and the companion).
   - A new building is allowed only if no less is left for necessary spending in the years ahead. The governor who passed on that rule gave his own library and baths their upkeep money.
   - Why a judge says "aha": it puts the plan's order (running money first, building second) into one emperor's sentence, and then shows a giver keeping the same order. Place: 3.0, or the lead-in to 3d, one clause.
2. **Nicaea read whole: spent first, not held first** (rows 7, 8 and 26; idea 2).
   - The city's money went first by being spent, with its accounts never examined, and Pliny warned against spending more "to save the money already disbursed".
   - Why a judge says "aha": it shows why Laura's figure goes first by being owned and held until 2033. It is SD-3's evidence clause with the sunk-cost error attached. Place: 3d if the comfort letter leaves room; otherwise the store.
3. **"For the sake of which they were promised"** (row 13; idea 4).
   - Every promise toward a building carries the building as its condition.
   - Why it matters to a judge: it limits 3d's "unconditional" to markets and co-sponsors, and confirms that round 9's sentence ("my own $115,000 comes first either way") is the right shape. It is a precision fix rather than an aha, and it closes a hole a careful judge could open.
