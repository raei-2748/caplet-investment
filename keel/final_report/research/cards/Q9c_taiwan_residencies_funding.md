**Claims tested**
1. Keep, kill or change: "Taiwan's best-known residencies run on city or national government money, year by year, so a
   private founder who owns ten years of operating money before opening offers something the public model does not:
   certainty beyond one budget year."
2. Keep, kill or change: "Private residencies in Taiwan depend on yearly Ministry subsidies and project grants, and the
   ones that shrank did so when those lapsed."
3. Keep, kill or change: "A co-sponsor in Taiwan (a foundation, a company's foundation, a city) would count Laura's owned
   money as the anchor and fund programmes on top, as Q11d found for the Ministry's rules."

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** A long promise (ten operating years) set beside funders who can vote
money only one budget year at a time (the annual-budget rule of public finance). Public administration solved it with a
renewable operating contract laid over yearly appropriations: Taipei commissions its residency operator three years at
a time and votes the money every year. Nonprofit finance solved it with owned money: a locked endowment (the Taipei
operator's NT$56.5m) or a founder's own funds (Bamboo Curtain Studio). Laura's plan is the second answer applied to
operating money: the ten operating payments are owned in Treasuries before they fall due (IPS l.11; CANON A4, A33).
The failure neither answer covers, and the one that ended most of the residencies found here, is losing the site.

| 1 | Public residencies run on yearly government money, so owned money gives certainty the public model lacks | **CHANGE** | Keep the first half. Treasure Hill's 2026 funding plan is NT$44.072m: 84.6% from Taipei's cultural department, 90.2% from government in all; the 2027 plan is NT$54.79m: 89.5% city, 93.9% government (TCF budgets; Keel arithmetic). The Ministry's village grant is one year, receipts by 30 November, and "may be reduced or stopped" with the legislature's budget. Change the second half. The public model does hold certainty beyond one year, of another kind: a three-year renewable operating contract (Treasure Hill's, renewed every three years since 2013, runs to 31 Dec 2027), a city that owns the site, and an operator with a locked endowment of NT$56,534,255 of which only interest is spent. What it lacks is the money itself in advance. The fair contrast is ten years of operating money owned, against a renewable contract whose money is voted each year. "Something the public model does not" is a slogan and only half true: drop it. |
| 2 | Private residencies depend on yearly Ministry grants and shrank when those lapsed | **KILL** (as worded; a narrower claim replaces it) | The best-documented private residency, Bamboo Curtain Studio (1995 to end 2021), ran mostly on its founder's own resources and contracted projects; public money came in short runs (central repair money 2008-2011, NCAF operating grants 2009-2011, the Ministry in 2012, 2014 and 2015, Taipei in 2013). It closed over a planned road beside its riverbank site, the pandemic, the loss of a whole group of senior staff in 2019-2020, and then its founder's death (30 Aug 2021). No lapsed grant is named by any source. Private villages do take Ministry money (2025: NT$2.0m to a company-run puppetry village, NT$0.9m each to a company-run village and to Grand View's family foundation; gazette excerpt), but it is small. The budget-cut failure is documented at PUBLIC villages: Chiayi's council cut the warehouse rent budget in 2008 and the village shrank into a closed group of six local artists; Pier-2's first operator left in 2003 when central money ended (Q11d row 9). Replacement for the store: private residencies in Taiwan ran on their founders' money with small public grants on top and closed when they lost a site, staff or a founder; the ones that shrank after a budget cut were public. |
| 3 | A Taiwanese co-sponsor counts Laura's owned money as the anchor and funds programmes on top | **CHANGE** | The Ministry's village rule (28 Oct 2025) asks every applicant for an estimate of operating funds, proof of ownership or right of use of the site, and proof of other bodies' resources. It then pays one year of programme and equipment, at most NT$3m (about US$91,600 at 32.768), after receipts, with no second public grant for the same work. It sets no matching ratio for a private applicant: the 50%-90% shares by fiscal tier applied to cities and counties, and since Oct 2025 cities are off the applicant list. So owned money is read as proof the residency can run, a precondition, not an "anchor" that is matched. Foundations add money only inside Art. 21's 10% single-grantee cap (Q11d row 14). Scale: her $50,000 a year is NT$1.64m (Q9 row 35), about one 2025 Ministry village grant (NT$0.9m-2.0m). |

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. Treasure Hill: a three-year contract with yearly money (rows 12-21)**
- What is it? Taipei's flagship residency, a city-owned historic village run by the Taipei Culture Foundation on a
  three-year renewable administrative contract (since 2013, now to 31 Dec 2027). Its 2026 funding plan is NT$44.072m,
  84.6% from the city and 90.2% from government; 2027 is NT$54.79m, 93.9% government. The Ministry adds about a
  twentieth (5.8% of its 2025 public money). Gifts are tiny (NT$0.674m in the 2026 plan).
- Did it work? Yes, for sixteen years: open since 2010, 65 artists in 2025, budget rising. But its two siblings under
  the same yearly city money ended: Taipei Artist Village lost its site (2023), Grass Mountain stopped (2012).
- How is Laura's case like it? A Taiwanese co-sponsor knows this shape: one base funder pays the running costs, others
  add programme money on top. For her residency's operations, Laura is the base funder.
- How is it not? The city's base is a yearly vote backed by taxes and an owned site. Laura's base is money already
  owned in Treasuries from January 2028, fixed at $50,000 a year in US dollars (IPS l.11, l.17; CANON A4, A33). A city's
  base can grow (up NT$11.7m for 2027); hers cannot, and she has no site rights through the plan.
- What Caplet takes: 3d, at most a half-clause hung on "the ten years owned to 2042": that public residencies in Taiwan
  are paid one budget year at a time. No number and no rule names (SKELETON 3d's CUT list). The figures stay in the
  store. No exhibit.

**Card 2. The Ministry's village grant: proof first, one year of programme after (rows 1-11)**
- What is it? The Ministry of Culture's art-village subsidy (directions of 28 Oct 2025): one year, at most NT$3m per
  application, a living allowance of NT$800 per resident per day, paid after receipts, no double public money,
  equipment kept by the village's owner. The applicant must show an estimate of operating funds, its right to the site
  and other bodies' resources. Cities were dropped from the applicant list, and the 2021 allowance for two-year plans is
  gone.
- Did it work? As a yearly top-up, yes: about a dozen villages a year, private and public alike, at NT$0.9m-2.0m each
  in 2025, with NCAF reviewing every round.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her ten operating payments and her owned bottom ($95,000, NUMBERS 4) are the "proof of
  operating funds" the rule asks for before it pays a year of programme.
- How is it not? Nothing in the rule matches or multiplies an owner's money; there is no ratio for a private applicant.
  The "anchor" in claim 3 is Keel's word, not Taiwan's.
- What Caplet takes: store only (3d cuts the Ministry's rules and the match). One guard for the 27 Nov drafting: never
  say a Taiwanese funder "matches" Laura's money. If 3d names what co-sponsors fund, "programme money a year at a time"
  is accurate.

**Card 3. Bamboo Curtain Studio: a founder's money, closed by a road (rows 27-31, 34)**
- What is it? Taiwan's first privately run international residency (1995 to end 2021) on a 2,645 m2 farm complex near
  Danshui, which hosted about 250 creatives by 2013 and was Res Artis's Asian contact. Money: mostly its founder's own
  resources and contracted projects; public grants in short runs (2008-2015).
- Did it work? For 26 years. It ended by choice in 2020-2021 because a planned road would spoil its riverbank site, the
  pandemic struck and a whole group of senior staff had left; its founder died in August 2021 before it closed.
- How is Laura's case like it? A founder-funded residency with public money on top, the order Laura's plan sets.
- How is it not? The founder's money was spent as needed, never set aside for years ahead; the studio changed its legal
  form in 2011 to take sponsorship after losses. Laura's ten operating payments are owned before they fall due.
- What Caplet takes: store only. Its use is negative: it kills claim 2 as worded, so 3d must not say private
  residencies in Taiwan fail when grants stop. The grant-calendar quote (row 29) is the best Taiwanese evidence that
  yearly money costs planning.

**Card 4. Chiayi Railway Art Village: the budget cut that shrank a residency (rows 24-26; Q11d row 9)**
- What is it? A city-run railway-warehouse residency (1999-2020). In 2008 the city council cut the cultural bureau's
  rent budget for the warehouses; six local artists took over the leases and the open village became a closed local
  group. The warehouses were demolished for the elevated railway after July 2020.
- Did it work? It survived the cut in a smaller form for twelve years, then lost its site. Taichung's 20 Warehouse

- How is Laura's case like it? It is the IPS's own risk in Taiwanese form: "co-sponsors cut their budgets" (l.9), and

- What Caplet takes: store only. If 3e needs one real example behind "co-sponsors cut their budgets", this is the

**Card 5. Grand View Art Village: a private founder builds first (rows 32-33)**

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE.**
- What holds: Taiwan's best-known residencies are government budget lines. Treasure Hill 2026: 90.2% government
  (84.6% city); 2027: 93.9% (89.5% city); 2018: about 86% (Q9 row 32). The Ministry's grant is one year, cut to the
  legislature's budget, and paid after receipts. Gifts are under 1% of TCF's money. A Taiwanese observer says the
  public sector is still the main funder (row 33).
- What does not hold: "certainty beyond one budget year" is not absent from the public model. The operator holds a
  three-year renewable contract (renewed every three years since 2013), the city owns the site, and TCF holds a locked
  NT$56.5m endowment. What is missing is money owned in advance: every year's running money is voted that year, and the
  Ministry's multi-year option was removed after 2021.
- Store wording (a note, not prose): owned money for ten operating years against a renewable contract whose money is
  voted each year. Avoid "something the public model does not" and any ranking of public against private.
- Where it lands: the half-clause in 3d (card 1), no number; or store only.

**Claim 2: KILL as worded.**
- Bamboo Curtain, the only private Taiwanese residency with a full public record found, lived mostly on its founder's
  money and project income, took public money in short runs, and closed for site, pandemic, staff and founder reasons.
  No source names a lapsed grant as a cause.
- The pattern the claim describes is real but public: Chiayi (council rent cut, 2008), Pier-2 (central money ended,
  2003; Q11d row 9), Taichung (unsubsidised operator by 2015).
- Private residencies do take Ministry money (NT$0.9m-2.0m in 2025), and Grand View took it in its first full year,
  but no private case shrank when it stopped.
- Replacement for the store: private residencies in Taiwan ran on their founders' money with small public grants on
  top and closed when they lost a site, staff or a founder; the ones that shrank after a budget cut were public.
- Where it lands: store only. Its job is to keep a false claim out of 3d.

**Claim 3: CHANGE.**
- What holds: Taiwanese public funders do fund programmes on top of a base, and they check the base first (estimate
  of operating funds, right to the site, other bodies' resources: point 8). Taipei's own evaluation scores its operator
  on outside money brought on top of the city base (row 21).
- What does not hold: "count as the anchor" implies a match or a multiplier. The village rule sets none for a private
  applicant; the 50%-90% fiscal-tier shares were for cities and counties, which are now off the list; the venue grant
  Q11d found goes only through local governments. Foundations can add money only inside Art. 21's 10% cap on any one
  grantee (Q11d row 14), and they too budget a year at a time (Q11d row 16).
- Store wording (a note): a Taiwanese funder reads Laura's owned money as proof the residency can run, then funds one
  year of programme at a time, at most NT$3m from the Ministry, paid after the work. Nothing in the rules makes her
  money a match.
- Scale (row 35): $50,000 a year = NT$1.64m, about one Ministry village grant; $115,000 = NT$3.77m (NUMBERS 8j).
- Where it lands: store only; the risk note in row 5 guards 3d.
