- **Problem class:** making an argument memorable to an expert reader, who reads it once among many, without asking the reader to do the writer's work. **Fields that solved it first:** the psychology of comprehension (Bartlett's "effort after meaning", 1932; Bransford and Johnson's context studies, 1972-73; Auble and Franks, 1978), the cognitive psychology of insight (Metcalfe and Wiebe 1987 to Kounios and Beeman 2014, Danek's magic-trick studies), curiosity research (Berlyne 1960, Loewenstein 1994), and science education's conceptual-change work on refutation text (Hynd, Guzzetti 1993, Kendeou's KReC framework).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with sizes and three conditions.**
  - Solutions people found with an aha were recalled 14 days later 64.4% of the time against 52.4% without one (Danek et al. 2013: 50 people, 34 magic tricks). Across two later studies the aha-to-recall link was about d = 0.30 a week on, alongside separate effects of being right and of feeling sure (Danek & Wiley 2020).
  - The wider "reached, not told" effect: generating an item beats reading it by d = 0.40 across 86 studies and 17,711 people (Bertsch et al. 2007).
  - **Condition 1: the reader need not solve it.** A solution *shown* after a short failed attempt still produced an aha (on 75% of solvable verbal riddles; Kizilirmak, Thuerich et al. 2016) and better memory 24 hours later (recognised 48% against 39%, d = 0.96). In a second study, seeing the answer after failing gave an aha on 22% of images, and that aha helped memory a week later on its own account; finding the answer oneself helped more (Kizilirmak et al. 2016). Shown solutions to camouflaged pictures were still known a week later 56-57% of the time (Ludmer et al. 2011).
  - **Condition 2: the gap must be short, and the frame must come first.** A puzzling sentence followed several seconds later by its key word was recalled 44-49% of the time, against 21-24% when the key sat inside the sentence (Zaromb et al. 2010). But a key given after a whole passage did nothing: recall 3.60 of 14 ideas, the same as no key, against 8.00 when the key came first (Bransford & Johnson 1973).
  - **Condition 3: it is lab evidence.** Every study used riddles, tricks, pictures or single sentences. No study found tests an argument in a report, or expert readers.
  - **Judge's note:** being shown the answer a sentence after the puzzle beats being told it in the same breath, and readers cannot feel the difference ("subjects were not aware of this benefit", Zaromb et al. 2010).
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Keep "a held belief first". Kill "the reader makes the last step themselves".
  - Insight needs a mental picture that then reorganises. An impasse is not required (Kounios & Beeman 2014), and the reorganising step can be handed to the reader (induced insight, claim 1).
  - What the reader must do is hold the puzzle for a moment, which they do anyway when a stated fact contradicts what they expected. That is Loewenstein's third trigger of curiosity ("violation of expectations"); his first trigger is "the posing of a question", the route Caplet banned. So no question mark is needed, and none is wanted.
  - Curiosity "requires a preexisting knowledge base" and rises with knowledge (Loewenstein 1994). Finance judges are most curious about gaps in finance.
  - Curiosity peaks when people are about 50% sure of an answer (Kang et al. 2009).
  - A belief held with high confidence is corrected more often once shown wrong (the hypercorrection effect: retest accuracy 79% against 70% for low-confidence errors; Metcalfe et al. 2012). After a week, though, correction fell from 86% to 56%, and confident errors came back when the right answer was forgotten (Butler et al. 2011).
  - **Judge's note:** an aha needs no question and no work from the judge: a true fact that breaks a confident expectation, with the key one sentence later.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, sized smaller for adult and expert readers.**
  - Refutation text beat plain text by g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons (3,869 readers). The trim-and-fill correction gives 0.28; for university readers it is 0.33 (Schroeder & Kucera 2022).
  - A preregistered meta-analysis of 76 studies and 294 effects found g = 0.37 for gaining accurate beliefs and -0.38 for losing wrong ones, with no widespread backfire (Danielson et al. 2024).
  - It works only on a belief actually held: "a common misconception must be known to write a refutation text" (Schroeder & Kucera 2022).
  - With knowledgeable professionals, one study's gain faded at a delay (Paynter et al. 2019, as reported by Danielson et al.). Tippett's review found no significant benefit in grades 11 and up across 11 studies (as reported by Schroeder & Kucera).
  - The Debunking Handbook's order: fact first if it is "clear, pithy, and sticky", the myth once, "explain how the myth misleads", then the fact again.
  - **Judge's note:** a refutation works only on a belief the reader holds, so each aha names a belief someone real holds, never a straw man.
- **Claim 4 (added): CHANGE.** Ahas are remembered for standing out, so they need plain text around them.
  - Bizarre sentences are remembered better only in mixed lists. Raising or lowering the surprise had "little impact" (McDaniel et al. 1995). Humour's memory advantage likewise appeared only "within-subjects", mixed with plain sentences (Schmidt 1994).
  - Interesting but irrelevant material lowered learning across 58 studies, and the harm was larger on paper (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).
  - An aha also makes whatever sits beside it feel true, false claims included (d = 0.61; Laukkonen et al. 2020). In Danek et al. (2013), 75 of 329 aha solutions were wrong (Keel arithmetic, 23%).
  - So: about one a page with plain pages between (the register's rule holds), each doing its part's job, and each true. An overclaimed aha in front of expert judges is worse than none: cold test 2 caught row 5b's overclaim, which "fails: overclaims" (2.3).

## Setting the register's rows (notes, never prose; Keel inference from the rows below)

- **The shape of every row (rows 1, 4, 5, 10 below):**
  - Name the belief and who really holds it: a rule, an industry figure, a court, a common reading. Never "you" and never "many think".
  - Say in one sentence why it misleads.
  - Give the true fact with its number, then let the fact come last.
  - The puzzle and its key sit in consecutive sentences, never a paragraph apart (Zaromb 2010; Bransford & Johnson 1973).
  - No question marks. The gap is opened by a stated fact that breaks an expectation (Loewenstein's third trigger).
- **2.0 (the definition beside Table 1).** This is Bransford and Johnson's "context before". Define ink and pencil before the reader meets the table's rows, so the rows read as already sorted. A definition given after the table would act as "context after" and do nothing. The definition is the frame, not an aha.
- **Row 2 (2.3).** The fact is pithy, so lead with it (Debunking Handbook): ten promises each 99 in 100 safe miss one about 1 time in 10, against one issuer carrying all ten. The holder of the belief is the duty to diversify itself (GIW: "staggering maturity dates"). The 1 - 0.99^10 step is one a judge can check in their head (fair play, Van Dine rule 15). State it all the same.
- **Row 1 (3c, Neyman).** The fact is not pithy, so explain why the usual reading misleads before stating the fact. The holder of the belief is the ordinary reading of a confidence level as a chance (Q74). Puzzle sentence: Neyman's "zero or one, and nobody knows which". Key sentence, next: after the move Laura knows which, because both ends are Treasuries she owns. Fact last: the range she states once both ends are owned.
- **Row 7 (3c, the forecast and speaking early).** This row holds two violated expectations; close one gap before opening the next (Loewenstein: a gap needs attention focused on it). Rows 1 and 7 share 3c, so set the range paragraph between them as plain ground (claim 4).
- **Row 5 (3a).** It scored strongest (4.3) because it already has every refutation part: real holders (a pension's "100% funded at market"; the IRS's 1977 trust test), why the belief misleads (5.12% needed on every waiting dollar), and the replacement standard (paid even if cash waiting earns nothing). Use it as the template for the others.
- **Row 4 (3.0).** The principle's one home. "Fact last" here; the fact never comes back as a second aha. Its figures may recur plainly (a table cell, a later number), which guards against Butler et al.'s return of confident errors.
- **Row 3 (2.5).** Keep the setup to one sentence. Curiosity "tends to disappoint when satisfied" (Loewenstein 1994), so a long build-up to a modest key loses the reader.
- **Row 10 (3f, 2.7, the weakest).** Refutation theory predicts this score. The belief corrected is the team's 27 Sep belief, not one the judge holds, so the judge watches a correction instead of making one. Set it on a belief the judge does hold: that a crash is the danger (the readers' own assumption, already in the row's "not a crash"), or that 3.2% sounds like high certainty. Then the team's change of mind and the judge's land together.
- **Rows 6, 8, 9.** Row 6 answers the question rows 1 and 7 raise ("why a range?") and must sit close enough to carry it. Row 8 is a contrast of two facts (60-80% against 100% owned) and needs no refutation frame. Row 9 is a limit: its truth is "no tendency", so it is not set as an aha against a belief. Its honesty is the point, and claim 4's truth rule matters most here.
- **Conceit turns.** A turn sits with or before its aha's number, never as the key sentence.
  - A key that fits the wrong mapping is worse than none: an irrelevant cue lowered recall below no cue (Bransford & Johnson 1973).
  - An aha lends felt truth to whatever is beside it (Laukkonen et al. 2020), so the turn may never be the evidence.
  - Kounios and Beeman count "comprehension of a joke or metaphor" as insight. A turn can be its own small aha only when it is defined first (Q42) and carries the same mapping.
- **Cold tests (PROCESS).**
  - Readers' ratings cannot see the delayed-key benefit (Zaromb et al. 2010), and ratings are not memory (Q60 claim 4). So the "remembered after 40 reports" question is the main score, not aha strength.
  - Add two cheap fields: the reader's confidence in the assumption they name ("how sure, 0-100"), which predicts how hard the correction lands; and a free recall of each row's figure after filler items, which is Danek's own measure.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 4 with row 5 (induced insight), for every aha row and for Caplet's "our job" rule.** A shown answer still produces an aha and better memory: 48% against 39% recognised a day later, d = 0.96. So the report states every answer and asks nothing of the judge. The judge only holds a puzzle for one sentence. This resolves the apparent clash between "let the reader finish" and "never hand the judge our job".
2. **Rows 10 and 11 (puzzle then key, one sentence apart), for the setting of all ten rows and for 2.0.** A delayed key roughly doubles recall (44-49% against 21-24%), but a key after a whole passage does nothing (3.60 against 8.00 of 14 when the frame comes first). Ahas sit at sentence scale inside paragraphs whose frame comes first. 2.0's definition comes before Table 1.
3. **Row 25 with rows 26 and 31 (refutation, g = 0.41, 0.33 for university readers), for rows 1, 2, 5 and 10.** The tested form is: a belief someone really holds, why it misleads, then the fact. The evidence also shows why row 5 scored 4.3 and row 10 scored 2.7.
4. **Row 22 with row 2 (an aha makes even false claims feel true; 23% of aha solutions were wrong), for 3c and 3e and for the conceit.** Expert judges punish a manufactured aha. Every aha stands on its own figure, and the conceit turn is never the evidence.
5. **Row 32 with row 33 (distinctiveness needs plain ground), for the register's density rule.** Ahas are remembered because they stand out, so plain pages lie between them, at most two in a part, and rows 1 and 7 in 3c are separated by the range paragraph. Among 40 reports, the plain report with placed ahas is the "mixed list" that judges remember.

## Gaps

- **No study of ahas in argued prose or with expert readers.** Every insight-memory study used riddles, magic tricks, pictures or single sentences. Real-world aha research (e.g., Skaar & Reber 2020) was seen only as a citation. A study of insight while reading economics or legal argument would close this.
- **Laukkonen et al. (2022), "Irrelevant insights make worldviews ring true"**: abstract not read (the web-fetch budget and the Firecrawl, Parallel and Consensus allowances ran out during the run).
- **Guzzetti et al. (1993)**: the original's effect sizes were not read; the 2024 secondary table gives three figures without saying which strategy is which.
- **Paynter et al. (2019)** (refutation fading in knowledgeable professionals) and **Slamecka & Fevreiski (1983)** were read only as cited.
- **Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001)**, the original hypercorrection paper, was not opened. Two later studies stand in (rows 20-21).
- **Sundararajan & Adesope (2020)**: effect size not read (press release only).
- **Minto's Pyramid Principle**: the book was not read, so whether its "question" is meant to be written or left implicit is open (row 36).
- **Kendeou and O'Brien's KReC framework** (2014) and Kendeou et al.'s finding that refutation plus explanation beats refutation alone were seen only as citations inside the meta-analyses.
- **Brod, Hasselhorn and Bunge (2018)**, on surprise after a prediction boosting learning, was not read; it would test whether a reader's unstated expectation acts as a prediction.
