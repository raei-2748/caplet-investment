# Q3b. Floors in institutions: why "safe" holdings broke at Orange County (1994), in the UK gilt crisis (2022) and at SVB (2023), and what stops Laura's plan breaking the same way

- **Date:** Fri 13 Nov 2026, Sydney morning.
- **Report parts:** 3e (assumptions, limits and what to watch; Table 3), 3a (the commitment and the reserve), 3c (the 2031 move), 3d (a check on the co-sponsor words); most of it is for the store only.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "All three broke for one reason: the safe asset was safe, but the promise it backed could be called before the asset matured (leverage, collateral calls, deposits that could leave)."
  2. "Nothing Laura owes can be demanded before the Treasury that pays it matures, so her plan cannot be made a forced seller." Tested against the 2031 move, a co-sponsor falling short, the funds' term-end mechanics, a payment due on a holiday, a custodian's failure and a debt-limit delay.
  3. "SVB shows 'held to maturity' is safe only if you can hold: the report should say why Laura can (fixed payment dates, no leverage, no outside claims), in one clause." Tested for each of the ten payments and for the floor.
- **Problem class:** forced-seller risk, or a funding mismatch: a holder that is solvent on paper must sell long holdings before they pay, because a claim against it falls due first. **Fields that solved it:** bank supervision (the "intent and ability" test for held-to-maturity securities; Barr's review), pension regulation (the Pensions Regulator's collateral buffer, sized in basis points and in days), public-fund law (California's "safety, liquidity, and yield, in that order") and the bond desk's dedicated, cash-flow-matched portfolio, which is what Laura's ladder is (not immunisation, not a duration hedge).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Each failure needed two things at once: a claim that could be called within days, and holdings worth less than that claim on the day (the FDIC's own pairing for SVB, row 15). The claim's wording is wrong in three places.
  - Not all the holdings were safe. Orange County's were leveraged agency notes: over 40% structured notes, at least 32% inverse floaters (row 1). SVB's were mostly agency mortgage bonds of 10 years or more (row 12). Only the gilts were plain government bonds like Laura's.
  - In the gilt crisis the promise the gilts backed, the pensions, was never called. The borrowing on top of the gilts was called: the schemes "generally benefit from rises in bond yields" (row 20; Q2a row 6).
  - The party that called was a lender at Orange County (repo lenders that would not roll), the bank's own depositors at SVB (a promise payable on request) and margin counterparties in the gilt crisis.
  - Sizes. Orange County: 2.7 times leverage; a $1.69bn loss; five days from the announced loss to bankruptcy. SVB: held-to-maturity losses of $15,160m against equity of $16,004m; about 85% of deposits gone or asked for within two days. Gilt crisis: calls "in excess of £70 billion"; three business days from the first sales to managers who "had no choice but to begin selling".
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** It is true in the sense that matters for the ten payments from January 2028: no outside party can demand any of Laura's Treasuries before it pays. She has no loan, no margin and no depositor, and each payment has a fixed date. Each payment's cash arrives half a month to 10.5 months before its date (NUMBERS 8h). The six tests:
  - **The 2031 move** is a scheduled sale of shares, not a forced one. No figure is stated before the move (NUMBERS 3), and the payments are unchanged with VT at zero (NUMBERS 4, the deletion test).
  - **A co-sponsor falling short** draws on her flexibility (about $21,300, NUMBERS 4), never on the Treasuries. The facility contribution is capped at the stated top (CANON D12).
  - **The funds' term-end:** the fund board can extend a fund's end without a shareholder vote. An extension past a payment brings the swap step (SKELETON Exhibits item 4), made when the fund holds almost nothing but cash. Whether the board can end a fund early is UNVERIFIED and unsized; it is the one route by which someone else could end a holding.
  - **A holiday:** the Treasury pays the next business day, and NUMBERS 8h's figures do not change.
  - **A custodian's failure** brings a delay, not a sale: $589,655 at the end against SIPC's $500,000 (NUMBERS 8k).
  - **A debt-limit delay** makes a payment late, never forces a sale. More than five business days late is a Treasury default (reason 2).
  - Two wording changes:
    - "by anyone else": Laura herself can draw on the Treasuries, because the wall around them is a policy, not a law (row 33, unsized).
    - "from January 2028": before then the ten lean on the 2028 deposit. They are $54,595 short at no interest without the top-up (NUMBERS 2), and a missed deposit leaves 2033 $3,016 short at forwards (NUMBERS 5).
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Keep the lesson: what counts is the ability to hold, not the intention. Change the clause in three ways:
  - Add "each payment's cash arrives before its date".
  - Read "held to maturity" as "held to its end", because the five iBond funds pay their net asset value (CANON C2, D5).
  - Date it from January 2028.
  - Holding by holding:
    - 2038-2042 (the five T-bonds): yes.
    - 2033-2037 (the five iBond funds): yes, to each fund's end, subject to the board's date (row 28).
    - The floor: yes to December 2032. Its one claimant is the facility contribution, paid from its payout. Anyone asking for that money earlier would force a sale at market price (unsized; 3d's single close date prevents it, row 29).
    - The moved half: only after the move (the holding is UNVERIFIED, CANON A32).
- **Where it lands:**
  - One clause in 3e inside "why only these two". The two reasons are the only two flows the plan does not own: money coming in (the 2028 deposit) and the Treasury's payments. Every failure here broke on a flow the institution did not own and had not named.
  - SVB named once at most. Orange County and the gilt crisis go in the store only.
  - No new rule, no new row and no new exhibit.

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Intent is not ability (rows 7, 14, 33)
- **What is it?** Orange County's treasurer told his Board in 1993 that his policy was "to hold all securities purchased to maturity" and that there was "no risk of principal loss" (SEC, 1996). SVB booked 78% of its securities as held to maturity, a label that needs "the intent and ability" to hold (Barr, 2023).
- **Did it work?** No. Both meant to hold, and neither could. Orange County's lenders would not roll their repos, and SVB's depositors left. The paper loss became real only through the forced sale (Jorion: $1.6bn "locked in").
- **How is Laura's case like it?** IPS l.13 says "Held to the end, these pay what they say". In shape it is the same sentence as the treasurer's. A judge who knows these cases will ask what makes it different.
- **How is it not?** Nothing outside Laura can make her sell. She has no lender, no margin, no depositor and no collateral. Each payment has a fixed date, and its cash arrives half a month to 10.5 months early (NUMBERS 8h). Her intention is backed by a missing route, not by willpower. The one remaining route is Laura herself (row 33).
- **What Caplet takes.** In 3e, one clause on ability, never intention, inside "why only these two" (Card 2). No new number. CANON A8, C2 and C3 keep their meanings. The risk to IPS l.13's wording is recorded in row 7, not fixed (the IPS is frozen).

### Card 2. Two ingredients, and the two flows the plan does not own (rows 3, 11, 15, 20, 34)
- **What is it?** The FDIC's own pairing for SVB: "large volumes of uninsured deposits and large amounts of underwater securities". In each case a claim could be called within days, and the holdings were worth less than the claim on that day.
- **Did it work?** As a diagnosis, yes, for all three. The callable claims were Orange County's repo lenders (rolling every few months against holdings of nearly four years), SVB's deposits payable on request (94% uninsured) and the LDI funds' margin counterparties (calls over £70bn). In LDI the pensions themselves were never called; the schemes "generally benefit from rises in bond yields".
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her ladder shows paper losses whenever rates rise (about $234 a basis point, NUMBERS 1). She has the second ingredient on paper.
- **How is it not?** She never has the first. The only flows her payments depend on that she does not own on day one are the 2028 deposit and the Treasury's own payments, and those are exactly the two reasons (IPS l.17).
- **What Caplet takes.** 3e: the reason "why only these two" is short. They are the only flows the plan cannot own in advance. Every failure here broke on a flow it had not named. SVB is named at most once; there are no case figures in the report. Headline number: none new (3e keeps its own).

### Card 3. Time, not money (rows 5, 16, 22, 23)
- **What is it?** All three ended on a clock. Orange County took five days from the announced loss to bankruptcy. SVB took under 48 hours from announcement to closure, with about 85% of deposits gone or asked for. In the gilt crisis it was three business days to forced sales, and recapitalisation "designed for calmer times" was "simply too slow" though the schemes had "both the means and the incentive". The regulator's fix is a size and a time: 250bp, replenished "within five days" (TPR, 2023).
- **Did it work?** TPR's buffers are now held, and recapitalisation starts at higher levels (row 23; the 300-400bp figure is UNVERIFIED, see Gaps).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her plan also has a clock: each payment date, the five-business-day default test, and fund-end slack of 4 to 9 business days (SKELETON Exhibits item 4).
- **How is it not?** Her cash arrives early, by months for the T-bonds and by about half a month for the funds (NUMBERS 8h). No one else's request sets any of her dates.
- **What Caplet takes.** Table 3 already has the time dimension (five business days; the slack). No change. Store only, as the source for why the five-business-day test is the right shape (TPR's five days).

### Card 4. The 2031 move: a dated sale with nothing promised on it (rows 17, 26)
- **What is it?** SVB's repair, the sale and share issue of 8 Mar, announced its loss and started the run. A promise or an announcement made before the money is in hand is what gets tested.
- **Did it work?** For SVB, no. For Laura's plan, the range rule is the opposite design: "No figure is stated before the move" (NUMBERS 3).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The 2031 move is the plan's only scheduled sale. It sells shares on a fixed date, a "scheduled margin call" in store row H7-036's terms. VT on 1 Jan 2031 is $36,989, with a 1-in-20 low of $19,535 (NUMBERS 4, MODEL).
- **How is it not?** No claim rides on the sale price. The top is worked out after the move, the bottom has been fixed since January 2028, and the payments are unchanged with VT at zero (NUMBERS 4). The cost of breaking this is measured: a top stated 1, 3, 6 or 12 months early is not owned in 2.9%, 6.3%, 8.7% or 10.7% of house paths (NUMBERS 4).
- **What Caplet takes.** 3c, at most one clause tying "no figure before the move" to why the move cannot be forced. The measured cost of an early figure stays in the store (density: 3c carries the range and its confidence).

### Card 5. Wrong-way money: her flexibility (rows 24, 27)
- **What is it?** TPR warns against meeting a cash call with assets that fall in the same event that causes the call.
- **Did it work?** It is a regulator's rule since April 2023. The evidence behind it is the gilt crisis itself, where gilt sales met gilt-driven calls.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** One of the three uses of her flexibility is "a co-sponsor falling short" (IPS l.15). Co-sponsors cut budgets in the same recession that cuts her income and her shares (IPS l.9). Her flexibility is about $21,300, with a house 1-in-20 low of $9,881 (NUMBERS 4).
- **How is it not?** It touches only the facility. The ten never draw on it, and the facility contribution is capped at the top (CANON D12).
- **What Caplet takes.** Nothing new in the report. SKELETON 3d and 3e already record the risk; this card gives it a regulator's source for the store. Never rank the three uses (rules: "ranked by no one").

### Card 6. The two parties left who can end a holding: the fund's board and Laura (rows 28, 29, 33)
- **What is it?** Once lenders, depositors and margin are gone, two parties can still end a holding early.
  - The fund's board. It "may extend the termination date" with no shareholder vote; whether it can end a fund early is UNVERIFIED.
  - Laura herself. The IPS is not a trust (H3-120).
- **Did it work?** Three iBond Treasury funds ended on schedule in 2023-2025 (NUMBERS 8h; Q16). No extension case was found (Q16).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** An extension past a payment, or an early end, would make her act on a date she did not pick. Her own need in a recession could do the same.
- **How is it not?** Neither party is a creditor. An extension meets the swap step, when the fund holds almost nothing but cash. Laura has promised the ten to herself, so no third party calls them.
- **What Caplet takes.**

  - Read the statement of additional information for an early-end power before 27 Nov (lead's call). If there is one, it goes in row 4's note as an instrument change, never a third reason.
