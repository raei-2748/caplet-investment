## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Three of its four parts survive, but "short" measures the wrong thing, and "works only if" is too strong.
  - **What limits a watcher is firings that ask for nothing, not rows on a page.** Hospitals: 85-99% of alarm signals need no clinical action, several hundred per patient a day (Joint Commission, 2013a); 187 audible alarms per ICU bed a day even after the quiet ones were muted (Drew et al., 2014). Nurses slowed as non-actionable alarms in the past two hours passed 30 and then 80: in intensive care 2.8, 5.3 and 8.5 minutes to respond (Bonafide et al., 2015). Google caps a pager at about 2 incidents per 12-hour shift (Spadaccini, 2016). The process industry calls more than 10 alarms in 10 minutes a flood (ISA 18.2, quoted in Ruskin & Clebone Ruskin, 2025).
  - **Table 3 sits far below every one of those limits.** It gives about six dated looks in six years (the January 2027, 2028 and 2031 trade days, 1 Dec 2027, 31 March 2028, 31 October 2032 for the late-2032 fund's final year; Keel arithmetic on Table 3's cells), plus three event rows that fire only if something happens. Its seven rows in levels of 2, 3 and 2 sit inside WHO's 5-9 items per section (store Q30) and Cowan's three to five chunks held at once (Cowan, 2001), and fit on one page as a dashboard should (Few, 2004). Nothing in the evidence argues for fewer rows, and nothing argues for more.
  - **Each row that can fire needs a stated threshold. A row that watches only can say it has none.** Row 6 ("Any level" / "None") is what Google calls logging: "No one needs to look at this information" until something else prompts it (Treynor Sloss, 2016a). It cannot cry wolf.
  - **"Works only if" is necessary, not enough.** Ontario's hospitals reported 98% use of a surgical checklist and saw no significant change in deaths, 0.71% to 0.65% (Urbach et al., 2014). The checklist that worked, in Michigan, named five acts (Pronovost et al., 2006). So each "Changes:" cell must name an act and who does it. Table 3's cells mostly do, and row 3's "nothing before it" is an honest act of none.
  - **Rewritten claim:** "A watch table for one person and her asset manager works when each row that can fire triggers on the event itself, not a forecast of it, with a stated threshold, one named reader and a date to look; the rows are sorted into three levels by what a firing asks for (act, look closer, nothing), and every 'Changes' cell names an act. Length is a page limit, not the binding one."
- **Claim 2: KILL.** Laura's risk is the opposite of alarm fatigue: too little to watch. The commonest failure in aviation awareness errors reported to NASA's safety reporting system was not perceiving information at all (76.3% at level 1), with "failure to monitor or observe available information" the largest single category (Jones & Endsley, 1996); in 35% of errors the information was there and not attended to (Endsley, 2000). Google calls this "operational underload": "knowledge gaps are discovered only when an incident occurs" (Spadaccini, 2016). Telling a watcher to stay alert did nothing in Mackworth's 1948 clock test (Klein & Feltmate, 2025). So a row needs a dated occasion to be read, not an instruction to watch closely.
  - **Judge's note:** the danger for a table read a handful of times over six years is not too many alarms but too few: the first real one arrives to a reader who has never had to act on it. That is why Table 3's two plan-changing rows each carry a date or a counted delay.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Three levels, as Table 3 already has, not two. Certified airliners must sort alerts into warning, caution and advisory by how fast the crew must respond (14 CFR 25.1322(b)); Google sorts monitoring output into pages, tickets and logging (Treynor Sloss, 2016b). In a lab, a three-stage alarm beat a yes/no alarm on trust, compliance and performance (Wiczorek & Manzey, 2014), but the evidence is mixed: in a later test a cautious yes/no alarm had the fewest joint misses, and four stages risked users treating "the two lower and the two higher stages" alike (Zirk et al., 2020). So: three levels, with the top level set cautiously (it fires only on the event itself), and never a fourth.
- **Claim 4: KILL.** A forecast of a rare event is a poor trigger. At a base rate of 1 in 1,000, even a detector right 99.9% of the time gives "only 1 in 59 alarms" true (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). Google: "only worry about very definite, very imminent causes" (Ewaschuk, 2016). All 13 debt-limit standoffs of 2011-2025 ended without a missed payment (store Q15-024), so a plan-changing debt-limit trigger would have been wrong every time it fired. Table 3 already has this right: the debt-limit row changes "nothing before it", and the default row fires on the payment itself, "over five business days late".
  - **Judge's note:** a smart reader expects the watch to be on the cause (Congress, the debt limit); the safer design watches the symptom (a payment not arriving), because the cause cries wolf and the symptom cannot.
- **Claim 5: KILL.** A list ticked is not a list acted on (Urbach et al., 2014: 98% reported use, no significant effect). Lists work when each item is a specific act aimed at one failure (Pronovost et al., 2006: median infection rate 2.7 to 0 per 1,000 catheter-days within three months).
