## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Keynes's own record sides with Darren

- (a) Keynes wrote in 1936 that it is "better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally",
  and from 1921 to 1946 he ran part of his Cambridge college's money, from about 1932 in a few shares he believed in,
  and beat the UK market by several points a year (15.97% a year against 10.37% for an equal-weighted UK index).
- (b) An index fund is the most conventional holding there is, so Darren's objection has the best pedigree in economics:
  picking skill exists, and a copout can be a team protecting its reputation instead of serving its client.
- (c) It breaks on the calendar. The college had no date to meet, so it could wait through Keynes's worst year (24.88
  points behind the market in 1926-27), but Laura names her top on 1 January 2031, three years after VT is bought, and
  one such year in the half that moves would cost her about one $5,000 step of her range (Keel arithmetic, row 14). The
  break teaches that for Laura failing conventionally protects the client, not the team, because the co-sponsors who hear
  her figure watch the same market.
- **Decision it serves:** VT kept (IPS l.15); the move on 1 January 2031 (l.15; NUMBERS 8r). **Part:** 3f (Darren's
  case at its strongest, one clause); 3c (why a top resting on picks could not be named three years ahead).
- **Source:** Keynes (1936), ch. 12, section V; Chambers and Dimson (2012), pp. 14-15, 19-22, 50.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Every finance judge knows the beauty contest; few know that its author was a winning stock
  picker whose method backs Darren, and that the answer to him is a date, not a slogan.

### 2. Grossman and Stiglitz: the copout is a free ride, and the free ride is the point

- (a) In 1980 Grossman and Stiglitz showed that if prices already held everything investors know, nobody would pay to
  find anything out, and then prices could not hold it; so a market needs pickers, and they are paid by those who don't
  pick.
- (b) Darren's word was therefore accurate: VT rides free on other people's research, and the decision was to ride free
  on purpose, because Laura's $29,594 of shares carries no figure she has stated and the pickers' verdict costs her
  0.06% a year.
- (c) It breaks at scale, because if everyone rode free, prices would stop carrying information, and US index funds
  first held more money than active funds at the end of 2023 ($13.3 trillion against $13.2 trillion). The break teaches
  that the team's answer is about Laura's size and purpose, not about markets: the charge is right about the world, and
  the free ride is still right for her.
- Two more sources sharpen where Darren was right. Pedersen (2018) shows that Sharpe's sum leaves out new shares and
  index changes (Sharpe's own footnote 3: "To keep things simple, we ignore them"), so pickers as a group can add "a
  modest but nontrivial amount of value" before fees. Bernstein's 2016 note put the charge at its loudest: passive
  investing is "worse than either a centrally planned economy" or an active one.
- **Decision it serves:** VT held and left alone (IPS l.15, l.17). **Part:** 3f (where Darren was right, one clause);
  2.3. **Source:** Grossman and Stiglitz (1980), abstract and p. 405; Pedersen (2018), pp. 21-30; Morningstar data as
  reported (Investment News NZ, 2024); Bernstein as reported (InvestmentNews, 2016).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for the paper; the aha is a team that concedes the charge and keeps the fund for a
  reason about its client.

### 3. Hayek's man on the spot: ten picks and one refusal

- (a) Hayek argued in 1945 that the knowledge that matters most is "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of
  time and place", which only the person on the spot has, and that prices carry everything else with a remarkable
  "economy of knowledge".
- (b) The team's particular knowledge was of time and place, ten Januaries when Laura must pay and a building in Taiwan,
  so it chose ten Treasuries one at a time, each due before its payment ("whichever works best for each year", IPS
  l.13), and refused to choose among 10,000 companies whose prices already hold more than any team in Sydney knows.
- (c) It breaks because choosing a Treasury for a date is not a forecast at all: it matches a calendar that no price
  contains, so it needs no edge over the market. The break teaches the moment's lesson: "picking" names two acts,
  matching what you know about the client and forecasting what the market does not know, and the copout charge, fairly
  put, was only about the second; knowledge of place even argues against a Taiwan fund, because the building already
  carries Taiwan's risks (Q46 reason 6).
- **Decision it serves:** the ten Treasuries chosen year by year (IPS l.13; TNA l.15); one world fund (l.15).
  **Part:** 3f (the "after": what the team understands now); 2.3 or 2.5 (one clause).
- **Source:** Hayek (1945), sections III, V and VI.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A judge knows Hayek, but not his "time and place" mapped onto a payment calendar and a
  building, and the mapping turns the moment from humility into a division of labour the IPS already shows.

### 4. Owning everything: the only sure way to own the few, and still a pick

- (a) Bessembinder found that 1,092 companies, 4.3% of nearly 26,000 US stocks since 1926, made all of the market's net
  gains, which helps explain "why active strategies, which tend to be poorly diversified, most often underperform".
- (b) The only sure way to own those few companies is to own all the others too, so the copout is the one strategy
  certain to hold the winners, while twenty picks will most likely miss them.
- (c) It breaks where Darren was right, because owning everything is still a choice: Sharpe's own sum begins "First a
  market must be selected", and VT's market puts 62.4% of the money in the US and 23.5% in ten companies. The break
  teaches that VT does not remove opinion; it uses the one opinion that is published, which is why co-sponsors can test
  Laura's top against fifteen houses' forecasts for world shares (NUMBERS 8p; Q46 row 1).
- **Decision it serves:** one fund of the widest market (IPS l.15). **Part:** 3f (one clause, the inversion); 2.3.
- **Source:** Bessembinder (2018) as summarised by the W. P. Carey School; Sharpe (1991), p. 7; VT's weights from store
  H9-103 and Q117-011.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for Bessembinder (already in the store); the inversion, that the copout is the only
  strategy certain to hold the winners and that "not picking" is the widest pick, is the new turn.

### 5. Buffett's bet kept its prize in Treasuries

- (a) In December 2007 Buffett bet Protégé Partners that an S&P 500 index fund would beat five funds of hedge funds over
  ten years, and to fund the $1 million prize each side paid $318,250 for Treasury bonds that would pay $500,000 when
  the bet ended.
- (b) So the most famous contest between picking and not picking kept its promised figure in Treasuries and argued only
  about the rest, which is the shape of Laura's plan: what she states is owned in Treasuries, and only money promised to
  no one sits in shares.
- (c) It breaks in November 2012, when the two sides sold the bonds and bought 11,200 shares of one company, Berkshire,
  but only because Buffett "agreed to make up any shortfall". Laura has nobody to make up a shortfall (her payments must
  never depend "on anybody else's money", IPS l.11), so her move runs the other way, shares into Treasuries before she
  names a figure; and the break teaches that even the index fund's champion picked a single stock once someone stood
  behind the figure promised.
- The bet's bonds were zero-coupon strips, which Wharton bars for Laura this year: the parallel is the act, never the
  instrument.
- **Decision it serves:** the payments and the bottom held in Treasuries (IPS l.11, l.15); the 2031 move (l.15).
  **Part:** 3f (a closing clause) or 3c (why the top moves before it is named).
- **Source:** Berkshire Hathaway (2018), 2017 letter, pp. 11-13; Berkshire Hathaway (2017), 2016 letter; Long Bets,
  Bet 362.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the bet is pedigree; how its prize was held, and what moving it into shares required, is aha.

### 6. Galton's ox was weighed

- (a) In 1907 Galton collected 787 sixpenny guesses of a fat ox's weight at a livestock show in Plymouth, and the
  middle guess, 1,207 lb, was only 9 lb above the weight measured after the ox was slaughtered, 1,198 lb.
- (b) A share price is the same kind of middle guess, made by thousands of people with money on it, so VT holds the
  crowd's estimate of 10,000 companies instead of a team's estimate of twenty, which makes it the better pencil.
- (c) It breaks because Galton could check his crowd, since the ox was weighed, while a share is never weighed on a set
  day and a Treasury held to its end has one, a printed date when it pays out. The break teaches the conceit: however
  good the crowd's guess, it stays pencil, and Laura states a figure only when something she owns has a weighing day.
- Keynes's beauty contest is the second break: Galton's guessers judged the ox, while investors judge one another's
  guesses ("the third degree").
- **Decision it serves:** the ink rule (CONCEIT; IPS l.15, "money you already own"). **Part:** 3c or 2.0 (one image
  for pencil); 3f only if its one conceit turn is free.
- **Source:** Galton (1907); Keynes (1936), ch. 12, section V.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Galton is known for the crowd's accuracy; the weighing day as the line between pencil and
  ink is new and true.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 3, ten picks and one refusal (rows 15-17; 3f's "after", one clause in 2.3).** It turns "copout" from humility
   into a division of labour the IPS already shows: the team picked where it knew Laura's dates, which no price
   contains, and refused where it knew less than the price. A judge knows Hayek but has not seen "time and place" fit a
   payment calendar and a building.
2. **Idea 5, the bet's prize in Treasuries (rows 18-20; 3f's closing clause or 3c).** The most famous index-against-
   picker contest built Laura's structure by itself, and the one time it moved its promised figure into shares, Buffett
   had to guarantee it. Laura has no guarantor, so her move runs the other way.
3. **Idea 1, Keynes's record sides with Darren (rows 11-14; 3f).** It honours the objection with the best pedigree there
   is, then answers it with Laura's date: one Keynes-sized bad year in picked shares costs a whole $5,000 step of the top
   she names.

## Three ways the moment could be told

**A. "Copping a plea".**
- Frame: the word's first sense (row 1). Keeping VT is a plea to the lesser charge, the market's return less 0.06%,
  instead of a trial with twenty picks, most of which lose to Treasury bills.
- Erudite idea: Bessembinder's skew (row 9), with Grossman and Stiglitz (row 2) for where Darren was right.
- The thinking that changed: before, the team had a confession, a general reason any team could give (TNA l.23);
  after, it had a bargain priced for Laura (a top co-sponsors can check, flexibility that holds in bad years).
- Risk: wit at a teammate's expense. Darren must never be the butt; the first sense is the team's joke on itself, said
  once and dryly. A pun can read as dodging the charge, so it must be followed at once by where he was right.

**B. "Ten picks and one refusal".**
- Frame: the team chose ten Treasuries one by one, each for its January, then declined one choice (rows 15-17).
- Erudite idea: Hayek's "particular circumstances of time and place".
- The thinking that changed: the argument moved from "conviction or copout" to "what do we know that no price
  contains?", and the answer was Laura's calendar, never a company.
- Risk: it reads as defensive if it opens the paragraph. "Picked" must never sound like stock picking, the counts must
  match the IPS (ten for the payments), and no one teammate is credited with the Treasury choices.

**C. "Keep the prize in Treasuries".**
- Frame: Buffett's bet held its promised $1 million in Treasuries and moved it into one stock only with his personal
  guarantee (rows 18-20).
- Erudite idea: shares can sit above a stated figure only when someone stands behind that figure, and for Laura only an
  owned Treasury does.
- The thinking that changed: the team argued about which shares; what it understands now is that no stated figure ever
  rests on shares, which is why the shares were the one place an argument was safe to have.
- Risk: Buffett is the most quoted name in investing, so this can read as name-dropping. The bet's bonds were strips,
  which Laura may not hold, so the text says "Treasury bonds" and nothing more. The bet's result is never evidence for
  Laura's plan. And the frame is long for about 220 words.
