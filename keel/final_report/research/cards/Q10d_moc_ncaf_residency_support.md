**Problem class and the field that solved it.** The problem is co-funding that is conditional, paid back against
receipts and voted each year. Will a public co-funder's money arrive? How much, when, and on what conditions? Public
budgeting and grant administration answer it with the funder's own rulebook:
- who may apply;
- what the money may buy;
- the window and the payment trigger;
- the budget-dependence clause.
They also use the appropriation record: what was proposed, cut, frozen and passed, and how late. For Taiwan that
record is:
- the Ministry of Culture's directions and their 13 versions (2008-2025);
- NCAF's 2027 standards and its operating-grant rules;
- the grant lists for 2023, 2025 and 2026;
- the budget series from the Legislative Yuan's research office and the Ministry's statistics site (2006-2024);
- the 2020-21 COVID special budgets;
- the 2025-27 budget fights.
Nonprofit-finance research on how stable government revenue is was not read today (gap).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
   - **"One-year" and "operating" hold.** The art-village grant has been one year at a time since December 2024. The
     only multi-year option it ever offered (up to two years, 2021-2024) has been removed. The 2026 grants ran from
     NT$0.7m to NT$1.8m against a NT$3m cap; the mean was NT$1.23m, about $37,450.
   - **"No match for a private founder's capital" holds in both senses.** No matching ratio for a private applicant
     was found. The amounts are yearly running money, not capital.
   - **"Small" needs a scale.** One typical grant is about a third of the $115,000 central contribution. The same
     village can win it year after year: Treasure Hill, Lize, 橋仔頭 and Soulangh were funded in 2023, 2025 and 2026.
   - **"Cannot be counted on for the building" holds for construction.** But the grant's space plan does pay for
     equipment and facilities within the cap, and in 2009 the grant paid only for hardware.
   - **"Come late" holds, by design.** Money follows receipts. In 2026 the village list (29 Jan) and NCAF's operating
     list (13 Feb) stood on a national budget the legislature passed only on 14 Aug, after 351 days.
   - **"Most likely to fall short" cannot be shown.** No source ranks co-sponsors. The state's shortfalls in Taiwan
     come from yearly competition (two private villages funded in 2025 are not on the 2026 list), council and
     legislature votes, and rules that change. They do not come from recessions.
   - Reword: "Taiwan's public money for a residency is yearly, competitive running money, paid after the work and voted
     each year. It pays for a programme, not a building."
2. **Claim 2: CHANGE (keep the first half, kill the "so").**
   - In 2008-10 the Council for Cultural Affairs' spending rose 29.1% and then 22.5%, and its grants line rose 71.6% in
     2009.
   - NCAF's regular grants dipped about 6% over 2008-09 (Q4b row 29).
   - In 2020-21 the Ministry added NT$10.18bn of COVID relief on top of a regular budget of about NT$19.5bn. NCAF's
     grant total was flat in 2020 (-0.2%).
   - The "so" does not follow. Taiwan's company arts giving also held up in 2008-10 (Q4b row 24). The state's real risk
     is political: in 2025 the legislature made the largest cut and freeze ever made to the Ministry's budget, and in
     2026 it passed the budget 351 days late.
   - No source shows which co-sponsor type is more likely to fall short.
   - **Risk to record against IPS l.9 and CANON B4:** "co-sponsors cut their budgets" in a recession does not describe
     Taiwan's central culture budget in 2008-10 or 2020-21.
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.**
   - The order holds. Public operating money can only follow Laura's December 2032 money by at least one application
     cycle. The village grant now wants a residency that already runs residencies regularly, with professional staff.
     NCAF's operating grant wants one year of registration and one full-time employee.
   - "Private capital is what qualifies it" is wrong as a rule. The written tests are:
     - legal form (any registered body for the village grant; non-profits only for NCAF's operating grant);
     - a site right (ownership or right of use, so a lease can count);
     - an operating record;
     - an operating-funds estimate and proof of the applicant's finances.
     No rule asks for owned capital or an owned building. Capital is the practical way to meet the tests, not a test.

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. A rulebook that moved from building money to running money (文化部藝術村營運扶植補助作業要點, 13
versions, 2008-2025).**
- **What is it?** The Ministry's grant for art villages.
  - 2009: hardware improvement only, up to NT$2m. Private studios and companies could apply on a two-year lease.
  - 2013: a residency category and a hardware category.
  - 2021: cities added, and a cross-year plan of at most two years for villages funded more than three years.
  - 2023: a NT$3m yearly cap per application.
  - Dec 2024: cross-year plans removed; the grant now defines an art village as one that "regularly" runs residencies
    with professional staff, and living costs rose to NT$800 a day.
  - Oct 2025: cities removed.
- **Did it work?** It still runs, and about a dozen villages are funded each year (Q9c row 10). The same established
  villages return year after year (rows 11-12).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** It is the public programme a Taiwanese residency would look to first. Its terms show
  what a public co-sponsor's money is: one year, after receipts, voted each year, with hosting duties.
- **How is it not?** It never paid to build a residency. Its capital years paid for improving existing space. A
  residency that is not yet running is outside its current definition.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3e: public running money for a residency is yearly and depends on rules that change
  about every 16 months, so the plan counts none of it. For 3d: say what Laura's money is for (the facility and its
  first years) and what public money is for (the programme once it runs).

**Card 2. The 2026 list: who gets public village money, and who drops out (文化部 2026e).**
- **What is it?** NT$13.5m to 11 grantees, each NT$0.7m-1.8m. None got near the NT$3m cap. Two are companies. Six
  are private or independent and five are public or city-linked.
- **Did it work?** The four longest-funded villages (Treasure Hill, Lize, 橋仔頭, Soulangh) were funded in 2023, 2025
  and 2026. Two private villages funded in 2025 are not on the 2026 list, one of them the private founder's village
  Q9c row 32 calls the closest Taiwanese case to Laura's.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A typical year's grant (about $37,450) is about a third of the $115,000 central
  contribution. For a residency, a public co-sponsor is a yearly line that can recur, not a capital partner.
- **How is it not?** Laura's money is one-off and owned. The grant is renewable at the Ministry's choice and
  conditional on the legislature.
- **What Caplet takes.** The co-sponsor shortfall that can be seen in Taiwan is a private village missing a year's
  list, not a recession. The kept half's third use (IPS l.15) is real. The card does not size it (case l.106-107).

**Card 3. In bad years Taiwan's state added money; in political years it cut (rows 20-27).**
- **What is it?**
  - 2008-10: Council spending +29.1% and +22.5%; grants +71.6% in 2009.
  - 2020-21: NT$10.18bn of COVID relief on top of a regular budget of about NT$19.5bn; NCAF's total flat.
  - 2025: the legislature's largest-ever cut and freeze of the Ministry's budget, during a year of strong growth.
  - 2026: the budget passed 351 days late.
  - 2027: a proposal 7.9% higher.
- **Did it work?** Through both recessions the aggregates held or rose (NCAF's regular grants dipped about 6% in
  2008-09, Q4b row 29). The cuts came from votes, not from the economy.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** IPS l.15 lists "a co-sponsor falling short". In Taiwan's public money that has meant
  a budget fight, a late budget or a lost competition. It has not meant a recession cut.
- **How is it not?** Aggregates are not grants to one residency. Laura's co-sponsors may be private, and Q4b found
  Taiwan's company arts giving at a record in 2008-10 too.
- **What Caplet takes.** Record a risk against IPS l.9 (CANON B4). The Final Report should not say Taiwan's government
  cut arts budgets in a recession. If 3e names a watch item for public co-sponsors, it is the legislature's calendar,
  not a share index.

**Card 4. The one public grant for a space's staff and rent (NCAF 視覺藝術組織營運補助專案 rules, 2023).**
- **What is it?** Non-profits registered at least a year, with at least one full-time staff member, running visual-arts
  work, may ask each November for the next year's salaries, admin and venue rent: NT$350,000-900,000 each, NT$15m in
  all. Results come in February or March of the funded year. Payment follows receipts under a contract, and the amount
  can be cut with the legislature's budget.
- **Did it work?** 21 of 27 applicants were funded in 2025 and 23 of 26 in 2026. Two 2026 art villages hold this grant
  and the Ministry's.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** It is the closest thing to public operating support. It asks for a year's existence
  and an employee first, which is the mild, real form of claim 3.
- **How is it not?** It is for visual-arts organisations, small (about $10,680-$27,470 each) and non-profit only. A
  residency of another form may not fit. The card makes no ruling.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3d: the order "her money first, public money later" matches the public funders' own
  rules. Laura's money is what lets a residency get to the start line.

**Card 5. When public money could arrive, set against December 2032 (rows 8, 15, 18-19, 30).**
- **What is it?** The floor fund pays on or about 15 Dec 2032 (CANON C8).
  - The village window for a calendar year opens in November or December of the year before, and money follows
    receipts by 30 November.
  - NCAF's operating grant opens each November for the next year and decides in February or March.
  - NCAF's project rounds decide in April and September, and pay when the work ends.
- **Did it work?** These windows have run every year since 2021-22 (village) and 2018 (NCAF operating).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Public money for the residency's running can only start after her contribution
  has paid for the facility and a first season.
- **How is it not?** Rules and windows change (13 village versions). NCAF project money for an opening programme could
  be decided in September 2032, before Laura pays, though it is paid after the work.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3d: the sequence is the funders' own, not Caplet's assumption.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1. CHANGE.**
- **Keep:**
  - "one-year" (rows 3, 6);
  - "operating" (rows 3, 16, 18);
  - "cannot be counted on for the building" (rows 4-5, 16, 28: construction is never paid; equipment and facilities
    are paid within the cap);
  - "come late" (paid after receipts, rows 3, 15, 18; lists announced on an unpassed budget in 2026, row 26);
  - "no match": no matching ratio for a private applicant was found in the rules read, and the amounts are running
    money.
- **Change "small" to a scale.** A typical grant is NT$1.23m (about $37,450), about a third of the $115,000 central
  contribution, for one year. Established villages win it most years (rows 9, 11).
- **Kill "the co-sponsor most likely to fall short".** No source ranks co-sponsor types. The shortfalls seen in Taiwan's
  public money are a lost year's competition (row 11), a council or legislature vote (Q9c row 24; rows 25-26), and
  rules that change (row 7).
- **Also:** WORDS keeps the half kept's three uses unranked. This verdict ranks no co-sponsor and no use.
- **Replacement for the store:** "Taiwan's public money for a residency is yearly, competitive running money, paid
  after the work and voted each year; it pays for a programme, not a building, and a residency that is not yet running
  cannot apply."

**Claim 2. CHANGE.**
- **Keep "held up in past downturns", with the numbers:**
  - Council spending +29.1% (2009) and +22.5% (2010), grants +71.6% in 2009 (row 20);
  - NCAF's regular grants -3.6% and -2.8% in 2008-09 (Q4b row 29);
  - NT$10.18bn of COVID relief (row 22) and NCAF's total -0.2% in 2020 (row 23);
  - a 9.0% fall in the Ministry's regular budget in 2021 (row 21) while relief flowed.
- **Kill the "so".** Taiwan's company arts giving also held up in 2008-10 (Q4b row 24). The public side's real risk is
  political (rows 25-26), and no source ranks the two.
- **Replacement:** "In Taiwan's last two downturns, central arts money held up or rose; its cuts came from the
  legislature in 2025-26, in a boom."
- **Risk to record (frozen plan, no change):** IPS l.9's "co-sponsors cut their budgets" is general. Q4b supports it
  for corporate sponsors and US state arts budgets. For Taiwan's central culture budget it fails in 2008-10 and
  2020-21. The Final Report should not attribute recession cuts to Taiwan's government.

**Claim 3. CHANGE.**
- **Keep the order.** By the funders' own windows, public running money follows Laura's December 2032 money by at least
  one cycle (rows 8, 30). The village grant wants a residency that already runs residencies regularly (row 1), and
  NCAF's operating grant wants a year's registration and one full-time employee (row 18).
- **Kill "private capital is what qualifies it".** The written tests are legal form, a site right (a lease counts),
  an operating record and an operating-funds estimate with proof of finances (rows 2, 29). Capital is how a founder
  meets them in practice.
- **Change "a building and a track record first"** to "a site right, a running residency and, for NCAF, a year of
  registration first."
- **No eligibility ruling on Laura.** Registration, residence and legal-form questions are Taiwan law, out of scope.
- **Replacement:** "Taiwan's public funders support a residency once it is registered, has its site and is running.
  Laura's December 2032 money is what gets it there, so her contribution comes first and public money, if any, follows
  year by year."

**What the report could use (store notes, not prose).**
- 3b: the half kept's third use is real for public co-sponsors. Taiwan's own pattern is a yearly list a residency can
  drop off, plus budget fights.
- 3d: the funders' own sequence (owned money and site, then a running season, then yearly public programme money).
  Also name which funder pays which part, because public funders do not double-fund one item (rows 3, 15, 18).
- 3e: a limit, "public programme rules and amounts change; the plan counts none of them". A watch item for public
  co-sponsors: the legislature's budget calendar.
