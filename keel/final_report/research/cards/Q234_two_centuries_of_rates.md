- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** placing one price in its long history, and asking whether any level is "normal" (a reference-class question). Financial history built the data first: Macaulay's bond yields for the NBER (1938), then Homer's *A History of Interest Rates* (1963; Sylla's editions 1991, 1996, 2005). The answer to "is it high?" is a percentile rank, and it depends on the window chosen. Economics asked the theory question as the "natural rate" (Wicksell, 1898; not read this run).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The answer depends on the window, so the report names one or says nothing.
  - **Since 1871** (1,870 months; Keel arithmetic): 4.86% is higher than 73.3% of months. Median 3.84%, mean 4.49%, upper quartile 5.05%. A little above the middle.
  - **The 10-year's own record since April 1953** (FRED GS10, 883 months): median 4.73%. 4.86% is higher than 51.5% of months. This is the middle.
  - **The long end, each against its own record:** the 20-year at 5.29% is higher than 57.2% of months since 1953 (median 4.84%). The 30-year at 5.26% is higher than 48.1% of months since 1977 (median 5.45%).
  - **By era:**
    - 1871-1965: lower than 4.86% in 93.7% of months (range 1.95%-5.58%).
    - 1966-2026: lower in only 41.4% of months (median 5.71%).
    - August 2007 to August 2026: no monthly average reached 4.86%.
  - **Homer and Sylla's view:** economists and historians "do not always agree on how to read the chart, especially on how to define normality" (p. 554).
  - **Judge's note:** 4.86% looks high only to a memory that starts in 2008. Against the 10-year's own 73 years it is the median.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Say the plan is *priced* at an ordinary rate. The rate is not something the team bought, and "lucky" is the wrong word for it.
  - **The purchase date.** The plan buys in January 2027 at that day's price. The report prices it on the 6 Nov curve (NUMBERS 0). The WInS fills' timing within 2026 was "luck of timing, not skill" (8b). That is a statement about one year, not about history.
  - **Neither extreme.** 4.86% is 4.3 points above the 2020 low (0.52%, 4 Aug 2020) and about 11 points below the 1981 peak (15.84%, 30 Sep 1981; FRED DGS10).
  - **Unusual only against recent years.** October 2026's monthly 5.13% was the highest since May 2002 (5.16%).
  - **Judge's note:** from 1871 to 1965 a 4.86% long rate would have counted as high. From 1966 on it would have counted as low.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** A lower rate costs the building, not the payments, unless rates go back to 2020's lows.
  - **Where the cost lands.** The first deposit is spent whole at today's level ("your entire first deposit", IPS l.13), so the first purchase cannot grow. A lower rate moves cost into the 2028 deposit. Its first slice is $39,162 today. After falls of 0.25 / 0.5 / 1 / 2 points it is $45,729 / $52,449 / $66,368 / $96,303 (NUMBERS 5). The floor and VT shrink almost dollar for dollar. After about a 1.3-point fall, "most" fails and VT gets nothing (NUMBERS 5).
  - **When the payments themselves are at risk.** The ten stay paid to the report's standard until a fall of 3.57 points, and the ladder alone until 3.89 points (NUMBERS 5). If the whole curve moved by the same amount, that is a 10-year near 1.3% and near 1.0% (Keel arithmetic, approximate).
  - **How often history went that low.** In 155 years only 13 months fell below 1.29% and 10 below 0.97%, all between March 2020 and August 2021. On the real curve of 4 Aug 2020 the ten cost $470,217, more than both deposits (8e).
  - **Judge's note:** by design, the order of spending sends every dollar of a lower rate to the payments first. The building pays for falling rates, never the promise.
- **Claim 4: KEEP as history; KILL as a forecast or a reason to time the purchase.**
  - **The book.** Homer and Sylla date five "great cycles" since 1798. They "reveal no uniformity of timing or scope" (p. 336). The longest bull market ran 1861-1899 and the longest bear market 1946-1981.
  - **The waves on this series** (Keel arithmetic; turning months approximate before 1953):

    | Years | Move | Length |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1871-1899 | down 2.2 points | 28 years |
    | 1899-1921 | up 2.0 | 22 years |
    | 1921-1941 | down 3.1 | 20 years |
    | 1946-1981 | up 13.1 | 35.7 years |
    | 1981-2020 | down 14.7 | 38.8 years |
    | 2020-Oct 2026 | up 4.5 so far | 6.3 years |

  - **Sylla's own figure moves:** "20 to 30 year cycles" (Feb 2018), "three to four decades" (Apr 2020), "at least a decade and often several decades" (Feb 2024).
  - **His turn call.** He called "a turning point" in February 2018, when the 10-year was 2.86%. It then fell to 0.62% (July 2020) before it turned.
  - **Judge's note:** the historian who knows the waves best called the turn about two and a half years early. A plan that buys on a named date and completes the ladder "even if yields fall" (IPS l.13) needs no such call.
- **Claim 5: CHANGE the label; 8e's figures stand.**
  - **What Shiller says.** He built 1871-1952 "from series in Sydney Homer's A History of Interest Rates" (NBER WP 13558, n. 4).
  - **What Homer says.**
    - US government yields are the best guide only "From 1798 until 1863" (p. 286).
    - After that come municipal bonds for two decades, then prime railroad bonds.
    - Note-circulation privileges, tax exemptions and few long issues "deprive us of a usable series for several decades" (p. 445).
  - **What the numbers show (Keel arithmetic).**
    - 1886-1920: Shiller's decade averages match Macaulay's railroad bonds and S&P's municipal bonds within 0.1 point.
    - 1919-1953: they match the Treasury's long-bond averages.
  - **The wording.** Say "long-term high-grade US bond yields since 1871 (US government from 1919)". Never say "Treasury yields since 1871".
- **Does the level matter for a plan that holds each Treasury to its date?**
  - **Not once a holding is bought.** Held to the end they "pay what they say" (IPS l.13), and the ten are sized to be paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Rates matter on three days only:** January 2027 (what the ten cost), January 2028 (the top-up, the extra Treasuries and the floor fund's payout) and the 2031 move (what the moved half buys).
  - **Homer's harshest number shows why.** A constant-maturity 30-year 2½% bond lost 83% of its price from 1946 to 1981 (p. 367). That bond never gets nearer its date. Each of Laura's ten does, and pays face at the end.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 1-4 with row 31: one window, said once.** 4.86% is the median of the 10-year's own record since 1953, a little above the middle of 155 years, and the highest since 2007. Part 2.5 (the rare year, beside 8e) or 3e. It turns "rare" into "rare lately, ordinary in history". This is the judge's aha that the late-2026 level is not a lucky extreme.
2. **Rows 33-34: a lower rate costs the building, not the payments.** The exception is a return to the lows of 2020-21, which reached only 13 of 1,870 months. Part 3e (limits) and Table 2's note. It answers the obvious "what if you had bought in 2020?" with the register's own thresholds.
3. **Rows 17, 27-28: waves without a clock.** Homer and Sylla: "no uniformity of timing or scope". The book's living author called the turn about two and a half years early. Part 2.5 or 3f, one clause. It explains why IPS l.13 buys on a date and completes the ladder rather than waiting for a "normal" rate.
4. **Row 20: Homer's 83% is a bond that never matures.** Each of Laura's ten nears its date and pays face. Part 3a, one sentence. The worst bond bear market in US history cost a holder to the date no dollars, which is the plain reason the level after purchase does not matter.
5. **Rows 8-11: the label.** Use "long-term high-grade US bond yields since 1871 (US government from 1919)". Never use "Treasury yields since 1871". This binds every part that cites 8e. A bond judge who knows Homer would catch the error.
