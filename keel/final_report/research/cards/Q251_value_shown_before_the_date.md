- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. A government-backed holding shown below its promised sum is ordinary, and issuers explain it by giving the date it reaches the sum (US Series EE savings bonds).
  2. The municipal market shows a capital appreciation bond at its "accreted value" before maturity.
  3. A target-maturity bond fund's value moves toward what its bonds will pay as the end date nears, but it is not a fixed sum.
  4. A broker's statement must show a value, and FINRA's rules decide what it shows.
  5. Funders and gift offices book a pledge at its stated sum, not at the day's value of the assets behind it.
- **Problem class:** stating, before its date, the value of a claim to a fixed sum on that date, to a reader who will rely on it; the gap between "what it would fetch today" and "what it will pay then". **Field that solved it first:** the US Treasury's savings bond of 1935, which printed on each bond's face a table of what it was worth every six months until it paid its face value (rows 6-7); the municipal market's tables of compound accreted values in official statements (rows 11-14); then accounting's amortized cost and present-value rules (rows 31-35) and broker statement rules (rows 23-28).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, and sharper than the claim. The issuer does say the date, and on that date it adds money itself.**
  - **TreasuryDirect, today:** "For EE bonds you buy now, we guarantee that the bond will double in value in 20 years, even if we have to add money at 20 years to make that happen." "If you keep the bond for 20 years, we will make a one-time adjustment, if necessary, to fulfill this guarantee." The rule is in law: at original maturity a book-entry bond's redemption value "shall not be less than double the purchase price" (31 CFR 351.35(f)).
  - **What the account shows before then is the cash-today figure,** at the fixed rate: "values shown in TreasuryDirect and the Calculator don't include the last three months of interest. That's because if you cash a bond before 5 years, we don't pay you the final three months of interest."
  - **How far below the sum (Keel arithmetic on TreasuryDirect's rule and rate table):** an EE bond bought at 0.10% (the rate for bonds issued November 2015 to October 2022, among others) shows $10,197 per $10,000 at 19½ years, 51% of the $20,000 the Treasury pays six months later. At today's 2.90% it shows $17,532 at 19½ years (88%). On the day of the 2031 move the building's money shows $111,651, 97.1% of the $115,000 top (NUMBERS 8y; Keel arithmetic).
  - **Change:** an EE bond's figure is a redemption value set by the issuer's formula, never a market price, and it never falls. The building's statement is a market price, which a rise in yields can lower for a while (8y D). So the parallel is the shape (below the sum, dated, made up on the day), never "the statement cannot fall".
  - **The oldest form:** the 1935 "baby bond" was sold for $18.75 to pay $25 in ten years. The Treasury's leaflet said "Each bond bears the promise ... to pay to the owner the full amount (maturity value) stated on the bond 10 years from the date of issue", and printed the table ($18.75 the first year, then $19.00 ... $24.50, then $25.00 at maturity). In 1942: "A table of redemption values for each bond appears on its face."
  - **Judge's note:** a US savings bond bought at 0.10% shows about half its promised sum six months before its date, and the Treasury adds the other half on the day. Nobody calls that a shortfall, because the bond says the date. Laura's statement shows 97% of her top on the move day, and her letter says the date.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with the right term and one limit.**
  - **The MSRB's term is "compound accreted value":** "The compound accreted value as of a given time is equal to the initial principal amount plus the accretion ... to that date. A security's compound accreted value may differ from its market value depending on current interest rates. A schedule of compound accreted values for a security often is made available to investors, such as in the official statement."
  - **Real tables:** Conroe Independent School District (Texas, 1998) printed, per $5,000 of maturity amount at a 4.125% yield, $3,874.70 on 17 Nov 1998, $4,607.92 on 15 Feb 2003, $4,898.96 on 15 Aug 2004 and $5,000.00 on 15 Feb 2005, with "straight-line interpolation" between. Two years before maturity the table shows 92.2% of the sum, six months before 98.0% (Keel arithmetic). Martins Mill ISD (2009) gave its table "for informational purposes only".
  - **Limit:** an accreted value is the path at the yield at issue, not what a statement shows. A broker statement shows a market estimate (MSRB statement guide, row 23). For the building, the analogue of the accreted-value table is 8y's "expected statement on later dates at today's forward rates"; the analogue of the maturity value is what the Treasuries will pay by December 2032.
  - **Judge's note:** issuers of zero-coupon municipal bonds have printed a dated table of "what this bond is worth today" in the official statement for decades. The lead-in's page beside the broker's statement is that table, not a new invention.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with what is new on the sponsor's own page.**
  - **Toward what its bonds pay:** "Mature like a bond: Similar to individual bonds, iBonds ETFs have a specified maturity date, so there is less exposure to interest rate risk as maturity approaches" (BlackRock). At the end the price meets the payout: IBDQ's "final distribution of $25.13 to shareholders on December 15th, in line with the closing price on the final day of trading" (iShares case study; a corporate fund).
  - **Not a fixed sum** (already in the store: "do not seek to return any predetermined amount", Q3a-025; distributions up, end payout down, Q39-008).
  - **New:** IBTM's own page carries an "Estimated Net Acquisition Yield Calculator", a hold-to-the-end yield after the 0.07% fee for a price you enter, "for illustrative purposes only", and warns that the realized yield depends on "the yield earned on these proceeds during the final year". So the sponsor prints a forward figure beside the price, but as a yield, not a dollar sum, and not fixed. A fund share has no face to print.
  - **Judge's note:** a Treasury bond's line on a statement shows its face as the quantity; a fund's line shows only shares and a price. The one thing a co-sponsor cannot read off IBTM's line is the sum, which is why 8y works it out.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. FINRA requires a statement of positions, not a method of valuing a listed ETF. Its one valuation rule is about a different product, and it runs the other way.**
  - **What the rules say:** Rule 2231(a) requires "a description of any securities positions, money balances, or account activity" (Q230). A valuation rule exists only for unlisted REITs and direct participation programs (2231(c)): a "per share estimated value ... reasonably designed to ensure that [it] is reliable".
  - **Why that rule exists:** brokers used to print the offering price: "The offering price, typically $10 per share, often remains constant on customer account statements during this period even though various costs and fees have reduced investors' principal" (FINRA Regulatory Notice 15-02). The SEC approved the fix to end "displaying a DPP or REIT security's immutable offering price as its per share estimated value ... despite the fact that the value ... fluctuates".
  - **The required sentence on distributions:** "IMPORTANT—Part of your distribution includes a return of capital. Any distribution that represents a return of capital reduces the estimated per share value shown on your account statement" (2231(c)(2)(A)). It is the regulator's own sentence for 8y's point that money paid out leaves the line it came from. For IBTM the distributions are mostly interest, so the disanalogy is recorded.
  - **Where the figure comes from:** "For securities listed on a stock exchange, the price on your account statement will be the closing price"; prices are "intended to be representative only" (an investor guide hosted by the New Jersey Bureau of Securities). For municipal bonds "the prices used for determining market value are estimates", and unrealized gain or loss is shown "as if they had been sold on the statement date" (MSRB).
  - **A precision note for 8y:** a statement shows IBTM at its closing market price, not its net asset value. On normal days the two differ by 0.06% to 0.09% (NUMBERS section 0), so 8y's figures stand, but the lead-in should say "market value", never "net asset value".
  - **Judge's note:** regulators spent years stopping statements from showing a steady $10 "par" where the value had fallen. A statement that can show less than the promise is the honest kind, and the honest reply is to put the dated sum beside it, never in place of it.
- **Claim 5: CHANGE. Campaigns count the stated sum (Q18-005, not repeated). A gift office's audited books carry a multi-year pledge below its sum and let it climb to the sum, and donors read the climb as part of the gift.**
  - **The climb:** "Subsequent accruals of the interest element shall be accounted for as contribution income by donees and contribution expense by donors" (FAS 116 para 20). Unless fair value is elected, the discount "is not revised to reflect market conditions at each measurement date. Instead, it is amortized over the receivable's scheduled term, typically using the interest method" (PwC). A textbook pledge of $10,000 due in five years at 3% is carried at $8,629.09, then $8,887.96, ... $10,000 (Antle and Garstka).
  - **How donors read it:** FASB first proposed calling the climb interest. Respondents said "the interest element is a component of the contribution or that donors perceive it as part of their contribution", and FASB agreed it was "likely to result in more understandable reporting" (FAS 116 para 115).
  - **Limit:** a pledge's path is fixed when it is booked; a market value moves with rates. Whose books, and when, depends on the residency's form, which is outside the case (Q213 row 14).
  - **Judge's note:** in 2031 a US recipient's books would carry her $115,000 letter, due January 2033, below $115,000 too, and for the same reason as the statement: the money is due later. Held at the top is worth less than the top, and so is the promise.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 4 (with rows 1-3), 3d's lead-in and the aha register.** A US savings bond bought at 0.10% shows about half its promised sum six months before its date, and the Treasury adds the rest on the day; the building's money shows 97% of the top on the move day. It turns "below the top" from a worry into the most ordinary thing a government bond does.
2. **Rows 6-7 (with row 12), 3d and Table 3's statement line.** In 1935 the Treasury printed on each bond's face what it was worth every six months until it paid its sum; school districts still print such tables. The page beside the broker's statement (8y "expected statement on later dates") has a ninety-year pedigree.
3. **Rows 28-29, 3d's words and WORDS.** Regulators made brokers stop showing a steady promise-like $10 where the value had fallen. It backs C11 and C17 from outside: the statement shows the day's market value, and $115,000 is never put in its place.
4. **Row 27, C11's one fact.** FINRA's own required sentence says a distribution "reduces the estimated per share value shown on your account statement". It is the regulator's model for why the building's money is the lots plus the distributions they have paid.
5. **Rows 31-32 (with row 39), 3d and the aha register.** Under US GAAP her letter itself would be carried below $115,000 in 2031 and climb to it, and FASB's respondents said donors see the climb as part of the gift. Held at the top is worth less than the top, and so is the promise.

## Gaps

- No real broker's statement disclosure for an ETF line was read (whether it shows the closing price or net asset value). The New Jersey-hosted guide (row 22) says listed securities show the closing price; its authorship is not confirmed.
- TreasuryDirect's own display of marketable Treasuries held there (par only, or a market value too) was not found at source; FS Publication 009 lists "par, interest, and refund payments" but no valuation.
- IFRS 9's treatment of open-ended fund units (whether they fail the payments-of-principal-and-interest test and so sit at fair value) was not read at source.
- No study was found of how funders or gift officers react to a statement value below a pledged sum. The nearest evidence is FAS 116 para 115 (row 32) and FINRA's REIT history (rows 28-30). Store H7-064 holds the reference-point argument.
- How Taiwanese brokers show bond funds on statements, and how Taiwanese gift offices book multi-year pledges, was not read.
- The iShares case study's table of matured funds (initial and final NAV) could not be matched to fund names in the extract read.
- The Duluth (row 14), Kishwaukee and 1942 circular texts were read only as extracts of the documents.
