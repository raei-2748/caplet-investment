- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Laura's 2028 deposit is a sponsor contribution, and pension law calls the risk that the sponsor's income falls just when the fund needs it a covenant risk, assessed with the scheme's own risk." This is the institutional form of IPS l.9.
  2. "Regulators prefer short recovery plans; the plan's top-up is a one-year recovery plan, and the extra Treasuries are bought, not promised."
  3. "The IPS's first reason to change, a missed deposit (nothing received by 31 March 2028), is what pension law treats as a sponsor's failure to pay."
- **Problem class:** funding a fixed promise from a sponsor whose own income is risky and can fall in the same downturn that lowers the fund. **Field that solved it first:** pension regulation and actuarial practice: US minimum funding law (shortfall amortisation and the waiver for "temporary substantial business hardship", 26 U.S.C. 412 and 430), the UK Pensions Regulator's employer covenant regime (codes of 2014 and 2024, integrated risk management guidance of 2015), and the Dutch FTK recovery plans (2007, Pensioenwet art. 138-140). The failures that taught it: Bethlehem Steel, United Airlines and the steel and airline claims on the PBGC in 2002-2009; US states' contribution shortfalls in the Great Recession.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE the words; KEEP the idea.**
  - **The regulator's words are "double-up" and "concentration of risk", not "covenant risk".** TPR's integrated risk management guidance (Dec 2015), Example 8: a house builder's scheme holding property "could 'double-up' the risks associated with property markets". "If the property market went into decline it is possible that the scheme's funding level would decrease at a time when the strength of the employer covenant is weakening and the availability of additional contributions to the scheme becomes limited." The 2014 code (para 109): "general economic changes which can affect both the scheme and the employer covenant".
  - **The 2024 code never says "correlation".** It reaches the same risk through stress tests: enough employer cash "to recover both the existing deficit (if any) and any further deficit that could arise from a scheme-related stress event" (journey planning para 13), with "a downside event with a probability of one-in-six" as the minimum (Annual Funding Statement 2025). Where the code does say "covenant risk" (employer covenant para 12), it means funding falling or "an unexpected employer insolvency event".
  - **The remedy matches IPS l.9.** In Example 8's second scenario, the employer's fortunes ride on emerging markets, so the trustees cut the scheme's emerging-market holdings: "managed by diversification of scheme assets". IPS l.9 makes the same move for a writer: "your portfolio should be a counterbalance to that – definitely not a contributor".
  - **"Sponsor contribution": like in shape, unlike in law.** A UK covenant is a legal obligation to the scheme. TPR: "You should be cautious about any reliance on entities without such an obligation. No additional funding or investment risk is expected to be taken based on informal support." Laura owes her portfolio nothing in law. The case only says she "will contribute an additional $150,000 at the beginning of 2028" (case l.43-45). By TPR's test the deposit is informal support. The plan states nothing that rests on it before it arrives: the floor and the top are stated only once bought.
  - **Judge's note:** IPS l.9 is the regulator's own textbook example in a writer's clothes. A house builder whose pension fund holds property "doubles up"; a writer whose portfolio held shares would too.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE in three parts.**
  - **"Regulators prefer short recovery plans": half true.** UK law since 2024 says a deficit's recovery period "must be as soon as the employer can reasonably afford" (SI 2024/462, reg 20(4)(a)). That is speed tied to affordability, with no maximum length. In a downturn that hits sponsors and funds together, regulators give time:
    - UK: the mean recovery plan was 9.7 years for valuations of Sep 2008-Sep 2009 (tranche 4), against 8.1 in tranche 1 and 4.4 in tranche 18. 34.7% of tranche-4 plans ran 10 years or more.
    - UK 2020: "trustees should be open to requests to reduce or suspend DRCs".
    - Netherlands 2009: the short recovery period went from 3 years to 5 "als gevolg van de scherpe koersval op de beurzen en de lage rentestand" (because of the sharp fall in share prices and low interest rates). 2020: from 10 years to 12.
    - US: 7-year amortisation (Pension Protection Act 2006) became 2+7 or 15 years for 2008-2011 (2010 Act), then 15 years for every plan from 2022 (American Rescue Plan Act 2021), with old shortfalls reset to zero.
    - US waivers: "temporary substantial business hardship", where one test is whether "the sales and profits of the industry concerned are depressed or declining" (26 U.S.C. 412(c)(2)(C)).
    - **And the weaker the sponsor, the longer the plan leans on it.** UK mean plan length, weak covenant against strong: 10.9 against 6.4 years (tranche 7), 5.5 against 3.3 (tranche 18).
  - **"The top-up is a one-year recovery plan": KEEP for the $2,388 only.** On a pension-style measure (today's forward rates) the $300,000 buys 99.2% of the ten's $302,287 (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 2). The $2,287 of IBTM left unbought in January 2027 is finished by the top-up, $2,388, in January 2028: one payment, 12 months later (NUMBERS section 1, build order; section 0: $2,286 at net asset value). UK schemes 80-90% funded had recovery plans averaging 4.6 years (tranche 18).
  - **The $35,894 is not a recovery plan on any pension measure. CHANGE.** At forward rates nothing is left to recover once the top-up is paid. The extra Treasuries are the report's answer to the case's ask to size the operating reserve, held in Treasuries as IPS l.11 says, so the ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing. In pension terms that is a margin in the funding standard, not a deficit repaid. Never put them inside IPS l.13's step.
  - **"Bought, not promised": KEEP, dated.** From January 2028 the top-up and the extra Treasuries are owned. Before then all $38,282 rests on the deposit: 25.5% of it, for 12 months (NUMBERS 5). TPR says why buying matters: an employer's "contingent funding mechanism ... does not support additional risk as it does not add additional covenant support". A promise from the same sponsor to pay more later adds nothing.
  - **Judge's note:** UK pension law gives the weakest sponsors the longest recovery plans. Laura's plan does the opposite. Her income can shrink with the markets, so it leans on her for one deposit, 12 months after the first, and spends that deposit's first dollars on the payments.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Like a missed contribution in its trigger; unlike it in its remedy. Closer to a sponsor's exit.**
  - **In law a missed contribution is a debt.** UK: the amount unpaid "shall be treated as such a debt" from the employer to the trustees, reported to the Regulator and members "within a reasonable period" if material (Pensions Act 2004 s.228). US: once over $1,000,000 is unpaid, "there shall be a lien in favor of the plan" on all the sponsor's and its group's property, enforced "only by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation" (26 U.S.C. 430(k)). The promise survives the miss, and the plan becomes a creditor.
  - **Laura's plan has no creditor and no debt.** Apart from the two deposits, "she will neither add to nor withdraw from the portfolio before 2033" (case l.62-64). A missed deposit ends her support for good, like a sponsor's insolvency (Studebaker, store Q2a-014; Bethlehem Steel, row 28). The IPS's answer is to change the plan, not to collect.
  - **What it costs (NUMBERS 5):** the 2033 payment $3,016 short at forward rates, the ten $57,455 short if cash waiting earns nothing, and no floor and no VT.
  - **A smaller deposit is not a reason to change** (NUMBERS 5): the plan absorbs it. US law likewise does not count an approved hardship waiver as a failure to pay.
  - **The same-downturn case, from pension history:** Bethlehem Steel's plan went from 97% funded (1999) to 45% at termination (Dec 2002). The stock market fell (the S&P 500 returned -38%), interest rates fell, and "sponsor contributions were not sufficient to offset the changes" (GAO).
  - **Laura's version is sized in NUMBERS 5.** At today's yields the payments' standard survives a 74.5% smaller deposit. After a 1-point fall in yields before January 2027 it survives 56.4%; after 2 points, 36.5%.
  - **Judge's note:** in pension law a missed contribution is a debt someone can collect. In Laura's plan it is just a fact, because no one can collect from her. So the plan is finished with money she already owns, and the deposit's first dollars go to the payments.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 1 (with row 2): TPR's "double-up" example.** 3a or 2.0. IPS l.9's career-as-shares idea is the pension regulator's own textbook case (a house builder's fund holding property), and the regulator's fix is the IPS's: diversify away from the sponsor's driver. It gives the IPS's thesis an institutional pedigree in one clause.
2. **Row 40 (with rows 18, 24, 35): when one downturn hits sponsor and fund, regulators give time.** 3a. UK recovery plans averaged 9.7 years in 2008-09; the US and the Dutch lengthened theirs too. Laura's payment dates cannot move and no money comes after 2028, so the plan has no time to give. That is why it owns the payments before it states anything.
3. **Row 19: in pension practice the weakest sponsors get the longest plans** (5.5 against 3.3 years in the latest tranche). 3a or 2.3. The plan reverses it: one deposit, 12 months, first dollars to the payments. A sharp "a smart reader had it backwards" point.
4. **Row 28 (with row 42): Bethlehem Steel, 97% to 45% funded in three years** as shares fell, rates fell and the sponsor stopped paying. 3e areas (2) and (3). It is the real version of the risk NUMBERS 5 sizes for Laura: a recession that shrinks the deposit while yields fall.
5. **Row 43 (with rows 26-27): a missed contribution is a debt; a missed deposit is a fact.** Table 3 and 3e. It shows why reason 1 changes the plan rather than chasing money, and why "nothing received by 31 March 2028" is the right line.
