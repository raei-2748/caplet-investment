- **Claims tested:**

  2. "Archives demand permanent ink for records meant to last, which fits ink as what is owned."
  3. "In law, ink usually means signed or decided, the reading the conceit must avoid": which legal parallels push a reader toward "signed"?
  4. "Taiwan's rules for official documents and court filings require ink."
- **Problem class:** reading which marks on one page are final when final and provisional marks sit side by side, and making a record last until it is needed. **Field that solved it first:** the English ecclesiastical probate courts for the first (Hawkes v Hawkes and Edwards v Astley, 1828: a pencil change beside ink is presumed a draft); archival science and its standards for the second (ISO 11798; the US and UK national archives' reading-room rules).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The law treats pencil as writing. It reads pencil *beside ink on the same page* as a sign of a draft, and only as a presumption that evidence can overturn.
  - **Pencil alone is valid.** "There is no authority for saying that where the law requires a contract to be in writing, that writing must be in ink" (Geary v Physic, 1826, a pencil indorsement on a note). Under the Wills Act 1837 "a will may be written and signed in pencil" (Hayes and Jarman). In 2014 a Scottish court upheld a will written and signed wholly in pencil (Jollie v Lennie, in Mason). Pennsylvania (1877): a will "wholly written and signed with lead pencil" was valid.
  - **Pencil beside ink is presumed a draft, in England.** The textbook rule: "where alterations in pencil only are made, they are deliberative; when in ink, they are final and absolute" (Hawkes v Hawkes, 1828; Edwards v Astley, 1828; in Jarman, 1893). In the Goods of Adams (1872) is the case the conceit wants. A printed will form had its blanks filled partly in ink and partly in pencil, with ink written over some pencil and some pencil rubbed out. Lord Penzance held "the writing in ink is the will, and not the pencil writing". The ink was "capable of being read straight through" with the printed words, and probate was granted without the pencil.
  - **It can be overturned.** "All the cases show that the signing in pencil affords a prima facie presumption that the act is only deliberative; yet it may be shown to be otherwise" (Lord Brougham, Bateman v Pennington, 1840; probate granted).
  - **Australia and the US.** Australian practitioners still apply it: "Pencil alterations are generally disregarded as being merely deliberative" (NSW, 2020). The US split. New Hampshire (1889) followed Jarman. Pennsylvania (Tomlinson's Estate, 1890) rejected the presumption, and West Virginia (1908) called it "so foreclosed by authority that but brief reference to it is merited", adding: "Such is the American authority."
  - **Say:** "an English court once read only the ink". **Never:** "the law treats pencil as provisional".
  - **Judge's note:** in 1872 an English court ran the report's deletion test. It set aside the pencil and found the will "capable of being read straight through" in ink.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** It is half true, and the true half is stronger than the claim.
  - **Archives do want lasting marks on the record.** ISO 11798 covers records kept "for long periods of time". It finds "carbon black ink" "consistently reliable" and warns of "acidic inks".
  - **But archives ban ink beside the record.** US federal rule: "You must use only pencils in research rooms where original documents are used" (36 CFR 1254.36(a)). NARA: "Ink poses a serious risk to original records". The UK National Archives allows "Only graphite pencils (without erasers)" (Rules, r. 20).
  - **Most everyday ink fails the test.** Most felt-tip and ballpoint pens "do not meet archival specifications" (NARA).
  - **So ink is kept for the record and pencil for everyone else.** That is exactly the conceit's split: ink for what Laura owns; every model in the report is a reader's pencil note beside it.
  - **The permanence standard also separates lasting from signed.** ISO 11798 does not apply to "legal documents, e.g. banking documents, where the authenticity is of primary interest".
  - **Judge's note:** the archive is the one place that bans ink. Every note made beside an original record must be in pencil, because "Inked notations (and accidental markings) are often permanent and cannot be removed" (NARA).
- **Claim 3: KEEP, as a disanalogy with a list.**
  - **The law's ink is mostly a signature.** UK law calls it a "'wet' signature" (2026 regulations). The clergy are told to use "permanent black ink ... when signing".
  - **The probate presumption reads pencil as "deliberative", meaning not yet decided.** The plan's pencil is decided but not yet owned: the 2028 order and the 2031 move are fixed in the frozen IPS (l.13, l.15). A probate turn would therefore tell a judge that the top is undecided and that the IPS is a draft (guard 3).
  - **Never as turns** (row 37): Bateman v Pennington, Geary v Physic, Kleine v Kleine, US v Thompson, Lucas v James, "wet ink", the clergy's signing ink, cheque ink, and court ink rules.
  - **Usable, because they point to "owned" or "lasting to its date"** (row 38): Adams's ink read straight through; Australia's "without more" test, with "read and signed ... is not determinative"; ISO 11798's split of permanence from authenticity; ISO 12757-2's rule that the paper fails before the line; the archives' pencil-only rooms.
  - **Judge's note:** Australian courts ask whether a paper would operate "without more" and hold that signing "is not determinative". The same move the conceit makes from "signed" to "owned" is already in the law of the team's own country.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE.**
  - **The courts:** a handwritten civil pleading must be "以黑色或藍色墨水書寫" (in black or blue ink) unless the court agrees (民事訴訟書狀規則 art. 3, amended 29 Aug 2025).
  - **The Executive Yuan, 2023:** erasable friction pens, whose writing can be "rubbed away" and whose ink changes colour with heat, are unfit for signing official papers.
  - **But the Executive Yuan's document manual is silent on pencil.** Asked in 2024 whether officials may annotate originals in pencil, it answered "本手冊並未規定" (the manual does not say).
  - **Judge's note:** in the residency's own country the cabinet ruled that ink which can be rubbed away is not ink fit to sign with. That is the reason Laura names no top before the move.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 1, In the Goods of Adams (3e).** In 1872 an English court read a will in ink alone and found it "capable of being read straight through". It is the deletion test (shares at zero: the ten unchanged, the range one figure, $95,000) carried out by a real court, so a lawyer-judge meets it as fact, not image.
2. **Row 20, ISO 12757-2 (3e).** Documentary ink is defined as a line that cannot vanish before the paper shows damage. It gives V2.2's "a US default is not a smudge: it is the paper" a standard behind it, from a field no finance reader expects.
3. **Rows 21 and 24 with 39, the archives' pencil-only rooms (3c, 3e).** The judge expects archives to demand ink; they ban it beside the record. Every model in the report is a reader's pencil note beside what Laura owns, and can be wrong without marking it.
4. **Rows 16-17, "without more" and "read and signed ... not determinative" (2.0).** These defend the definition against the "signed" misreading with the law's own move. Ink is a figure that will be paid with nothing more to happen. That is why the bottom waits for January 2028 and the top for the move.
5. **Row 29, the Executive Yuan's friction-pen ruling (3c).** Taiwan's cabinet ruled that ink which heat can rub away is unfit to sign with. A top stated before the move is that ink: it looks final and could vanish in about 11 of 100 futures. Use it once, for "ink that can vanish is not ink", never for signing.

## Gaps

- **In the Goods of Adams** was read in a reproduction of the ICLR report (Scribd) and in two treatises. The Law Reports volume itself was not opened.
- **Original reports not read.** Hawkes v Hawkes, Edwards v Astley, Bateman v Pennington and Dickinson v Dickinson were read only as quoted in treatises and later cases. Estate of Tomlinson, 133 Pa. 245 (1890), was read only as quoted in LaRue v Lee and Heller Estate; its CourtListener page, https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6481643/estate-of-tomlinson/, would not load.
- **Williams.** Williams on Wills was not read. Adams's counsel cites "Williams' Executors"; one scanned edition's margin note was seen in a search excerpt only.
- **No Australian pencil case read at source.** Perpetual Trustees (WA) v Gailey [1999] WASC 61 (unwitnessed handwritten amendments) was found but not opened, and the cases in Bartier Perry's footnote 16 were not traced.
- **Read only in Mason's summary:** Jollie v Lennie [2014] CSOH 45, Co-operative Bank v Tipper [1996], US v Thompson (1823) and Lucas v James (1849). The publication year of Mason's book and of Bigelow's treatise was not confirmed.
- **The slate will.** Jarman's American notes say "a will written on a slate cannot be admitted to probate as a written will. Reed v. ...". The case name was cut off and the case was not found. It would test the line between pencil (valid) and an erasable medium (not valid).
- **ISO bodies not read.** For ISO 11798 and ISO 12757-2 only the scope, introduction and requirements were read: the test methods were not, nor whether ISO 12757-2 has a later edition. ISO 11798's third edition (under publication, Nov 2026) was not seen.
- **Taiwan.** Section 拾 of the 文書處理手冊 (112年9月 edition), "文書用具及處理標準", was not read because the fetch budget ran out. 公證法施行細則 (notarial deeds) and household-registration form rules were not checked for ink requirements.
- **IRS Publication 1141.** The sentence after "because SSA scanners read" was truncated, and SSA's own explanation of dropout ink was not found.
- **Banks.** No primary bank rule refusing cheques written in pencil was found; only regulators' fraud guidance (row 34).
- **Archives.** No UK National Archives guidance on what ink records must be *created* in was found; the Kew rules cover readers only.
- **Tools.** Firecrawl scrape credits ran out early, the shared WebFetch budget was exhausted, and Parallel was rate-limited once. Several pages were therefore read through search-index excerpts of the page itself, each marked so.
