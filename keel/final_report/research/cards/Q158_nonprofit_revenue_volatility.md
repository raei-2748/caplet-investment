- **Problem class and the field that met it first:** revenue risk in an organisation that cannot sell shares or raise taxes: how much each income source swings, how the swings move together, and what a safe source is worth. Finance met it first (Markowitz's portfolio selection, 1952). Kingma (1993) carried it into nonprofit revenue. Corporate failure prediction (Altman 1968) gave nonprofit research its warning ratios: Tuckman and Chang (1991), then Greenlee and Trussel (2000). Public finance's rainy-day funds are the government cousin (Searing and St. Clair 2025 point to them).

## Verdicts in one screen

**Claim 1. KEEP "can fall in the same downturn", and name what falls.**
- **US donations move with the economy.** Over 1990-2017, donations correlated 0.68 with real GDP, program revenue -0.40, and all nonprofit revenue -0.04. Arts, culture and humanities revenue correlated 0.51, the third most procyclical subsector (Searing and St. Clair 2025, row 11).
- **Income sources can fall at once.** In 2008 "multiple revenue sources such as government funding, private donations, and investment income declined simultaneously" (Choi 2025, row 10).
- **Diversification stopped working when it was needed.** It cut revenue swings in normal years, but "this effect did not persist during and after the Recession" (row 8).
- **US public arts money is cut in both kinds of shock, and late.** State arts budgets fell 6% in FY2009 and 17.9% in FY2021 (NASAA, row 37).
- **Taiwan is different.** Public money held or rose:
  - In 2009 the flagship troupe grant doubled to NT$232.45m (row 41).
  - In 2020 the National Performing Arts Center's government money rose 7.8% while its own income fell 44.3% (row 44; Keel arithmetic).
  - So in Taiwan, "can fall" points at companies, ticket income and untied gifts (Q50), never at the government (CANON B4).
- **Judge's note:** the sector's standard defence, many income sources, failed in exactly the year it was needed. A source that cannot fall is worth more than a spread of sources that can all fall at once.

**Claim 2. CHANGE: "grants fall in recessions" is wrong in general, and "worth more than its size" has no number.**
- **Grants did not fall everywhere.** In US 990 data for 2007-09, more government grants lowered revenue swings (-0.321 points) and more investment income raised them most (+1.431 points) (Choi 2025, row 9).
- **In 2020 US arts gifts rose.** Unrestricted contributed revenue was +3% after inflation, and government money +12% from relief. Earned revenue collapsed (SMU DataArts, row 34).
- **In Taiwan in 2020 earned income fell, not grants.** Ticket sales fell 33.8% (row 43). The national centre's performance income fell 50.5% while its state money rose (row 44).
- **What is true.** Gifts and earned income swing, and the arts swing more than other fields (rows 6, 11, 36, 38). Swings predict closure: a 10% rise in revenue volatility predicts 7-14% more risk of dissolving (D. Mayer 2023, row 14).
- **What predictable money buys.** Leaders with multiyear operating money named the ability to plan (66%), time to focus on their work (30%) and the capacity to invest in staff (26%) (CEP 2020, row 16). In 2020, groups with reserves cut hours and staff less (row 15).
- **No study prices a zero-volatility stream in dollars.** The truest form of the claim: "running money that cannot stop is worth more to a residency than a gift of the same size that can". Never give a multiplier.
- **Disanalogies to keep.**
  - The sum is fixed in dollars: at 2.4% inflation the 2042 payment buys 19.2% less than the 2033 one (NUMBERS 8d, Keel arithmetic), and arts costs tend to rise faster than prices (row 39).
  - A secure grant can make a charity fundraise less: about 75% crowd-out, mostly through cut fundraising (row 19).
  - The payments end in 2042 (row 45).
- **Judge's note:** in Taiwan in 2020 the troupes kept safe were the ones whose budgets were fixed the year before. Their own ticket money had "no impact on their annual budgets" (Ji 2022, row 42). Laura's payments are bought in January 2027, six years before the first falls due.

**Claim 3. CHANGE: one steady source is not the danger; one source that can stop is.**
- **The two meta-analyses find little.** One finds a "small, positive" link between diversification and financial health (Hung and Hager 2019, row 24). The other finds "little effect on financial vulnerability" and a slightly negative effect on capacity (Lu, Lin and Wang 2019, row 25).
- **Concentration is safe when the main source is steady.** It goes with lower risk for groups that rely on government grants or earned income (Qu 2019, row 26). Nine in ten US nonprofits that reach $50m take 90% from one source (Foster, row 5).
- **The "one main source" ratio failed its first prediction test** (Greenlee and Trussel 2000, row 30). This matches Q140 row 13: "dependence on grant funding, not overall concentration".
- **The field's own goal is predictability, not a count of sources** (Kingma 1993, row 28; Saerim Kim 2023, row 27).
- **Truest form:** Laura's owned payments are the steady centre the residency builds around. They do not make its income "diversified".
- **Judge's note:** the slogan "diversify your revenue" has a small effect in the field's own meta-analyses. What protects a nonprofit is a main source that cannot be withdrawn.

**Claim 4. CHANGE: NORI's figure is a minimum of three months, not "three to six".**
- **What the white paper says.** It suggests a minimum of "25 percent or 3 months of the annual expense budget" at the lowest point of the year, and says "there is no one size fits all" (row 32). The 3-6 months is Propel's (store Q16-038).
- **The aha is in NORI's appendix (row 33).** A group without a reserve, for example one "new and trying to reach a sustainable size", should "define in writing this 'Plan B' which names sources (to include: held assets, organizations and individuals)". Laura's plan is that Plan B, named and dated, held by an individual.
- **Judge's note:** the reserve standard funders know has a written exception for a new organisation whose fallback is an individual's held assets. That describes Laura's residency, except that her fallback is bought before the residency opens.

## Five questions for the strong rows

**Row 8 (Choi 2025): diversification failed in the 2008 recession.**
- **What is it?** A nine-year panel of US nonprofits' tax returns (2004-2012), testing whether spreading income across sources kept revenue steady before, during and after the Great Recession.
- **Did it work?** In normal years, yes. In the recession the protection vanished: "this effect did not persist during and after the Recession", and with the recession dated 2008-09 diversification slightly raised volatility (+0.108 points).
- **How Laura's case is like it.** A residency funded by co-sponsors, gifts and fees has exactly the spread this study tested, and the IPS (l.9) names the same bad years.
- **How it is not.** It covers US nonprofits and one recession. A residency in Taiwan faces a government that held its arts money in 2009 and 2020 (rows 41, 44).
- **What Caplet takes.** One clause for 3d: spreading money across funders protects in ordinary years, and in a bad one several can fall at once, which is why the ten payments rest on Treasuries rather than on anybody else's money (IPS l.11).

**Row 33 (NORI's written Plan B).**
- **What is it?** The reserve guidance US funders know: hold at least three months, unless the group is new and names in writing a Plan B, "held assets, organizations and individuals".
- **Did it work?** It is guidance. Nearly half of the 3,154 DC nonprofits it describes were below its minimum (row 32).
- **How Laura's case is like it.** A new residency, a named individual, held assets, written down: the IPS is that Plan B, dated and paid in advance.
- **How it is not.** NORI pictures a fallback for emergencies. Laura's Treasuries are the running money itself, paid on a schedule (Q135 row 33).
- **What Caplet takes.** A sentence for 3a: what funders call an operating reserve is three months at least, and Laura's is ten years of her own share, bought before the doors open.

**Row 42 (Ji 2022: Taiwan's budgets fixed a year ahead).**
- **What is it?** A Taiwanese theatre scholar's account of COVID-19 and the performing arts in Taiwan.
- **Did it work?** Public troupes whose budgets were set the year before were "relatively free from the effects of the pandemic", while ticket sales across Taiwan fell a third (row 43).
- **How Laura's case is like it.** Her residency's $50,000 is fixed in advance and does not depend on this year's tickets, gifts or shares.
- **How it is not.** Those troupes are state bodies whose budgets the legislature can still cut (in 2025 it made its largest cut and freeze to the Ministry of Culture's budget, Q10d). Laura's payments are owned, not voted.
- **What Caplet takes.** Taiwan's own evidence that money fixed before the year begins is what kept arts groups running in 2020. It is a Taiwanese fact for 3d, not an American one.

**Row 18 (a foundation stepped back from multiyear grants in the downturn).**
- **What is it?** CEP's 2026 account of a small US foundation's multiyear grant pilot.
- **Did it work?** The foundation withdrew from long commitments in the 2008-09 downturn "when long-term commitments felt riskier", and came back years later.
- **How Laura's case is like it.** A co-sponsor's multi-year promise is a promise that can be withdrawn in a downturn. Laura's ten are owned, not promised.
- **How it is not.** It is one foundation, told as an anecdote. Q50 found Taiwan's big named sponsors gave more in 2008-09.
- **What Caplet takes.** For 3d, said once: other funders' long promises are hardest to keep in a downturn, and Laura's share does not need keeping, because it is already owned.

**Row 19 (Andreoni and Payne 2011: secure money can make a charity fundraise less).**
- **What is it?** A panel of 8,000+ US charities: government grants crowded out about 75% of private giving, mostly because the charities cut their own fundraising.
- **Did it work?** It is a measured effect: a $10,000 grant cut fundraising by $1,410 and gifts by $7,570.
- **How Laura's case is like it.** A residency with ten years of running money secured could relax its fundraising.
- **How it is not.** A founder's gift is not a government grant. Field data on crowd-out are mixed (+$0.06 per $1, row 20), and one funder's money can signal quality and draw others in (rows 21, 22).
- **What Caplet takes.** An honest limit for 3e: owned running money is not a reason to stop raising money. The paper's own fix is a match, which the store's matching rows already support (Q11a, Q25).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 8 (3d, 3e).** In the 2008 recession, diversification stopped protecting US nonprofits. This is the strongest evidence for why the ten payments rest on Treasuries, not on spreading money across funders.
2. **Row 42 with rows 43-44 (3d, CANON B4).** In Taiwan in 2020, the groups kept safe had budgets fixed the year before. Ticket income fell a third while the state's money rose. It is Taiwanese evidence for Laura's design, and it keeps the report from saying Taiwan's government cut arts money.
3. **Row 33 (3a).** NORI's written exception is a "Plan B" of held assets named by an individual, for a new organisation. Laura's plan is that exception, bought before the residency opens. It connects the case's term "operating reserve" to the funders' own standard.
4. **Row 16 (3d).** Predictable multi-year money lets a group plan (66%) and invest in staff (26%). "It is incredibly difficult to commit to hiring employees with only 12 months' worth of funding guaranteed." This is what owned payments let the residency do, in a director's own words.
5. **Row 11 with row 14 (3d).** Arts revenue moves with GDP (0.51), and revenue swings predict closure (+10% volatility, +7-14% dissolution risk). Together they explain why a stream that cannot swing is worth more to a residency than its size suggests, without a made-up multiplier.

## Gaps

- **Carroll and Stater (2009) full text.** The coefficients and the 1993-2003 impact table were not read. OUP is paywalled, and the author's ResearchGate PDF returned HTTP 400. The sample size comes from SSIR and M. Kim (2014) only.
- **W. J. Mayer et al. (2014).** The prompt asked for numbers by source. Only the abstract was read (Sage paywall). The next round should find a working-paper version.
- **Tuckman and Chang (1991), Greenlee and Trussel (2000), Kingma (1993).** None was read at source. All three rest on secondary accounts, and the accounts disagree on Greenlee and Trussel's definition of distress (row 30). Hager (2001) was read at source by Q140 and was not re-read.
- **M. Kim (2017, NVSQ), the published version of row 6.** Not read. Neither was Heutel's (2014) published version, so whether his crowd-in holds mainly for young organisations was not checked.
- **SMU DataArts / NCAR swings by source in 2008-09.** Not found. Only 2020 was read (rows 34-35).
- **Asia.** No survey of revenue volatility by source for Taiwanese residencies or small arts nonprofits was found.
  - Not read: the Ministry of Culture research report at stat.moc.gov.tw (Research_Download idno=1095), the CNA article of 26 May 2020 on the NT$320m box-office loss, and the Hong Kong ADC Annual Arts Survey income tables (hkadc.org.hk). The WebFetch hourly budget ran out first.
  - Japan and Korea were not reached.
- **A price for a zero-volatility stream.** No source puts a dollar value on running money that cannot stop (for example, a certainty-equivalent for a nonprofit). The next round could try public finance on rainy-day funds and stabilisation, or the "cost of volatility" in nonprofit economics.
