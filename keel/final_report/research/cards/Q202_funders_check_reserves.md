## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. The funders' warning is that the building eats the running money; Laura's plan gives the building its own money so it cannot

- (a) A consultant writing for arts funders in the Grantmakers in the Arts journal (Oman, 2018) reports that even organisations "with well-laid plans" for working capital and early-years money "often find it difficult to protect these funds from covering cost overruns, fundraising shortfalls", and that trying to raise that money after the doors open "is often an exercise in futility — why give money if the project is complete?".
- (b) Laura's plan meets that warning by how it is built, not by a promise: the ten payments sit in Treasuries bought in January 2027, each maturing before its payment, and only a missed deposit or a Treasury default can change the plan (IPS l.13, l.17), while overruns have money of their own, the half of the shares "promised to no one" (IPS l.15). So a building overrun in 2032 can shrink her flexibility but cannot reach her running money.
- (c) It breaks because Oman is writing about the organisation's own reserves, which a programme officer reads in the residency's accounts, while Laura's Treasuries are held for her and appear nowhere in those accounts. The protection is therefore visible to a co-sponsor only if 3d states the ten payments as a written, dated commitment to the residency, and it protects her $50,000 a year, not the rest of the residency's running budget.
- **Decision it serves:** keeping the operating reserve apart from the facility money (IPS l.11, l.13, l.17) and giving overruns the half kept (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3a (one sentence on why the two pots are separate); 3d (the written commitment).
- **Source:** Oman (2018), read at source; IPS l.11-17.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Matching promised money with Treasuries is pedigree (liability-driven investing); that arts funders name the building eating the running money as the usual way early-years money dies, and that the plan's split answers exactly that, is not something a finance judge would already know.

### Idea 2. What builders ran short of in the 2008 crash was running money, and what they asked for was a bridge from the building campaign to the yearly budget

- (a) Late in 2008 the Kresge Foundation surveyed the organisations it was helping to finish building campaigns: "approximately two-thirds" said they did "not have adequate operational reserves to buffer the anticipated impacts of the economic crisis", and the commonest request for their operations was "operating support" or "bridge support between the capital campaign and the annual fund" (Kresge, 2008).
- (b) That bridge, the first years after the building money is spent and before yearly giving has settled, is when Laura's payments fall due, from January 2033, and her part of it is owned from January 2027, four years before a 2031 downturn could reach the co-sponsor meetings. This is the funder-side reason that owning the ten years ahead matters: a crash during the campaign cannot make her part of the bridge late or smaller.
- (c) It breaks in three places. The survey is self-reported, covers Kresge's grantees in every field with no counts published, and asks about cover for a crisis rather than ordinary running; and Laura's part is a fixed $50,000 a year, so a crash can still cut the co-sponsors' programme money that makes up the rest of each year. Her base removes one uncertainty from the residency's running budget, not the residency's exposure to a downturn.
- **Decision it serves:** the ten payments bought first, in January 2027, completed from the 2028 deposit if needed (IPS l.13).
- **Report part:** 3d (one clause on why she bought her running money years before asking for anyone else's); 3a.
- **Source:** Kresge Foundation (2008), read at source; NUMBERS 5 (the order of purchase).
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche funder evidence; the "bridge" is the aha, because it names the gap her payments fill in the funders' own words.

### Idea 3. The lab result was about the cost of asking, not the cost of running, so it does not belong in 3d; funders' rules carry its point instead

- (a) In Gneezy, Keenan and Gneezy's field test, 40,000 past US donors to similar causes got a letter for a $20,000 education project, and the letter saying a donor had given "$10,000 to cover all the overhead costs associated with raising the needed donations" got 8.55% of them to give, against 4.75% when the same gift was called seed money, while the average gift stayed at about $27 (Keel arithmetic).
- (b) So the result shows that small donors give more often when someone else has paid the cost of asking ("administrative and fundraising costs", in the paper's words). Laura's payments are not that cost, and her co-sponsors are institutions also asked for yearly programme money, so the store's line "told a lead donor had already paid the running costs" (Q150-018, SD-2; Q22-029) misdescribes the experiment and cannot carry 3d.
- (c) The point the store wanted survives where funders write it down themselves. A capital funder such as the Mass Cultural Council will not pay "Regular operating activities of an organization" yet requires an applicant to show the project's "impact ... on the organization's future operations"; Taiwan's public budgets "most often" pay repairs rather than the running money museums need (Q100-028). A founder who brings dated running money pays part of what those rules leave out.
- **Decision it serves:** 3d's co-sponsor language (what the ten payments are called and why they matter to a funder).
- **Report part:** 3d. Gneezy goes to the reserves; the funders' rules replace it.
- **Source:** Gneezy, Keenan and Gneezy (2014), read at source; Mass Cultural Council (2026), read at source; Q100-028.
- **Pedigree or aha:** Gneezy is KNOWN (a *Science* paper). The finding here is a correction: both judges said the population does not transfer; the paper itself shows the object does not either.

### Idea 4. What $50,000 a year is to a residency: the case already says "toward", and real residencies give it a size

- (a) The case calls the ten payments "$50,000 toward the residency's operating expenses", names the need as "reliable support for its early operations", leaves "any operating support beyond Laura's commitment" to co-sponsors, grants and fees, and puts every year after 2042 outside its scope (case l.84-92).
- (b) Measured against real residencies at today's prices (Keel arithmetic at NT$32.768), $50,000 a year is about one and a half times the smallest Taiwanese residency contract found (Fangliao's F3, about NT$1.10m a year), about a third of Hsinchu's Railway Art Village (NT$4.0m-5.4m), a tenth of Taipei Artist Village in 2018 and 3.7% of Treasure Hill's 2026 plan; among US residencies it is 45% of the smallest (Hewnoaks) and 1-13% of the rest. For the "small, community-oriented space" the case describes, it is a base of a third to all of today's running costs, so "covers the decade" is false in general.
- (c) The NT$14m the attack cites is Hong-Gah Museum's yearly running cost in 2003, a free-entry museum paid for by its founder's earnings, not a residency, so it should leave the register as a yardstick, although the attack's conclusion holds without it. And because the payments are fixed while costs rise (at 2.4% a year the 2042 payment buys about 70% of today's $50,000, NUMBERS 8d), the base's share of a residency's costs falls through the decade, which is why the honest word is "base", fixed and owned, never "covers".
- **Decision it serves:** 3.0's governing sentence and 3d's description of the ten payments (INTENT's "a base, not the residency's whole budget").
- **Report part:** 3.0 and 3d (words only; one yardstick at most, "about a third of a small Taiwanese residency's running costs today"); 3e for the falling share.
- **Source:** case l.84-92; Q156-002, Q156-003, Q156-012, Q9-032, Q9c-015, H9-119, Q100-017; NUMBERS 8d.
- **Pedigree or aha:** not an aha; it is the honesty the campaign director asks for, with numbers behind it.

### Idea 5. Funders do not lack operating money; they lack operating money promised for years

- (a) Among the largest US foundations in 2023, unrestricted operating support was 32% of arts grant dollars and capital only 12% (Candid's data for Grantmakers in the Arts), yet in the Center for Effective Philanthropy's grantee data for the decade before 2020 only 12.4% of grants were both operating support and multi-year (Q55-018).
- (b) So "Laura gives the kind of money funders give least" is false of operating money and true of operating money fixed for ten years and already held. That term is also what Taiwan's own system lacks, since residency running money there is voted one year at a time (card Q156), and it is the fact to put before a co-sponsor.
- (c) It breaks because US foundations are not Taiwan's co-sponsors, and because the same data say the scarce co-sponsor ask is building money: capital was 12% of arts dollars in 2023, and large foundations' capital-campaign giving after 2008 "averaged little more than half of prerecession levels" (Oman, 2018). So 3d should ask co-sponsors for the building and for programmes, and show the running base as begun, never as finished.
- **Decision it serves:** 3d's ask (building and programme money from co-sponsors); 3.0's governing sentence.
- **Report part:** 3d.
- **Source:** Grantmakers in the Arts / Lawrence (n.d., 2023 data), read at source; Oman (2018), read at source; Q55-018.
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche; a half-aha, because it corrects a claim the store was close to making.

### Idea 6. The honest base is also the better fundraising line

- (a) Funders punish money that looks like too much as well as too little: a held reserve can create "the appearance that the organization has more money, and therefore less need" (Kresge, via Q22-038), Calabrese found funders cut support when reserves passed five times the operating budget (Q135-016), and the Mass Cultural Council requires "a demonstrated financial need for a grant".
- (b) "Covers the decade" would invite exactly the question "then why do you need us?", while "a base of $50,000 a year toward running costs, already held" shows strength and need at once: a third or less of a Hsinchu-sized budget leaves co-sponsors a plain, honest role.
- (c) It breaks because no reading of Laura's ten payments comes near Calabrese's five years of budget, so the risk sits in the wording, not the money; and because her Treasuries are not the residency's reserve at all, which is again why the written commitment of Idea 1 matters.
- **Decision it serves:** 3d's wording.
- **Report part:** 3d.
- **Source:** Q22-038, Q135-016; Mass Cultural Council (2026), read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for fundraisers; useful as the reason the campaign director's honesty costs Laura nothing.
