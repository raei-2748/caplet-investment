- **Problem class:** portfolio choice against dated liabilities, with a safety constraint on what is promised and a free surplus. **Field that solved it first:** actuarial science (insurers' ruin theory, which Roy cites in his own footnote), turned into portfolio theory by Roy (1952) in the same year as Markowitz. ## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with two finds the store lacks.**
  - **Roy's money standard.** Roy (1952) writes that, measured in money, "the price of money in terms of itself is clearly unity and the standard error of this estimate is zero". Laura's payments are fixed in dollars. So Roy's own yardstick makes the Treasury that pays those dollars on the date the asset with zero error.
  - **Roy's ten-year passage.** The same paper argues that a scheme "which will yield its fruit in ten years time" should not depend on short borrowing, because of "uncertainty as to the rates of interest ruling when we have to reborrow". That is the case for matching dates, made in 1952.
  - **Markowitz's place.** His 1952 rule ("expected return a desirable thing and variance of return an undesirable thing") is a one-period rule with no dated debts. In Caplet's plan it governs only VT. Chhabra (2005) gives MPT the same place: only the middle bucket "should be diversified as prescribed by MPT".
  - **Judge's note:** Markowitz himself wrote that "Roy can claim an equal share of this honor" (Markowitz 1999, quoted by Rubinstein 2002). The payments follow the other founder of portfolio theory, and the shares follow the first.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. BPT's layers, filled with the CAPM's two funds, for a reason BPT's investor ignores.**
  - **What BPT predicts.** Shefrin and Statman (2000) contrast portfolios "of CAPM investors", which "combine the market portfolio and the risk-free security", with BPT investors' portfolios, which "resemble combinations of bonds and lottery tickets". Their layered investors "act as if they overlook covariances".
  - **What Caplet holds.** Caplet keeps the layers: pay, promise, grow. It fills them with the CAPM's two funds: Treasuries matched to dates, and VT, a world market portfolio. Waring and Whitney (2009) give this exact pair a name: "a new risk-free asset, the investor's liability-matching asset portfolio", plus "a slightly redefined world market portfolio", forming "a new two-fund theorem".
  - **Why it holds them.** The reason for the layers is a covariance. Laura's income, her co-sponsors' budgets and share prices fall in the same recession (IPS).
  - **Chhabra's buckets.** Chhabra's (2005) aspirational bucket holds "a family-owned business that forms a significant part of an individual's wealth" and options "strongly correlated to human capital and source of income". Laura's career is that bucket. The portfolio adds none (Keel inference).
  - **Judge's note:** behavioural finance predicts a layered investor holds bonds plus lottery tickets and ignores how the layers move together. Caplet's layers hold the textbook's two funds, and exist because of how they move together.
- **Claim 3: KILL.**
  - **The sources' own rule.** Waring and Siegel (2015 working paper): a risk-free spending schedule needs "a ladder of risk-free bonds, inflation-protected bonds if protecting real consumption, nominal if protecting nominal consumption".
  - **Why the earlier designs use inflation-linked bonds.** Bodie (with Clowes, 2003; with Taqqu, 2011) and Sharpe's lockboxes ("Zero-coupon TIPS") protect a retiree's real spending. Laura's ten payments are fixed $50,000 sums in dollars. So the nominal Treasury is the matched asset (TNA note 1). TIPS are also not allowed this year.
  - **Where inflation does bite.** Roy counts inflation as a disaster ("to, say, 70 per cent of their former worth"). For Laura that risk falls on the building, priced in Taiwan, and the half kept is the cushion for it.
  - **Judge's note:** the famous safety-first designs use inflation-linked bonds because their clients' needs are real. Laura's are in dollars, and the rule its authors wrote says nominal for nominal.
- **Claim 4: CHANGE. The aim is Merton's, the lock is Zwecher's, and the rule is Kataoka's. It is all done by calendar, so it stays buy-and-hold (Q3a).**
  - **Merton (2014):** "takes as much risk out of the portfolio as possible once the goal is achieved, avoiding a scenario in which the saver achieves his goal only to fall below it".
  - **Zwecher, as Pfau summarises him (search excerpt, UNVERIFIED):** "at-risk flooring" (money that could buy the floor) becomes "locked-in flooring" (bought outright).
  - **Kataoka (via Shefrin and Statman):** maximise the subsistence level so the chance of falling below it "does not exceed a predetermined α". The range rule is Kataoka with α at zero on owned holdings. The bottom is what the floor's own Treasuries pay, cut and rounded down: $98,380 becomes $95,000 (NUMBERS 4).
  - **Where Caplet differs.** Merton's and Zwecher's versions run continuously on prices or funded status. Caplet's is one trade, half the shares, on a date written in 2026, before the co-sponsor meetings.
  - **Judge's note:** Caplet takes risk out once a figure is about to be promised, not once a price is reached. That turns a Nobel laureate's dynamic rule into a single trade written in advance.
- **Claim 5: CHANGE.**
  - **Kitces (2015):** even safety-first plans "Are Probability-Based". The real split is "risk transfer versus risk retention".
  - **The plan in Kitces's terms.** The payments' market risk is transferred to Treasuries in January 2027. The range's market risk is retained until 2031, then transferred. The IPS's two reasons name the only events that reopen the plan.
  - **What stays strong.** The strong word stays: the payments are guaranteed by the Treasuries that pay them.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 10 with row 42 (2.3 and the lineage exhibit).** Caplet keeps BPT's layers and fills them with the CAPM's two funds, which Waring and Whitney call the liability-matching asset plus the world market portfolio. A judge who expects "behavioural buckets" finds textbook contents, chosen for a covariance reason (row 11).
2. **Row 28 with row 13 (3c).** Merton's "takes as much risk out of the portfolio as possible once the goal is achieved" is the 2031 move. Kataoka's criterion with α at zero is the range rule. Caplet does both once, by date, so the plan stays buy-and-hold.
3. **Rows 1, 2 and 8 (2.0 or 2.3).** Roy (1952) gives the money standard (zero error for a promise in dollars) and the case for matching a ten-year plan's dates. Markowitz said Roy deserves "an equal share" of MPT's honour. One sentence places the plan in the founding year of portfolio theory.
4. **Row 15 (2.3, 3.0).** In Chhabra's framework, Laura's career is the aspirational bucket, so the portfolio holds only personal and market risk. This also settles the mapping dispute between store rows H3-027 and H7-099.
5. **Rows 37 and 40 (3a, 3e).** Sharpe's sealed lockbox per year is the ladder. "You still have the key" becomes Caplet's two reasons, the only keys written down. Runner-up: row 43 (Waring and Siegel, "nominal if protecting nominal consumption"), which settles the TIPS question in 3e.
