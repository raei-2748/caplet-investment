- **Claims tested:**
  1. "Section 3e's assumptions and limits read clearest when each is said as the one thing that would have to happen for a promise to fail (a reverse stress test), not as a list of inputs."
  2. "The plan has exactly the few failure conditions it claims: the IPS's two reasons (a missed 2028 deposit, a Treasury default) and the range's own condition (fund costs beyond the fees passing about 3% of value with cash waiting earning nothing)."
  3. "A reverse stress test would add other failure conditions for Laura: a fund ended or merged early, and a building-cost and Taiwan-dollar burst beyond her flexibility."
  4. "A US default belongs once in 3e because it is the one event common to every promise."
- **Problem class:** stating the limits of a promise as the conditions under which it fails, for readers who must rely on it. **Fields that solved it first:** reliability engineering (fault trees and minimal cut sets, Bell Labs 1961, codified for the US nuclear regulator in NUREG-0492, 1981); then bank and insurance supervision after 2008 (Basel Committee 2009, Principle 9; the UK FSA's SYSC 20 from 14 Dec 2010; IAIS ICP 16 from 2011; EBA 2018; the PRA's 2025 proposal for insurers); project management (Klein's premortem, 2007, on Mitchell, Russo and Pennington, 1989). US pension actuaries' ASOP 51 (2017) has no reverse test.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. The supervisors' form is two steps, and it replaces 3e's list of limits, not its list of assumptions.**
  - **The form.** "The ORSA report should define what constitutes business failure and then detail what events could drive that outcome" (PRA, 2025, draft supervisory statement on the ORSA, 8.2). The EBA: a reverse stress test "starts from the identification of the pre-defined outcome ... and then explores scenarios and circumstances that might cause this to occur" (EBA/GL/2018/04, definition 10). Basel: "start from a known stress test outcome ... and then asking what events could lead to such an outcome" (BCBS, 2009, Principle 9).
  - **Two more parts the rules add.** The likelihood of each circumstance (SYSC 20.2.1R(1): "assess the likelihood that such events could crystallise") and "the time that the firm would have to react" (SYSC 20.2.5G; PRA SS31/15 4.6). For Laura that is one clause per promise: the warning time and the Table 3 row that watches it.
  - **It can be said in words.** Reverse stress testing "may be largely qualitative in nature" (IAIS ICP 16.2.21, Dec 2024). That fits the density cut (only area (1) carries figures in 3e's prose).
  - **What it does not replace.** Wharton asks to "Clearly identify important assumptions, uncertainties, and limitations". The assumptions stay on the case's five areas (card Q37). The reverse form is how the *limits* are said: one sentence per promise (the ten payments; a figure she has stated; her flexibility), each naming the smallest set of events that breaks it.
  - **The clarity half is not proven.** No source read compares a "what would have to happen" sentence with a list of inputs on lay readers. The regulators use the form to find weak points and focus management action (IAIS: "adequate focus on the management actions"); the only communication claim found is to a board (IAIS ICP 16.1.16, 2011). The gain for 3e comes from elsewhere: three sentences instead of five areas' "what changes", and the answer first (card Q105). A blind two-version read is the test (Gaps).
  - **Judge's note:** a bank's forward stress test picks the shock and reports the damage; a reverse one picks the damage and reports the shock. Table 2 already does the first ("All paid" in every world); 3e can do the second.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. The lists differ by promise, and "two reasons" is not the same list as "what would break it".**
  - **The ten payments (a payment short or late).** Their minimal cut sets (the smallest groups of events that together break it; NUREG-0492) from NUMBERS:
    - a US default (single event; Table 3 row 2 calls a payment more than five business days late a default);
    - a missed 2028 deposit (single event): the 2033 payment $3,016 short at today's rates, the ten $57,455 short if cash waiting earns nothing, short in all 585 historical windows (NUMBERS 5);
    - yields falling more than 3.89 points before the ten are bought on 1 Jan 2027 (the whole deposit then goes to the ladder; the zero-interest standard breaks at 3.60; NUMBERS 5, Table 3's note). It closes on 1 Jan 2027. NUMBERS (d) found a fall of even 1.33 points within 56 days, from a yield like today's, in 0.09% of stretches since 1962;
    - fund costs beyond the 0.07% fee AND waiting cash earning under about 0.06% a year (a two-event set): about $236 short for each 0.1% of cost, on the 2036 and 2037 payments (NUMBERS 2, Tue 17 Nov). The ten carry no margin for this until the re-size planned for Wed 18 Nov (then costs up to the range's 0.5%); never say they do before it;
    - for lateness only, a broker failure while holdings sit above SIPC's $500,000 (31 Aug 2029 to Dec 2032, about $87,000 over at the peak; the history is delay, not loss, where assets were kept apart; NUMBERS 8t).
  - **A figure Laura has stated (the bottom from Jan 2028, the top after the 2031 move).**
    - a US default;
    - fund costs beyond the fees larger than the cover under the figure: centrally 3.09% of both funds' value for the top and 3.92% of the floor for the bottom with cash waiting earning nothing, 6.86% for the top at the interest expected, and at least 0.5% in every modelled path by construction (NUMBERS 3, "Bond desk 4's check figures");
    - one step not taken: the floor's payouts before its end ($15,569) and the moved half's ($1,162) kept in the short-term Treasury ETF until the contribution is paid (NUMBERS 3).
  - **The missed deposit is in none of the stated figures' sets.** No figure is stated before it is owned (IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"), so a missed deposit means no range, never a broken one (Table 3 row 1: "no range until paid").
  - **No fall in any price is in any set.** Shares at zero still leave the $95,000 bottom and every payment (NUMBERS 4's deletion test; 8p; Table 2 "All paid" in all worlds and 585 windows). This is IPS l.17's "A fall in the market is not one of them", now tested from the failure end.
  - **So "about 3%" is the central figure, not the condition.** The condition is "costs beyond the fees larger than the margin under the figure", which Laura can measure from the holdings on the day she states it; centrally that margin is more than ten times the frictions measured (3.09% against about 0.27%, NUMBERS 0; Keel arithmetic, 11.4 times).
  - **Judge's note:** the two reasons to change the plan are not the two ways a promise can break. A missed deposit breaks a payment but can never make a figure Laura has said untrue, because she says none before it is owned.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. One is an event Table 3 closes with an action; the other is a limit on her flexibility, not a broken promise.**
  - **A fund ended early, merged, or its end moved.** Each breaks a payment only if nothing is done. An early end brings the cash sooner (card Q36). A merger or a moved end is met by a sale into an earlier iBond fund or the short-term Treasury ETF (Table 3 row 4). These are "cut sets closed by an action", the middle tier of Table 3, which is the second step of SYSC 20.2.1R ("adopt effective arrangements ... to prevent or mitigate that risk").
  - **A caution for row 4.** "Any gap met from the extra Treasuries" holds only if waiting money earns something (0.06% a year covers a 0.1% cost). Under the zero-interest standard they are sized to $0 spare (NUMBERS 2). After the re-size planned for Wed 18 Nov it holds at zero interest for costs up to 0.5%.
  - **Building costs and the Taiwan dollar.** Centrally her flexibility ($29,823) runs out at a rise of 25.9% in the US-dollar cost of the building between the meetings and the contribution (NUMBERS 8q). Two-year rises passed 28.0% in 1 in 20 spans and reached 38.9% at worst (Jul 2006 to Jul 2008). That breaks no promise. It is the limit that her flexibility, sized as half the shares and not to a cost, can be outrun (IPS l.15; card Q9c: the plan answers for the money, not the site or the people).
- **Claim 4: KEEP.** In fault-tree terms a US default is the "common cause": the one basic event in the cut sets of every promise (NUREG-0492 ch. XI on "minimal cut sets potentially susceptible to common cause failures"). Every other event touches one promise. So it is named once, as SKELETON 3e and Book 19 already have it. Real-world test: when S&P cut the US to AA+ in August 2011, about 11,500 municipal securities tied to the federal government, pre-refunded bonds paid from Treasuries among them, went from AAA to AA+ with it (InvestmentNews, 8 Sep 2011). A rating moved; no payment did ("no direct impacts have materialized", GFOA).
- **A correction for anyone citing the rules.** The FCA's SYSC 20 (reverse stress testing) was "deleted in its entirety" from 31 Dec 2021 (FCA 2021/50). It survives for banks in the PRA Rulebook (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment, chapter 15; SS31/15 chapter 4) and for investment firms in MIFIDPRU 7. Never cite SYSC 20 as current.

## Best five for the report
