## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Franklin's figure needed every repaid pound lent again; Laura's ten payments need none of it

- **The parallel.** Franklin's £131,000 was £1,000 lent at 5% for a hundred years, with each borrower repaying a tenth a year so the money could be "again let out to fresh borrowers", and he hoped "no part of the Money will at any time lie dead".
- **What it shows.** The young married craftsmen stopped coming (the last loan was made about 1886), so Boston's fund grew about 4.5% a year instead of 5% and reached about three-fifths of his figure; Laura's ten payments are sized the other way round, to be paid even if every interest payment and every waiting dollar earns nothing, and the extra Treasuries are what that costs.
- **Where it breaks.** Franklin's interest depended on borrowers who could stop coming, while a Treasury's interest is fixed on the day it is bought, so what the two plans share is only the rate at which money that comes back early is lent again: Trading Note 1 named that risk for the 2037 bond, and the ladder as first sized carried it in full (short in 325 of 585 starting months since 1962), which is the gap the extra Treasuries close.
- **Decision it serves:** the standard the ten payments meet, "paid even if cash waiting earns nothing", and the extra Treasuries that buy it (3a).
- **Report part:** 3a, one sentence; the store for the figures.
- **Sources:** Franklin (1789) in Vierling (1923); Franklin Foundation v. Attorney General (1960); NUMBERS 2.
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. Every finance judge knows Franklin as compound interest's patron saint; almost none know his figure fell short as a reinvestment bet, the very risk the IPS's ladder had to be sized against.

### Idea 2. Franklin wrote his doubt into his figure; Laura leaves the doubtful part out

- **The parallel.** Franklin hedged his figure twice and then wrote that he had "perhaps too much flattered myself with a vain Fancy"; in 1923 a trust banker re-forecast Boston's 1991 total from the fund's own century of returns at $7.15 million (Franklin's figure was $19.76 million), and when the trust ended in 1991 it held $4.65 million.
- **What it shows.** Even a forecast built on a hundred years of the fund's own record missed by about half, so the only figure that cannot miss is money already owned; Laura's range rule counts each end as if no more interest is earned, so her central $115,000 top leaves out the $4,772 the late-2032 fund is expected to earn on its waiting cash at today's market rates (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4 and 8u).
- **Where it breaks.** Nobody planned around Franklin's figure or the banker's (a Massachusetts court called the miss "unimportant"), while co-sponsors will plan around Laura's, so writing the doubt into her words would not be enough: she removes it by owning the money first, and an early end of the late-2032 fund in 2031 is the one event that would make her top depend on waiting cash earning interest (up to $4,589 short if it earned nothing, NUMBERS 3).
- **Decision it serves:** the range rule (each end counted as if no more interest is earned) and "no figure is stated before the move" (3c); the early end as one limit (3e, Table 3 row 4).
- **Report part:** 3c, one clause for the second forecast; 3e, one clause for the early end.
- **Sources:** Franklin (1789) in Vierling (1923); Vierling (1923), notes 15-19; Franklin Foundation v. Attorney General (1960, 1993); NUMBERS 3, 4 and 8u.
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. Franklin's doubt about his own figure is almost unknown, and Vierling's 1923 audit is very niche. The pair predicts a pattern Laura's own model shows (row 14).

### Idea 3. What Carnegie actually paid for in Boston (corrects card Q167)

- **The parallel.** In 1904 Andrew Carnegie offered "to duplicate the Franklin Fund", naming its value to the cent ($408,396.48), on condition that Boston "must furnish the site", and in 1905 he paid exactly that, $408,000 of it in United States Steel Corporation bonds, although the fund had grown to $419,581.93 by then.
- **What it shows.** The co-funder matched the figure that already existed, never Franklin's projection, which is the 2031 meetings in small: co-sponsors size their side against the figure Laura states (SD-1), so hers has to exist on the day she names it.
- **Where it breaks.** Carnegie did not pay Boston's running money: his bonds' income was about a ninth of the school's income in 1921-22 (tuition and rents paid the rest), the bonds were called for early redemption in 1929, and in 1956 the school borrowed to meet its payroll because a court held that Carnegie's principal "cannot be used in payment of payrolls", so Boston's running money was never owned, the opposite of Laura's ten payments, which are bought before the building is asked for.
- **Decision it serves:** her figure stated once owned (3d); the ten payments bought before the building (3.0, 3a).
- **Report part:** 3d, one clause at most; the correction goes to the store, and card Q167 rows 8 and 9 should be re-rated.
- **Sources:** Franklin Foundation v. Boston (1957); Boston v. Curley (1931); Franklin Foundation v. Collector-Treasurer (1962); Vierling (1923).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha for 3d ("to the cent"), and a correction that removes a claim a judge could have checked and found wrong.

### Idea 4. The companion: Smithson's bequest, the same era and the opposite rule

- **The parallel.** James Smithson's $508,318 arrived in gold in 1838, the Treasury put it into 6% bonds of Arkansas and Michigan, both states defaulted, and in 1846 Congress lent the whole sum to the United States Treasury at 6% and ruled that every expense come "exclusively from the accruing interest, and not from the principal".
- **What it shows.** The building was paid only from interest already earned (the $242,129 that had accrued by July 1846), and the Regents put off finishing it until $150,000 more had been saved to pay for its upkeep, which is Laura's order: the running money owned first, the building figure stated only from what is already held, and all of it lent to the US Treasury.
- **Where it breaks.** Smithson's fund was an endowment that spends only interest for ever, while Laura's reserve spends its principal on ten dates and ends in 2042, so her reserve is never called an endowment; and Smithson's money was saved because Congress was "bound in honor to restore it", a rescue Laura gave up when the IPS said her payments would not depend "on anybody else's money".
- **Decision it serves:** Treasuries, not other bonds, for every promised dollar (IPS l.11-15); the ten payments before the building figure (3.0); the building figure only from money owned (3c).
- **Report part:** 3.0 or 3b, one sentence; or the store.
- **Sources:** An Act to establish the "Smithsonian Institution" (1846), section 2; Smithsonian Magazine (2013); Smithsonian Institution, Eighth annual report (Serial Set 741), pp. 115-116; U.S. House of Representatives (n.d.).
- **Pedigree or aha:** an aha. Judges know the Smithsonian; few know its founding money was lost once in state bonds, or that its law forbids spending anything but interest already earned.

## Best three for the report

1. **Franklin's figure was a bet on re-lending** (idea 1; rows 3, 9 and 18).
   - Franklin's £131,000 needed every repaid pound lent again at 5%; the craftsmen stopped coming, and Boston's fund reached about three-fifths of his figure.
   - Why a judge says "aha": it turns the most famous compound-interest story into the reinvestment risk the ladder was sized against, and shows the extra Treasuries as the price of not making Franklin's assumption. It belongs in 3a, one sentence.
2. **The second forecast missed too** (idea 2; rows 14 and 17).
   - A trust banker in 1923, using the fund's own hundred years, still overshot the 1991 total by about half; Laura's top leaves out the $4,772 of interest the late-2032 fund has still to earn.
   - Why a judge says "aha": it shows that no forecast, however well informed, gives a figure people can plan around, which is why each end is counted as if no more interest is earned and nothing is stated before the move. It belongs in 3c, one clause.
3. **Smithson's law: build only from interest already earned** (idea 4; rows 26-28).
   - After its first bonds defaulted, Congress lent Smithson's money to the US Treasury and allowed spending "exclusively from the accruing interest"; the Regents finished the building only once its upkeep was saved.
   - Why a judge says "aha": a bequest of Franklin's century, run on the opposite rule, shows Laura's order (running money first, the building figure from what is owned, all in Treasuries) in a founding law. It belongs in 3.0 or 3b, one sentence.

Card Q167's lead aha (Carnegie tried both halves) should be re-rated: rows 23 and 24 show that Boston's running money was never owned. What survives, and is stronger for 3d, is row 20: the co-funder matched the owned figure to the cent.

**Where it lands:** 3c, where it explains why each end of the range is counted as if no more interest is earned and nothing is stated before the move: Franklin stated interest still to be earned and fell two-fifths short, and even a forecast built on the fund's own century missed by half.
