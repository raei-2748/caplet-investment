  2. "Royalties above it come only if the book 'earns out'; most books never earn out (often given as '70%'), so for most authors the floor is the whole payment."

- **Problem class:** contingent pay with a promised floor and a variable top (an advance against royalties, a licence's minimum guarantee against royalties, a company sale's earn-out): where the floor sits against the forecast, where outcomes usually land, and who carries the gap.
- **Field that solved it first:** trade-publishing contract practice and its economics (Caves 2003; Towse 2018), with intellectual-property licensing (minimum guarantees and overages, Contreras 2022) and Delaware merger law (earn-outs, Airborne Health 2009). The antitrust record of US v. Bertelsmann (2022) is the best data set.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, CHANGED. A minimum kept once paid, but paid in quarters over years and owed until then (rows 1, 2, 9).**
  - **The court's words.** "An advance is an upfront payment against the royalties that an author may earn in the future" (opinion p.6). "The author is not required to pay back the advance" even if royalties never exceed it (n.3).
  - **The parts.** Now usually quarters, not thirds: "upon the signing", "upon delivery and acceptance of the manuscript", "upon publication", and "twelve months later" (n.3). The norm moved "from payouts in thirds to payouts in quarters" (p.8).
  - **The government's word.** "An advance is a guarantee of payment typically paid out in installments" (United States' proposed findings, ¶14). The court never calls an advance guaranteed.
  - **Say instead:** a floor kept once paid, paid in quarters over three to four years, and until then a publisher's promise: owed, not owned.
  - **Judge's note:** in her trade "guaranteed" means a publisher's promise. In the report it takes the Treasuries she owns. That is the line between owed and owned, drawn inside her own working life.
- **Claim 2: KEEP "most", from the court. KILL "70%" (rows 3-7).**
  - **The court:** "most authors do not 'earn out' their advances" (p.6).
  - **Estimates in its record (n.4):**
    - Hachette: "about half" earn out;
    - one agent: 20% of her authors, "on the generous side";
    - Andrew Wylie: 5% of the books he represents;
    - the government's economist: "more than 85% of author contracts for anticipated top-selling books never earn out".
  - **Australia runs the other way:** in the ASA's 2021 survey, "60% earned out their advance". Most of those advances were small (80.6% under $5,000).
  - **"70%" has no primary source.**
    - An agent traces "7 out of 10 titles" to a New York Times essay that "cited no source".
    - An author: "no publisher releases what percentage of their books earn out".
    - A Nov 2026 working paper says "around 30%" reach earn-out, citing a 2022 DOJ document that was not found among the court papers read here.
  - **For many books the floor is all there is.** The advance "may be the only compensation that the author will receive" (proposed findings, ¶28).
  - **Judge's note:** the size of the floor matters. Advances of $250,000 or more are 2% of books and 70% of advance money ($1 billion a year), and 85% of them never earn out. Small advances are reached more often.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE. The shapes run opposite on three counts; the plan reverses her trade (rows 10-17, 35-36).**
  - **(a) Both ends are owned when she names them.**
    - IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own".
    - The top moves only before the 2031 move, and nothing is stated while it moves (NUMBERS 4).
    - So the stated range has no royalty-like part.
  - **(b) Outcomes land at opposite ends.**
    - Most books stop at the floor.
    - Laura's contribution "Equals the stated top in every path" and "in every window" (NUMBERS 4, central $115,000), with about $7,500 above the top staying hers.
  - **(c) The floors sit in opposite places.**
    - An advance is bid toward the publisher's estimate: "books generally are sold to the highest bidder" (p.9). A Norton editor says the Big Five often "overpay". The UK authors' society writes of advances "in excess of realistic sales expectations ... to outbid other interested publishers".
    - Laura's bottom is counted below what the floor fund's bonds pay. She states $95,000, against $97,605 unrounded and a central payout of $102,365 (NUMBERS 4).
  - **(d) Who carries the gap.**
    - The publisher carries it, as a "loss", and can afford to because publishing is "a 'portfolio business'": "only 35 out of 100 books turn a profit", and "the top 4 percent of profitable titles generate 60 percent of profitability" (pp.5, 16).
    - Scholastic held a $92.7m reserve against unearned advances on 31 May 2026.
    - In Laura's plan nobody carries it, because the figure is owned. The one limit is fund costs beyond the 0.5% margin (TNA l.13: the payout "is not fixed").
  - **Where royalties really sit in the plan:** her flexibility (about $21,200 on 1 Jan 2033) plus anything above the top (about $7,500). That part is never stated and goes to Laura, the payer. In publishing the upside goes to the receiver.
  - **Say instead (3c):** "Her trade already has a range: a floor someone promises and a top most books never reach. Hers reverses both: both ends are owned before she names them, and the contribution lands on the top."
  - **Judge's note:** a judge who knows publishing expects outcomes at the floor. In this plan every tested outcome lands on the top.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, re-aimed. The reserve's reason fits the range's margin; its direction is the reverse of 3a's reading (rows 20-22).**
  - **The Authors Guild's reason for the reserve:** "it is often difficult to recover overpayments made to authors". Returns run "as high as 35–50%", and reserves can delay pay "by as much as a year".
  - **The plan's version of that reason:** a stated figure is hard to take back (case l.114: promising more "could damage her credibility"), so Laura holds back 0.5% and rounds each end down.
  - **The direction differs from CONCEIT 3a's reading.** A publisher's reserve holds back the receiver's money to protect the payer. The extra Treasuries ($36,774) are Laura's own money, held so that the residency is paid in full.
- **Claim 5: KILL as a turn; KEEP as 2.3 evidence and as "her next advance", a pencil example (row 37).**
  - "Advance" and "earn out" are words from her trade's contracts, not from drawing. Set up in a sentence they would be a second image, and every staged image outside pencil and ink has failed blind (round 7).
  - An advance is owed, so "advance" set beside "ink" pulls ink toward "promised" or "signed".
  - The shapes also run opposite (claim 3), so "her range is like an advance" would mislead a judge.
  - Round 7 found that the definition lands best with pencil examples from her own life, "her next advance" among them. Keep that one word there.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 35 (3c).** Her trade's floor is the usual outcome. In her plan the contribution lands on the stated top in every tested path (NUMBERS 4). This turns a familiar parallel into the report's sharpest reversal, and it is aimed at a judge who expects the floor.
2. **Row 12, with rows 13 and 30 (3c, 2.3).** A publisher can promise floors because it holds thousands of books ("only 35 out of 100 books turn a profit"). Laura holds one plan with ten dates, so she buys her floor instead of promising it. This explains IPS l.11 from inside her own industry.
3. **Row 28 (3a).** Licensing law says a licensor needs a guaranteed minimum when it has "fixed cost commitments, such as facility and personnel costs". That is the residency's position exactly, from an unexpected field. The plan's answer is ownership, not a counterparty's minimum.
4. **Row 9 (3c-3d; the conceit's "owned").** The US government's own word for an advance was "a guarantee of payment". A judge sees "guaranteed" mean a promise in her trade and a holding in her plan, with no comics word needed.
5. **Row 34 (3d).** Delaware's "an earn-out often converts today's disagreement over price into tomorrow's litigation over the outcome". It gives the reason Laura states a top only once the move has bought it, so co-sponsors are given a figure and not a formula.
