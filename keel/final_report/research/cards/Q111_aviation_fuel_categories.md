## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. Only the ten fit their fuel part cleanly (trip fuel). The extra Treasuries do contingency fuel's job, are sized the way additional fuel is, and end the way alternate fuel does. Her flexibility has extra fuel's named uses and discretionary fuel's owner.**
  - **The ten are trip fuel: KEEP.** Trip fuel is planned on forecast conditions. Contingency fuel then covers "deviations of an individual aeroplane from the expected fuel consumption data" and the like (EASA GM1 CAT.OP.MPA.181(g)). The ten are sized on today's forward rates, and each reaches $50,000 "only with its interest reinvested on the way" (NUMBERS 1, 2). Both are used up by the trip: each Treasury pays its payment and is gone.
  - **Contingency's sizing does not fit.** EASA's basic scheme carries "5 % of the planned trip fuel" or five minutes' holding at 1,500 ft, "whichever is the higher". It allows 3% where a fuel en-route alternate exists, or a statistical figure at 99% or 90% coverage of past deviations (AMC1 and AMC6 CAT.OP.MPA.181(c); GM2 CAT.OP.MPA.181).
  - **The extra Treasuries are not a percentage.** They are the cheapest set of Treasuries (an exact linear programme) that pays all ten if cash waiting earns nothing and every waiting dollar pays the bill fund's 0.15% fee (NUMBERS 2). Their cost comes out at 11.4% of the ten's $302,287: $34,369 in 1 Jan 2027 money (Keel arithmetic). A 5% contingency would be $15,114, a 3% one $9,069 (Keel arithmetic).
  - **A percentage margin failed the plan's own test.** Mon 9 Nov's call was a margin of $21,149, 7.0% of the ten (Keel arithmetic), sized for rates 3 points below forwards. It left the ten $22,192 short if interest earned nothing, and short in 35 of 585 historical windows, where only the half of VT kept could pay the gap (NUMBERS 2).
  - **Additional fuel is the closest fit by sizing.** It lets the aeroplane proceed "in the event of an engine failure or loss of pressurisation, from the most critical point along the route" to an alternate, hold 15 minutes and land (AMC1 CAT.OP.MPA.181(f)). And "additional fuel is only required if the minimum amount of fuel calculated in accordance with (a)(2) to (a)(5) is not sufficient for such an event" (AMC1 CAT.OP.MPA.150(b)). That is the extra Treasuries' rule: one named worst case, at each payment's worst wait, and only what the ten's own Treasuries do not already cover.
  - **Their end is alternate fuel's** (card Q59): planned to come back. If rates hold, what comes back from the extra Treasuries is worth about $45,500 in 1 January 2033 money ($45,456, NUMBERS 8h), all hers by the last payment in 2042.
  - **Her flexibility.** EASA splits the fuel above the minimum in two. Extra fuel is for "anticipated delays or specific operational constraints that can be predicted". Discretionary fuel is "a quantity at the sole discretion of the commander", whose discretion "is independent and cannot be encouraged or discouraged" (AMC1 CAT.OP.MPA.181(g), (h); GM1 CAT.OP.MPA.181(k)).
    - IPS l.15 names three uses in advance (building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short): extra fuel's kind.
    - Their use is hers, "promised to no one" (IPS l.15): discretionary fuel's owner.
    - Kept from card Q59: discretionary fuel may be burned to save the flight, while her flexibility never pays a payment (CANON A26). And its size, half the shares, was set by Caplet in the IPS, not chosen on the day.
  - **Judge's note:** an airline adds a percentage because nobody can bound tomorrow's winds. The plan can write down its worst case for waiting money, so it sizes to that case and the percentage (11.4%) falls out. A fixed 7% margin, bigger than an airline's 5%, failed the plan's own test in 35 of 585 historical starts.

- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Final reserve is planned to be landed with, and the emergency is defined in advance in fixed words: both KEEP. But until Wed 18 Nov the payments keep nothing beyond their worst tested case, and the two reasons are failures of supply, not a low tank.**
  - **Final reserve is protected.** The pilot must "continually ensure that the amount of usable fuel remaining on board is not less than the fuel required to proceed" (Annex 6 as amended in 2012, quoted by IFALPA; the bulletin's quotation stops there, and card Q59 holds the full declarations). EASA gives its purpose: "to ensure that a safe landing is made at any aerodrome when unforeseen circumstances may not allow to safely complete the flight, as originally planned" (GM1 CAT.OP.MPA.185(k)). For a turbine aeroplane it is "fuel to fly for 30 minutes at holding speed at 1 500 ft (450 m)" (AMC1 CAT.OP.MPA.150(b)).
  - **Every planned case keeps a margin for what was not foreseen.** The normal plan lands with 30 minutes' final reserve. Even the engine-failure case at the worst point keeps fuel to "hold there for 15 minutes at 1 500 ft" above the alternate before the approach (AMC1 CAT.OP.MPA.181(f)).
  - **The plan's worst tested case lands at $0.** The extra Treasuries are sized to exactly $0 spare at zero interest with the bill fund's fee. Without that fee, $1,414 is left after 2042 (NUMBERS 2, 8h). Fund costs beyond the 0.07% fee are not in the test: each 0.1% of them leaves the ten about $236 short, and the range's 0.5% leaves them $1,181 short, on the 2036-2037 payments (NUMBERS 2, `cost_cover.py` part B).
  - **The lead has already decided the fix.** The extra Treasuries get the range's 0.5% margin for fund costs beyond the fee, re-sized with the `plan.py` 365.25-day fix as the first job of Wed 18 Nov (NUMBERS section 9; ASK_HUMANS). Until that run they carry no such margin; never say they do. Aviation backs the call: every planned case keeps a margin for what the plan did not count.
  - **The emergency is defined in advance, in fixed words: KEEP.** MINIMUM FUEL ("not an emergency situation") and MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY FUEL (landing fuel predicted "less than the planned final reserve fuel") entered Annex 6 and PANS-ATM with Amendment 36, effective 15 November 2012. "Pilots should not expect any form of priority handling as a result of a 'MINIMUM FUEL' declaration" (IFALPA 13ATSBL01).
    - Table 3 does the same: two reasons in measurable words ("Nothing received by 31 March 2028"; "a payment over five business days late"), and watch rows that by themselves change nothing.
    - It even has both levels for the deposit: "Confirmed by 1 Dec 2027" is its MINIMUM FUEL, nothing by 31 March 2028 its MAYDAY.
    - "A fall in the market is not one of them" (IPS l.17) is the plan's "not an emergency situation".
  - **Where it differs.** MAYDAY FUEL is a threshold on the store itself: fuel predicted at landing below a set amount. The two reasons are not the store running low. They are money from outside failing to arrive: a deposit not received (fuel never loaded) and a Treasury not paying (fuel lost from a tank). The plan needs no low-tank trigger because its holdings pay fixed amounts on fixed dates (card Q59 row 42).
  - **A missed deposit is a dispatch failure.** "No person may dispatch or take off an airplane unless it has enough fuel" for the trip, the most distant alternate "(where required)" and 45 minutes (14 CFR 121.639, all domestic operations). Table 3 knows the gap before "take-off": the ten short $3,016 at today's rates, $57,455 if cash waiting earns nothing; "Never: a sale."
  - **Judge's note:** an airliner's worst planned case, an engine failure at the worst point, still keeps 15 minutes of holding fuel. Until Wed 18 Nov the payments' worst tested case ends at exactly $0, and the plan's own decision is to add a margin.

- **Claim 3: CHANGE. Crews were in every chain, but each official report also found the system around them at fault: words that did not exist, a fuel job nobody owned, a dispatch rule not applied, parts that did not match, an alarm that could not name a leak. Each report changed rules or procedures.**
  - **Avianca 052 (25 Jan 1990; NTSB AAR-91/04).** Probable cause: "the failure of the flight crew to adequately manage the aircraft's fuel load, and their failure to communicate an emergency fuel situation". Also contributing: "the flight crew's failure to use an airline operational control dispatch system", "inadequate traffic flow management by the FAA" and "the lack of standardized understandable terminology for pilots and controllers for minimum and emergency fuel states" (FAA Lessons Learned). The first officer asked for "priority" and said "we're running out of fuel sir"; "He did not use the word "emergency," as instructed by the captain". Sixty-five of the 149 passengers and eight of the nine crew were killed (FAA page).
  - **Air Canada 143 (23 Jul 1983, Gimli; Board of Inquiry, Lockwood, April 1985).** The aircraft "left each airport in turn with only half the amount of fuel the flight crew thought they had". The factor used "was 1.77" (litres to pounds), where litres to kilograms is "typically around .8". It flew with blank fuel gauges, which the Minimum Equipment List forbade. But the Board wrote: "To suggest that the accident was the result of a metric mix-up is simplistic and inaccurate", and responsibility "does not lie only with on-line maintenance personnel and flight crew". Among the corporate deficiencies it listed "Failure to Assign Responsibility for Fuel Load Calculations"; one recommendation heading is independent confirmation of the fuel load.
  - **Air Transat 236 (24 Aug 2001; GPIAA 22/ACCID/GPIAA/2001, 18 Oct 2004).** A fuel tube cracked against a hydraulic line, from "the mismatched installation of the post-mod fuel tube and pre-mod hydraulic tube" after an engine change. "Fuel losses or leaks themselves cannot be identified as such by the ECAM system". Investigators found "both pilots believed that the problem was a computer fault"; the captain opened the crossfeed "from memory without considering the caution note", which "put the fuel in the left tank at risk and made a serious fuel-leak situation even worse". Had the FUEL LEAK checklist been followed, "the aircraft would not have run out of fuel before reaching Lajes" (FAA Lessons Learned). Both engines stopped; the aircraft glided to Lajes; two passengers were seriously hurt in the evacuation.
  - **What the plan takes.**
    - **Fixed words** (Avianca): Table 3's two reasons and watches.
    - **One owner per check** (Gimli's "Failure to Assign Responsibility"): Table 3's "Who" column names Laura for the deposit and the team for the Treasury payments.
    - **An independent check** (Gimli's recommendation): NUMBERS 8k's second calculation, by value conservation, agrees with the first to $1.48.
    - **One unit per figure** (Gimli): the register gives the same extra Treasuries as $34,369 (1 Jan 2027 money), $35,894 (their January 2028 price), $36,867 (face and fund dollars), $55,920 (what they pay if cash waiting earns nothing, added up undiscounted), $45,456 (what comes back, in 1 Jan 2033 money) and $74,123 (left by 1 Jan 2042 at forwards). Six true figures, six units (NUMBERS 2, 8h). WORDS already fixes how two of them are said: the price as $35,894, never a January 2027 form (M11), and what comes back as "about $45,500 in 1 January 2033 money".
    - **Never move good money into an unexplained loss** (Transat): Table 3's answer to a Treasury default is "cover re-checked and the range restated on what was paid. Never: a forced sale".
  - **Judge's note:** Gimli's fuel was measured correctly and converted wrongly, and the Board still refused to call it a metric mix-up: nobody owned the sum. Laura's plan names who owns each check in Table 3.

- **Claim 4: KILL as an image; KEEP as evidence.**
  - **A second image would cancel the first.** An inconsistent extension of a metaphor removed its framing effect (Thibodeau 2016, card Q42 row 12), and the conceit is developed one turn per part (CONCEIT V2.2).
  - **It would not even be one turn.** Claim 1 needs three fuel parts to describe one holding, so "the extra Treasuries are contingency fuel" is false and the true version takes a paragraph.
  - **What aviation supports is ink's test.** A dispatcher counts only fuel in the tanks at take-off (14 CFR 121.639), never fuel promised at a later stop. Ink counts only a figure "owned in Treasuries that will pay it on its date". The fuel rules are evidence that a working field already states only what it already holds.
  - **Use:** at most one plain sentence of evidence in 3e (fixed words for when the plan changes), with no fuel words attached to Laura's holdings: never "trip fuel", "contingency fuel" or "reserve fuel" for any part of her money.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 7-8 and 10 (additional fuel's "only required if" rule; the 7% margin that failed): 3a.** Aviation sizes its failure fuel as a top-up to a named worst case, counted after the other fuel. The extra Treasuries follow the same rule, and Mon 9 Nov's 7% margin, bigger than an airline's 5%, failed the plan's own test in 35 of 585 historical starts. It answers a judge's "why $35,894 and not a round percentage?"
2. **Rows 14 and 35 (every planned flight keeps a margin, plus a wear allowance on the worst case): 3a, with the Wed 18 Nov re-size.** Aviation adds 5% for engine wear on top of its worst named failure, unless the airline monitors fuel burn. The plan's 0.5% margin for fund costs beyond the fee will do the same job on top of the zero-interest case once the re-size has run; until then it is not there and must not be said.
3. **Rows 23-24 (Gimli: half the fuel the crew thought, and the Board's "simplistic and inaccurate"): 3e and Table 3.** A right measurement in the wrong unit, and a fuel sum nobody owned. The plan prints each figure with its unit (row 31) and names who owns each check (Table 3's "Who"). Kills claim 3's "not from the rules".
