- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "UK schemes that waited for higher yields before hedging fell into a trap. Their triggers did not fire for a decade while yields fell, and their deficits grew."
  2. "So the IPS rule to buy the ten in January 2027, on a date and not at a price, is the disciplined choice."
  3. "2022 is the honest counter-case: a waiter would have bought far cheaper."
  4. "The LDI crisis of September 2022 is a separate lesson, about borrowing, and Laura does not borrow."
- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:**
  - The problem: when to buy the assets that match a fixed promise if the price looks "too high", using a waiting rule or a date rule.
  - The fields that met it first, at scale: UK defined benefit (DB) pension liability-driven investing (LDI), with yield triggers and "fair value" hedging in 2005-2022, and Dutch pension funds' rate-dependent hedge ladders ("rentestaffels").
  - Their regulators wrote the post-mortems: TPR (2016) and DNB (2015).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP, with three changes.**
  - **The trap was real, and it was named while it was happening.**
    - Hymans Robertson (2018): "much time and effort was spent in setting triggers to hedge at higher yields than those prevailing in current markets". It adds that "with the benefit of 20:20 hindsight ... schemes collectively made the wrong call".
    - KPMG (2018): trustees "wearied of playing a seemingly endless waiting game with gilt yields".
    - The chief investment officer of XPS (2018): market-based triggers "never come and the situations where they don't come is when you most need them".
  - **How long the wait was (Keel arithmetic, UK 10-year, monthly).**
    - Below 4% for 149 months in a row (May 2010-Sep 2022). Below 3% for 133 (Aug 2011-Aug 2022).
    - Never back at 5% after July 2008, to the end of 2024.
    - Triggers 0.5 point above the yield in any month of 2008-2015 (96 starts): 25 fired within a year and 9 never fired by Dec 2024. Those that fired waited a median 93 months.
    - Triggers 1 point above: 6 fired within a year and 33 never fired.
  - **The deficits grew.**
    - The PPF 7800 aggregate deficit "reached an all-time high of £413 billion at the end of August 2016" (PPF, 2017). In early 2008 the same index had shown "a small aggregate surplus" (DWP, 2017, para 200).
    - The government's own account (DWP, 2017, para 98): "Much of this has resulted from schemes not hedging against the falls in gilt yields to historically low levels".
  - **Change (a): triggers were a minority policy.**
    - Aon's 2015 survey: 27% of schemes had pre-set triggers, 42% would hedge at "fair value", 21% had no policy, 9% would not hedge, and 1% would hedge at any price.
    - In 2013, 20% of schemes had a formal trigger strategy.
    - The common habit was waiting for "fair value", which did the same thing under a softer name.
  - **Change (b): triggers set near the market did fire.**
    - "a 10 or 20 basis point increase" (F&C, in European Pensions) was a common setting.
    - The trap was triggers set at "normal" yields, not every trigger.
  - **Change (c): the hindsight caveat stays.**
    - Hymans says it in the same sentence.
    - DNB (2015) gives the reason: waiting only pays if rates rise *faster than the forward curve already prices*.
  - **Judge's note:** the schemes that waited were not careless; they held a view, and the view was wrong for twelve years. A scheme that waited for a 5% 10-year gilt was still waiting at the end of 2024.

- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Keep "disciplined", but as "a rule that needs no view on yields". Never say "it buys cheaper".**
  - **On average, waiting gains nothing (Keel arithmetic, US 10-year, every start day 1962-2025).**
    - The rule tested: "buy when the 10-year is X points higher, or after one year at the latest".
    - It bought at a higher yield than day one in 77.8% of starts (X = 0.25), 65.8% (0.5) and 55.2% (1).
    - Its average gain was about zero: -0.015, +0.001 and +0.008 point.
  - **The sign of the gain depends on the era.** For the 0.5-point rule:
    - 1962-1980: +0.30 point.
    - 1981-2020: -0.18 point.
    - 2010-2019: -0.21 point (it fired in only 36.8% of starts).
  - **The Dutch back-test agrees.** Over 2000-2024, 2022 included, a ladder that only ever lowered the hedge "zou in ieder geval in de afgelopen 25 jaar geen meerwaarde hebben geboden" ("would in any case have added no value over the past 25 years"). "Over deze periode had een hogere renteafdekking beter uitgepakt" ("Over this period a higher rate hedge would have worked out better") (Alserda & Kuiper, 2025).
  - **Cambridge Associates (2019):** "very difficult for a tactical under-hedge to outperform the strategic hedge over a prolonged period".
  - **For Laura (NUMBERS 8e).** A price trigger at her own price would have waited about 16 years. Her price is the flat yield of 5.02% at which $300,000 buys all ten. From June 2007 to August 2026 that came on one day only, 19 Oct 2023, and on 0% of days in the 2010s.
  - **Why a date fits her (store H5-006).**
    - The ten are a promise from day one.
    - Her money arrives on a date.
    - The IPS takes no market view.
    - IPS l.13's completion from the 2028 deposit makes the date affordable.
  - **Judge's note:** a date does not beat a price by buying cheaper. Over 64 years of US data the two come out level. It beats a price because it needs no forecast, and the forecast the UK pension world relied on was wrong for a decade.

- **Claim 3: KEEP, sized and dated.**
  - **The size of the counter-case.**
    - The UK 10-year went from 0.21% (Jul 2020) to 4.11% (Oct 2022), and was 4.53% in Aug 2023.
    - The 30-year gilt "rose by 160 basis points in just four days, having had a yield of around 120 basis points at the start of the year" (FCA, 2023).
    - TPR (2023): "Schemes that had not hedged interest rate risks have improved funding levels (in some case by substantial amounts), while fully hedged schemes remain broadly the same."
  - **The other side.**
    - A 4% trigger set in April 2010 fired in October 2022, 149 months later.
    - In between, its scheme lived through the £413bn peak and paid deficit contributions. TPR counted £44bn in the three years to 2015 (via LCP; UNVERIFIED at source).
    - The Dutch 25-year back-test, 2022 included, still favours the higher hedge.
  - **Laura's mirror (NUMBERS 8e).** The ten cost $470,217 on 4 Aug 2020 (10-year 0.52%) and $299,293 on 19 Oct 2023.
  - **Judge's note:** 2022 rescued the waiters' funding levels. It did not make waiting a winning rule: the payoff came twelve years late, on a date no one could have named in advance.

- **Claim 4: CHANGE to "distinct but linked".**
  - **Leverage was how under-hedged schemes caught up without selling their shares.**
    - Bank of England (FPC, Oct 2022): LDI leverage "allowed pension scheme clients to increase their hedges against falling interest rates with a lower upfront investment".
    - Investment Association (2025): derivatives and repo let schemes "extend hedge coverage without fully committing scheme assets".
  - **The hedge was right; its financing broke.** Breeden (2022): higher rates "would in fact boost the funding position of DB pension schemes", so they "had the incentive to provide funds".
  - **Laura's position.**
    - Her hedge is paid in full in cash, and the first deposit is about the price of the ten (NUMBERS 1: $302,287 on 6 Nov).
    - Margin, borrowing and derivatives are banned for her plan.
  - **Judge's note:** the schemes that waited in the 2010s and the schemes that borrowed in 2022 were answering the same problem: a hedge they wanted that was bigger than their cash. Laura's hedge equals her cash, so she needs neither.

- **Disanalogies (each in a row).**
  - The IPS names a single date. Pensions hedged gradually, over 15-20 years, with swaps and leverage.
  - Pension sponsors kept paying in for years. Laura's only top-up is the 2028 deposit (IPS l.13; card Q97).
  - Many triggers were set on the funding level, not on a yield.
  - Laura cannot buy before her money lands in January 2027, so any wait could only lengthen her exposure, never shorten it (row 33).
  - The 2028 top-up depends on January 2027 prices and is paid in January 2028 money: two dates, no trigger (card Q98 row 26).

- **Link to card Q98.**
  - Q98 sized the cost of the date: in the eight weeks before January 2027 the top-up was $0 in 40.4% of windows and over $10,000 in 23.5%.
  - This card sizes the cost of the alternative. A waiting rule wins often and small, and loses rarely and big.
    - The 0.5-point rule wins about two starts in three, by +0.49 point on average.
    - It loses about one in three, by -0.94 point on average.
    - At $300 a basis point (NUMBERS 1; linear Keel arithmetic, rough) that is about $14,800 won against $28,300 lost.
  - The same shape appears in NUMBERS 8c's rival, which waits to buy the floor until 2031. It ends ahead in 59.8% of house paths, but walks into the meetings under $95,000 in 16.4%.

- **What the plan takes.**
  - 3a: one clause. A date needs no view on yields, with the UK 2010s as prior art.
  - 3e or Table 3: watch dates and the 10-year "for scale" only, never a yield level as a trigger (CANON A34).
  - 2.5 and 3f: the October 2026 fills told as luck (NUMBERS 8b).
  - Never claim the team weighed and rejected a trigger: that is not in the record.

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 11 and 21 (3a, one clause): waiting is a bet against the forward curve.**
   - DNB's footnote says hedging less pays only if rates rise faster than already priced in.
   - Over 64 years of US data, a "wait for higher yields" rule averages zero.
   - A smart reader thinks the date gave up a bargain. It gave up a coin toss.
2. **Rows 22 and 34 (3a or 3c): wins often and small, loses rarely and big.**
   - The waiting rule wins two starts in three, by about $14,800, and loses one in three, by about $28,300 (linear, rough).
   - NUMBERS 8c's rival shows the same shape for the floor.
   - The plan gives up the frequent small win to remove the rare large loss, twice over. This is the aha.
3. **Rows 4, 19 and 25 (3a or 2.5): "the latter never come".**
   - A UK 10-year gilt trigger at 4% waited 149 months.
   - A trigger at Laura's own price (5.02% flat) came on one day in nineteen years (NUMBERS 8e).
   - The prior art is a real decade, in pounds and in her dollars.
4. **Rows 15 and 40 (3e, the honest counter): 2022 rescued the waiters, twelve years late.**
   - TPR (2023) says unhedged schemes gained.
   - The Dutch 25-year back-test, 2022 included, still says a higher hedge did better.
   - Naming the counter-case and its date is what a judge trusts.
5. **Rows 43-44 (3e, one clause): the 2022 crisis was the same problem, solved by borrowing.**
   - Leverage let under-hedged schemes hedge "with a lower upfront investment".
   - The hedge was right and its financing broke.
   - Laura's hedge is paid in cash, so she needs neither the wait nor the loan.

## Gaps

- **Trigger levels in %:** no UK source read gives typical yield-trigger levels as yields. The only written schedule found is the Dutch one (row 13). UK triggers were often set on funding level (rows 8, 22 context) or as "10 or 20 basis points" above the market (row 9).
- **KPMG LDI surveys:** the PDFs themselves were not read. Figures come via IMA (2013), NAPF (2014), Actuarial Post, Chief Investment Officer and Institutional Money. Actuarial Post gives £675bn in its headline and £657bn in its text (row 36). The 35% "Extension Trigger" figure (row 38) is secondary only.
- **TPR statements:** 2016 was read from a mirror copy (silo.tips); TPR's archive copy sits on the National Archives and was not reached. 2015 and 2019 were read only through LCP and Sackers (rows 27-28).
- **Aon 2013:** the "4 in 10 schemes hit triggers" headline is not explained in the article (row 8). Aon's 2013 survey itself was not read.
- **Undated pages:** European Pensions (row 9), Mercer (row 5) and Ortec Finance (row 14).
- **Long gilts:** only the UK 10-year (monthly, OECD) was used. Bank of England 20- and 30-year curve data, which many triggers used, was not read. The UK run lengths would differ on long gilts.
- **Read in part only:** Cambridge Associates (web extract; the 5%-7% funded-status volatility figure not re-read), the TU Delft thesis (author not recorded), DNB Working Paper 801 (abstract only), and the 2024 letter to the Work and Pensions Committee (author not confirmed).
- **Not found:** an Insight Investment or Schroders survey of UK hedge ratios; any count of Dutch funds with rate ladders; any source measuring what waiting cost UK schemes in pounds.
- **Interest while waiting:** the US test counts yield points only. It does not credit the waiter with cash interest or the buyer with the bond's higher yield over the wait.
