## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1, "a probability that the outcome falls in the range": CHANGE.**
   - The kind is right. The case asks teams to "state how confident they are that her 2033 contribution will fall within that range" (case l.117-118): a probability of one named event, held by the team. In statistics that is a single-event, Bayesian kind of probability (Greenland et al.: a probability for one interval needs "a prior distribution" and is called a "credible" interval), not a "confidence level".
   - Neyman (1937), who coined the term, denied any in-between probability for one computed interval: "no probability statement ... may be made, that is except for the hypothetical and trivial ones", zero or one (row 2).
   - What changes: after the move it is not a forecast. Both ends are owned and the contribution is capped at the stated top, so the event happens in every tested future: 200,000 simulated, 1,770 stock windows since 1871, 585 joint windows since 1962, on the engine's holdings and on 3.50% and 4.00% coupons (NUMBERS 4, 8l). It is Neyman's "trivial" case, and Laura knows it is one. Say it as the register does: "certain" (NUMBERS 4; Book 19 allows "guaranteed"), with the basis, and the rule's exits named once (the IPS's two reasons, l.17; for the top, fund costs beyond the 0.5% cut, which still leave the gift inside the range: at 0.75%, the top is missed in 6.01% of simulated futures by at most $402, NUMBERS 8l).
   - Before the move the honest percentages answer a different question, which range she will be able to state (stated top at least $100,000 in 92.2% of simulated futures, at least $115,000 in 59.8%; NUMBERS 4).
   - **Judge's note:** the man who invented confidence intervals wrote that any one interval holds its target with probability zero or one, and nobody knows which. Laura's is the rare range where you do know: it is one.
2. **Claim 2, "the confidence belongs to the rule, not to a forecast": KEEP, with a statistician's pedigree and one change.**
   - It is Neyman's definition, not a debating move. A confidence interval "indicates a property of the procedure" (Hoekstra et al., row 7); Morey et al. say the accurate name is a "coverage procedure" (row 9); Neyman: the frequency of correct statements "in the long run", even when "the problem of estimation" differs each time (row 1).
   - It is also how regulators judge money set aside: a bank's 99.9% means losses beat its capital "on average once in a thousand years" (Basel); Australian insurers hold claims money meant to "prove to be sufficient to pay claims as they fall due 75 per cent of the time" (APRA). Each names the event, the money and a horizon (rows 18-21).
   - Change: the rule's record ("every tested future") holds because of construction, so a statistician will ask what the tests could not see. The ASA's task force: "those sources of variation that have not been addressed should also be identified" (row 13). Say what makes the record (owned Treasuries; the cap), its horizon (the bottom from January 2028, the top from the move before the 2031 meetings, both to 15 Dec 2032), and the exits, once.
   - **Judge's note:** Australia's insurers must hold enough to pay claims 75 times in 100; the stated bottom is paid in every tested future.
3. **Claim 3, "a statistician will read 'confidence' as coverage": CHANGE.**
   - She knows coverage is the textbook meaning: "the specified confidence level is called the coverage probability" (Greenland et al.). She will also see it does not apply here: the 2033 contribution is a future value, not a parameter, and NIST calls an interval for a future value a "prediction interval" "rather than" a confidence interval (row 15).
   - Most trained readers do not read coverage. Of 118 psychology researchers, 59% endorsed "There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies between 0.1 and 0.4" and 55% "We can be 95% confident ..."; 3% rejected all six false readings; self-rated experience did not help (Hoekstra et al. 2014, row 7). Lay readers hear belief: "the word 'confident', in lay use, is closely related to concepts of plausibility and belief" (Morey et al., row 9).
   - So: answer the case's word once, in the case's sense (how sure the event happens). Never "confidence interval", "confidence level" or "95% confident" for the owned range. A label below certainty would be Morey's submarine mistake turned round: "Reporting 50% certainty ... in a specific interval that surely contains the parameter would clearly be a mistake" (row 10).
   - **Judge's note:** most researchers misread a confidence interval as the chance for that one interval; Laura's range is the one case where that reading is true.
4. **Claim 4, "state one confidence percentage, as banks and insurers do": KILL after the move; KEEP before it.**
   - Regulators' levels (Basel 99.9%; Solvency II 99.5% and 85% over one year; APRA 75%; insurers' IFRS 17 disclosures from 65% to 88%) are chances that money set aside is enough for a promise that is itself uncertain. Laura sets her promise to what she owns, so any figure below 100% understates, and "100%" offered as a "confidence level" invites the "true by construction" objection (card Q4c row 26).
   - IFRS 17 makes an insurer that does not build by percentiles still state "the confidence level corresponding to the results of that technique" (row 22). Laura's equivalent is the record and the exits, not a number. Where the promisor is a government, HM Treasury dropped the disclosure for central government (row 23).
   - Before the move, percentages are right: they size which range she will state (row 39, Table 2).

**Which meaning the report should use.** The case's own: how sure the team is that the 2033 contribution falls inside the two figures Laura states. ICD 203 splits it into the two answers Wharton asks for (FR instructions: "the level of confidence associated with that range and the factors that could affect it"): the likelihood of the event, and the confidence in its basis (row 30). In notes, the order 3c needs, which answers the cold readers' "confidence about what?" in the first clause (ICD 203: "the likelihood, timing, and nature of the outcome", row 31): (1) the event and when (the 2033 contribution, paid by the funds on 15 Dec 2032, between the two figures stated, never above the top); (2) how sure (certain: inside the range in every tested future); (3) because (both ends are Treasuries she owns, worked out on the trade day and cut by 0.5%; the gift is capped at the top); (4) the exits, once, as the rule's conditions, not a hedge; (5) separately, the before-the-move question, in Table 2. No percentage label, no second "high confidence" label (IPCC para 11E, row 28).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 (3c, the aha).** Neyman's own paper says one interval holds its target with probability zero or one and nobody knows which; Laura's range is the one case where she does know, which turns the textbook's weakness into the plan's point in one sentence.
2. **Rows 36 and 31 (3c's first clause).** The case itself uses "confidence" three ways within 13 lines, which is why three cold readers asked what it is about; ICD 203's "likelihood, timing, and nature of the outcome" gives the fix: name the event, its date and its form before saying how sure.
3. **Row 21 (3c or 3e, one clause).** Australia's insurers must hold claims money that is "sufficient ... 75 per cent of the time"; the stated bottom is sufficient in every tested future. A regulator's own word for the case's question, from the team's home country, and a scale a judge feels at once.
4. **Row 7 (why no percentage label).** 59% of researchers read a 95% interval as a 95% chance for that interval; a percentage beside Laura's owned range would be heard the same way, and would understate it (row 10).
5. **Row 24 (3c or Table 2's note).** S&P defines its top rating by surviving the Great Depression, not by a probability; replayed with US stocks bought in July 1929, the rule still gives an owned $95,000 to $100,000 (with the caveat that the replay moves stocks only).
