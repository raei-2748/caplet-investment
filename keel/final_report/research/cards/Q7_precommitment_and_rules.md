# Q7 research cards: is buying the floor early, with only two reasons to change, a precommitment, and what is it worth?

Research run, Mon 9 Nov 2026 (Sydney). Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose. Checked against Caplet's
FINAL IPS (`keel/final_report/FINAL_IPS_as_submitted.txt`) and the locked TNA
(`keel/drafts/writing_room_2026-11-03/tna_as_submitted.txt`). The plan is frozen: a "yes" in the Clash column is a
risk to note in the Final Report, never a change to propose.

Claim tested: "Committing the floor early and writing down in advance the only reasons to change the plan is a
precommitment; its value is credibility (a promise others can rely on) and protection from panic, and the fields that
manage long promises use the same device."
Problem class: decision theory and institutional design (commitment, rules versus discretion, escape clauses), plus
pension practice (journey plans, de-risking, buy-ins) and the investment policy statement standard.

## Verdict: CHANGE (keep the core, correct two parts)

1. **"It is a precommitment": KEEP, but say which kind.** The 2028 floor purchase is a commitment in Schelling's
   strategic sense (it binds Laura so that others can rely on her), not a self-control device: the leading definition
   excludes arrangements with "a strategic purpose with respect to others" (row 10). Before 2031 it is SOFT: Laura can
   sell an ETF on any trading day and nothing outside her enforces the rule (rows 10, 11, 32). The bonds bought, not the
   sentence written, do the binding. From the 2031 announcement, her reputation before co-sponsors makes it harder
   (rows 2, 3).
2. **"Its value is credibility": KEEP, and add its price.** Credibility against flexibility is the textbook trade
   (Lohmann, row 5). NUMBERS 8c prices it: about $8,000 lower in the middle and behind the rival in about 60% of house
   paths, for a bottom that the rival misses in 18.7% of house paths and 10.7% of history (MODEL, row 30). The case
   names the cost of the opposite: over-promising "could damage her credibility" (case p.3).
3. **"And protection from panic": CHANGE.** Panic selling is rarer and its measured cost smaller than the usual
   figures: about 70% of Vanguard investors changed nothing in the 2020 crash, and the "1.2 points a year" timing cost
   falls to 0.10% a year on re-examination (rows 16-19). The structure also removes the occasion for panic: held to the
   end, the ladder and the floor give a market fall nothing to act on (A8). The rule's second job is protection from
   second thoughts in BOTH directions, including the optimist who will not move half of VT in a rising 2030 (row 16:
   optimists sold most in 2020; Giglio et al.).
4. **"The fields that manage long promises use the same device": KEEP, with three differences.** Central banks
   (rules with escape clauses), the gold standard, UK and US pension de-risking, buy-ins, the CFA IPS standard and
   Norway's fund all use it (rows 4-9, 20-27). Differences: they trigger on prices or funded status where FINAL
   triggers on dates; regulators, trustees or courts stand behind them where nothing stands behind Laura; and they
   state a path back after an exception, which FINAL does not (row 9).

Claim as changed (research wording for the store, not report prose): buying the floor in January 2028 and naming in
advance the only two reasons to change are commitment devices of the kind central banks, pension schemes and the IPS
standard use. Their main value is credibility with co-sponsors, a bottom Laura can name three years ahead that markets
cannot lower, paid for with flexibility (about $8,000 in the middle, MODEL). Before 2031 the commitment is soft (Laura
could undo it); from the 2031 announcement it rests on her reputation. Protection from panic is real but secondary,
and works against over-confidence as much as fear.

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. Owning, not saying: the floor is a commitment in Schelling's sense
- **What is it?** Schelling (1956, 1960): bargaining power can come from binding yourself, a "voluntary but
  irreversible sacrifice of freedom of choice"; a promise to a partner needs enforcement or an audience; "Talk can be
  cheap when moves are not". Rows 1-3. The self-control literature defines its devices as having no strategic purpose
  towards others (Bryan, Karlan & Nelson 2010, row 10).
- **Did it work?** As theory it is mainstream (Schelling, Nobel 2005; Kydland & Prescott, Nobel 2004, row 4); in
  practice, central-bank independence and gold convertibility are the standard cases (rows 5-7).
- **How is Laura like it?** Co-sponsors' gifts are outside her control, hers outside theirs (Schelling's promise). Buying
  the floor in Jan 2028 and moving half of VT before 2031 are moves, not words: "whatever figure you choose is money you
  already own" (IPS l.15). The case names the audience and the penalty: over-promising "could damage her credibility and
  lose the confidence or participation of co-sponsors" (case p.3).
- **How is she not?** The floor is an ETF she can sell any day (row 11), and before 2031 no one outside knows the plan,
  so until then the binding is soft (row 32). Co-sponsors are partners, not adversaries.
- **What Caplet takes:** call it a commitment to others made real by owning the money, not a self-control trick. The
  bonds bought do the work; the sentence names it.

### Card 2. The price of a promise: credibility bought with flexibility
- **What is it?** Lohmann (1992): a rule earns credibility and costs flexibility; the optimal design keeps a costly
  escape for extreme shocks (row 5). Contingent immunisation (Leibowitz & Weinberger 1982) is the opposite choice: keep
  the upside and lock the floor "only when absolutely necessary" (row 28).
- **Did it work?** In Laura's numbers, yes, at a stated price: locking in Jan 2028 rather than Jan 2031 costs about
  $8,000 in the middle and leaves FINAL behind in about 60% of house paths; waiting leaves her under the $110,000 bottom
  in 18.7% of house paths and 10.7% of history, and with about $26,000 from a July 1929 start (MODEL, NUMBERS 8c, row 30).
- **How is Laura like it?** She needs a number others can plan on three years ahead (the 2031 meetings), which is a
  credibility problem, not a return problem.
- **How is she not?** A central bank's escape clause is a choice it may make; Laura's two reasons are events. And a
  bond manager using contingent immunisation answers to no one until the horizon; Laura's floor is announced midway.
- **What Caplet takes:** the trade has a textbook name and a measured price. The judge who asks "why not wait and lock
  in later, like contingent immunisation?" gets NUMBERS 8c.

### Card 3. The two reasons are escape clauses, and after January 2028 only one is live
- **What is it?** Rules with escape clauses: the gold standard was suspended only in "a wartime emergency" and restored
  at the old price (Bordo & Kydland, row 6); clauses work when outsiders can verify the emergency (Bordo & Rockoff, row 7);
  lenders forgive "excusable" default in "identifiably bad states of the world" (Grossman & Van Huyck, row 8); the IMF's
  test asks for few, clear triggers outside the government's control and a path back (row 9).
- **Did it work?** The contingent gold rule held for the core countries for about a century; where it was only "a
  desirable goal", it gave no credibility (row 6).
- **How is Laura like it?** Two named triggers, and the one tempting non-trigger ruled out in writing ("A fall in the
  market is not one of them"). Because no money moves before 2033 except the two deposits (case p.2), a missed deposit
  can only happen in Jan 2028, before the floor exists. From then on the only exit is a US Treasury default: outside her
  control, public, and the single state in which no Treasury-based plan could pay (row 31).
- **How is she not?** No path back is stated (IMF iii); the deposit trigger is partly in Laura's own hands; a smaller
  deposit is not named (CANON D9). No authority checks that a trigger really happened.
- **What Caplet takes:** the two reasons pass the IMF test where it matters, at the 2031 meetings. Risk to note, not a
  change: the Final Report can show what the plan's existing order does after a missed or smaller deposit (CANON A34,
  D9) without adding a rule.

### Card 4. Pension journey plans: Laura's reliability period ends the month the floor is bought
- **What is it?** UK law (SI 2024/462, reg 5) defines "low dependency": assets resilient enough that "further employer
  contributions are not expected to be required". TPR's code splits the journey into a "reliability period" of certain
  sponsor cash and the time after; risk should fall as the relevant date nears; plans are linear, horizon (de-risk "at
  once, at the relevant date") or stepped (rows 22-23). US plans de-risk at funded-status triggers, sometimes as a
  ratchet (row 25); UK schemes buy the promise outright from insurers, £47.8bn in 2024 (row 26).
- **Did it work?** It is now law and market practice: TPR estimates around 60% of surveyed schemes are in surplus on a
  buy-out basis (row 26).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her "sponsor" is her career, and its last cash into the plan is the Jan 2028 deposit, so her
  reliability period ends the month the floor is bought. FINAL is a stepped plan; the rival is the horizon plan.
- **How is she not?** Trustees can "push back the relevant date in the event of adverse experience" (row 24); Laura's
  2031 meetings cannot move. Pensions trigger on prices; FINAL on dates. No regulator reviews Laura's plan.
- **What Caplet takes:** the pension vocabulary fits FINAL closely (stepped plan, date-triggered ratchet, home-made
  buy-in), and the one difference, a date she cannot move, is the reason to buy the floor early.

### Card 5. Panic, measured: smaller than the folklore, and it runs both ways
- **What is it?** In the 2020 crash about 70% of Vanguard investors changed nothing; the average cut to equities was
  about one point; the optimists sold most (row 16). True panic sales cluster in crashes and cost most through staying
  out afterwards (row 17). Morningstar's 1.2-points-a-year "gap" (row 18) shrinks to 0.10% a year for timing on
  re-examination (row 19). The CFA standard still names emotional responses in market disruption as the IPS's main job
  (rows 20-21). The best-known lab study of self-imposed deadlines is retracted (row 14).
- **Did it work?** Written rules do appear to help: Norway's 2007 rule carried it through 2008 (row 27). The size of
  the panic cost for a typical investor is disputed.
- **How is Laura like it?** Only VT can be panic-sold at a loss; the ladder and the floor are held to the end (CANON A8;
  TNA: "we'll simply ignore price noise in the interim"). The structure removes the occasion for panic as much as it
  forbids it.
- **How is she not?** She is one person with a written plan and an adviser, not an average brokerage account. Her bigger
  temptation may be optimism: keeping all of VT in a rising 2030 instead of moving half (the NUMBERS 8c rival, which wins
  in about 60% of paths).
- **What Caplet takes:** recast "protection from panic" as protection from second thoughts in both directions, with
  credibility as the main value. If a cost figure is used, give 1.2% and 0.10% together, not 1.2% alone.

### Card 6. Where the analogy breaks: Laura's own discretion and no regulator
- **What is it?** Every institution above has something outside the decision-maker: a central bank's legal mandate
  (Lohmann), a fiscal council (IMF), a regulator (TPR, DNB), an insurer (buy-ins), a ministry and parliament (Norway). The self-control literature warns that a weak commitment can be worse than none: 55% of clients broke
  contracts with self-chosen penalties (John, row 13). Elster: in politics people bind others, not themselves (row 15).
- **Did it work?** Where nothing outside enforces the rule, the evidence is mixed: Ashraf et al. found a lock helped
  savers (row 12); John found self-chosen weak locks harmed most users (row 13).
- **How is Laura like it?** She adopts the plan, and from 2031 the co-sponsors become the outside party: her reputation
  with them is the enforcement (rows 3, 32).
- **How is she not?** Before 2031 Laura can rewrite the plan at will; Caplet's rule binds her for the co-sponsors'
  benefit (Seip's pattern), and the CFA standard expects her adviser to review it as markets change (row 20).
- **What Caplet takes:** state the plan's commitment honestly: real from the day bonds are bought, soft as a written
  rule until 2031, reputational after. Risk to note (row 20): the IPS names change triggers but no review; a yearly
  check that changes nothing is consistent with it (CANON A34).

### Card 7. Institutions that held a rule or bent a promise: Norway and the Netherlands
- **What is it?** Norway set a 60% equity rule in 2007 and bought more than NOK 1 trillion of shares through the 2008-09
  fall (row 27). Dutch funds that cut pensions and stopped indexation after 2008 lost members' trust; the new law moves
  to rules that promise less (row 29).
- **Did it work?** Norway's rule held and its governor credited rebalancing rules; Dutch trust fell with cuts (DNB WP
  783: more indexation, more trust).
- **How is Laura like it?** The 2031 range is a long promise to people who decide whether to join. The Dutch lesson is
  to promise firmly only what cannot bend; FINAL promises the bottom "without doubt" and lets the top move with markets
  until 2031 (IPS l.15; TNA note on VT).
- **How is she not?** Norway's rule bought shares in a fall; Laura's does nothing in a fall and sells half of VT on a
  date. Pension members cannot leave; co-sponsors can simply not give.
- **What Caplet takes:** two real institutions, one that kept a rule through a crash and one that lost trust by bending
  promises, bracket the case for naming a floor and owning it early.

## (c) What would surprise a judge

1. **After January 2028 only one of the two reasons can still happen.** A missed deposit can only occur in Jan 2028
   (case p.2), before the floor exists; every promise in 2031 has a single exit, a US Treasury default, which is outside
   Laura's control, visible to all and the one state no Treasury plan could survive. It meets the IMF's escape-clause
   test and Grossman & Van Huyck's "excusable" test exactly when the co-sponsors hear the range (rows 8, 9, 31).
2. **Laura's reliability period ends the month her floor is bought.** In UK pension terms her sponsor's last certain
   cash is the Jan 2028 deposit; the code would expect low dependency from then, which is what the floor purchase does
   (rows 22-23).
3. **The textbook floor technique would have waited.** Contingent immunisation locks in "only when absolutely
   necessary"; that is the rival design, and NUMBERS 8c prices the choice: about $8,000 in the middle against an 18.7%
   chance (house) of missing the stated bottom (rows 28, 30).
4. **By its own leading definition, the floor is not a "commitment device".** Bryan, Karlan & Nelson exclude
   arrangements with a strategic purpose towards others; the floor's purpose is the co-sponsors. It is Schelling's
   commitment, not Ulysses' mast (rows 1, 10, 15).
5. **Panic is rarer and cheaper than the usual figure.** About 70% of Vanguard investors changed nothing in the 2020
   crash, the optimists sold most, and the "1.2 points a year" timing cost becomes 0.10% on re-examination (rows 16-19).
6. **The famous lab study of self-imposed deadlines was retracted in September 2026** after a failed replication (row 14).
7. **Norway kept its rule through 2008 and bought more than NOK 1 trillion of shares**; its governor later said many
   investors "waited for a long time, probably too long" (row 27).
