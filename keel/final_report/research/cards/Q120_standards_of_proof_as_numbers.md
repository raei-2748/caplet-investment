- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** turning a verbal standard of certainty into a number that strangers read the same way. The law argued it first (Hale's 5:1 and Blackstone's 10:1 ratios; Kaplan's 1968 formula turned a ratio into a threshold) and measured it first (Simon and Mahan, 1971; Judge Weinstein's poll of his own bench in *Fatico*, 1978; McCauliff's survey of 171 federal judges, 1982). The first regulator to write "a high degree of confidence" beside a number was the SEC, for oil and gas reserves (2008 rule: at least 90%). Intelligence (Kent, 1964; the UK yardstick), climate science (IPCC) and accounting (IFRS's likelihood words, measured by the Korean and Australian standard setters in 2016) followed with scales.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE (the numbers hold; the sentence must be rebuilt around IPS l.9).**
   - About 90% is the floor of "high" wherever a profession writes a number down. Federal judges put "beyond a reasonable doubt" at 90.28% on average (171 judges, 1981; McCauliff Table 8), and 124 trial judges put it at 90.1% thirty years later (SD 7.0; Hansberry et al., 2016). The SEC defines "reasonable certainty" as "a high degree of confidence" and, for probabilistic estimates, "at least a 90% probability" (17 CFR 210.4-10(a)(24)). The OCC tells bank examiners to sample at "no less than 90 percent" confidence, "conventionally used as a minimum". Audit firms test controls at 90-95% confidence, "consistent with ... a high level of assurance".
   - The top words start at 95%: "almost certain" is ≈95% to <100% on the UK yardstick (PHIA, 2025) and 95-99% on ICD 203 (card Q4c row 4). Regulators of money set aside against a promise go higher and name a horizon: a Dutch pension fund must hold enough that "met een zekerheid van 97½ procent" it does not fall below its liabilities within one year (Pensioenwet art. 132(2): "with a certainty of 97½ percent"; the minister: "eens in de 40 jaar", once in 40 years); Solvency II 99.5% a year (Q8b); Basel 99.9% a year (Q74).
   - The first plan's 96.8 in 100 (6,453 short of 200,000 simulated markets, 1 in 31.0; NUMBERS 8s) clears the average judge, every one of the nine judges in *Fatico* who gave a number (76% to 95%), the SEC's 90% and the OCC's floor, and both intelligence scales would call it "almost certain". It fails for the 21 of 171 federal judges (about 1 in 8, Keel arithmetic) who said "beyond a reasonable doubt" means 100%. The per-year regulatory levels measure a different event (falling below liabilities, then a recovery plan) over a different horizon, so they are context, never a pass or a fail.
   - What changes: "would pass a judge's 'beyond reasonable doubt'" becomes "clears the bar most professions write down for 'high'", and "was still not the plan, because the same deposits could buy none" becomes two sentences in the right order: the team dropped the plan for how a writer is paid (IPS l.9, in the team's words; CANON A3), and the check (27 Sep) then showed what the change bought: about 1 in 31 against none from January 2028 (NUMBERS 8s, 2).
   - It also fits the IPS's own words: "You asked that the payments be met with a 'high degree of certainty', so we decided to take that one step further" (IPS l.11). The professions' numbers say how high "high" is; "one step further" is the escrow standard (all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing; NUMBERS 2).
   - **Judge's note:** a federal judge's "beyond a reasonable doubt" averages 90%, the SEC's "high degree of confidence" is 90%, and British intelligence calls anything from about 95% "almost certain". The first plan's 96.8 in 100 cleared all three. The team still dropped it, for how a writer is paid, and the check found the same two deposits could leave no payment short from January 2028.
2. **Claim 2: KEEP, with a legal pedigree and one limit.**
   - The Supreme Court: a standard of proof "serves to allocate the risk of error between the litigants and to indicate the relative importance attached to the ultimate decision" (Addington v. Texas, 1979). Justice Harlan: the choice "should, in a rational world, reflect an assessment of the comparative social disutility of each" error (In re Winship, 1970). Tribe (1971, as quoted in two later works): the trial's aim is "the greatest certainty that seems reasonably attainable". The decision formula makes it arithmetic: a threshold of 1/(1 + cost ratio) gives 83% for Hale's 5:1 and 91% for Blackstone's 10:1 (Hamer, 2004).
   - Money people do the same. The OCC: "The confidence level should increase as the potential impact increases." Audit firms ask 30% confidence of a sample "when analytical procedures are effective and inherent and control risk are assessed as being low" and 95% for significant risks. The European Commission's audit authorities: at least 60% when a system gives high assurance, at least 90% when it gives low. The CPSC refused to fix "high degree of assurance" at 95% at all and defined it by its evidence.
   - So "high" is set by what an error would cost and by what can be had. For Laura both point one way: IPS l.11 calls the operating commitment "fixed and non-negotiable", and the same deposits bought into Treasuries first leave none short from January 2028 at a price that, at today's forwards, comes back to her all but $31 of fund fees (NUMBERS 8h; card Q87).
   - The limit: within one kind of decision the law fixes the bar and does not let it slide with each case. England's Court of Appeal deprecates telling jurors that "sure" differs from "certain", and jurors should be told "the standard of proof is not variable", though 18.7% of 1,117 potential jurors thought a murder needs more (City study). So "relative" means: chosen once, against the stakes and what is attainable, then held. That is what the report does (one standard, NUMBERS 2), never a standard re-set path by path.
   - **Judge's note:** the law never fixed "certain" at one number. It sets the bar by what being wrong would cost, and Tribe called the goal "the greatest certainty that seems reasonably attainable". Laura's two deposits could attain more than the first plan did, which is why its 96.8 in 100 was a price, not a pass.
3. **Claim 3: KEEP, sharpened: the number beside the word where there is a forecast; the basis where there is a construction.**
   - The spread is wide in every profession measured. No federal judge put "beyond a reasonable doubt" below 50% and 21 of 171 put it at 100% (McCauliff); the ten judges in *Fatico* from 76% to 95% and one "Cannot Estimate Numerically"; Judge Weinstein: "This wide variation confirms the wisdom of Maimonides". Of 1,178 NSW jurors, 55.4% read the phrase as "sure", 22.9% "almost sure", 11.6% "very likely" and 10.1% "pretty likely" (BOCSAR, 2008). Lay jurors and students put the civil standard near 7.5 in 10 where judges put it at 5.5 (Simon and Mahan): readers squeeze the scale toward the middle. Australian accountants read IFRS's "probable" at 62.0% and Korean at 71.3%; across earlier studies auditors' means for "probable" ran from 0.40 to 0.78.
   - The framing moves the number as much as the reader does: one instruction ("proof ... willing to act") was read at 89.1%, the other ("doubt ... hesitate") at 39.9% (Dhami, Lundrigan and Mueller-Johnson).
   - The professions' fix where outsiders read the words: the number beside the word (the SEC's 90%; IPCC's ranges; the UK yardstick, printed with the assessment; an accounting experiment in which a numeric scale "reduces interpersonal variability"). Where a number would mislead, they state the basis instead: the CPSC's "evidence-based demonstration"; the UK uses words because "most intelligence judgements are not based on quantitative data"; and the US forensic science commission advised courts not to require "a reasonable scientific certainty".
   - For Laura: a number beside the word where the claim is a forecast (the first plan's 1 in 31; the before-the-move spreads in Table 2's note); the basis where the claim is a construction (all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing; both ends of the range owned before they are stated), with no percentage label in 3c (card Q74; Kent's "statement of fact", card Q4c row 3).
   - **Judge's note:** one judge in eight told a 1981 survey that "beyond a reasonable doubt" means 100%; Judge Weinstein wrote that it "can never be set at certainty or 100% probability" because a court cannot buy that much certainty. Laura's deposits can buy it for the payments, which is why her report can say "certain" there and needs a number only where it forecasts.
4. **Claim 4: KILL as worded; CHANGE to "the nearest regulators split between a number and a method".**
   - No regulator found defines the case's exact words, "a high degree of certainty". The nearest twins: the SEC's "high degree of confidence" (a number, 90%, and a plain gloss, "much more likely to be achieved than not"); the Dutch Pensions Act's "zekerheid van 97½ procent" (a certainty written as a number, per year); the CPSC's "high degree of assurance" (a method; it "decided against" 95%); the PCAOB's reasonable assurance ("less than 100 percent. But how much less is not stated").
   - The report can take one of each: the method (the escrow standard) as the definition, a count as its evidence (none short in every house path and in all 585 historical rate windows from January 2028; NUMBERS 8s, 2), and the first plan's 1 in 31 as the comparison that shows what "one step further" bought.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 40 (3f's second moment; aha row 10).** The professions' own bars (a judge's 90, the SEC's 90, "almost certain" from 95) all pass 96.8 in 100, so a judge sees that "high" did not rule the first plan out: a writer's income did, and the check priced what the change bought. This is the "I had it backwards" in version B, now with sources.
2. **Row 15 (3a).** The SEC writes "a high degree of confidence" beside "at least a 90% probability": the closest any regulator comes to the case's words, and a clean way to show the report's standard sits above it ("one step further", IPS l.11).
3. **Row 14 with row 9 (3a, claim 2).** Tribe's "the greatest certainty that seems reasonably attainable" and the Supreme Court's "relative importance attached to the ultimate decision" make "measured against what the same money can buy" a borrowed legal idea, not a debating move.
4. **Row 5 with row 2 (3a or 3f).** Judge Weinstein: a court can "never" demand 100% because it would acquit everyone; one federal judge in eight still said 100%. Laura's deposits can buy certainty for the payments without giving up the top, which is the plan's point in one contrast.
5. **Row 18 (3c, 3a).** The CPSC defines "high degree of assurance" by its evidence and "decided against" 95%: a regulator's precedent for stating the basis, not a percentage label, for what is owned.
