- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Co-sponsors will hear the bottom, $95,000, as the promise."
  2. "Calling the range 'certain' tells co-sponsors that both ends are safe."
  3. "'$115,000, or $95,000 if ...' is clearer than '$95,000 to $115,000'."
  4. "A range needs a confidence number (for example '90% confident') to be believed."
  5. "'Up to $115,000' is a safe way to say the top."
- **Problem class:** saying a bounded amount so that each listener hears the point the speaker means: which bound a word picks out, which point of a bare range a reader counts, and how the stated basis changes both. **Field that solved it first:** accounting standard-setting ruled first on which point of a range a reader should book (FASB Interpretation No. 14, 1976: the minimum when no point is better); advertising regulators then ruled on which point a reader hears (FTC 2012 on "up to"; the UK ASA's 2018 broadband rule); the Oslo group of psychologists (Teigen, Juanchich, Halberg, Løhre, Hohle) explains why.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.** No single end is "heard". It depends on the reader and on the word attached.
   - A bare number in a gain frame is read as "at least" by 58% and 54% (Mandel 2015).
   - "Certain" plus an amount is read as "at least" by 74.5-78.7% (Juanchich et al. 2013).
   - "Up to" is read as the full amount by 45.6% (FTC 2012).
   - Listeners prefer the figure that is worst for them (Halberg et al. 2009).
   - Readers with a stake read an unexplained range their own way (Dieckmann et al. 2017; EFSA 2019).
   - So cautious co-sponsors will count $95,000 and hopeful ones will hear $115,000, unless Laura names each end.
   - **Judge's note:** professionals split as well. US GAAP books the minimum of a range when no point is better (ASC 450-20-30-1) and IFRS books the midpoint (IAS 37.39), so the same "$95,000 to $115,000" counts as $95,000 or $105,000 (Keel arithmetic). Once one figure is named as the better estimate, both standards book that figure.
2. **Claim 2: KILL as worded.** "Certain" attached to an amount makes listeners hear a minimum, so "certain to be $115,000" is heard as "$115,000 or more". The contribution is never more than the stated top (NUMBERS 4; CANON D12).
   - The authors advise avoiding "certain" and "possible" with amounts (Juanchich et al. 2013).
   - Fix: attach the confidence word (certain, guaranteed, owned) to the holdings, not to the amount: both figures are paid by Treasuries she already owns.
   - **Judge's note:** the word a reader expects to make a top safe is the word that turns it into a floor.
3. **Claim 3: KILL after the move; KEEP for the report's view from today.** "X, or Y if Z" needs a true Z.
   - After the move no event separates the two ends. Both are Treasuries held to late 2032, and a missed deposit is already past.
   - The funds' own costs above the 0.5% cut lower the top by about $120 for each 0.1% (SKELETON 3c; NUMBERS 4). They never take it to $95,000.
   - Before the move the Z is real: if world shares fell to zero, the range would be one figure, $95,000 (NUMBERS 4, deletion test). The report can use the "if" form for the plan seen from today. Laura's 2031 words cannot.
   - Two named figures, each with its basis, is a recognised form: the SEC permits "several projections based on varying assumptions" (17 CFR 229.10(b)(2)(i)); Norway gives Parliament a ceiling and the agency a steering figure. Laura's two figures rest on ownership, not on assumptions.
   - **Judge's note:** the cold readers' question ("if both ends are owned, why a range?") has a plain answer. The bottom is what has been owned since January 2028 whatever shares did. The top is what the move added. The case asks for a range, and she intends the top.
4. **Claim 4: KILL.** A percentage invites the uniform, bell or U-shaped guesses that readers put on any range (Dieckmann et al. 2015, 2017; EFSA 2019). Laura's figures have no spread to describe: both are owned and the contribution is the top.
   - Best and worst case together raised high trust by 7.9 points over a single most-likely figure (N = 1,174). The gain vanished when an open-ended "cannot be quantified" caveat was added (Howe et al. 2019).
   - So: two figures, each named, then stop. This fits Book 19: no default caveat after the figures.
5. **Claim 5: KILL.** "Up to" is read as the full amount and as what most people get. A disclosure did not significantly weaken that reading (FTC 2012; replicated for a loan's "as low as" rate, Gutenberg 2016).
   - Laura's top would pass the strictest test any regulator has set for "up to": the FTC's "likely to achieve ... under normal circumstances", the ASA's 10% of items and the ASA's 50% of users. It is paid in every tested path (NUMBERS 4).
   - But "up to" sounds like a hedge on a figure that is not hedged, and it hides the bottom that cautious co-sponsors want to count.
   - **Judge's note:** hers would be the rare "up to" that is paid in full, which is a reason to say the figure plainly instead.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 2 (with row 1), 3c's confidence sentence.** Three in four listeners hear "certain" plus an amount as "at least". The word a reader expects to make the top safe turns it into a floor, so "certain" goes on the Treasuries, not on the sum.
2. **Rows 21-22, 3c (an aha for a finance judge).** US GAAP would book a bare "$95,000 to $115,000" at $95,000 and IFRS at $105,000. Naming $115,000 as the figure she intends makes both book what she will pay.
3. **Row 32, 3c's answer to the cold readers.** After the move there is no true "if" between the two ends, so Laura's words are a range with named ends. The "if" form belongs only to the report's view from today, through the deletion test.
4. **Row 10 (with row 11), 3d's words for co-sponsors.** 45.6% read "up to 47%" as 47%, and a disclosure did not significantly change that. Hers would be the rare "up to" that is paid in full, which is a reason to say the figure plainly.
5. **Row 17, 3d and 3e.** Both bounds raised trust by 7.9 points, and an open-ended caveat erased the gain. Two named figures, then stop: this is evidence for Book 19's ban on a default caveat after Treasury figures.

## Gaps

- No study of donors, foundations or public co-funders hearing a founder's pledge stated as a range. Every reading above comes from consumers, investors, lab vignettes or regulators.
- No study comparing "Y to X" with "X, or Y if Z" directly. Row 32's verdict is Keel inference, not a test.
- "Guaranteed" plus an amount was not found tested. Row 1 covers "certain" only, so whether "guaranteed $115,000" is also heard as "at least" is open.
- Full texts not read: Dieckmann et al. 2015 and 2017, Biswas and Burton 1993, Halberg et al. 2009, Hohle and Teigen 2018, Howe et al. 2019, Loschelder et al. 2016, and Teigen and Jørgensen 2005 (title only).
- Halberg and Teigen (2009, JBDM 22, "When are lower interval bounds preferred to upper bounds?") was seen only as a citation in Teigen (2023), which reports that "more than" statements vastly outnumber "less than" ones for money, distances and durations.
- Budescu's IPCC work on numeric ranges (as distinct from verbal terms) was not newly read; Q4c and Q60 r37 hold the verbal-term and three-figure tests.
- Løhre and Teigen on lower and upper bounds was not found beyond Q19 r26.
- No IOSCO guidance on stating ranges was found. US and UK sources were found instead (SEC Item 10, FCA, ESAs).
- How a nonprofit books a conditional or ranged pledge (ASC 958-605) was not read. Rows 21-22 rest on loss-provision rules by analogy.
- A cold-reader test of the labelled-ends wording against a bare range is the next step (Q60's methods).
