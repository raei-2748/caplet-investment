# Q7c. Endowment spending rules: what they teach about Laura's rule, and where the analogy breaks

- **Date:** Sun 15 Nov 2026, Sydney (sources read Sat 14 Nov to Sun 15 Nov 2026).
- **Report parts:** 3a (the commitment, the operating reserve, why only two reasons), 3b (her facility contribution and her flexibility: how a funder that draws on a risky pool treats a building), 3c (when the bottom and the top are owned), 3d (reading a co-sponsor's pledge), 3e (what would not change the plan). Most rows are for the store only.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.
- **Not repeated:** card Q2c rows 1-35 (Yale's rule in its 2010-11 words; Yale's and Harvard's payout cuts after 2008-09; Brown et al.'s 20-22bp hoarding and faculty cuts; Harvard FY2009's operating cash, swaps and debt; Brandeis; NYC Opera; JEHT; UPMIFA's spending factors and its limited-duration comment; Tobin's "guardians" quote; Merton; Dybvig; the CFA split), card Q4b rows 4, 5, 8, 11 (the 5% foundation minimum and payout behaviour), card Q7 (precommitment devices, pension journey plans, no outside enforcer of Laura's rule), card Q3b (forced sellers), and store rows H3-064 (Yale's 4.0-6.5% band), H3-082 and H8-113 (Allston; W-05's caveat on cause), H6-065, H3-109 and Q18-001 to Q18-004 (pledges, recipient side), Q7-009 (escape-clause design), Q15-013, H5-124 and Q9b-005.
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Endowment spending rules are written rules with written exceptions" (Tobin 1974; the Yale/Stanford hybrid rule; UPMIFA's 7% presumption; the NACUBO-Commonfund average effective rate). What triggers allow a departure, and who decides?
  2. "Smoothing rules and fixed dedications are different families: a spending rule draws from a risky pool; Laura's plan buys each promise first." Does the comparison help a judge or mislead, and what one sentence does it support?
  3. "A funder drawing on a risky pool cuts capital projects first in a fall; Laura's bottom is owned before she states it."
  4. "Foundations under the 5% rule pledge multi-year grants and count the pledges as owned."
- **Problem class:** a payout rule over a pool that can fall, with its escape clause: who may depart from the rule, on what trigger, and what is protected first when the pool falls. **Field that solved it first:** university endowment management (Tobin's 1974 frame; the Yale and Stanford hybrid rule), then uniform charity law (UPMIFA 2006, whose 7% presumption comes from a 1975 Massachusetts statute) and US private-foundation tax law (IRC 4942 and its set-aside rule). The fixed, dated side of the contrast belongs to the bond desk's dedicated portfolio (card Q2c row 32; card Q3b).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The rules are written. The departures from them mostly are not: a standing board decides each year, and no list of triggers is written in advance.
  - **Yale.** The formula is written (Policy 2202): 80% of last year's spending plus 20% of a 5.25% target on the value two years back, adjusted for inflation, inside 4.0%-6.5%. The Yale Corporation sets the target. The policy's only written exceptions are distributions to individual gifts (the Controller's Office, consulting the Provost, General Counsel or an Officer) and the deficit-fund line: spending continues while a fund is above 70% of its historic dollar value (rows 1-2).
  - **Yale in 2025.** A new 8% tax, which Yale reckons equals a 12.5% cut in endowment spending, did not change the rule: "Nothing about market volatility or the need to cushion the budget from it has changed, so we do not plan to change the spending rate or the smoothing rule" (row 3).
  - **Stanford.** The trustees set the payout every year at their February or April meeting from a "tentative" figure that the formula gives. In 2009 they cut faster than the formula (10% in FY2010, then 15%) because the rule "was never designed to deal with one-year downturns that are more than two standard deviations from the mean". The Faculty Senate was told they had "Suspended endowment 'smoothing rule'". The trigger was named after the fall (row 4). Today's rule has a written corridor of 4.25%-6.25% with fallback formulas (row 5).
  - **Harvard.** The formula is "presumptive guidance". The Corporation approves each year's distribution plus "decapitalizations": $47.8m in FY2025 and $38.7m in FY2024, "typically one-time or time-limited" (row 6).
  - **All US colleges (NCSE FY2025, 657 institutions, $944.3bn).** The average effective spending rate was 4.9% against an average policy rate of 4.7%. Of the $33.4bn withdrawn, 83.5% was within policy and 5.3% was "special appropriations above the normal spending policy": about $1.8bn, Keel arithmetic (row 7).
  - **Who controls.** "The university usually retains direct control over endowment payout rates" (Brown et al., 2014). After losses the departures go downward (row 8; Q2c row 8 has the size).
  - **The law.** UPMIFA's 7% presumption is optional, left out by most states (row 10), and rebuttable on the institution's own showing that "circumstances in a particular year" justify more. The law judges the decision "not by hindsight" (s.7; row 12). Changing a donor's restriction needs the donor's consent, a court with the Attorney General notified, or, for a fund under $25,000 and over 20 years old, the Attorney General's silence after 60 days (s.6; row 11).
  - **The contrast with IPS l.17.** IPS l.17 is the reverse pattern. It names two reasons in advance, names a non-reason (a market fall), and has no board to depart from it. It is stricter on paper than any endowment rule read here, and it has no outside guard: no trustees, court or Attorney General (card Q7).
- **Claim 2: KEEP, as a contrast only, scoped.** It helps a judge in one clause and misleads if it goes further.
  - **Why endowments smooth.** About two-thirds of Yale's costs are pay, and returns reach the budget "two fiscal years" later. Endowments smooth an unknown, rising flow (row 14). Laura's ten are fixed nominal sums on fixed dates: there is nothing to smooth.
  - **Tobin's aim, taken strictly.** In Woglom's (2003) reading it points toward "the safest assets" (row 15). Taken over a fixed ten years, it points to buying each payment.
  - **The law.** UPMIFA's own comment says a fund spent over ten years must spend above 7%, and that a building fund might take rules "not subject to UPMIFA" (row 17).
  - **Scale.** Kresge's grant promises are 2.7% of its investments (Keel arithmetic, row 19). Laura's ten payments, $500,000 nominal, are 111% of the $450,000 she deposits. A pool carries promises that are small beside it; Laura's are not, so each is bought.
  - **The sentence it supports (note form):** endowments steady a payout drawn from money that can fall, and their boards may depart from the rule each year; Laura's payments are each bought before they are owed, so there is nothing to steady, and her plan names its only two departures.
  - **Where it misleads:** calling the operating reserve an "endowment" or its payments a "spending rule"; any hint of perpetuity; offering her flexibility as an endowment for the residency (CANON A30, D12); carrying the law's word for spending ("prudent") onto the rule (row 20).
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** "Cuts capital projects first" holds only for projects not yet begun and not yet paid for, and the cause was the pool together with shut debt markets and slower gifts, not the pool alone.
  - **Yale, October 2008:** it would slow capital investment "before curtailing ongoing operations" (row 21).
  - **Yale, December 2008:** construction starts were deferred because "we will not be able to issue as much debt", and "we are not cancelling any projects that have been approved; we are simply delaying the initiation of construction" (row 22).
  - **Yale, February 2009:** "Before deciding to take further action to reduce the operating budget, we scrutinized our capital investment plans." It deferred up to $2bn over five years, then still cut pay and non-salary costs 7.5% each, because "capital delays alone will not be sufficient" (row 23).
  - **What went ahead at Yale:** projects under construction, urgent maintenance and "fully gift-funded projects such as the Art Gallery renovation" (row 24).
  - **Stanford, 2009:** "suspended or cancelled over $1 billion of planned construction projects", with 400-500 staff laid off (row 25).
  - **2025, a non-market shock:** both Yale and Harvard again deferred building projects among their first actions (row 27).
  - **Reworded claim:** a funder that draws on a risky pool defers the projects it has not yet paid for and finishes the ones already paid for. The floor is paid for in January 2028, three years before the bottom is stated (NUMBERS 4). The rival that waits to buy is under $95,000 in 15.8% of house paths and 9.1% of history windows (NUMBERS 8c) (row 28).
- **Claim 4: KILL as worded; CHANGE to "owed, not owned".** Read at source, foundations book an unconditional multi-year grant as a liability (grants payable, discounted to present value), never as money set apart. The money stays in the invested pool and is met from liquidity and credit lines. Conditional grants are not booked at all.
  - **Kresge 2025:** grants payable $104.2m net, discounted at 4.18%; about $41.7m of conditional grants not recorded; $1.20bn liquid within a year; $225m of undrawn credit lines (row 33). Gates, Wallace and Rockefeller report the same treatment (row 34).
  - **Tax law:** IRC 4942 counts a grant when it is paid (cash basis, row 30). The exception is an approved set-aside for a named project such as a building, paid within 60 months, which is "a bookkeeping entry ... signifying a pledge or obligation". The money set aside is still counted in the invested base for the 5% minimum (rows 31-32).
  - **Laura's order is the reverse:** the asset first, the figure after (row 35).

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. A written rule with an unwritten exit (rows 1-13)
- **What is it?** Endowment spending rules are written formulas: Yale's 80/20 rule inside 4.0%-6.5%, Stanford's corridor of 4.25%-6.25%, Harvard's "presumptive guidance". Above each formula sits a board that sets the payout every year: the Yale Corporation, Stanford's trustees at their February or April meeting, the Harvard Corporation. No list of triggers for departing from the rule was found in any text read. The law adds a soft 7% line that most states left out (rows 9-10) and outside guards (donor, court, Attorney General) only for donors' restrictions (row 11).
- **Did it work?** As a brake in ordinary years, yes: the average effective rate was 4.9% against a policy rate of 4.7% in FY2025, with 5.3% of withdrawals above policy (row 7). In a large fall the boards departed from their rules: Stanford cut faster than its formula in 2009 because the rule "was never designed" for a fall beyond two standard deviations (row 4). In 2025 Yale kept its rule through a tax worth a 12.5% cut in spending, saying the rule was about market volatility and that had not changed (row 3).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Both are rules written in advance to stop the present from spending the future. IPS l.17 names what is not a reason ("A fall in the market is not one of them"), in the same shape as Yale's 2025 answer.
- **How is it not?** Endowments name the formula and leave departures to a board. Laura's plan names its two departures (a missed 2028 deposit, a Treasury default) and has no board, no court and no Attorney General behind it (card Q7). Endowment boards departed in the very case Laura's rule excludes: a market fall.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a, inside "why only these two": the contrast in at most one clause (row 20). 3e: a market fall, a yield move or a co-sponsor's cut changes nothing; no trigger added (CANON A34). Row 12's "not by hindsight" can inform 3f's reflection on judging a rule by its day, as a note, not a citation of charity law as Laura's standard.

### Card 2. Smoothing a flow against buying each payment (rows 14-20)
- **What is it?** Smoothing exists because an endowment pays for a flow of unknown and rising size, mostly salaries: about two-thirds of Yale's costs (row 14). Tobin's formula was built "to avoid swings in income due to transient fluctuations in securities prices" (row 15). The payout from a smoothed pool is a maximum that an underwater fund can miss, and "Any missed payout cannot be made up later" (row 18).
- **Did it work?** For its purpose, mostly. It spread 2008-09 over several years (Q2c rows 3-7), at the cost of cuts that went on into FY2011.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Both protect future spending from present prices. Woglom (2003) shows that Tobin's equity aim, taken strictly, points to the safest assets (row 15). Over a fixed ten years that aim is met by the ladder.
- **How is it not?** Laura's ten payments are fixed in dollars and dated, so there is no flow to steady. Her promises are 111% of her deposits in nominal terms (row 19), against a foundation's 2.7% (Kresge) or a university's fraction of revenue, so a pool could not carry them. The law itself puts a ten-year spend-down (spending above 7%) and a building fund outside the endowment family (row 17).
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a: one clause of contrast at most, in the note form of row 20. The words to avoid: endowment or spending rule for the operating reserve (IPS l.17), any perpetuity, her flexibility as a residency endowment (CANON A30, D12), and the law's word for spending carried onto the rule. Exhibits: none.

### Card 3. Paid-for projects go ahead; unfunded ones wait (rows 21-25, 27, 28)
- **What is it?** Yale in 2008-09 and Stanford in 2009 deferred planned buildings before deeper operating cuts. Yale deferred up to $2bn over five years, Stanford over $1bn. They finished what was under construction or "fully gift-funded" (the Yale Art Gallery). In 2025 both Yale and Harvard again deferred building projects among their first moves, under a non-market shock.
- **Did it work?** It bought time, not enough on its own: "capital delays alone will not be sufficient", so Yale also cut non-faculty pay 7.5% (row 23), and Stanford laid off 400-500 staff (row 25). Approved projects were delayed, not cancelled (row 22).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her facility contribution is the capital-project side of her money. The floor that backs its bottom is paid for in January 2028, before it is stated in 2031 (NUMBERS 4). In the universities' terms it is the paid-for kind, which went ahead.
- **How is it not?** The universities' cause was the pool together with shut debt markets and slower gifts; Laura borrows nothing. Their buildings were their own; hers is a contribution to someone else's facility. Her money with the deferrable shape is the half promised to no one, which is never stated, so nothing is announced and then withdrawn (CANON A30). The claim "cuts capital projects first" is too broad: the universities cut what was not yet paid for.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3b or 3c, beside Harvard's Allston case (SKELETON 3b; store H8-113 with W-05's caveat): "defers what it has not yet paid for", never "cuts buildings first". NUMBERS 8c's rival (under $95,000 in 15.8% of house paths) is the number that shows what waiting to buy would cost. 3d: a co-sponsor's share drawn from a pool and not yet set aside is the deferrable kind (row 29).

### Card 4. After 2009 the universities walled operating cash off from the pool (row 26)
- **What is it?** Stanford borrowed $1bn in 2009 and held about $800m "in a segregated account of highly liquid securities, unless a true emergency demands their use". Harvard, whose operating cash sat inside the risky pool in 2009 (Q2c row 13), now keeps "liquidity outside of the endowment for operating purposes": $2.4bn at 30 June 2025.
- **Did it work?** It is untested by a fall of 2009's size. In FY2025 Harvard still issued $1.2bn of debt for liquidity and capital.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** The lesson is the same as IPS l.11: money that must be paid on a date is kept out of the risky pool.
- **How is it not?** Their wall holds about a year of operating cash; Laura's holds ten dated payments, each in its own Treasury. Both walls are policies, not law, and Laura herself could draw on hers (card Q3b row 33).
- **What Caplet takes.** 3a: a possible outside example for the wall, one clause, if 3a needs one beside the escrow standard. Not for the co-sponsor words.

### Card 5. Foundations: owed, not owned (rows 30-35)
- **What is it?** A foundation's unconditional multi-year grant is booked as grants payable, a liability at present value (Kresge discounts at the 3-month bill rate, 4.18%). A conditional grant is not booked at all (Kresge about $41.7m; Gates $2.6bn). Tax law counts a grant when it is paid; the exception is a set-aside for a named project such as a building, which is "a bookkeeping entry". The money set aside stays in the invested pool and still counts toward the 5% base (PLR 202527019).
- **Did it work?** For large foundations, yes: Kresge's promises are about 2.7% of its investments, with $1.20bn liquid within a year and $225m of credit lines (row 33). Where the pool itself failed, the promises failed with it (JEHT, Q2c row 24).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A co-sponsor's pledge to her facility, especially a matching one over a campaign of several years, is exactly the PLR's shape: dated, recorded, paid within five years.
- **How is it not?** Laura's order is the reverse: the asset first (January 2028), the figure after (2031). A foundation states the figure first and holds the asset in a pool it may also lose.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d: a question for co-sponsors' letters, unconditional or conditional, to sit with the store's Q18 rows on the recipient side. 3b: the contrast in one clause, if needed. The 5% minimum stays in Q4b row 5 and does not enter the report.

### Card 6. Yale's refusal to change its rule in 2025 (row 3)
- **What is it?** Facing an 8% endowment tax from July 2026 (about $300m a year, equal in Yale's reckoning to a 12.5% cut in endowment spending), Yale's provost wrote that the smoothing rule exists "to prevent market volatility from hurting our long-term ability to advance Yale's mission. Nothing about market volatility or the need to cushion the budget from it has changed, so we do not plan to change the spending rate or the smoothing rule."
- **Did it work?** Too early to judge. Yale adjusted the budget instead: 5% non-salary cuts, a smaller raise pool, a hiring pause and delayed buildings (row 27).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A rule kept because the shock is not the thing the rule was built for. IPS l.17 does the same in advance for a market fall.
- **How is it not?** Yale had other levers (a $2.3bn yearly endowment draw and a large budget). Before 2033 Laura's only spare money is her flexibility; after 2033 the ten are already bought.
- **What Caplet takes.** Store only, or 3e as a reading behind "what would not change the plan". Never as a claim that Laura's rule and Yale's are the same kind.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
- Stanford's president told the faculty in 2009 that "blindly applying" the smoothing rule would bring "more than five years of decreasing annual payouts". The trustees departed from the rule to cut faster, not to spend more (row 4).
- In 2025 Yale faced a tax equal to a 12.5% cut in its endowment spending and kept its rule unchanged, on the ground that the rule was built for market volatility and nothing about that had changed (row 3).
- In 2009 the Yale buildings that went ahead were the ones already paid for: projects under construction and "fully gift-funded projects such as the Art Gallery renovation". About $2bn of approved projects waited (row 24).
- US tax law lets a foundation count a building grant years before it pays by making "a bookkeeping entry", and the money set aside stays invested (rows 31-32).
- The uniform endowment law says in its own comment that a ten-year spend-down must spend above 7%, and that a building fund might take rules "not subject to UPMIFA" (row 17).
