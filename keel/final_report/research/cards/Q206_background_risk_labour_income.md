## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. A risk you cannot sell makes every other risk less welcome, even an unrelated one

- (a) Pratt and Zeckhauser (1987), then Kimball (1993), proved that a cautious person who is forced to carry one risk she cannot get rid of should take less of a second risk, even when the two have nothing to do with each other: in Kimball's words, "being forced to face an undesirable risk reduces the optimal investment in a risky security with an independent return", or more plainly, "risks should tend to crowd each other out". Economists call the risk she is stuck with "background risk", and a writer's career, which no one can buy from her or insure, is a textbook case of it.
- (b) So IPS l.9's conclusion (the portfolio should be "a counterbalance ... definitely not a contributor") needs only the words "one you cannot sell", not the word "shares": even if Laura's income never moved with share prices at all, a large income that arrives in uncertain lumps is a reason not to stack market risk on money she has promised. The judges' evidence that her trade and the share market parted in some years therefore weakens the IPS's metaphor, a career "in effect ... a large holding of shares", but not the decision it led to.
- (c) It breaks in two places, and both teach something. The theorem needs a person whose caution eases as she gets richer (in the jargon, decreasing absolute risk aversion and decreasing prudence; Guiso and Paiella's survey finds people are like this), and the measured effect for ordinary wage earners is small, about 2 percentage points less in shares for Italian households with above-average income uncertainty (Guiso, Jappelli and Terlizzese, as weighed by Fagereng, Guiso and Pistaferri 2018). It is large for two groups only, business owners, who "hold less wealth in stocks than other similarly wealthy households" (Heaton and Lucas 2000b), and people facing a small chance of a disastrous year (Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout 2005); Laura is in both, a sole trader paid in lumps whose one payment into the plan could fail to come.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.9's reason for l.11's split (promised money in Treasuries; "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares").
- **Report part:** 2.3, the name D4 asks for.
- **Source:** Kimball (1993) abstract and (1991) introduction; Pratt and Zeckhauser (1987); Gollier and Pratt (1996); Guiso et al. (1996); Fagereng et al. (2018); Heaton and Lucas (2000b); Cocco et al. (2005).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to the finance professor, who named it; what lands for the other judges is the move: the IPS's conclusion survives the loss of its weakest premise.

### Idea 2. A writer's income is neither a bond nor a share, and the textbook has an answer for that middle case

- (a) The textbook treats a steady salary like a bond, so a young earner should hold more shares; Viceira (2001) showed that as the income gets riskier, even with no link to the market, that extra room for shares shrinks "towards the level of retired investors", and only a positive link with share prices pushes it lower still.
- (b) That answers the bond judge's "idiosyncratic, bond-like human capital argues for more equities, not fewer": true for a salary, not for a writer whose advance arrives in two to six pieces, whose royalties come twice a year and whose speaking fees vanish first in a slump (Q12 rows 1, 8; Q95 row 12), so her career gives little reason to add shares and, being a business owner's income, some reason to hold fewer (Curcuru et al. 2004: business income has "higher volatility and higher correlation ... with stock returns" than wages).
- (c) It breaks because the textbook investor lives off her portfolio, while Laura's living costs are met outside it (case l.61-64) and the portfolio pays fixed promises, so the career theory cannot set the size of her share holding; the shares, 6.58% of the $450,000 plan (NUMBERS 8g), are what is left after the promises, as SKELETON 2.3 already says. The break tells the writers to use the career theory for direction only, never to explain the 7%.
- **Decision it serves:** the size of the share sleeve (VT bought from what the 2028 deposit leaves).
- **Report part:** 2.3, one clause; the rest to the store.
- **Source:** Viceira (2001) and (1999) abstracts; Campbell and Viceira (2002 draft), chapter 6; Curcuru et al. (2004).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree; it is the exact reply to attack 15's conclusion.

### Idea 3. The plan waits for the career's answer before it buys a single share

- (a) The background-risk theorems are about two risks held at the same time; Laura's plan holds no shares during 2027, while her 2028 deposit is still unknown (the first deposit buys the ten, IPS l.13), and buys its first share in January 2028, after the deposit has arrived, with only what is left once the payments' $39,162 and the floor are bought (NUMBERS 5).
- (b) So the plan never has to guess how her career moves with share prices, because the career's one payment into the plan is in hand before any share risk is taken, and the shares then shrink faster than the deposit: a deposit 25% smaller ($112,500) buys 42% less VT ($17,087 against $29,594; Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 5), a half-size deposit buys none, and only a deposit more than about three-quarters smaller (73.9%) leaves the payments short of their standard (NUMBERS 8u). A bad year for her career turns into less share risk and a smaller building contribution, never a missed payment, which is what the theory asks for, done by the calendar instead of by an estimate.
- (c) It breaks because her career goes on after 2028 and still carries risk for her own life; but the case puts her living costs outside the portfolio and lets no money in or out before 2033 except the two deposits (case l.61-64), so after January 2028 her career and the portfolio no longer touch, and the shares still held to 2033 are the half promised to no one (IPS l.15). The break shows where l.9's worry really lives: in one dated cash flow, the January 2028 deposit, which is exactly what "later and smaller" describes.
- **Decision it serves:** the order of purchase in January 2028 (IPS l.13-15: complete the ladder, then the late-2032 fund, then VT).
- **Report part:** 2.3 (one sentence after the name); 3e note 1.
- **Source:** IPS l.13-15; NUMBERS 5 ("The floor shrinks almost dollar for dollar" table; the later-curve note on money left over in January 2028) and 8u; Kimball (1993) for the theorem's "forced to face".
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge expects the team to estimate a correlation it cannot measure; the plan sidesteps it by buying shares only after the uncertain money has come in.

### Idea 4. Same shock, slower clock: 2020 read by the month, not the calendar year

- (a) The store's best counterexample to IPS l.9 says 2020 "hit the career while shares rose" (Census receipts of independent artists, writers and performers -12.4%, shares +18.02% for the year); read by the month, the S&P 500 fell 33.9% in 33 days, from its record close on 19 February to 23 March 2020, in the same weeks talks, fairs and launches were cancelled, and closed above its old record on 18 August 2020.
- (b) So the same shock hit both, but shares fell fast and recovered in six months, while a writer's new money stayed down for one to two years (arts sole proprietors' receipts -23.4% in 2020 and below 2019 still in 2021, Q148 rows 16-18; speakers about two years, Q12b row 12); 2008-09 has the same shape a year apart (shares -36.55% in 2008; one-person artists' and writers' receipts -7.8% in 2009, while shares rose 25.94%, Q148 row 5). The honest reading of l.9's "during that same period" is "in the same downturn, on a slower clock", and a slow clock is exactly what a deposit due on a fixed date has to fear.
- (c) It breaks because two episodes do not make a correlation, and other years ran the other way (2022: shares -18.04% while the occupation boomed; 2023: shares +26.06% while the employer wage bill fell 8.42%, Q148 rows 24-25), so the report claims no beta and no steady link, only that a recession "can" bring both, as 2008-09 and 2020 did. It also exposes a figure to change: the "-11.6% in 2009" in ATTACKS D4 and SKELETON 3e note 4 is the whole arts, entertainment and recreation sector, gyms included (card Q148); the writer's figure is -7.8%.
- **Decision it serves:** how the report reads IPS l.9's "during that same period" (CANON B4, PARTLY).
- **Report part:** 2.3 (the dated example); 3e note 1.
- **Source:** S&P Global (2020); History of Market (n.d.); Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (2020); Reuters (2020); Q148 rows 5, 16-18, 24-25; Q12b row 12; Damodaran's file via Q1.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha for the judges who cited 2020: their counterexample is a calendar-year mirage, and the month-by-month record supports the IPS's timing claim.

### Idea 5. Flexibility is insurance, and in a slump a writer's insurance has no buyer

- (a) Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992) showed that someone who can work more after a bad market year can hold more shares, because "labor supply flexibility creates a kind of insurance against adverse investment outcomes"; their model lets the wage risk be "perfectly hedged", and their own footnote expects the opposite, less risk-taking, "if his wage was very risky".
- (b) A writer can always write more, but the insurance pays only if someone buys the extra work: in November 2008 Houghton Mifflin Harcourt "turned off the spigot" on new books, speaking budgets "were cut completely" (Q1 rows 31, 36), and in April 2020 cancelled talks were the commonest loss among 940 Authors Guild members; so Laura's flexibility is weakest in exactly the year it would have to pay out.
- (c) It breaks because BMS's insurance protects the worker's own spending, which the case covers outside the portfolio, and in good years Laura's flexibility is real; for the ten payments the case shuts the channel anyway, since no money goes in after 2028 (Q1 row 47). The textbook's best reason for a young professional to own more shares is switched off twice for these promises, once by her trade and once by the case.
- **Decision it serves:** promised money in Treasuries, not in shares she could later top up (IPS l.11).
- **Report part:** 2.3 or the store (one clause at most).
- **Source:** Bodie et al. (1992; NBER 1992); Publishers Weekly (2020); Q1 rows 31, 36, 47.
- **Pedigree or aha:** BMS is pedigree; the "no buyer in a slump" break is niche.

### Idea 6. What matters is the worst deposit, and the plan names it in advance

- (a) Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005) found that ignoring income risk costs little "except when we allow for a disastrous labor income shock": a 0.5% yearly chance of a zero-income year "has a large crowding out effect" on shares, almost the same when income falls to 10% of normal instead of zero, and "a crucial determinant ... is the lower bound for the income distribution".
- (b) Laura's plan has published its lower bound: the payments need only the 2028 deposit's first $39,162 (26.1%), so the deposit could arrive 73.9% smaller and all ten would still be paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 8u); and the disaster itself, no deposit at all, is named in advance as one of the IPS's two reasons to change (the ten $57,455 short at zero interest, NUMBERS 5).
- (c) It breaks because CGM's disaster is a year of no income to live on, with borrowing, while Laura's is one missing transfer into a portfolio that borrows nothing; the break shows why the plan treats the two cases differently: a smaller deposit only shrinks the building money (NUMBERS 5: "not one of the two reasons"), and a missing one changes the plan.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.17's first reason to change ("a missed deposit"); l.13's completion from the 2028 deposit.
- **Report part:** 3e note 1; store.
- **Source:** Cocco et al. (2005), abstract, introduction, section 4.2 and footnote 13; NUMBERS 5 and 8u; Q33 row 18 and Q134 row 39 for the existing rows.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree with a niche quotation; it gives the "about three-quarters" figure a finance pedigree.

## Best three for the report

**Fixed-income PM, attack 15: right about the fix, wrong about the conclusion; the claim changes.** He is right that l.9 should be read as the timing and size of the 2028 deposit, shown with the instalment and royalty-timing rows (Q12 rows 1, 4, 8; rows 23-25 here). His conclusion, "idiosyncratic, bond-like human capital argues for more equities, not fewer", does not fit a writer. Her income is not bond-like, and as income risk rises the extra room for shares shrinks "towards the level of retired investors" (Viceira 2001; rows 9, 11). Business owners hold fewer shares (Heaton and Lucas 2000b; row 15). BMS's flexibility needs a buyer, and buyers vanish in slumps (rows 17-19). Nor is 2020 evidence of "moving apart": month by month, the share crash and the cancellations came in the same weeks (row 20).

**Finance professor, attack 4: it stands, and the claim changes as he asks, with one refinement.** The mechanism is background risk, and Treasuries do not hedge a career; what they remove is the payments' dependence on it (row 28). "The same downturn" stays only as "can", dated to 2008-09: shares fell 36.55% in 2008, and one-person artists' and writers' receipts fell 7.8% in 2009. It is never put as a measured link. The refinement: the co-timing evidence is better than he says, because his 2020 counterexample is a calendar-year effect (rows 20-21).

**Plain reader, attack 2: it stands.** The one frame is the deposit's timing and size, named as background risk. Drop the 2023 genre row (manga; D4) and the boom-year rows, and never redefine who the co-sponsors are. Replace "-11.6%" in D4 and SKELETON 3e note 4 with the writers' -7.8% (row 22).

**What the report should claim (notes for Fri 27 Nov's writers, not prose).** A writer is paid in pieces: an advance on signing, delivery and publication, then royalties twice a year. In 2009 and in 2020 that new money came later and smaller, while share prices fell faster and recovered sooner. A large risk that can be neither sold nor insured is what economists call background risk. A cautious person who carries one should take less of any other risk, even an unrelated one (Kimball, 1993). So Laura's plan buys shares only after her 2028 deposit has arrived, and only with what the payments and the floor leave. **The finance name to use:** background risk (Kimball 1993; Heaton and Lucas 2000 for business income). Never "beta". Never "natural hedge". "Hedge" appears only as the IPS's quoted word, beside what it hedges.

1. **Row 29 with row 12 (idea 3), 2.3 and 3e.** The theory is about two risks held at once. The plan holds no shares while the deposit is unknown, then buys them with what the deposit leaves: a quarter smaller deposit means 42% less VT, a half-size deposit none, and the payments are whole down to a deposit about three-quarters smaller. A judge expects a correlation the team cannot measure; instead the order of purchase settles the risk before any share is bought.
2. **Rows 20-21 (idea 4), 2.3.** The judges' strongest counterexample, "2020 hit the career while shares rose", rests on calendar years. By the month, shares fell 33.9% in 33 days in the same weeks the talks and fairs were cancelled, and were back by August, while a writer's new money took one to two years. "During that same period" becomes "in the same downturn, on a slower clock". A slow clock is exactly what a deposit due on a fixed date has to fear.
3. **Rows 1, 2 and 14-15 (idea 1), 2.3.** Background risk keeps l.9's conclusion without its weakest premise: "risks should tend to crowd each other out" even when they are unrelated. It is honest about size, since the effect is small for wage earners and large for business owners and for a chance of disaster, and Laura is both. This is the name the professor asked for, used to show the team knows where its own metaphor is loose.

Where it lands: 2.3 (Diversify Laura, not the portfolio). It explains IPS l.9's decision to keep promised money out of shares. The mechanism has a name, background risk, and the January 2028 order of purchase settles that risk before any share is bought.
