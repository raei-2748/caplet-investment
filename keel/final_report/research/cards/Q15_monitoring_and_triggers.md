FINAL IPS (`keel/final_report/FINAL_IPS_as_submitted.txt`), the locked TNA
(`keel/drafts/writing_room_2026-11-03/tna_as_submitted.txt`), `keel/final_report/NUMBERS.md` (curve of 6 Nov 2026,

risk to note in the Final Report, never a change to propose. Q7 already holds commitment theory, the CFA
individual-investor IPS, the UK funding regulations, TPR journey planning, the US glide paths and Q7 row 31 (after
January 2028 only one of the two reasons can still happen); none of that is repeated here.

Wharton's requirement (CASE_MAP E2): "Explain what Laura or her asset management team should monitor as 2031 and 2033
approach and how changing conditions could affect your recommendations."
The IPS line it must sit with (A34, IPS l.17): "There are only 2 possible reasons we would change our plan – a missed
deposit or a Treasury default. A fall in the market is not one of them."
So the section has one shape: watch many things, act on two.

Claims tested:
1. Professional liability-matchers separate a watch list from a short list of action triggers.
2. "Treasury default" is the only market event that could break a payment, and it can be watched.
3. Everything else Laura watches changes a number, never a payment.
4. Precommitted triggers work better with scheduled monitoring and responses written in advance.

Problem class: institutional risk governance (bank recovery indicators, liquidity contingency plans), liability-driven
and hold-to-maturity practice (insurers' matching adjustment, pension funding-level triggers, municipal defeasance
escrows, endowment IPSs), sovereign debt operations (the 1979 delay, debt-limit X-dates, payment-delay playbooks), plus
behavioural planning (progress monitoring, if-then plans). Prior art came first from each field; the analogy to Laura
is noted per row.

## Verdicts

1. **Claim 1, watch list versus triggers: CHANGE (keep the core).** The professionals do separate watching from
   acting, but in three tiers, not two:
   - early-warning indicators that only prompt a look (EBA 2016 "early warning signals"; Basel's early warning
     indicators "cause an assessment");
   - thresholds that prompt a decision but never an automatic one (EBA 2021: "the principle of non-automaticity is
     embedded"; Pennon triggers 5, 2 and 1: "the Trustees will decide"; the Fed's 2012 thresholds were "not triggers");
   - a very few automatic triggers, mostly one-way (Pennon trigger 3 and Hendy de-risk automatically when funding
     improves and are never "re-risked" after a fall).
   The closest kin to Laura are not pension funds or endowments but hold-to-maturity books. A defeasance escrow is
   irrevocable, sends statements on a schedule, gives notice "immediately" if the securities are not sufficient, and
   allows a swap only with a new verification at a zero reinvestment rate (Fort Bend 2024). An insurer's matching
   adjustment portfolio keeps its assignment for the life of the obligations unless the cash flows "have materially
   changed" (Solvency II Art. 77b); the PRA: it "may change only in limited circumstances which are out of the control
   of the firm". Laura's two reasons are the two ways her cash flows can change: money in (the 2028 deposit) and money
   out of the bonds (the US Treasury). Laura's plan therefore has a long watch list, two event triggers, dated steps,
   and no numeric thresholds. Endowment IPSs (quarterly watch, annual review) have no short list of reasons at all;
   they are the wrong model for her.
2. **Claim 2, default is the only payment-breaker and can be watched: CHANGE.**
   - (a) Default is the only event that can stop a payment outright. It is not the only market event that can shorten
     one: reinvestment at lower rates (with Keel's margin, short in 6.0% of 16-year windows since 1962, worst $12,117
     across all ten payments, NUMBERS 2) and an iBond fund's final months in cash (up to about 2% of a rung, NUMBERS 3).
     CANON C9 already says default is not the only residual.
   - (b) "Default" needs a definition. The 1979 event was a late payment, not a refusal: a back-office failure, under
     three weeks from the first late bill (26 Apr) to normal processing (14 May), paper-check holders only, and no
     compensation because Treasury "has no authority" to pay it (CRS 2016). The market playbook for a debt-limit delay
     rolls each maturity one business day at a time (TMPG 2021).
   - (c) It can be watched, except for a 1979-style accident. Public warning from the day the limit bound to the
     X-date: 78 days (2011), 151 (2013), 137 (2023) (Keel arithmetic from FOMC and Treasury dates). GAO's "acute
     market concern" lasted 19 to 122 days per episode. Thirteen impasses since 2011 all ended with the limit
     suspended or raised and no missed payment (GAO 2026). The bill curve kinks at the X-date (up to 47bp in Oct 2013)
     and "quickly renormalized" after (FOMC 2011).
   - (d) The next X-date is projected for late 2027 to early 2028 (BPC, NTU, Wrightson via Roll Call, CBO), around the
     January 2028 completion and floor purchase: the one time the plan's own buying meets a live default debate.
   - (e) Timing slack: Laura's five T-bonds mature 47 to 320 days before their payments; her five iBond rungs and the
     floor end about 15 Dec, 17 days before 1 Jan. A 1979-length delay is absorbed by the T-bonds and is tight only
     for an iBond rung in its last weeks (Keel inference).
   - (f) A new watch item that is neither reason: an iBond fund's board "may extend the termination date" (IBTM
     prospectus).
3. **Claim 3, everything else changes a number, never a payment: CHANGE (mostly KEEP).** Every signal in the table
   below changes a figure (the completion's size, the bottom, the top before the 2031 move, the kept half, the gift's
   value in NT$, the co-sponsor message) and none changes an instruction. Three things can still touch a payment and
   must be named, none of them a reason to change the plan: the rate path after 2027 (6.0% of history windows, worst
   2.4% of $500,000), the iBond cash wait (up to about 2% of a rung), and a 2028 deposit cut by more than about 98% at
   today's yields (77% if yields first fall 1 point, 53% if 2) (NUMBERS 2, 3, 5). A smaller deposit is not a missed
   one (CANON D9).
4. **Claim 4, triggers work better with scheduled monitoring and responses written in advance: KEEP, with two
   limits.** Monitoring progress raises goal attainment (138 studies, d = 0.40), more when the results are written down
   and reported (Harkin et al. 2016). If-then plans add d = .27 to .66 across 642 tests, more when written in if-then
   form and rehearsed (Sheeran et al. 2024). Teams with written "we-if-then" plans made more informed decisions in the
   authors' own lab programme (Thurmer et al. 2020). The organisational evidence is regulator practice, not trials
   (TPR: pre-agreed triggers let the employer "react to it quickly"; Basel: plans "regularly tested"). Limit one: a
   plan written in advance binds only those who agreed it (Treasury never endorsed the TMPG and SIFMA playbooks, GAO
   2024). Limit two: a numeric threshold invites re-setting as it nears (the Fed dropped its 6.5% unemployment
   threshold in March 2014); an event trigger such as "a missed deposit" does not. Laura's schedule is the plan's own
   calendar. The IPS names the two reasons but not what she does when one happens: a gap to note, not a change.

## Best five for the report

1. **The two reasons are the two ways the cash flows can change (rows 4-6).** Hold-to-maturity professionals watch on a
   schedule and change only when the cash flows themselves change: the insurer's matching adjustment ("materially
   changed"; "out of the control of the firm") and the defeasance escrow ("notify ... immediately" if the securities
   are not sufficient). Laura's book has one flow in (the 2028 deposit) and one source out (the US Treasury).
2. **An alarm is not an action (rows 1-2, 8-9, 40).** Banks, pensions and the Fed separate signals that prompt a look
   from the few events that prompt action, and pensions never "re-risk" after a fall. Laura's watch list is long and
   moves nothing; her triggers are two events, not numbers, so they cannot be argued away as they near.
3. **Default can be watched, with dates (rows 15, 23-24, 30).** Thirteen standoffs since 2011, no missed payment;
   78 to 151 days of public warning; a free public signal (bills maturing just after the X-date). The one surprise,
   1979, was a back-office delay of under three weeks for paper checks. Laura's T-bonds mature 47 to 320 days early.
4. **The next standoff lands on Laura's busiest month (row 26).** Projections put the X-date in late 2027 to early
   2028, around the January 2028 completion and floor. What it can change is the price she pays, not the plan.
5. **Watch on the plan's own calendar, written down, with the "then" drafted (rows 11, 38-39).** A yearly written
   check plus the dated steps; closer watching only near an X-date and in each fund's last six months. Recorded,
   reported monitoring and if-then plans have the best evidence behind them.
