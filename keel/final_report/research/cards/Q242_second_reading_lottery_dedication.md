- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** paying fixed sums on fixed dates from assets bought now. British life-office actuaries framed it first (Redington and Haynes and Kirton, both 1952), naming both the general method, which needs trades, and the special case that needs none ("absolute" matching). US pension practice (Leibowitz at Salomon Brothers, late 1970s to 1986) gave cash matching the name "dedication". Public bodies (lotteries, municipal defeasance escrows) run it as written policy today.

- **Claim (d): CHANGE. The contrast is not "Redington's immunisation trades, dedication does not". Redington named both cases.**
  - **The general case trades.** Redington coined "immunization" as "a particular form of matching" (p.289). For a fund "kept stationary by the influx of new business the theory entails a steady process of selling shorts and buying longs in order to maintain the same mean term", and after a rate change "a small rearrangement of the portfolio will be required" (p.293).
  - **He also named the case that needs no trades.** For a fund that "without further new business would decline", the asset-proceeds can be "arranged to coincide absolutely with the liability-outgo", which is "absolutely immune to all changes in interest however violent": "This special case of immunization can be described as 'absolute' matching" (p.292). The discussion opener adds that "no subsequent changes of investment were required in that case" (pp.316-317).
  - **His single-payment example draws the lottery's line.** An asset "with no running yield ... maturing in 10 years" immunizes a 10-year liability "without further complication". With interest-bearing assets, the bond must run longer: "11.485 years" (p.291).
  - **Laura's ten are not absolute matches.** Their interest arrives years before the payments (NUMBERS l.124-125: 7.76 years against 9.94). The extra Treasuries cover that gap.
  - **Corrected words:** "Redington (1952), who named immunisation, kept a fund safe by trading as rates and new business moved; the one case he found needing no later trades he called 'absolute' matching, where what the bonds pay coincides with what is owed. Laura's ten come as close to it as coupon bonds allow, and the extra Treasuries cover the interest that arrives early." Never call the ten "immunised" or "absolutely matched".
  - **Judge's note:** in 1952 Redington already gave the lottery's answer (a zero, "without further complication") and Laura's problem (with coupon bonds, the bond must outlast the payment). Wharton's STRIPS ban moves her from his easy case to his hard one. She meets it with bonds that end before each payment, so nothing is ever sold, and with $36,774 of extra Treasuries.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 28 (3a's break, 2.0's pedigree).** Redington in 1952 said a zero settles a dated payment "without further complication", and that a coupon bond has to outlast the date. That puts the lottery and Laura on either side of one line, a line Wharton's STRIPS ban drew for her. A fixed-income judge expects the extra Treasuries to look nervous. Here they turn out to be the answer to a problem named seventy-four years ago.
2. **Row 29 with row 31 (2.2; claim d).** Redington's own "absolute" matching is "absolutely immune to all changes in interest however violent", and "no subsequent changes of investment were required in that case". This replaces the loose two-camp story with the founding paper's own distinction. It also stops the report calling the ten "immunised".
3. **Row 18 with row 16 (2.0; SB-1, GOLDMINE 29).** "Cash-matching, the simplest form of dedication" and "In many cases ... no reinvestment" fix two labels before a bond reader catches them. Caplet chose the stricter of the two aims Leibowitz names, and that choice can be said in his words.
4. **Rows 1, 4 and 5 (3a's quotation).** The quotation is exact for the policy cited, and the date is certified by Resolution 2025-FIN-003. A revision is pending that would change "in" to "into", and its adoption cannot be confirmed before 5 Dec. Quoting from "sufficient" survives either outcome.
5. **Row 8 (3a's break, corrected).** The lottery's municipal bonds pay interest, so the break must say "its Treasuries", not "it". This one-word fix keeps the parallel true.
