**Problem class and the field that solved it.**
- Claim 1 is a denominator question in liability-relative investing. Pension asset-liability management solved it:
  surplus optimisation (Sharpe and Tint 1990; Leibowitz, Kogelman and Bader 1992) and liability-driven investment
  with a shortfall penalty (Ang, Chen and Sundaresan 2013).
- Claim 2 belongs to household finance: life-cycle portfolio choice with labour income that cannot be traded
  (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson 1992; Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout 2005; Benzoni, Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein 2007;
  Heaton and Lucas on business owners).
- Claim 3 is a private-wealth practice question: goals-based allocation. The CFA Level III curriculum covers it with
  its hedging/return-seeking and goals-based approaches; Das, Markowitz, Scheid and Statman (2010) and Chhabra (2005)
  give the theory and the practitioner frame.

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. The 27%-against-23% clause (rows 1-8, 14)
- **What is it?** SKELETON 2.3 answers the professor's "why about 7%?" by saying that about 27% of the money left
  after the payments goes into VT, "near the textbook's 23%" (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson 1992, Table 1).
- **Did it work?** The BMS half works: within their example, 73% to 23% changes only the career's risk (row 1). The
  comparison does not work. Laura's 26.7% is the frozen book's split (row 6), not a model output. It is a central
  figure on one definition of surplus: about 20.2% on a market-value surplus (row 8), 23.5% median where "most" binds
  (row 7), and half that after the 2031 move. BMS's level comes from invented inputs (row 3). Applied to Laura's
  small free money with those same inputs, the textbook gives about -5% to 6%, or 29% if the whole $450,000 is
  counted (row 5).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her promised money is held apart, so the money promised to no one is the right
  base in principle: surplus theory and the CFA's two-portfolio approach both put risk preferences on what is left
  (rows 9, 25).
- **How is it not?** BMS describe a whole household's wealth. The case keeps her other resources outside this pot
  (row 14) and gives no figure for her career's value or its share-like part. A professor can rebut "near 23%" with
  BMS's own formula.
- **What Caplet takes.** 2.3 keeps "a riskier career, all else equal, cuts shares from 73% to 23% (Bodie, Merton and
  Samuelson 1992)" as its one number, for direction only. The 27% and "near" clause leaves the report. "Why about
  7%?" is answered by card 4's goal logic, with the dollars of row 33 in a figure note. The arithmetic of rows 5 and 8
  stays in the store as the defence if a judge raises the textbook level.

### Card 2. Two promises put Laura at the bottom of the "U" (rows 9-12)
- **What is it?** In liability-driven investing with a shortfall penalty, the share of shares is U-shaped in the
  funding ratio. It is lowest when assets just cover what is promised, and higher both for funds well short and for
  funds well over (Ang, Chen and Sundaresan 2013).
- **Did it work?** As a model result, yes. ACS show the minimum near a funding ratio of 1.0 to 1.03 and quote the
  reason: "Over-funded plans also can afford to take on more risk".
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Against the ten payments alone she is well over-funded, about 1.5 times (Keel
  arithmetic), which would argue for more shares. Her second promise, the bottom she will state in 2031, takes most
  of what is left. On both promises she is near 1.07 to 1.17 (Keel arithmetic, rough), where ACS's managers hold the
  fewest shares. This is the formal reason the shares are few, and it is not the career.
- **How is it not?** It is a one-year pension model. Its minimum is still 18% to 45% shares (row 12), and its
  liability moves partly with shares, while hers is fixed (row 10).
- **What Caplet takes.** Store only: a professor's-challenge note ("the shares are few because she makes a second
  promise, not because of a textbook weight"). If the funding ratios are ever used, the numbers owner must add them
  to NUMBERS first.

### Card 3. Some shares, not none: what the career literature and business owners say (rows 15-22)
- **What is it?** The household-finance answer to "how many shares for someone whose income moves with the
  economy?".
- **Did it work?** The direction holds in every source: fewer shares (CGM, Viceira, BMS). "None" appears only in
  BCG's corner: a young investor with very share-like wages, high risk aversion and no short sales. Real business
  owners in the 1998 SCF held about 0.6 to 0.7 of their liquid assets in shares whatever the size of their business.
  Only their shares-to-net-worth fell, from .24 to .02 (Heaton and Lucas, Table 2.3).
- **How is Laura's case like it?** She is self-employed, which puts her on the positive-correlation side (CGM
  footnote 1). Her income comes in lumpy advances, closer to CGM's disaster channel (row 18; Q12). The case puts her
  early in her working life (graduated 2018, l.16).
- **How is it not?** These papers size a person's whole portfolio. Hers is a pot with two fixed purposes, her living
  costs are outside it (row 14), and the evidence on her income is PARTLY (CANON B4).
- **What Caplet takes.** Never write "a small share weight is the textbook answer". The defensible chain (row 23):
  the career is why promised money is never in shares (IPS l.9, l.11), and the two promises are why little money is
  left. Store only for the numbers; at most one clause in 2.3 if space allows, "the textbooks say fewer shares, not
  none". Record the risk in row 24: keep l.9's "counterbalance", never "hedge the career".

### Card 4. Goal by goal: the case's "appropriate balance" is the CFA's two-portfolio approach (rows 25-30)
- **What is it?** The CFA Level III curriculum's two approaches. Hedging/return-seeking: "Any remaining funds are
  invested in the return-seeking portfolio". Goals-based: sub-portfolios, each with "its own time horizon and
  required probability of success". The same idea appears in Das, Markowitz, Scheid and Statman (2010) and in
  Chhabra's "certainty / high probability / possibility" (2005).
- **Did it work?** DMSS show that the sum of goal portfolios is mean-variance efficient with short sales, and loses
  only a few basis points without them. Misjudging one overall risk aversion costs more: 2.5 to 44 basis points.
  It is in the curriculum every CFA candidate studies.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Exactly three goals with three required certainties. The ten payments: "high
  degree of certainty", so Treasuries. The bottom she will state: owned from January 2028, so a Treasury fund. The
  top and her flexibility: VT. The case's own sentence is the method: "an appropriate balance between pursuing growth
  and protecting the capital required for her goals" (l.72-73). The IPS l.11 already says it: "only money you
  haven't promised yet goes into shares".
- **How is it not?** The curriculum's worked examples are pensions and retirees with equity-sensitive liabilities;
  Laura's are fixed and nominal, which only makes the hedge simpler (row 10).
- **What Caplet takes.** This is the frame for both judges. The practitioner's "why any shares?" gets the
  curriculum's own answer: money not needed for a goal goes into a return-seeking portfolio. The growth-minded
  judge's "why so few?" gets: each goal gets the assets its required certainty allows. 3b carries one clause of this
  logic; 2.3 carries the same logic in place of the 27% clause. Cite the CFA reading once (References), or Chhabra if
  a named author reads better. DMSS stays in the store as the efficiency defence.

### Card 5. Pricing the dose both ways, and naming whose money (rows 31-33)
- **What is it?** NUMBERS 8n prices the plan's split of the money left in January 2028 against all-Treasury (A) and
  three larger share doses (C to E).
- **Did it work?** Yes, as a benchmark. Against A, the shares cost the facility a median $15,000. They give Laura
  $20,000 more median flexibility and $5,000 more median of all money, at the same 1-in-20 low of $159,000. Against C
  to E, the plan keeps a bottom she owns of $95,000 instead of $65,000 or less, and a contribution under $95,000 in 3
  of 100 futures instead of 26 to 79. It gives up a median $4,000 to $12,000 of all money.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** It answers the case's two tests in one table: "Preserves appropriate financial
  flexibility" (l.145), and a range co-sponsors can count on (3c).
- **How is it not?** C to E break the frozen "Most of the 2028 deposit" (IPS l.15), so they are benchmarks, not
  options. In the simulated futures, stocks and rates are drawn apart (NUMBERS 7).
- **What Caplet takes.** Drop "cost little" unless it says whose money: little for Laura, $15,000 at the median for
  the facility. 3b: one clause, goal logic plus the $95,000 bottom (already 3c's figure, so no new headline number).
  Table 2's note: two lines with A and C (the $15,000 against $20,000; the $95,000 against $65,000). D, E and the
  1871 lows go to the store.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE. Drop "near the textbook's 23%"; keep BMS for direction only.**
- Like for like inside BMS: yes. Cases 1 and 3 share financial wealth ($300,000), career value ($500,000) and the
  40% target. Only the share-like part of the career changes ($100,000 to $250,000). Case 2 does not share the inputs
  (row 2).
- Is surplus the right denominator? In principle yes. With the promised money held apart, BMS's formula applies to
  the free money: surplus theory and the CFA two-portfolio approach both act on what is left (rows 9, 25). In
  practice no single surplus figure exists. It is $111,718 or $147,612 depending on how the top-up's no-interest part
  is counted, and the shares' part of it moves with the path and halves before 2031 (rows 7, 8).
- What number is fair? None from the textbook. BMS's level needs Laura's career value, its share-like part and her
  target, which the case does not give. Her other resources sit outside the pot (row 14). With BMS's own inputs, the
  formula gives about -5% to 29% depending on the base (row 5).
- The 26.7% is also not a computed answer but the frozen book's split (row 6). The clause sets a design ratio beside
  a model output and calls them near.
- One clause, or cut? Keep one clause in 2.3: the 73% to 23% direction, worded "a riskier career, all else equal".
  Cut the 27% comparison. Answer "why about 7%?" with the goal logic (card 4), and if a number is wanted, row 33's
  dollars in a figure note. Lead's decision: keep BMS as direction only (recommended), or cut BMS's number too and
  cite it in words.

**Claim 2: CHANGE.**
- Direction: supported everywhere. A share-like or disaster-prone career means fewer shares than otherwise (BMS,
  CGM, Viceira, BCG).
- Size: model-specific. "None" is BCG's corner case (young, very share-like wages, risk aversion 5, no short sales).
  For an investor with some wealth, CGM keep shares positive. Business owners keep their liquid portfolios about
  0.6-0.7 in shares (Heaton and Lucas Table 2.3).
- Where a self-employed author sits: on the positive-correlation side (CGM footnote 1), with lumpy, disaster-like
  income (Q12 rows 1-5), early in her working life (case l.16). That means fewer shares in her total wealth than an
  employee's. It does not imply a small weight in this pot.
- The evidence supports "some shares" over "no shares" for a person. The small weight in THIS pot comes from the two
  promises (row 23; card 2). The career explains only why promised money holds none.
- So never say "a small share weight is the textbook answer". Say the career is why the promises sit in Treasuries;
  the size follows from the promises.
- Risks recorded, no change: the pitch's "So we hedge it!" (row 24); BCG's corner read literally (row 15); Heaton and
  Lucas's liquid-asset ratio (row 20) if a judge knows it.

**Claim 3: KEEP the substance, CHANGE the frame.**
- Substance holds. Against all-Treasury, the shares raise Laura's median flexibility from $9,000 to $29,000 and her
  median of all money by $5,000, at the same 1-in-20 low. Against more shares, the plan keeps the bottom she owns at
  $95,000 (8n).
- "Cost little" is half true. It is false for the facility: $15,000 of the median contribution, and the stated top is
  below A's single figure in 95.0% of futures (8n check against C8).
- Frame for the growth-minded judge: the case asked for "an appropriate balance between pursuing growth and
  protecting the capital required for her goals" (l.72-73). The plan grows only money no goal needs, and every extra
  share dollar comes out of the bottom she tells co-sponsors (row 32).
- Frame for the practitioner: the CFA hedging/return-seeking approach puts "any remaining funds" in a return-seeking
  portfolio. Without shares her flexibility is only what the floor pays above its stated figure (8n: $9,000 median;
  $10,761 centrally), so the shares are the plan's only real source of the flexibility the case requires (l.103,
  l.145).
- Landing: 3b, one clause; Table 2's note, two lines (A and C); store, the rest (card 5).
