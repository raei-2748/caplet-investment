## Verdicts on the four claims

1. **"A committed floor from Laura works as seed money / a lead gift that raises what co-sponsors give": KEEP, with
   three conditions.** Announced seed money raised giving in every field test read today: nearly sixfold when it rose
   from 10% to 67% of the goal (List & Lucking-Reiley 2002), +18% dollars and +23% donors (Rondeau & List 2008), average
   gift from EUR 74.3 to EUR 132 (Huck, Rasul & Shephard 2015). Conditions: (a) it works mainly when the good is lumpy,
   with a high fixed cost, which a building is (Bracha, Menietti & Vesterlund 2011: 40% to 76% provision; "no role when
   fixed costs are small"); (b) it works through information, when followers cannot see the project's value for
   themselves (Potters, Sefton & Vesterlund 2005, 2007; Andreoni 2006), which is co-sponsors' position in 2031; (c) its
   size against the gap matters (the smaller 2007 opera lead gift drew fewer donors). Announce it as given; do not
   offer it as a match (rows 6-11).
2. **"An all-or-nothing pledge (assurance contract) is the right shape for the co-sponsor ask": CHANGE.** Right for the
   co-sponsors' pledges to a building (all-or-nothing campaigns succeeded twice as often as keep-it-all, 34% vs 17%,
   with bigger goals: Cumming, Leboeuf & Schwienbacher 2020; a refund raised gifts 20%: List & Lucking-Reiley), wrong
   for Laura's own floor, which the IPS promises "without doubt" (IPS l.15; A22, C5). Refund bonuses lifted lab success
   from 43.5% to 67.0% (Cason, Tabarrok & Zubrickas 2021), but only the kept half could pay one, and it is "promised to
   no one" (clash, rows 16-18). Store H5-018 already proposed the split; this run adds the evidence and the caution.
3. **"Telling co-sponsors a range rather than a single figure helps the raise": CHANGE, close to KILL as worded.** No
   study read today shows that announcing a range raises a charitable raise. Uncertainty about how much is needed is what
   breaks group funding (Barrett & Dannenberg 2012: catastrophe avoided in 18 of 20 groups with a known threshold, none
   with an uncertain one), and risk gives reluctant givers an excuse (Exley 2016). The range earns its place as a
   credibility tool the case requires: numeric ranges cost little trust (van der Bles et al. 2020) and keep trust when an
   outcome lands low (Joslyn & LeClerc 2012). Its fundraising value comes from being narrow and owned by the time
   co-sponsors plan, which is what MOVE2031 does (Keel inference; "Early and credible commitment helps groups to cope
   with uncertainty", Dannenberg et al. 2015).
4. **Other finds that fit Laura:** (a) gifts that complete a project are over twice as large, and support jumps near a
   goal and drops once it is reached (Wash 2013; Kuppuswamy & Bayus 2017), so overruns arrive when funders have
   relaxed, which is the kept half's job; (b) raises succeed narrowly or fail badly (Kickstarter failures reached 10.3%
   of goal on average: Mollick 2014), so "a co-sponsor falling short" means a small gap or a large one, and the kept
   half can only bridge the small one; (c) matching gifts crowd out the donor's own money and can lower giving after the
   match ends (Huck & Rasul 2011; Meier 2007), which matters because Laura will ask co-sponsors again for operating
   support (case p.3); (d) professionals who publish ranges are over-confident: managers put 78.5% confidence on ranges
   that held 31.2% of the time (Call, Hribar, Skinner & Volant).

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. Seed money works because a building is lumpy
- **What is it?** A fixed-cost good gives nothing until enough money is raised, so each funder worries the total will
  fall short. An announced first gift tells everyone the goal is reachable (Andreoni 1998, row 4).
- **Did it work?** Yes, in every field test read: nearly sixfold when seed rose from 10% to 67% of the goal (row 2);
  +18% dollars and +23% donors (row 6); average gift +78% (row 8). The lab pins down when: with a high fixed cost,
  provision rose from 40% to 76%; with a low one, seed had "no role" (row 4).
- **How is Laura like it?** A residency building is the purest fixed-cost good: half a building is not half a
  residency. Wharton's case says the same in its own words (row 1). Kresge's rule that 20-50% be raised before a
  challenge grant (store H9-131) is the same logic used by a real building funder.
- **How is she not?** The tests used charities' seed money and small household gifts; Laura is the founder and her
  co-sponsors are organisations. Her seed share cannot be worked out because the facility cost is not set (case p.4),
  and the smaller 2007 opera lead gift drew fewer donors (row 9), so size matters.
- **What Caplet takes:** the floor's job is coordination, and it does that job best for exactly the kind of good Laura is
  funding. CLASH note: the floor is an iBond fund payout, not a fixed sum (C5); the figure announced must be one the
  payout will reach, or the seed's credibility is the thing at risk.

### Card 2. Laura's floor is a signal from the person who knows the project best
- **What is it?** When followers cannot judge a project, they copy an informed leader (row 13); the leader must give
  enough to be believed (row 12); a respected name adds weight (row 14).
- **Did it work?** Leading by example raised giving only when followers lacked information, and had "no effect" when
  returns were known (row 13). The Gates-named match beat an anonymous one and the effect lasted (row 14).
- **How is Laura like it?** In 2031 the residency is an idea; co-sponsors cannot see its value. Money Laura already owns
  and has set aside is costly to her, so it is believable.
- **How is she not?** Andreoni's leader is an outsider who inspected the charity; Laura is the founder, and a founder's
  optimism can be discounted. Her signal is commitment, an owner's stake (store H3-044, H3-045), not an independent
  check. A first co-sponsor with a known name in the arts could add what her own money cannot (Keel inference).
- **What Caplet takes:** two separate effects, information and coordination, both named in the case (row 1). The
  owned floor carries the information; it does not need to be dressed as anything else.

### Card 3. Announce, do not match
- **What is it?** A lead donor can either announce a gift or offer to match others. Matching sounds stronger.
- **Did it work?** No better in the field: the opera's 1:1 match cut donors' own average gift from EUR 132 to EUR 92.3,
  and the best scheme was to announce and not match (rows 7-8); the Sierra Club match did no better than the challenge
  gift (row 6); seed beat a 1:1 match in 40,000 letters (row 11); after a match ended, giving rates fell (row 10).
- **How is Laura like it?** She will be asked how co-sponsor money and hers fit together; "I'll match you" is the
  obvious answer and, on this evidence, the weaker one.
- **How is she not?** The tests are small gifts from individuals; organisations may like matches for their own
  reporting. Laura will also ask again for operating support (case p.3), which makes Meier's long-run loss relevant.
- **What Caplet takes:** the IPS shape (a minimum owned and announced, not conditional on anyone) is the shape the field
  evidence favours. CLASH note: any matching scheme from the floor would make the floor depend on others' money,
  against "the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt" (IPS l.15, A22).

### Card 4. All-or-nothing for co-sponsors, unconditional for Laura
- **What is it?** An assurance contract collects pledges only if the total covers the cost, and refunds otherwise
  (row 15); a dominant assurance contract adds a bonus if it fails (row 16).
- **Did it work?** Refunds raised gifts 20% (row 3). All-or-nothing campaigns succeeded twice as often as keep-it-all
  with bigger goals (34% vs 17%, row 21). Early refund bonuses raised lab success from 43.5% to 67.0% and paid for
  themselves (row 17).
- **How is Laura like it?** A building is not scalable, which is where all-or-nothing beat keep-it-all; operating
  support is scalable, where keep-it-all is normal (every verified non-profit used it, row 21).
- **How is she not?** Laura is the leader, not one pledger among equals: her floor is already promised and owned.
  Lab groups of 10 are not a handful of organisations with boards and budget years.
- **What Caplet takes:** co-sponsors' facility pledges can be all-or-nothing; Laura's floor stays unconditional.
  CLASH note: a refund bonus would have to come from the kept half, which the IPS says is "promised to no one"
  (IPS l.15, A30). That is a reason not to use one, not a change.

### Card 5. Raises succeed narrowly or fail badly, and support fades once the goal is met
- **What is it?** Crowdfunding data show where shortfalls actually land, and when funders lose interest.
- **Did it work?** Kickstarter failures reached on average 10.3% of their goal, and only 3% passed half; a quarter of
  successes cleared by 3% or less (row 19). Support rises near the goal and drops after it (row 22); gifts that
  complete a project are over twice as large (row 23). Over 75% of funded projects ran late (row 20).
- **How is Laura like it?** The IPS names "a co-sponsor falling short" as one use of the kept half (A31). The data say
  shortfalls are either small or very large. Overruns and a stronger Taiwan dollar arrive after the raise has closed,
  when funders have stopped paying attention.
- **How is she not?** These are online campaigns with many small backers; a building funded by a few organisations
  can fail when one large co-sponsor leaves, which is a lumpier risk again.
- **What Caplet takes:** the kept half's honest scope: it can bridge a small gap or a late overrun, not replace a
  co-sponsor that walks away (Keel inference). CANON D12 already asks for the definition; this data says where the line
  sits in practice.

### Card 6. Uncertainty about how much is needed breaks group funding; early owned commitment fixes it
- **What is it?** In threshold games, players who know the target cooperate; players who do not, fail.
- **Did it work?** With the threshold known, groups avoided catastrophe 18 of 20 times; with it uncertain, catastrophe
  was certain in 16 of 20 groups (row 25). Uncertainty about the size of the benefit did no harm. Even modest ranges
  wiped out cooperation, with the cut-off between ranges about 7% and 13% wide (row 26). Unknown spreads were worse
  than known ones, and "early and credible commitment" helped (row 27). Counter-case: for highly valued goods, theory
  says uncertainty can raise giving, with weak lab support (row 28).
- **How is Laura like it?** Co-sponsors must fill the gap between the facility cost and Laura's gift. The floor owned
  from Jan 2028 and the move of the promised half before 2031 (MOVE2031) take Laura's part of the uncertainty away
  before anyone is asked: an early, credible commitment.
- **How is she not?** These are climate games among equals; the facility cost itself stays uncertain, and estimating it
  is outside scope (case p.4).
- **What Caplet takes:** the strongest research case for MOVE2031: the plan removes the uncertainty co-sponsors would
  otherwise face about Laura's share. CLASH note: TNA l.21 ("a rise before 2031 lifts the top of the range co-sponsors
  hear") means the top moves until the move (D4); the timing of the move before the first meeting carries the point.

### Card 7. The range is a credibility tool, not a fundraising lever
- **What is it?** The case requires a range plus a confidence (case p.3). The question is what a range does to the
  listener.
- **Did it work?** For trust, yes: numeric ranges cost little trust (row 31), and stated uncertainty protected trust after
  a wrong forecast (row 32). For giving, no study read shows a range raises a raise; risk gives reluctant givers an
  excuse (row 30), and a risky prospect can be valued below its worst outcome (row 29, contested). Readers prefer
  narrow ranges over both points and wide ranges (row 33). Corporate readers expect results near the top of a range
  (row 34), and managers' 78.5% confidence matched a 31.2% hit rate (row 35).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her listeners include organisations whose finance staff read company guidance; they will
  read her range the way they read guidance.
- **How is she not?** Guidance is about earnings outcomes; Laura's top, after the 2031 move, is money she owns, so her
  range can be far better calibrated than guidance (Keel inference).
- **What Caplet takes:** the range protects credibility; the owned floor and a narrow, owned band do the fundraising.
  CLASH note: none with the IPS; D12 asks for a single definition of "facility contribution" so that a reader trained on
  guidance does not take the top as the expected figure.

## (c) What would surprise a judge

1. Knowing the target matters more than knowing the payoff: groups that knew the threshold avoided catastrophe 18 of 20
   times; groups that did not, almost never, while uncertainty about the size of the benefit did no harm (row 25). The
   IPS's move before 2031 is, in this light, a coordination device for co-sponsors.
2. Seed money only helps when the project is lumpy: in the lab, sequence raised provision from 40% to 76% with a high
   fixed cost and did nothing with a low one (row 4). A building is the textbook case.
3. A matching offer can lower what donors give from their own pockets (EUR 132 to EUR 92.3 at 1:1, row 8), and giving
   rates fell after a match ended (row 10).
4. Crowdfunding raises do not fail by a little: failures averaged 10.3% of goal (row 19). A reserve meant for "a
   co-sponsor falling short" is mostly useful for small gaps (Keel inference).
5. All-or-nothing campaigns set bigger goals and still succeeded twice as often (row 21), yet every verified non-profit
   in that sample chose keep-it-all.
6. Managers who publish ranges were 78.5% confident and right 31.2% of the time (row 35).
7. The completing gift is the biggest: on DonorsChoose the pull to finish a project added about 16% of all donations
   (row 23).
