Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):
1. "With a few unequal funders, the order and the conditions of commitments decide whether a building gets funded: a
   lead commits first, others commit conditionally on the total being reached."
2. "Institutions already use conditional, last-money and matching commitments for capital projects, so co-sponsors
   will recognise the form."
3. "The dominant assurance contract's refund bonus has no place in Laura's plan."

Problem class: a threshold (step-level) public good paid for by a few unequal contributors who can move in some order.
It is a coordination problem with two parts: agreeing which project to fund (selection) and closing the gap (effort).
Fields that solved it: experimental economics of threshold public goods and mechanism design (assurance contracts,
refund bonuses); and capital-grant administration, where foundation and public funders have long paid "last money"
only once everyone else is committed.

Case note: the case names no co-sponsor types. It says only that remaining facility cost "may come from co-sponsors,
grants, collaborators, program fees, continued business income, or other sources" (case p.3). The brief's "a
government culture agency, one or two foundations, a corporate sponsor, perhaps a university" is an assumption. It is
used below as an illustration only.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1 (order and conditions decide it; a lead first, others conditional): CHANGE.**
   - Order matters more than inequality does. Four unequal players moving at once funded a project in 47.8% of
     rounds. When the richest moved first while the others were scattered: 75.6%. When the richest moved last after
     the others had agreed on one project: 79.4%. Moving in sequence adds 0.189 to the chance of success (p < 0.01)
     (Corazzini, Cotton & Longo 2025; rows 8-11).
   - Inequality alone is mixed: 5 of 10 equal groups against 2 of 10 unequal groups reached the target (Tavoni et al.
     2011), but 53.9% against 50.0% in another design (Corazzini et al. 2024) (rows 1, 5).
   - A stated commitment repairs it: non-binding pledges took unequal groups from 2 of 10 to 6 of 10 (one-sided
     p = 0.085), and players kept them (row 2).
   - Change to the wording: not "a lead first, the others conditionally", but one owned lead first (Laura), the
     scattered or smaller funders next on a condition, and the largest funder last as "last money", if it wishes. The
     best order flips with whether the others are already organised. Laura's dated, owned figure is what organises
     them. The condition is the facility's total, never each other's amounts.
2. **Claim 2 (institutions use these forms; co-sponsors will recognise them): KEEP, split into three forms.**
   - All-or-nothing challenge: Kresge's grant "is paid in full only when the campaign succeeds completely", typically
     "8% to 10% of the private fundraising amount" (rows 16-18).
   - Last-money capital grant: Arts Council England pays nothing until 100% of partnership funding is "confirmed in
     writing", "otherwise, the initial payment will be withheld"; Washington State pays up to 33% of eligible costs
     (maximum $2,000,000) and only "after all other money needed to complete the project has been raised" (rows 19-23).
   - Proportional match: the NEA takes back "any NEA funds you drew down but could not match" (row 24).
   - In all three the funder sets the condition, its money is never at risk before the condition, and the applicant
     carries the gap and any overrun (row 25). New risk found: two funders cannot both be last (row 26).
3. **Claim 3 (no refund bonus in Laura's plan): KEEP, now confirmed from the full texts.**
   - The bonus is an enforceable promise paid from the designer's own budget (Tabarrok 1998; Zubrickas 2014).
     Laura's only unpromised money is the half kept, "promised to no one" (IPS l.15; A30), $21,347 at 1 Jan 2033 on
     the central path (NUMBERS 4, MODEL).
   - Zubrickas's variant, where a lead donor funds the bonus by sharing out her own gift if the raise fails, would
     make Laura's contribution payable to co-sponsors instead of the facility. That breaks A22, C5 and D12.
   - It is not free. Under differing private values Tabarrok's own model provides the good only half the time, so
     the bonus is paid half the time. In the lab it was paid in 39.5% (20% bonus) and 33.0% (early bonus) of
     campaigns (Keel arithmetic on Q11a row 17).
   - Honest counterpoint: with five unequal players a 25% bonus did lift late funding from 52.0% to 65.5% of rounds
     (Cason & Zubrickas 2017). It can work; Laura has no money she could pay it with.

The one finding that most changes the plan: the public capital funders read here are last money by rule. So 3d should
put Laura's figure first and unconditional, ask the others for pledges conditional only on the facility's total by one
date, and leave room for the largest funder to come last.

## (b) Cards

### Card 1. Lead when the table is scattered, anchor when it is organised (rows 8-11)
- **What is it?** A 2025 lab study of groups of four with one "star" holding more money. It compares the star moving
  first, last, or with everyone. It also compares a majority already agreed on one project with a scattered one.
- **Did it work?** Yes. Sequence beat simultaneity in both settings: 47.8% to 75.6% (star first, scattered) and 58.9%
  to 79.4% (star last, agreed). No one paid for the order: the star still carried more than its head start, and
  everyone earned more.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A few unequal funders and one lumpy project. Laura can move first with money she
  already owns, so her figure works as the organiser.
- **How is it not?** Lab groups of four, tokens, 12 rounds and a choice among labelled projects. Laura may not be the
  largest funder; the paper's star is. It is a working paper, not yet peer reviewed.
- **What Caplet takes.** Part 3d: Laura's figure first and dated; the others' pledges next; room for the largest funder
  to come last. Part 3e (monitoring): who is organised by 2031. No exhibit. No number from NUMBERS needed.

### Card 2. Public capital funders are last money by rule (rows 19-23)
- **What is it?** Arts Council England will not release a capital grant's first payment until 100% of partnership
  funding is "confirmed in writing". Washington State pays up to a third of eligible costs, only after "all other
  money needed to complete the project has been raised".
- **Did it work?** It is standing policy in both, built to protect public money. Washington's funds lapse at the end
  of the two-year budget unless re-appropriated, which happens "generally" and "only once".
- **How is Laura's case like it?** If a public culture body joins in 2031, it will likely come last and count only
  committed money. Laura's figure then has to be in a form the residency can show in writing.
- **How is it not?** Both are Western public programmes; the residency is in Taiwan and the case names no funder type.
  Taiwan's own capital rules were not read (Gaps).
- **What Caplet takes.** Part 3d: one close date and written commitments; Laura's figure as the one piece already
  secured. Part 3e: watch the anchor funder's budget clock. Q22 checklist item 4 (status and form) gains its reason.

### Card 3. Kresge: not a match, paid only on full success (rows 16-18)
- **What is it?** For decades the best-known capital challenge funder pledged about 8% to 10% of a private campaign
  and paid it only if the whole goal was raised by a deadline.
- **Did it work?** It was Kresge's central capital tool for decades; its own guide now calls it "no longer the
  centerpiece of our work". No success rate for challenges was found (Gaps). The guide does show the weak link it
  feared: pending government money, which it asked applicants to backstop.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Co-sponsors will know the all-or-nothing form. The condition is the total, so no
  one is asked to match anyone.
- **How is it not?** Kresge was the last funder, not the first; Laura is first. Kresge now uses the tool only "on
  occasion" (2011 guide).
- **What Caplet takes.** Part 3d: ask for pledges conditional on the facility's total, not matches. Never offer the
  kept half to backstop a pending grant (A30). Part 3b may cite the backstop warning as the reason the kept half stays
  unnamed to co-sponsors in amount.

### Card 4. Unequal tables are repaired by stated commitments and early signals (rows 1-4)
- **What is it?** A 2011 PNAS experiment: groups of six had to reach a joint target or lose half their money, with
  equal or unequal wealth, with or without non-binding pledges.
- **Did it work?** Inequality cut success from 5 of 10 to 2 of 10; pledges lifted unequal groups to 6 of 10, and
  players kept them. Success turned on early signals by the rich; the poor would not cover for them.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Unequal co-sponsors deciding over a period, where words come before money.
- **How is it not?** A loss game, not a gift; students; small samples (p = 0.085 for the pledge effect).
- **What Caplet takes.** Part 3d: a clear, early, owned statement is the cheap repair for an unequal table. Part 3f
  (Team Reflection, if used): the team chose words before money for the same reason.

### Card 5. The refund bonus fails on who pays, not on whether it works (rows 27-33)
- **What is it?** Tabarrok's dominant assurance contract pays every pledger a bonus if the raise fails, so pledging
  becomes the best move whatever the others do.
- **Did it work?** In theory, with identical known values, always. With differing values, half the time (Tabarrok's
  own section 3). In the lab a 25% bonus helped unequal groups (52.0% to 65.5%); a 75% bonus cut success to 23%.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** A few pivotal funders and a building that is all or nothing.
- **How is it not?** The bonus is the designer's own enforceable promise. Laura's only unpromised money is the half
  kept ($21,347 central, NUMBERS 4, MODEL). The lead-funded variant would hand her contribution to co-sponsors if the
  raise fails.
- **What Caplet takes.** Part 3d: no bonus, from any of her money. Part 3b: the half kept stays promised to no one, and
  this is one more reason. No number quoted to co-sponsors.

## (c) Verdicts

### Claim 1: CHANGE
- Evidence that order decides it is the strongest result in this card (rows 8-11). Moving in sequence added 0.189 to
  the success probability; organisation alone added nothing significant. The best order depends on the others:
  - scattered others: the big player first (75.6% against 62.8% last and 47.8% together);
  - agreed others: the big player last (79.4% against 71.1% first and 58.9% together).
- Evidence on inequality itself is mixed. It hurt in a loss game with six players (rows 1, 12). It did not hurt in a
  selection game with four (row 5). The disagreement over which project matters more than the size gap (rows 5, 10).
- The largest player's choice tends to become the project (row 6, about 96%; row 10, 60.0% against 15.6%), unless the
  others are already agreed. This is the "wealthy-interest bias".
- What this means for Laura's case:
  - Laura is the natural first mover, but not because she is the largest. Her figure is owned and dated before anyone
    else is asked (floor bought January 2028; top owned before the 2031 meetings; A22, A32). A fixed, visible figure
    is what "organises" the table in the paper's sense.
  - Once others have agreed around her plan, the paper and the public rules both point the same way: the largest
    funder does best coming last (rows 9, 19-23).
  - The condition should be the facility's total. Pledges conditional on each other's amounts invite the deadlock in
    row 26 and the self-serving fairness argument in row 4.
- Disanalogy to keep in view: every lab result here uses groups of 4 to 6 students, tokens and repeated rounds.
  Institutions decide once, through boards and budget cycles. Use them for direction, not for sizes.
- Not a strategy change. The IPS already puts Laura's figure first and owned (l.15). This refines only the words in 3d.

### Claim 2: KEEP, as three forms
- What is conditional, on what, by when, and what happens if it fails:

| Scheme | What is conditional | On what | By when | If the condition fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kresge challenge grant (rows 16-18) | The whole grant, "paid when grant conditions are met" | The full campaign goal raised; other money "committed, imminent or backstopped" | The "challenge deadline" set at award | Not paid ("paid in full only when the campaign succeeds completely"); the guide states no partial payment |
| Arts Council England capital grant (rows 19-20) | Every payment; the first needs 100% partnership funding "confirmed in writing" | All other funding confirmed; the final 10% also needs completion and accounts | Before the project starts (Round 1: by the first payment, 30 June 2026) | "the initial payment will be withheld"; no extra money for overruns |
| Washington Building for the Arts (rows 21-23) | Every draw; reimbursement only | "the entire amount of non-state matching funds must be committed"; site control; security | The two-year budget, then one usual re-appropriation | Funds "lapse unless reappropriated"; Commerce may rescind |
| NEA cost share (row 24) | The federal share, pro rata | A 1:1 non-federal match | The period of performance | Unmatched funds returned; the grant shrinks, it does not vanish |

- Common pattern (row 25): the funder writes the condition, never risks its money first, and leaves the gap and any
  overrun with the applicant. So a co-sponsor will read "my pledge stands if the facility's total is covered by date
  X" as normal. Laura does not need a new mechanism, and should not offer an unusual one (claim 3).
- New risk (row 26): last-money rules do not stack. If two co-sponsors both require every other pound in writing,
  neither can go first. Practice needs one anchor and one close date. Laura's owned figure is the one piece every
  conditional funder can count from the start, provided it exists in writing (Q18 item 4; Q22 checklist item 4).
- Limits: all four schemes are American or English. Taiwan's capital grant rules, and how a Taiwan public body would
  treat a foreign donor's pledge, were not read (Gaps).

### Claim 3: KEEP, with the exact reasoning
1. **The bonus is the designer's enforceable promise.** Tabarrok pays it "from the entrepreneur", and assumes it is
   enforced "for the same reason that lotteries pay out the prizes" (row 27). Zubrickas puts it in "own budget" and
   asks for "a credible capacity of raising the money promised" (row 30).
2. **Laura has no unpromised money but the half kept.** The ten payments are the reserve (IPS l.11, l.17; case p.3).
   The floor and the moved half are the contribution (D12). Only the half kept is "promised to no one" (IPS l.15; A30).
   Promising it to co-sponsors as a bonus breaks that line. Q11a's finding stands.
3. **The lead-funded variant does not escape it.** Zubrickas's own example has the lead donor share out his gift if
   the raise fails (row 30). For Laura that means her contribution would be paid to co-sponsors, not to the
   facility. That turns a floor promised "without doubt" (A22, C5) into a conditional side payment, and changes what
   "facility contribution" means (D12).
4. **It would actually be paid.** Under differing values Tabarrok's own model provides the good with probability 1/2
   (row 28). Lab bonuses were paid in 33.0% to 39.5% of campaigns (row 33). It is an expected cost, not insurance that
   never pays.
5. **A few pivotal funders gain least.** With every co-sponsor needed, each is already pivotal (row 29). In the lab the
   bonus helped most in larger groups (row 32), and a big bonus made things worse (row 31).
6. **The honest counterpoint.** With five unequal players a 25% bonus did lift late funding from 52.0% to 65.5% of
   rounds (row 32). The objection is not that it fails. It is that Laura cannot fund it without breaking the frozen IPS.
7. **It was designed for a seller.** Tabarrok's designer is a "profit seeking entrepreneur" who keeps the surplus;
   Laura is a donor. Its quality-signal use (row 34) is what her owned floor already provides, at no cost.
