## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Behind a promise there is no such thing as "a little" in shares

(a) Roy judged a holding by one thing, the chance that it ends below a disaster level. Suppose we hold a Treasury that will pay a $50,000 payment exactly, and we move a slice of its money into world shares. The payment now falls short whenever shares do worse than the Treasury by the date. That chance is the same whether the slice is 1% or all of it; only the size of the miss changes. On our house figures it is about 1 in 3 for the 2033 payment: 34.5%, with a median miss of $99 for a 1% slice and $9,867 for the whole (Keel arithmetic).

(b) So on Roy's own scale the chance of a missed payment does not shrink as the shares shrink: it stays where it is and drops to nil only at none. That is why our IPS puts promised money "straight into Treasuries" (l.11), and it is why "ours is to take none" (l.19) is the only zero on Roy's scale, not a matter of temperament. Shares can sit behind a promise without raising its chance of a miss only if they are smaller than the money above the promise, so that even a total loss leaves the payment whole. And money above a promise is, in the IPS's words, money "you haven't promised yet".

(c) Roy's yardstick counts a $99 miss the same as a $9,867 one, because it measures a chance and not a size. So the plan does not lean on his count. It leans on the IPS's own test, that no payment "will depend on a share price" (CANON A4), which a $99 dependence fails just as surely. Roy also had one date; Laura has ten, and small chances add up. If each payment carried a 1-in-100 chance, the chance of some miss over the ten would lie between 1 in 100 and 1 in 10 (Keel arithmetic, the union bound); only nil on each date makes nil over ten.

- **Decision it serves:** every promised dollar in Treasuries and none in shares (IPS l.11-13; CANON A5, A6).
- **Report part:** 3a, one sentence with the 1-in-3 and the $99; 2.0 as the reason behind the governing thought.
- **Source:** Roy (1952), pp. 434-435 (the ratio (m − d)/σ and the line from D); Keel arithmetic.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows that a smaller stake means a smaller loss. Few have seen that, when the safe money only just covers a promise, the chance of a miss does not shrink with the stake at all.

### 2. Roy's corner and Roy's rocks: one sentence of his splits the plan in two

(a) Roy wrote that economic life is like "navigation in poorly charted waters", where the task is "avoiding known rocks of uncertain position", and that is why he needed probabilities. He gave one clause to the case where some holding has no spread at all ("if |W| = 0 we can find a position of complete and blissful security") and moved on. He had chosen to measure wealth in real goods, and in real terms no such holding exists.

(b) Laura's payments are fixed in dollars (the case: "a fixed $50,000 ... not adjusted for inflation"), on known dates. So they are rocks of known position. And in dollars, a Treasury that ends just before each date is the holding with no spread that Roy set aside: her ladder lives in his one-clause corner. Her facility faces rocks of uncertain position (an overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short), priced in Taiwan's money and building costs. There the plan does what Roy's paper does: it spreads (VT's thousands of companies) and it keeps sea room (half the shares promised to no one).

(c) The payments were not always in the corner. In our first plan the ten would have been bought on 1 January 2033 at that day's prices, $16,089 dearer for each point lower in yields (NUMBERS 8s), with money that sat mostly in shares until then. So a rock fixed in dollars had an uncertain position in money. Buying the ten in January 2027 is what moved them into the corner. The break teaches where certainty comes from: it comes from the date of purchase, not from the size of the payment.

- **Decision it serves:** the ladder bought in January 2027 (IPS l.13), and the split between owned payments and a flexible facility (l.15).
- **Report part:** 3a or 2.3, two sentences; the first plan's moving rock to 3f only as the team's own fact, never with Roy's name (3f names one outside source).
- **Source:** Roy (1952), pp. 432 and 437; NUMBERS 8s; CANON A14 (nominal payments, inflation on the facility side).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. "Complete and blissful security" is a phrase almost nobody quotes, and the plan sits exactly where it points.

### 3. Laura writes her own disaster level, and only after she owns it (Kataoka at a chance of zero)

(a) Roy took the disaster level as fixed before the dice are thrown ("taken to be a constant"). Kataoka (1963) turned the question round: given the chance of failure you will accept, what is the highest level you can reach? Banks now call that figure Value at Risk; a stochastic-programming textbook notes that Kataoka formulated the first such model "already in 1963" (Kall and Mayer, p. 145).

(b) The case fixes one disaster level for Laura (ten payments of $50,000) and asks her to write the other herself, in 2031. She writes it only once she owns it. Ask Kataoka's question with the chance set at zero, and shares answer $0, because they can fall to nothing. The late-2032 Treasury fund answers with what its holdings pay if nothing more earns interest, and that is exactly how the range rule computes the bottom (NUMBERS 0). After the 2031 move the moved half answers the same way, so the top becomes a zero-chance level too. That is why the bottom comes from the Treasury fund, and why no top is stated before the move (IPS l.15).

(c) Roy's ordinary man faces a disaster level set by the world, while Laura's stated range is one she chooses. So she can set it under what she already owns, an option Roy's man never has. The price is a figure that is lower and later than a forecast would give: nothing is stated before the move. The building's own risks stay outside her figure, with the co-sponsors and her flexibility. Her figure is certain; the building's costs are not.

- **Decision it serves:** the range rule (each end at its zero-interest value, cut by 0.5% and rounded down; NUMBERS 0 and 4) and "whatever figure you choose is money you already own" (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3c, one clause ("a Value at Risk at a chance of zero, which only an owned Treasury can give"); 2.0 as the spine "Laura buys first and states second".
- **Source:** Econometric Society page for Kataoka (1963); Kall and Mayer (2005), p. 145; Roy (1952), p. 433; Sullivan (2008) on Roy anticipating Value at Risk.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha in its application (a bank's risk number at zero chance is the range rule); Value at Risk itself is pedigree.

### 4. Telser's trader held hedged and unhedged stocks at once: the 2028 deposit's order

(a) Telser (1955) took a merchant who hedges his stocks with futures. The merchant seeks the highest expected income, subject to the chance of a disaster level staying under a set value. Telser showed that it is rational to hold hedged and unhedged positions at the same time (Johnson's summary of Telser).

(b) We use the 2028 deposit in Telser's order, with the chance set at zero. The first money completes the ten (the top-up) and buys the floor, both owned; the rest goes where expected return is highest, VT. Roy alone would have stopped at the first step: once Treasuries more than meet a disaster level, his working ratio (m − d)/σ is largest with no shares at all. His true aim, the smallest chance, cannot tell apart holdings that all have no chance. It is Telser's second step, "then seek return", that lets VT in.

(c) Telser's merchant picks his chance and his disaster level in advance, and he hedges goods he must sell. Half of Laura's shares are promised to no one, so they have no disaster level at all, and Telser's constraint never reaches them. That is why the IPS can call that half her flexibility rather than a speculation.

- **Decision it serves:** the 2028 split: the top-up first, then "most" to the floor, then VT (IPS l.13-15; CANON A19).
- **Report part:** 3b or 2.2, one clause; References.
- **Source:** Johnson's note 7 on Telser (1955); Roy (1952), pp. 434 and 437; Telser itself paywalled.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for a finance judge (Telser's constraint is the textbook form); the "Roy alone would buy only Treasuries" step is the useful turn.

### 5. Roy's own inequality can promise nothing from shares

(a) Roy refused to assume a shape for returns, so he used the Bienaymé-Chebyshev inequality, which needs only an average and a spread. He admitted that when the expected gain is smaller than one spread, it says only that the chance of disaster is "less than or equal to unity, which is not very helpful".

(b) On our house figures, $1 in world shares never gets far past that line. The best Roy's inequality can say, at any horizon, is that the chance of ending below $1 is at most about 91% (at twelve years; about 48% with the sharper one-sided form). Take a figure stated two years ahead at 95% confidence. Under his inequality it is worth nothing; if returns are single-peaked (his footnote 5) it is worth 36 cents; on a bell curve, 73 cents; on the lognormal curve our model uses, 79 cents (Keel arithmetic). So any confidence level drawn from shares is mostly a statement about the curve one assumes. A Treasury's figure assumes none.

(c) Roy's inequality is the most pessimistic tool on purpose, and nobody prices shares as if they had no shape. So this does not show that shares are bad. It shows that a share-based confidence level rests on its assumed curve. That is why Laura states a figure only once it is in Treasuries, and why the range carries the word "certain" rather than a percentage (no "95%" label; CLAUDE.md, card Q71).

- **Decision it serves:** no figure stated before the 2031 move; "certain" for the stated range (IPS l.15; 3c's confidence).
- **Report part:** 3c, one sentence at most ("from shares, the same 95% is worth anything from nothing to 79 cents on the dollar, depending on the curve one assumes"); otherwise the store.
- **Source:** Roy (1952), pp. 434-435 and n. 5; Keel arithmetic.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Chebyshev is textbook; using the founder of safety-first's own tool to show how much of a confidence level is assumption is not.

### 6. Roy's test for which assets to hold is the IPS's career argument

(a) Roy's rule decides whether to hold an asset by one question: if everything else you own fell to the disaster level, what would this asset then be worth? ("If the prices of all the other assets fell to the critical price, what is the best (linear) estimate of price of the asset"). Hold it only if the answer is above the disaster level.

(b) Our IPS asks that question of shares against Laura's career. In a recession her advances "come later and smaller", co-sponsors cut their budgets, and "shares fall". So shares fail Roy's test for promised money. A Treasury that ends before the payment passes it, because it pays its face whatever happened to everything else.

(c) Roy's "other assets" sit in one portfolio on one date. Laura's career sits outside the portfolio, and after January 2028 no career money enters the plan. So the test bites where her career still touches the plan: the 2028 deposit (NUMBERS 5) and the co-sponsors in 2031-2033. It does not bite on payments already owned. The report should therefore place the career argument at the 2028 split and the range, and rest the payments on ownership.

- **Decision it serves:** the IPS's thesis that her career is "a large holding of shares ... you cannot sell" (l.9; CANON A1, A2).
- **Report part:** 2.3's lead, one clause of lineage ("the question Roy asked of every asset in 1952").
- **Source:** Roy (1952), p. 438.
- **Pedigree or aha:** half aha. The human-capital hedge is known (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson, card Q33). That Roy's 1952 rule already asks it, asset by asset, is not.

## Best three for the report

1. **Q189-001 with Q189-002 (idea 1), for 3a.** When Treasuries only just cover a promise, a 1% slice of shares and a 100% slice carry the same chance of a miss, about 1 in 3 for the 2033 payment; only the size differs ($99 against $9,867). So "take none" is the one zero on Roy's scale, and shares belong only to money above the promises. A judge expects a smaller stake to mean a smaller risk, and here it does not.
2. **Q189-011 with Q189-012 and Q189-014 (idea 3), for 3c.** The range's bottom is a Value at Risk at a chance of zero, the question Kataoka first asked in 1963. Shares answer $0 and the late-2032 fund answers its zero-interest payout, which is exactly how the range rule works. Laura, unlike Roy's man, writes her own disaster level, so she writes it under what she owns.
3. **Q189-005 with Q189-006 and Q189-007 (idea 2), for 3a or 2.3.** Roy's "known rocks of uncertain position" and his one-clause "complete and blissful security" split the plan in two: payments fixed in dollars sit in his corner, and the facility, priced in Taiwan, sails his waters with sea room. The first plan left the payments' rock unfixed until 2033; buying in 2027 fixed it.

Reserve: idea 5 (Q189-018, Q189-019) for one sentence in 3c on what a share-based 95% is worth under different curves.
