- **Problem class and the field that solved it first:** pricing many promises of future money when some will not be paid, and lending against them. Solved first by credit: a bad-debt reserve on receivables (the expected loss) and, for a lender, an advance rate on a borrowing base (lend only 80-90 cents on each eligible dollar owed, and hold collateral). Lincoln Center's bank applied exactly that to a concert hall's building pledges (row 5). Charities' accounting (FASB ASC 958-310, card Q90) books the same two cuts, time and doubt.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **At large, rich institutions the allowance for doubt is small: 1.0% to 3.3%.** With cards Q53 and Q90, more than twenty statements now run from 0% (MCASD, Tulsa, Fox Cities in 2010) to 19.8% (MoMA), with the big halls and universities at 1-3 cents in the dollar.
- **But the allowance is a smooth average, and the losses come in lumps:**
  - Lincoln Center wrote off one building pledge ("Write-off of uncollectible pledge", $3.04M in the "Redevelopment and other physical plant" column) in FY2025: 4.4% of its year-start capital-campaign pledges, twelve times its whole allowance a year earlier.
  - The University of Illinois Foundation wrote off $14.0M in FY2024 ("Uncollectible contributions receivable written off totaled $14,012 and $5,876 [thousand]"): 5.6% of its year-start pledges, 1.8 times the allowance it had set.
  - The Kennedy Center's FY2025 Form 990 carries a $48 million "bad debt expense", "essentially a write-down of previously recorded revenue that an organization no longer expects to collect" (The Atlantic); it had been owed $118.3M of pledges and grants a year earlier, so about two-fifths (Keel arithmetic; not all of it need be pledges). The cause was not a market fall: it followed a change of board and chair in February 2025 (rows 12-14).
- **And it sits on a few names.** Carnegie Hall: "Amounts receivable from five donors represented 65% ... of gross contributions receivable" (June 2025).

*Judge's note:* a charity's books say 1 to 3 cents of each pledged dollar will fail. One year at one famous hall, a single building pledge cost twelve times that reserve. For a residency with a handful of co-sponsors, "a co-sponsor falling short" is one name, not a percentage.

**Claim 2. CHANGE: building pledges run longer and depend on the building; no study shows they fail more often year for year.**

- **The cases point one way.**
  - Ruth Eckerd Hall held 10.0% against capital pledges and 5.4% against annual ones (card Q90 row 7).
  - Lincoln Center's FY2025 write-off was 99% a building pledge ($3,042 of $3,067 thousand).
  - The Royal Ontario Museum's building campaign: $220M raised privately, $30M still owed in 2012, "a fulfilment rate of 86 per cent" by the Globe and Mail's documents, against the museum's own "98-per-cent fulfilment rate", which counted "pledges on track to be paid".
  - In the crisis: the lead donor's "payments were delayed in the wake of the 2009 financial crisis"; a ministry briefing said the museum had "received only 50 per cent of the donations promised that year"; Ontario "filled a $23-million void left by these donors" through a loan.
- **The mechanism is time and the project.**
  - Campaign counsel: "approximately 10% of the funds when you secure the pledge, with about 30% ... by the end of the active solicitation period" (18 months, five-year pledges), so about 70% is still a promise when the asking stops. Redemption is "directly influenced by ... The progress of the project for which the funds were raised"; "organizations with effective pledge redemption plans can expect to collect 90%-95%" (CDS, 2018).
  - Practitioners plan on 90% ("organizations are often told to expect a 90% pledge fulfillment rate for planning purposes", Better Fundraising, 2026).
- **A lender prices it.** Lincoln Center's bank lent against "90% of eligible capital campaign pledge payments due before the term loan's final maturity and either 80% or 90% of pledge payments receivable thereafter", could "automatically withdraw pledge payments", and held $18.0M of collateral against pledges due after June 2026. The American Museum of Natural History borrowed $57M as "bridge financing in advance of receipt of donor pledge payments for the Gilder Center".
- **2020 left no mark at one hall.** Carnegie Hall's allowance was its lowest of five years at June 2021 (0.20%).

*Judge's note:* a bank lending against a concert hall's building pledges counted 80 to 90 cents on the dollar and still wanted collateral. The field that prices a promise best is the one that has to lend against it.

**Claim 3. CHANGE: no Taiwanese or East Asian statistic found; what was read points away from long written pledges.**

- Taiwan's Ministry of Culture accounting rule for national cultural foundations sets the accrual basis ("會計基礎，採權責發生制", art. 5), sends anything not covered to general standards (art. 2) and requires a formal receipt for every gift received (art. 27). It has no article on pledges (認捐) or an allowance for them.
- Taiwan's largest private building gift was made in kind: Kuo's NT$150億 (2007) to NTU was "建物的實物捐贈", and "台大自始至終未經手一毛錢的現金" (NTU never handled a dollar of cash). In 2019 NTU still lacked "80億元到100億元" for equipment while the donor's further gifts were "口頭允諾" (verbal promises), until a contract for NT$200億 in all was signed on 13 June 2019.
- With the store's rows (receipts on arrival, forms sent with the cash, a public NT$5,000萬 figure withdrawn, thanks stepped up by "實際到位金額"), the Taiwanese pattern is that a co-sponsor falls short through a figure that was never written down, or through equipment and fit-out that do not follow, more often than through a written pledge left unpaid. That is Keel's inference.

*Judge's note:* in Taiwan a big donor may hand over a finished building, not a cheque. Then "falling short" shows up as an empty equipment budget, not an unpaid pledge.

**Claim 4. KEEP for ordinary years and small co-sponsors; CHANGE for a crisis or a co-sponsor her own size (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 8o, 8p, 8u; illustration only, case l.106-107 and l.164-166, CANON A31).**

- **Against a co-sponsor pledge the size of her stated top ($115,000):**
  - a typical allowance (1% to 3%) is $1,150 to $3,450;
  - the planning 10% is $11,500;
  - an ROM-like 14% is $16,100;
  - ASU's 2010 allowance (30.7%) is $35,300;
  - the Baptist campaign's 37% is $42,550;
  - a Kennedy-like 41% is $47,150.
- **What her flexibility covers.** Her flexibility, $21,179 ($28,682 with anything above the top), covers 18.4% (24.9%) of such a pledge (NUMBERS 8o).
- **The honest unit is one co-sponsor.** Her flexibility could replace in full a co-sponsor who pledged up to about $21,000 (about $28,700 with anything above the top), or about a fifth (a quarter) of one her own size.
- **The bad years can coincide.** If shares are flat to the move and then 40% down by January 2033, her flexibility is $8,852 ($17,427 with anything above the top; NUMBERS 8p): 8.0% (15.8%) of a $110,000 pledge. Co-sponsors' promises *can* fail in the same downturn (ASU, the ROM), never "fall together".
- **One pool, three uses.** After a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar ($11,500, NUMBERS 8j), $17,182 of the $28,682 is left.

*Judge's note:* her flexibility covers an ordinary year's doubt several times over, and the planning 10% about twice. It does not replace a co-sponsor her own size. That is why her own figure is owned before she states it.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 4 (one building pledge written off at Lincoln Center), 3e area 5.** A famous campus set aside a quarter of a cent per pledged dollar, then lost 4.4 cents on one building pledge. It shows a judge that co-sponsor risk comes as a name, not an average. That is the gap between the planning figure and the real risk.
2. **Row 28 (her flexibility could stand in for a co-sponsor of about $21,000, or a fifth of one her own size), 3b.** It gives "a co-sponsor falling short" a size from the register alone. It does not estimate the facility or size a contingency fund.
3. **Row 17 (a $5 million pledge "contingent on the success of his investment"), 3d or 3e as the conceit's one turn.** A co-sponsor's figure written on his own investment is pencil. Laura's is written on Treasuries she owns. The parallel is true, short and surprising.
4. **Row 5 (a bank lends 80 to 90 cents on a building pledge), 3e or 3d.** The field that has to lend against promises discounts them and wants collateral. Laura's contribution needs no bridge loan.
5. **Rows 12-14 (the Kennedy Center's write-off of about two-fifths after a change of leadership), 3e area 5.** A co-sponsor can fall short with no market fall at all. Only her flexibility, promised to no one, meets that kind of loss. Say the dated facts and take no side.
