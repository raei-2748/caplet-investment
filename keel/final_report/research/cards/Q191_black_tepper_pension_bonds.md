- Problem class: who should carry the share risk when one pool of money owes fixed promises to one party and its leftover belongs to another. ## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. Black's rule inside one person: whoever owns what is left over carries the share risk, because the promised payments cannot rise

(a) In 1980 Fischer Black argued that a company's pension fund should hold only bonds, because a pensioner's benefit is fixed, so "Gains and losses in the pension fund are borne entirely by the firm": the fund is "almost as if" it were the company's own money, and the shareholders, who own whatever is left after the pensions are paid, carry all its share risk.
(b) Laura's plan holds the same two claims inside one person's money: the residency holds a fixed claim (ten payments of $50,000, which can never rise) and Laura holds whatever is left (her flexibility), so a gain on shares held behind the payments could only ever reach her, and a loss larger than her flexibility could only fall on the residency, since no new money arrives after January 2028 (the case; card Q97 rows 41-43). IPS l.11 gives shares only to money that is hers alone, which is Black's rule placed in one life.
(c) It breaks on the people: a pension's owners and its pensioners are different people, and the law keeps their money apart (Black: the funds "are legally distinct from the firm"), while Laura is both the founder who must pay and the owner of what is left, and no law separates the ten payments' Treasuries from her flexibility. In her plan the fence is a rule she wrote (IPS l.17: only two reasons to change it), not a trust she was given, so the report has to give the rule's reasons, because the reasons are all that hold the fence up.

- Decision: IPS l.11 (no share money behind any payment, CANON A5); l.15 (half the shares promised to no one, A30); l.17 (the two reasons, A34).
- Report part: 2.0's governing thought, one clause of pedigree ("the rule pension economists reached in 1980"); 2.3.
- Source: Black (1980), NBER WP 533, abstract and body; Tepper (1981), NBER WP 661, p. 6; EMS (2003), s.1.1.
- Pedigree or aha: PEDIGREE for a pension-literate judge (EMS: the theory "starts with the work of Black (1980) and Tepper (1981)"). The new part is the break: one person has no trust deed, so her rule must carry its own reasons.

### 2. Without tax, Black and Tepper said the owner would not care; Laura must care, because she cannot undo the choice

(a) Most of Black's and Tepper's case was a tax trade (the fund earns bond interest tax-free while the company deducts the interest it pays: "Shifting $2 million from stocks to bonds has a present value for the firm's stockholders of about $1 million"), and both said that without the tax the owner would not care, Black because "a firm's stockholders may not care how the pension fund investments are divided between bonds and stocks", Tepper because "investors can offset any corporate asset/liability decision on personal account".
(b) Taxes are out of scope in Laura's case, so the IPS cannot borrow their headline reason; it stands instead on the condition their indifference needs, and Laura fails that condition three times: she cannot sell her largest holding (her career, "one you cannot sell", IPS l.9), she cannot borrow (margin is banned for her plan, COMPETITION.md l.84), and she can add no money after January 2028. She cannot undo on her own account a choice made inside the portfolio, so for her the mix inside it is the risk she carries, beside a career that already moves with shares.
(c) Tepper concluded that shares in a pension fund "can only be rationalized by appealing to violations of the perfect markets assumptions"; Laura's case turns that sentence round, because her violations (a career she cannot sell, no borrowing, no new money) are exactly what keep shares away from her promises. The founders of the argument needed a tax to make the mix matter, and Laura's case makes it matter with no tax at all, so the report gives this reason and never a tax one.

- Decision: IPS l.9 and l.11 (CANON A1, A2, A5): the portfolio as a counterbalance to her career, and Treasuries for every promised dollar.
- Report part: 2.3, one clause (a person who cannot undo the choice cannot be indifferent to it).
- Source: Black (1980), abstract and closing pages; Tepper (1981), pp. 2, 4, 15; IPS l.9.
- Pedigree or aha: AHA. A judge who knows Black (1980) knows it as a tax paper; that its author said the case vanishes without tax, and that Laura's case revives it without tax for a reason a teenager can follow, is the turn.

### 3. Exley, Mehta and Smith's zero sum, measured in Caplet's own first plan

(a) Exley, Mehta and Smith, the British actuaries who made the case without tax (1997, restated by them in 2003), found that "shareholders do not gain from an equity investment policy over bond investment", and that what is left over is "zero sum, in that a gain to members is a loss to shareholders, and vice versa".
(b) In Laura's case the residency stands where the members stand and Laura where the shareholders stand, and the team's first plan shows the transfer in figures: with 75% shares to 2030 and the ten bought only on 1 January 2033, it left the residency short in 6,453 of 200,000 modelled markets (about 1 in 31), a median $27,695 short where it fell short (NUMBERS 8s), while every dollar it earned above the ten's cost would have gone to Laura's side, because the payments cannot rise.
(c) It breaks where the pension fight came from: there the members and the shareholders are different people, so the transfer is a quarrel between them, while here both sides belong to Laura, so shares behind the promises would let her keep the gains while her own residency carried the losses she could not cover, which is why the IPS can say of these promises, "ours is to take none". The 1-in-31 is the check that priced the first plan, never the reason it was dropped (that is IPS l.9's, a writer's income, CANON A3), so it goes in 2.3 or 3a, never in 3f.

- Decision: IPS l.11's "one step further" (CANON A4): none of the ten depends on a share price.
- Report part: 2.3 or 3a, one clause; 3f keeps its own telling with no outside name.
- Source: EMS (2003), s.1.1 and s.2.1; NUMBERS 8s (the 27 Sep model, re-run).
- Pedigree or aha: AHA for a judge outside UK pensions. The zero sum is known to pension actuaries; measured in the team's own first plan it is new, and it says who would have paid.

### 4. Boots cut risk in the fund so the company could carry more; the IPS's last line makes the same split for a person

(a) In 2000-2001 the Boots pension fund sold all its shares for long bonds, and in 2002 the company began a £300m share buyback "made possible by having moved the plan assets to matching bonds"; in its architects' words, "Reducing risk 'off balance sheet' in the pension fund allowed Boots to increase risk 'on balance sheet'".
(b) The IPS's closing lines draw the same line through Laura's life ("You take risks on the page. Fair enough; that's your job. With these promises, ours is to take none."): the risk the portfolio refuses behind the promises is the risk she already carries in her career (IPS l.9) and keeps on purpose in her flexibility (l.15). The residency is also spared what Ralfe, Speed and Palin call the pensioners' "double jeopardy" (the company failing when the pension is short); the residency's version is shares falling in the same downturn in which co-sponsors' budgets can fall (IPS l.9).
(c) It breaks on choice: Boots chose to add risk to its balance sheet after cutting it in the fund, and could borrow to do so, while Laura never chose her on-balance-sheet risk (her career) and cannot borrow, so for her the argument runs one way only, and the portfolio sheds risk because her life already holds plenty, which is the IPS's own reason ("a counterbalance to that – definitely not a contributor"). Boots's timing was luck by its architects' own account ("Boots was lucky to sell equities into a falling equity market at an average FTSE level of 6,000"), and Laura's plan needs no timing at all: it buys the ten in January 2027 at whatever they cost and completes them in January 2028 (IPS l.13).

- Decision: the scope of IPS l.19 ("with these promises", CANON A6); IPS l.9 (A1, A2); l.15's three uses for her flexibility (A31).
- Report part: 2.3 or 3a, one clause. Never in 3f, never in Laura's words to co-sponsors.
- Source: Ralfe, Speed & Palin (2004), pp. 13, 17; IPS l.9, l.15, l.19.
- Pedigree or aha: AHA. The Boots buyback is little known outside UK pension circles, and it shows the IPS's last line is a capital-structure argument, not a slogan.

### 5. Boots stepped back in 2004 because the bond market ran out of years; Laura's ten end well inside it

(a) Three years after going all-bonds, in May 2004, the Boots trustees moved 15% of the £2.8bn fund (about £425m) out of bonds, some into shares, giving as their reason the bond market's reach, not a change of theory ("There are virtually no bonds issued with a maturity beyond 35 years"); Ralfe answered that the decision "increased risk for the 70,000 scheme members that their pensions will not be paid", and Exley, then at Mercer, that his advice was the same as in 2000-2001, "to be 100% in bonds".
(b) Laura's ten end on 1 January 2042, and each has a Treasury that matures before it (IPS l.13; the last a bond maturing 15 November 2041, NUMBERS 8g), so the one reason the first all-bond pension gave for stepping back cannot arise for her. She met only a small version of it: no Treasury bond matures between February 2031 and February 2036, a hole made when the US Treasury stopped selling 30-year bonds in October 2001, the same year Boots finished buying its long bonds (card Q93), and the plan filled the hole with iBond Treasury funds, staying inside Treasuries where the Boots trustees stepped outside bonds.
(c) Did it work? The Boots scheme reached the theory's end: on 24 November 2023 it insured all 53,000 members with Legal & General in a £4.8bn buy-in, so that "the Scheme will not be reliant on Boots to pay benefits", but Boots committed about £500m of extra contributions on the way. A pension's sponsor is still there decades later to close a gap, and Laura's is not after January 2028, so her match has to be complete on the day it is bought, which is why the extra Treasuries are sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 2).

- Decision: the ten bought in January 2027, each maturing before its payment (IPS l.13, CANON A7); the extra Treasuries (NUMBERS 2; WORDS); the iBond funds for 2033-2036 (CANON D13).
- Report part: 3a (why the match is complete when bought), one clause; 2.2 may take the 2001 coincidence in one clause beside Q93's hole.
- Source: IPE (2004); Professional Pensions (28 May 2004); Professional Adviser (28 May 2004); Boots UK (2023); Legal & General (2023); card Q93; NUMBERS 2, 8g.
- Pedigree or aha: AHA. Few judges know Boots stepped back in 2004, and fewer the reason; that the reason cannot reach Laura's ten, and that her one small version of it was solved inside Treasuries, is checkable against Table 1.

### 6. Black's "debt capacity" is Laura's capacity to state a figure, with the shares' share set to nothing

(a) Black's second argument had nothing to do with tax: moving a pension fund from shares to bonds "will increase the firm's debt capacity, because it will reduce the volatility of the firm's future cash flows", because steadier assets let a company promise lenders more.
(b) Laura's promises to co-sponsors work the same way at the strictest setting: the bottom of her range is what the late-2032 fund will pay, "the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt" (IPS l.15), and the top she states is only what the shares moved into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings will add, "so whatever figure you choose is money you already own"; her capacity to promise is exactly what she owns in Treasuries, and shares give her none until they are moved.
(c) It breaks on degree: a company's lenders will lend something against risky assets, at a price, while Laura's plan lets shares support no promise at all, so her rule is Black's argument taken to its end. In the report's own terms ink is promise capacity, and pencil is capacity not yet bought (CONCEIT).

- Decision: the bottom from January 2028 and the top at the move (IPS l.15, CANON A22, A32); the conceit's rule that Laura states a figure only once it is inked.
- Report part: 3c, or 3d's reserves, one clause; never in Laura's own words.
- Source: Black (1980), abstract; IPS l.15; `keel/final_report/CONCEIT.md`.
- Pedigree or aha: NICHE. It gives the conceit a pedigree in corporate finance without changing a word of it.

## Best three for the report

1. Idea 2, rows 5, 10 and 13 (without tax, the founders of the bonds-only argument said the owner would not care, and Laura is the case where she must, because she cannot undo the choice): for 2.3, one clause. It inverts a famous paper's conclusion with a reason any reader follows, and it answers the judge who asks "why not shares, with six years to wait?" without a tax word.
2. Idea 4, rows 21-22 (Boots cut risk in the fund so the company could carry more; the IPS's last line makes the same split for a person): for 2.3 or 3a, one clause. It shows Caplet's closing line is a capital-structure argument with a real precedent, and the break (Laura never chose her career's risk and cannot borrow) is the IPS's own reason.
3. Idea 5, rows 26 and 30 (Boots stepped back in 2004 because no bonds ran past 35 years, which cannot happen to ten payments ending in 2042; and even Boots needed about £500m more from its company in 2023, which Laura could never ask for): for 3a, one clause. It says why her match must be complete when bought, with a case the judge can look up.
