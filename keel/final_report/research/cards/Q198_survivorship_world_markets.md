## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. In 1921 the whole world was the safe answer to "who will survive?"

- (a) In 1999 Jorion and Goetzmann rebuilt stock prices for 39 countries from 1921 to 1996, including the markets that
  later shut for years or for good, and found that a fund holding every country in proportion to its size, dead markets
  included, gained 4.04% a year above inflation from price rises alone, close to the 4.32% of the United States, the one
  big market that never closed, while the typical country gained 0.75%.
- (b) So refusing to guess which country would survive cost the world investor less than a third of a point a year, with
  smaller swings (11.05% against 15.83%), while guessing wrong could cost everything; VT makes the same refusal for
  Laura, among countries as well as among companies.
- (c) It breaks because the 1921 world fund was about half American by size, so it did well partly by holding a lot of
  the survivor (the authors: its growth "must be within 100 basis points of the U.S."), and VT today is 62.4% American.
  The break teaches that a world fund does not escape the bet on the survivor: it holds the market's current guess and
  lets that guess change by itself as countries rise and fall (the UK was 24.2% of the world's shares at the end of 1899
  and the US 14.5%; the US was 60.5% at the start of 2024), with no decision by Laura or the team.
- **Decision it serves:** one world fund (IPS l.15; TNA l.23). **Part:** 3f (the copout moment's "after", one clause);
  2.3 (why one fund).
- **Source:** Jorion and Goetzmann (1999), pp. 953-955, 971-975, Tables I, V, VI, VII; Dimson, Marsh and Staunton (2024),
  Figure 2.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows the headline (4.3% against 0.8%); few know that the same paper's world
  index, with the dead markets left in, nearly matched the survivor and in dollars beat it (7.25% against 6.95%), which
  answers "copout" one level up, at the level of countries.

### 2. A market died inside VT's own index in March 2022, and cost a world fund about a fifth of one percent

- (a) In September 2021 FTSE Russell, which builds the index VT copies, put Russia on its watch list for a possible
  promotion; in March 2022, after Russia invaded Ukraine and Moscow's exchange stopped foreigners selling, it deleted
  every Russian company from all its share indexes at a value of zero.
- (b) A world fund lost about a fifth of one percent (Russia was 0.19% of FTSE's All-World index that week), because
  Russia's share had already shrunk, from 6.2% of FTSE's emerging index in 2012 to 1.4% the day before the deletion:
  the mechanism Jorion and Goetzmann measured, "markets that die tend to have less weight when they do so". On Laura's
  $29,594 of VT the same loss is about $56 to $104 (Keel arithmetic), while a one-country fund, like the Taiwan fund
  raised when Darren called VT "a copout", stakes the whole sum on one country's survival.
- (c) It breaks for a large market that dies quickly: across their wartime closures Japan's shares fell 95% after
  inflation (1944-49) and Germany's 72%, and no shrinking weight protects a fund that is 62.4% one country. The break
  teaches that VT's protection is the market's own slow warning, which fails when a death is fast and big, and that is
  why no figure Laura states rests on VT until the moved half is in Treasuries.
- **Decision it serves:** one world fund (IPS l.15); the move before the 2031 meetings (l.15; NUMBERS 8r). **Part:** 3f
  (the copout moment; one sentence on the country Darren's alternative would have had to pick); 3b (her flexibility
  sits in a fund that has already lived through a market's death).
- **Source:** FTSE Russell (2022a, 2022b, 2022c); Realindex (2022); Vanguard (2022a, 2022b); Morningstar (Johnson, 2022);
  Jorion and Goetzmann (1999), pp. 960, 966-972; Dimson, Marsh and Staunton (2024).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. It is recent, checkable and inside VT's own index family; the watch list six months before
  the write-off shows that not even the index keeper could pick the survivor.

### 3. Survivorship can make a forecast wrong but never a sale price, so the ink rule is right whatever the puzzle's answer

- (a) In 1985 Mehra and Prescott showed that US shares had beaten Treasury bills by 6.18 points a year from 1889 to 1978,
  when their model of sensible savers allowed at most 0.35, and economists have offered three kinds of answer since:
  people fear risk far more than the model assumed, shares are paid for rare disasters the US record happens not to
  show (Rietz), or the record is a survivor's and the true premium is smaller (Brown, Goetzmann and Ross).
- (b) Laura's plan never needs to know which answer is right, because it states a figure only after the shares have been
  sold and the money sits in Treasuries: survivorship and the puzzle are about what shares can be expected to earn,
  and a sale price on 2 January 2031 is not an expectation but a fact about money she owns. Survivorship is the error of
  reading a survivor's record as the expectation; the ink rule states only what has already survived to the sale, and
  never calls it a forecast.
- (c) It breaks on her flexibility, because the answer still decides how much the half kept is worth (about $21,200 at
  7.5% a year, about $17,200 at 3.1%; NUMBERS 8v). Mehra's 2003 objection adds a twist: survival cannot explain the
  premium, he argues, because in crises "bonds are as likely to lose value as stocks", so the bias cancels in the gap;
  for Laura it does not cancel, because her Treasuries' return is read off the 2027 and 2028 prices she pays, and only
  her share return comes from history. The break teaches where the risk lives: the puzzle is free for her promises and
  costs only money promised to no one.
- **Decision it serves:** the ink rule, "whatever figure you choose is money you already own" (IPS l.15); the move
  (NUMBERS 8r). **Part:** 3c (why nothing from shares is stated before the move); 3e (one assumption).
- **Source:** Mehra and Prescott (1985), pp. 145-147, 156, 158; Mehra (2003), NBER version, sections on disaster states
  and survivorship and on the ex ante premium; Brown, Goetzmann and Ross (1995), pp. 853-854, 861-862; Li and Xu
  (2002), abstract.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the puzzle is pedigree; "three answers, one rule" and the non-cancelling bias are aha, because
  they show the plan is built to be indifferent to finance's most argued-over question.

### 4. Survival paints the bounce-back, and the IPS refuses to wait for it

- (a) In 1995 Brown, Goetzmann and Ross proved that a stock market studied only because it survived will look as if it
  always recovers after crashes, even if its prices really wandered at random, because the paths that fell and never
  came back closed and left no record ("At least part of the apparent mean reversion ... may be an artifact of survival").
- (b) The IPS refuses to lean on that look: the promised half moves into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings whatever the
  price, because "A fall in the market is not one of them" (l.17); after a 40% fall below cost the top is stated at
  $105,000 rather than $115,000 (NUMBERS 8p), instead of waiting for a recovery the survivor's record makes look sure.
- (c) It breaks because recovery is not only an illusion (Mehra writes that returns "have been documented to be mean
  reverting", and the half kept does wait until 2033 and gets the bounce if it comes); the break teaches the rule's real
  shape: waiting for a recovery is allowed, but only with money promised to no one.
- **Decision it serves:** the move on a date, not on a price (IPS l.15; NUMBERS 8r); the two reasons (l.17). **Part:**
  3b (the half kept may wait, the moved half may not); 3c (one clause).
- **Source:** Brown, Goetzmann and Ross (1995), pp. 853-854 and the section on long-term dependence; Mehra (2003), NBER
  version, pp. 33-34.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The survival result on mean reversion is little known outside academic finance; it gives the
  last sentence of IPS l.17 a reason a sceptical judge cannot wave away, and it answers the weakness the store recorded
  against the delay option (H2-050: "no mean reversion in the model").

### 5. How far to trust 7.5%: only as far as 3.4%

- (a) Ninety years of the most successful market's record measured its premium over bills as 6.18 points with a
  standard error of 1.76, so even that century cannot tell a premium of about 3 points from one of about 10 (two
  standard errors, Keel arithmetic), and Jorion and Goetzmann put the gap between the survivor and the typical country
  at 3.57 points a year (4.32% against 0.75%).
- (b) The plan needs far less precision than a figure printed to one decimal suggests: the stated top stays $115,000 for
  any return above about 3.4% a year to the move (NUMBERS 8v), and 7.5% less the whole survivor gap is about 3.9%, so
  the top survives even the harshest survivorship cut while the bottom and the ten payments use no share forecast.
- (c) It breaks on the horizon: the survivor gap is a century's average of price rises after inflation, and Laura's
  forecast is three years of dollar returns with dividends, so the subtraction is an illustration, not a re-run; and
  over three years the danger is not a slightly lower average but one bad draw (Table 2's $95,000 to $140,000), which
  no forecast settles. The break teaches that the forecast does a small job in the plan, which is the honest way to use
  a figure whose history may be a survivor's.
- **Decision it serves:** the 7.5% in Table 2's note; the rule that sets the top after the move. **Part:** 3e (the
  share assumption, one sentence); 3c (one clause).
- **Source:** Mehra and Prescott (1985), Table 1; Jorion and Goetzmann (1999), abstract and Table I; NUMBERS 8v.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the humility is pedigree; the number is aha: the forecast can lose the entire distance between
  the luckiest market and the typical one and the top Laura states does not move.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 3, survivorship can make a forecast wrong but never a sale price (rows 20, 24-27; 3c).** It gives the ink rule
   a reason a finance judge respects: the plan is built to be right whichever answer to the equity premium puzzle is
   true, and the one place the answer still bites, her flexibility, is money promised to no one. Mehra's objection, and
   why it does not cancel for Laura, shows the team read past the headline.
2. **Idea 2, a market died inside VT's own index in 2022 (rows 10-14, with rows 3 and 5; 3f, one sentence).** Recent,
   checkable and close to home: FTSE listed Russia for promotion in September 2021 and wrote it off at zero in March
   2022, and a world fund lost about a fifth of a percent because the dying market had already shrunk. It answers
   "copout" at the level of countries with a fact, not a slogan.
3. **Idea 4, survival paints the bounce-back and the IPS refuses to wait (rows 18, 25; 3b and 3c).** It turns the last
   sentence of IPS l.17 from a rule into a finding: the recovery that long records seem to promise is partly what
   survival alone would show, so the moved half goes on its date and only the half kept may wait.
