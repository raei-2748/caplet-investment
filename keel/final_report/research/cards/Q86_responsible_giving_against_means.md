## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE (the formula), KILL ("expect lead gifts near that"); Laura's contribution is generous, not timid, and is kept from recklessness by structure, not by smallness.**
  - **The formula is real and common, but it is a house rule, not a law.** Blackbaud's ResearchPoint: "Major Giving Capacity (MGC) is 5% of Total Confirmed Assets ... over a 5 year period". Hogan's textbook (2008, via a researcher's handout): total philanthropic capacity is 5% of net worth over five years. A consultant calls it "One of the most well-known (and widely-used) formulas" (Helen Brown Group, 2023).
  - **The share runs from 1% to 10%.** By age, 1% of net worth (ages 21-39) up to 5% (70 and over) (Hogan). DonorSearch rates a donor who "fully commits to an organization" at 10% of net worth "over a 3 to 5 year period". A public-media guide says "a percentage between 2%-5%". Kindsight applies 5% to 15% of a property's value.
  - **Even the period is disputed.** Five years (Blackbaud, Kindsight, University of Washington: "major gifts to be payable in installments over a five-year period"), against "total 'wallet' for philanthropic giving in a year" (Helen Brown Group, 2015) and "two to three years typically" (Helen Brown, 2014).
  - **Its own profession doubts it.** "The 5% figure we've been using isn't real" (Helen Brown, 2014: it came from 2008 IRS data in which itemizers with $100,000+ income gave 4.2% of discretionary income). "every formula for estimating gift capacity yields a wrong answer" (David Lamb, quoted 2015).
  - **Capacity is not the ask.** It is the total a donor could give "to all causes" (Kindsight). The ask is set with inclination and readiness. Blackbaud's organisation-specific rating, Target Gift Range, covers "a 12-month period". A lead gift is judged by its share of a campaign goal (Q11b r30; Q32), which the case rules out (case l.164-166).
  - **Laura, placed (Keel arithmetic).**
    - Her contribution is about $115,000, 25.6% of the $450,000 she puts in (Q32 r35). That is 5.1 times the 5% rule's share and 2.6 times DonorSearch's "fully commits" 10%.
    - The rules would rate a donor for $115,000 over five years only if she were worth $2.3 million (at 5%), $1.15 million (at 10%) or $11.5 million (at 1%, the youngest age band).
    - The promised money is $514,357 in 1 Jan 2033 money, 87.6% of what her deposits grow to (NUMBERS 8k). No capacity rule rates anyone to promise 87.6% of anything: every rule read assumes the donor keeps 90-99% of what is identified.
  - **So:** not timid; generous by every yardstick read. It would be reckless if the portfolio were all she had. The case says it is not: her "living expenses and short-term financial needs will be covered by income and financial resources outside the portfolio" (l.61-63). The plan makes the large share safe by owning the operating money first and keeping her flexibility. It does not do it by keeping the share small.
  - **Judge's note:** by the prospect researcher's own rule of thumb, $115,000 is the five-year giving capacity of someone worth $2.3 million. Laura reaches it from a $450,000 portfolio. That is safe only because the ten payments are owned first.
- **Claim 2: KEEP, with one change of frame; the plan meets the definition twice over.**
  - **The definition, in a private bank's own words.** Bernstein: "core capital" is "the minimum amount they need to maintain their lifestyle", sized "with a 95% degree of confidence" over 10,000 simulated markets. "Excess capital" is what can go "to descendants or philanthropic causes without jeopardizing their financial security" (2008).
  - **UBS:** "The Legacy strategy represents a family's surplus. Once the Liquidity and Longevity strategies are fully funded, excess assets are segregated into a Legacy strategy" (2018). Liquidity is "a 3-year bond ladder" matched to planned spending.
  - **In the academic literature:** Murphy's three streams (resources, spending, and "a stream of truly discretionary resources"; the more "permanent" the gap, the stronger the case for giving), and discernment beginning with "Clarification of general financial capacity and charitable resources" (Schervish, 2015).
  - **Laura's plan meets it twice.** Her lifestyle is outside the portfolio (case l.61-63). Inside it, the ten payments are funded first. They are held to a stricter test than the bank's 95%: owned in Treasuries that mature before each date. The contribution comes only from what is left, and her flexibility stays hers (IPS l.15).
  - **The change of frame.** For Laura, the "core" is not her lifestyle but her own earlier promise, the ten payments.
  - **Words:** never "surplus" for what comes back from the extra Treasuries (WORDS, entry "what comes back from the extra Treasuries"). Never the 95% label in 3c (CLAUDE.md, cards Q71 and Q74).
  - **Judge's note:** private banks put philanthropy in the riskiest bucket because it is surplus with no date ("Endowment-style portfolio with a high allocation to risk assets", UBS). Laura's money for the building sits in shares only while it is promised to no one. The day it becomes a figure co-sponsors hear, it moves into Treasuries. That is the IPS's governing thought in a wealth manager's own terms.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with numbers.**
  - **What the wealthy give each year.** US families worth $1-5 million gave 0.3% of net worth in a year, $5-10 million 0.2%, and $10 million or more 0.5% (1995 Survey of Consumer Finances; Schervish & Havens). In summary, the wealthy "should be characterized as giving around 5% of their income".
  - **Affluent households gave $34,917 on average in 2022** (Bank of America study), against an average wealth of $31 million in the sample. That is about 0.1% (Keel arithmetic).
  - **The Giving Pledge asks billionaires for "the majority of their wealth ... in their lifetime or wills", and it is "a moral commitment – not a legally binding one".**
  - **Giving What We Can asks for 10% of income,** or a wealth share "suggested at 2.5%".
  - **Laura (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 8k):** the plan promises 87.6% of what her deposits grow to, within six years, and owns it before it is stated. Of what remains after the operating reserve, the contribution is 79.4%.
  - **Caveat.** Her portfolio is a dedicated fund, not her net worth. The comparison shows the size of the promise, not that she is richer or more generous than a billionaire.
  - **Judge's note:** a Giving Pledge signer promises a majority over a lifetime, unwritten. Laura's plan promises about 87 cents of every portfolio dollar by 2033, and owns it before she says it.
- **Claim 4: KILL the objection; CHANGE "the half kept is responsible, not stingy" to "her flexibility is small, and it is for the project".**
  - **"Half" is half of the shares, not half of the money.** Centrally, her flexibility is about $21,300. With anything above the top it is about $29,800 (NUMBERS 4). That is 20.6% of what remains after the operating reserve, and 5.1% of all the money in the plan in 2033 ($589,655, NUMBERS 8k; Keel arithmetic).
  - **The profession keeps far more back.** Capacity rules assume 90-99% kept. The standard "give and keep an income" gift, the charitable gift annuity, is priced so that about half reaches the charity: "Since 1955 the ACGA has targeted a residuum ... of 50%". The Giving Pledge lets a signer keep just under half. Donor-advised funds paid out 25.2% of assets in FY2024 and hold the rest for later grants.
  - **The codes forbid the opposite of a reserve.** The UK code makes fundraisers weigh "whether a donation is likely to affect the person's ability to sufficiently care for themselves or leave them in financial hardship". The US gift planners' standard says "The consequences for ... the donor ... should be apparent, and the assumptions underlying any financial illustrations should be realistic". A gift that "harms the donor's financial well-being" is a red flag for undue influence (Rutgers).
  - **The case says it too:** "committing all remaining assets could limit her financial flexibility" (l.102-103).
  - **And her flexibility is not for her own spending.** The IPS names the project's own risks: "building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short" (l.15).
  - **Words:** "half kept" is a never word in the report (WORDS, entry "her flexibility").
  - **Judge's note:** "half the shares" sounds like half the money. It is about five cents of every dollar in the plan in 2033, and it guards the residency, not Laura's lifestyle.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 18 (UBS invests the giving bucket for growth; Laura's moves to Treasuries when promised), with rows 17 and 19.** Serves 2.0 and 3b. A judge expects philanthropy money to be the risky money. The plan agrees, but only while it is promised to no one, which is the IPS's governing thought in a wealth manager's own terms.
2. **Row 12 (the 5% rule places $115,000 as the five-year capacity of someone worth $2.3 million), with rows 1, 4 and 13.** Serves 3b. Answers "timid, generous or reckless": generous by the profession's own rule. Safe because the payments are owned first, not because the share is small. Use only with the case's limit (l.61-63).
3. **Row 34 (her flexibility is about five cents of every dollar in the plan in 2033).** Serves 3b. Kills the objection that keeping half the shares makes the contribution look small, with the register's own figures.
4. **Rows 15-16 (Bernstein's core and excess capital; Laura's core held to a stricter test).** Serves 3b and 3a. The professional definition of responsible giving is needs first, then give from what is left. The plan meets it twice: her lifestyle is outside the portfolio, and her ten payments are owned, not simulated.
5. **Rows 24-25 (the gift planners' standard and the UK hardship test).** Serves 3b and 3e. In the profession's ethics, a responsible gift shows its consequences for the donor and leaves her able to care for herself. The case and the IPS give Laura both.

- **Bank of America 2023** was read through extracted pages. Its reasons for giving less were not found in the parts read; the 2021 study's non-givers' reasons were used instead.
- **No source found** rates a founder's contribution to her own project against her means; every capacity rule is built for a charity ranking outside donors. No Taiwan prospect-research or wealth-planning practice was searched.
- **IRS Statistics of Income estate data** (charitable bequests as a share of the largest estates) was not read. A CRS report gives only the totals ($49.5 billion of bequests on estate returns filed in 2023).
- **Schervish and Havens (2001a)'s medians do not reconcile:** a median need of $20 million cannot be 67% above a median wealth of $35 million. The 67% is probably the median of each family's own ratio (Keel reading, UNVERIFIED).
