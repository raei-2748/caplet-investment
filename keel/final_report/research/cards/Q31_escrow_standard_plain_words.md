## (b) Cards

**B1. The right name for the standard: "full cash", not "escrowed to maturity" (rows 1-4).**
- *What is it?* MSRB's investor education and glossary separate two sufficiency tests. "Net cash refunding": enough
  "together with interest earnings thereon". "Full cash or gross refunding": enough "without reinvestment". An
  "escrowed-to-maturity" bond is defined by WHEN the escrow pays (to maturity, not to a call date) and its sufficiency
  includes "any permitted earnings". A verification report by a CPA or another independent party proves the numbers.
- *Did it work?* For municipal investors, yes: the terms are standard, and EMMA flags refunded bonds. For a lay
  reader, no evidence either way; the fresh reader (who knows bonds) could not parse "escrow verifier's standard".
- *How is Laura's case like it?* The arithmetic is the same: the ten are sized to pay with no earnings at all (NUMBERS
  2, MODEL), which is the gross or full-cash test.
- *How is it not?* No trustee, no escrow agreement, no verification report, holdings an escrow would not accept (five
  iBond funds and an ETF for waiting money, NUMBERS 2), and Laura may change the plan for two named reasons (IPS l.17).
- *What Caplet takes.* 3a: at most one clause for the bond reader, naming "full cash" (or "gross") arithmetic and
  saying it is the same test, not the same legal standing. INTENT item 1 and SKELETON 3a should stop saying "escrow
  verifier's standard" as a label; keep it as a source note. No figure.

**B2. The lottery annuity: the truest public analogue, kept off the page (rows 8-11).**
- *What is it?* A jackpot winner who takes the annuity: the cash pool "is used to buy government bonds that pay out over
  a 25-year period ... or over a 30-year period" (Wisconsin). California holds every investment "to maturity to meet the
  required prize payouts", structured to pay "prior to scheduled annuity prize payments", and keeps a reserve fund "to
  mitigate credit and reinvestment risk" ($1,888,260 against $791.4m par, about 0.24%; Keel arithmetic).
- *Did it work?* Operationally yes: California reports a $568.7m portfolio funding scheduled payments, 83.5% backed by
  the US government. As a lay explanation, Wisconsin's two sentences need no financial term.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* Money arrives once, buys government bonds that each pay before a fixed yearly date,
  and a separate pot covers reinvestment risk. That is Laura's ten plus the backstop.
- *How is it not?* Lotteries mostly hold STRIPS (zero coupons, nothing to reinvest); Wharton bans STRIPS for both of
  Laura's deposits, so her coupons need the backstop, and it is about 10.2% of her reserve (NUMBERS 8h, MODEL), about
  forty times the lottery's share. The tone (windfall, gambling) is wrong for a founder asking co-sponsors to trust her.
- *What Caplet takes.* No report words. It is the team's calibration test for the plain sentence (would it sit in a
  lottery FAQ?), and, if a judge-facing source is wanted for "a spare pot against reinvestment risk is normal practice",
  the California report is a real one.

**B3. Why the exception goes inside the sentence (rows 13-17, 28).**
- *What is it?* Fuzzy-trace theory: people keep the gist, and for catastrophic risks the gist is categorical (safe or
  risky). Reyna's informed-consent example: a 2% risk remembered as 10% is informed; as 0% it is not. FINRA 2210 bars a
  misleading omission of a qualification and allows footnotes only where they do not inhibit understanding.
- *Did it work?* FTT is supported across many health studies (Reyna, 2008); FINRA's standard is enforced practice.
  Neither was tested on investment-report readers.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* A missed operating payment is the catastrophic outcome for Laura's residency. A judge
  will file the ten as "safe" or "risky"; the exception decides whether "safe" is true.
- *How is it not?* Laura's residual is not a probability Caplet estimates; it is a named event (US default) plus small
  sized frictions (CANON C9).
- *What Caplet takes.* 3a and 2.0: the exception travels with the standard, in the frozen words "barring a US default"
  (CANON A4); never "the only exception"; never "guaranteed" (C13). Figure 1's title carries the exception too.

**B4. Saying the price: which one number (rows 16, 19-20, 31).**
- *What is it?* Regulators require benefits and limits with "equal prominence" (Rule 156) and warn that protection is
  bought by "sacrificing higher yield" (RN 09-73). The fresh reader asked for exactly this: "what the extra safety
  costs her in dollars, in one place".
- *Did it work?* The fresh reader read 111% easily but then asked why the range was only $95,000 to $115,000: the ratio
  invited a free-safety inference.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* The standard has a real price: the 2028 deposit's first $38,282 completes and backs
  the ten (NUMBERS 2), so the stated range is about $15,000 lower at each end than under the cheaper margin (NUMBERS 2
  and 4); at forward rates $45,456 of the backstop returns to Laura (NUMBERS 8h). All MODEL.
- *How is it not?* Unlike a PPN, nothing is lost for sure: the price is mostly a set-aside that comes back if rates hold.
- *What Caplet takes.* The gist number is $38,282 (about a quarter of the 2028 deposit): the price in her own money,
  certain in every world, and the answer to the reader's question. The next sentence says most of it returns at
  forward rates ($45,456). 111% moves to Figure 1 with its why; "$15,000 per end" belongs to 3c, where the margin
  comparison lives.

**B5. The 30-second repeat-back test (rows 32-33).** Design in (c), verdict 3.
- *What is it?* Paraphrase testing (federal plain-language practice) and teach-back (AHRQ): readers say in their own
  words what a passage means; no correction; no yes-or-no questions; a parroted answer may hide misunderstanding.
- *Did it work?* VBA found that readers who all called a letter clear read "service-connected" in several different
  ways, and that adding an approximate date to another letter "eliminated countless phone calls". AHRQ quotes a
  practice that found shorter visits and higher satisfaction with teach-back (a practice report, not a trial).
- *How is Laura's case like it?* Scorecard 3a needs clarity 5: "a lay judge repeats" the standard. That is a teach-back
  outcome, so test it as one.
- *How is it not?* Judges read once and score later, with no one to re-teach them, so Caplet's test is closed-book and
  timed, unlike AHRQ's open-handout teach-back.
- *What Caplet takes.* The protocol below, run by the next fresh reader on the 3a notes, then on the 3a prose after 27
  Nov, and its result logged as the clarity cell.
