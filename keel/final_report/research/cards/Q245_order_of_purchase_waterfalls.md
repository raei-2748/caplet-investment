- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**

  - B. "A revenue bond's flow of funds pays debt service before anything else."
  - C. "A securitisation's waterfall pays senior before junior."
  - D. "A sinking fund's priority" is a third institution of the same kind.

- **Problem class:** allocating an inflow of unknown size among claims of different rank, with the ranking fixed before the money arrives. **Fields that solved it first:** municipal revenue-bond finance in contract form (the bond resolution's "flow of funds"; its first use not dated in this run), then structured finance's "priority of payments" (disclosure required since Regulation AB, adopted 2004, and in the EU's STS rules of 2017); the law's default for a pot already short is the Bankruptcy Code's absolute priority rule; household finance's version is the buffer stock (Carroll 1997).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim A: CHANGE.** Ranking an uncertain inflow is standard: the MSRB calls the flow of funds "the order and priority of handling, depositing and disbursing pledged revenues, as set forth in the bond contract"; S&P lists the "typical" accounts; the SEC requires every public ABS prospectus to describe "the payment allocations, rights and distribution priorities" (17 CFR 229.1113(a)(2)); the EU requires STS deals to "clearly specify the priorities of payment". But "promised uses first" is wrong as a general rule: the first slot goes to whatever keeps the money coming (a utility's operation and maintenance; a securitisation's servicer and trustees), and only then the promise. Say "ranked uses, with the promise ahead of everything not needed to keep the money coming".
  - **Judge's note:** Laura can put the promise literally first only because her inflow has no running costs inside the portfolio: her living costs are met outside it (case l.61-64, as card Q206 cites).
- **Claim B: CHANGE.** "Debt service before anything else" is a gross revenue pledge, the rarer form. The common net pledge pays operation and maintenance first: "Revenues are used first to pay O&M when due and second to pay debt service" (California Debt Financing Guide); every city ordinance read puts O&M as the "First Charge and Lien" (Georgetown, Idaho, 2021; Ogden, Utah, 2020; Southfield, Michigan, 1998). Even gross-pledge contracts often let the trustee "pay operating expenses prior to debt service" (MSRB), and S&P says "ratings assigned do not reflect the payment order".
  - **Judge's note:** the order alone is not what protects bondholders; how much the inflow exceeds the senior claim is. The deposit's senior claim is its first $39,162 of $150,000 (NUMBERS 5), covered about 3.8 times (Keel arithmetic; row 11).
- **Claim C: KEEP, with two changes.** A live prospectus (Bridgecrest Lending Auto Securitization Trust 2025-1, 22 Jan 2025) pays sixteen steps in order: the servicer first, the trustees second, Class A interest third, principal by class, the reserve account thirteenth, "any funds remaining, to the certificateholders" last. Changes: (1) fees come before the senior class (as in B); (2) the senior class is still paid from the pool's later cash, protected by the junior money beneath it ("subordination"), whereas her ten are paid by Treasuries bought before the deposit's size is known. In ABS a principal shortfall is not even a default "until the final scheduled payment date"; her dates are hard.
- **Claim D: KILL as a third institution.** A sinking fund is one account inside the flow of funds ("A fund into which funds are placed to be used to redeem securities in accordance with a redemption schedule", MSRB), filled in its turn like the bond fund (Southfield's monthly set-asides cover principal "by maturity or sinking fund redemption"). Its own history in the store is of pots raided and replaced by serial bonds (Q62 rows 7, 17, 22), the opposite lesson. Fold it into B as the monthly set-aside before the due date.
- **Claim E: CHANGE.** Like: one inflow, a fixed order, a shortfall that lands on the bottom of the order first (NUMBERS 5: at $112,500 the floor money is $56,251 and VT $17,087; at $75,000 VT is $0; the ten keep their standard down to a deposit of $39,162, 73.9% smaller). Unlike, on all three of the brief's tests:
  - *Who sets the order:* an indenture writes "First ... Second ... Third" and a trustee applies it; her IPS writes a principle (l.11: "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares") and l.13 and l.15's uses, and the ranking between l.13 and l.15 is Keel's reading ("The IPS sets no order between its lines", NUMBERS 5; ASK_HUMANS, Fri 13 Nov). Say "the plan spends the deposit in this order", never "the IPS ranks its lines".
  - *Who can change it:* bondholders (Georgetown: 75% of owners, and each affected owner for any change to payment terms; TIA 316(b), Q176-019); for Laura no outside holder exists until the 2031 letter, and the IPS's two exits are events, not votes (l.17).
  - *What happens when short:* a waterfall draws its reserve, carries the deficiency to "the next succeeding monthly requirements" (Southfield), raises rates under its rate covenant, and in the end defaults; Laura has one deposit, no next month, no lever on her income, no default, no remedy, and no rule change (CANON D9). The shortfall lands on money promised to no one yet, because the building's figures are stated in 2031, three years after the deposit is known.
  - **Helps or decorates?** Helps as one clause naming the family plus that one difference; decorates as a list of three institutions. Aha tests already found "the order" not new to fixed-income readers (surprise 2-3, AHA_REGISTER); what is new is the timing.
  - **Judge's note:** a waterfall decides who loses when the money is short; this plan arranges that, while the money could still be short, nothing below the ten has been promised to anyone.
- **Claim F: KILL as worded; keep a narrower clause.** Kimball (1993) is static: "being forced to face an undesirable risk reduces the optimal investment in a risky security with an independent return". It says how much risk while the background risk is faced, never in what order one inflow is spent, so it backs Q206's point (no shares in 2027, while the deposit is unknown), not the ranking. The labour-income models that do put a household's first dollars into safe assets (the buffer stock: Carroll and Toche 2009; Gomes and Michaelides 2005) do so for the household's own spending, which the case puts outside the portfolio; the measured background-risk effects are small (Fagereng, Guiso and Pistaferri 2016, who argue the estimates are biased toward zero). The order comes from the claims side (the flow of funds), the absence of shares in 2027 from the risk side (Kimball). One clause each, never "Kimball says the first dollars go to Treasuries".

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 27, no junior claim exists while the deposit is unknown** (2.3, aha 2 one level deeper). It is the difference that makes the comparison an aha rather than pedigree: a waterfall decides who loses; Laura's calendar makes the loser money promised to no one yet.
2. **Row 10, a waterfall catches up next month; Laura has no next month** (2.3 and 3e). It explains why the payments were mostly bought from the first deposit and why the second deposit's senior slice is only 26.1%.
3. **Row 24, "a distribution algorithm, not a predictive model"** (2.3). The order answers every deposit size in advance and needs no forecast of a writer's income; it pairs with Q206 idea 3 from the claims side.
4. **Rows 2, 3 and 5 together, the promise is not first in most revenue bonds and ratings ignore the order** (wording, 2.3). They stop the report from saying "debt service before anything else", which a municipal-finance judge would catch.
5. **Row 11, coverage about 3.8 times against a city's 1.1 to 1.2** (2.3 or 3e, for a fixed-income judge). The same fact as "about three-quarters smaller" in the market's own unit; Keel arithmetic, to NUMBERS before any use.
