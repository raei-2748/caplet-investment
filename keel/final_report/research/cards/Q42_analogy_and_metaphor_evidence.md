# Q42 research cards: does a conceit carried through a report help its readers, and how should "pencil and ink" be used?

**Claims tested.** Each to keep, kill or change:
1. An extended metaphor carried through a long document helps first readers understand and remember a rule better than
   the plain statement does.
2. Placing each image inside a paragraph (never a part's first or last line) costs little against an opening frame.
3. Analogies mislead when the mapping breaks, so the guards ("true to the craft and true to the plan"; "drop it where
   it needs explaining twice") are warranted.
4. Expert readers (finance professors and practitioners as judges) respond differently from lay readers.

**Problem class and the fields that solved it.** A writing and persuasion problem: does figurative language help a
reader understand, accept and remember a rule, and when does it mislead? Answered by communication science (the
metaphor-persuasion meta-analysis, Sopory and Dillard, 2002; the "crime is a virus / beast" framing experiments and
their failed and successful replications, Thibodeau and Boroditsky, 2011; Steen et al., 2014; Thibodeau, 2016, 2017),
the cognitive psychology of analogy (structure-mapping, Gentner, 1983; Gentner and Markman, 1997; mixed metaphors and
the "career of metaphor", Gentner et al., 2001), science education (analogy-induced misconceptions, Spiro et al.,
1989; the Teaching-With-Analogies model, Glynn, 1994; the FAR guide, Harrison and Treagust, 2006), instructional design
(the expertise reversal effect, Kalyuga et al., 2003; analogy with knowledgeable learners, Donnelly and McDaniel, 2000;
analogies and the feeling of understanding, Wiley et al., 2018) and behavioural finance (metaphors in market
commentary, Morris et al., 2007).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.** Figurative language helps a little and not reliably: across 38 tests (3,945 people) metaphor beat
   plain wording by r = .07, and in about 40% of tests it did worse. Carrying one image through a text adds nothing
   on average, and it does not improve memory of the content (recall 84.6% either way). It helps only when each later
   use extends the same mapping specifically; one use that switches mapping cancels the effect (54.9% with a
   consistent extension, 50.1% with an inconsistent one, against 51.3% with none). The best-supported reason metaphor
   works at all is that it organises what follows, like a title. So the conceit's job is to organise one rule, kept to
   one mapping and few uses; it will not make the rule memorable on its own, and no study tested memory of a financial
   rule. The repeat-back test is the right check.
2. **Claim 2: CHANGE.** At the scale of a whole message, position matters a lot: a metaphor first in a message helped
   most (r = .123), one only in the conclusion hurt (r = -.063), and a one-word "beast" or "virus" frame worked as a
   short report's first sentence but did nothing as its last. "Never the last line" is backed by evidence. "Never a
   part's first line" is untested at that scale (and at paragraph scale the evidence favours an early, even opening,
   sentence); it is a voice rule from round 1 and should be labelled so. What the evidence does require: the definition comes before any number it frames (3.0 before 3a-3e; nothing before 3.0), and inside a
   paragraph the image comes before or with its number, never after it as a summary.
3. **Claim 3: KEEP, and make it stricter.** Every analogy breaks somewhere, and readers keep the analogy's core while
   forgetting the caveats told to them separately ("it is not enough merely to tell people what the limitations of an
   analogy are"). So the exception ("barring a US default", its date) must sit inside each use, as guard 5 already says.
   Two new holes: in everyday English "inked" means signed or committed, not owned, and conventional words are read
   in their everyday sense; and an expert will notice that ink cannot come off while a Treasury can be sold any day.
4. **Claim 4: CHANGE (partly unverified).** Readers who already know a field gain least from an explanatory device, and
   can lose: what helps novices "can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences" for experts; an
   analogy hurt knowledgeable learners when the material touched what they knew; and metaphor barely moves readers who
   already hold a view or trust the source (r = .023). No study found shows that experts find analogies patronising,
   and metaphor did not change how competent a writer seemed (r = -.006). For finance judges, the image may carry only
   the team's own rule and its dates, never explain what a Treasury or a default is.

**The three findings that change the plan most.**
- **One mapping, so fewer images.** Cut the two that start a second mapping: 2.1's "two-thirds reduction" (a size
  ratio, placed before the definition, and section 8 already admits it holds "for the ratio only") and 3f's ranks 10
  and 11 (they point "ink" at the team's WInS notes, which guard 5 forbids: "Ink never means bought, held or in
  WInS"). In 3c, trim, bleed and safe area is a second, spatial mapping; use it only if the blind readers on Mon 16 Nov
  repeat the range rule better with it than with the plain inking dates.
- **The everyday meaning points the wrong way.** Section 5 says "The conceit needs no explaining" because "pencilled in"
  and "inked a deal" are ordinary English. But ordinary readers take "inked" as "signed, committed", and conventional
  metaphors are read from their stored sense, not mapped. "Laura states a figure only once it is inked" then reads as
  "only once she has committed to it", which is circular and drops "owned". The 3.0 definition must say "owned in
  Treasuries that will pay it", once, as an explicit comparison, and the repeat-back test must score "owned", not
  "promised".
- **The judges are experts, and the effect is small.** The image may not explain finance, every image sentence must
  still say its number and rule with the image word struck out, and the 16 Nov test should score whether readers
  restate and apply the rule, not which version they prefer or found clearer (analogies make readers worse judges of
  their own understanding).

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. Small, often negative, and best as a title (Sopory & Dillard, 2002).**
- What is it? The standard meta-analysis of metaphor against literal wording in persuasion: 29 studies, 38 effect sizes,
  3,945 people, attitudes measured after a message. Seven moderators tested; six theories weighed.
- Did it work? Metaphor won on average, r = .07, about a 6-point swing in a binomial display, and lost in almost 40% of
  tests. It worked best early, single, novel and about a familiar topic (r = .42, but on three studies). Nine or more
  metaphors in a message came to almost nothing (.017). The theory best supported is superior organisation: the image
  works as a title that organises what follows.
- How is the report's case like it? The conceit is meant to make one rule easy to hold (Laura states a figure only
  once it is owned in Treasuries that will pay it) across 6-8 pages; the judges score Articulation and Creativity as
  well as the investing.
- How is it not? The studies measure attitude change in short messages, mostly with students; none measures a judge's
  score or memory of a financial rule; the "optimal" case rests on 197 people.
- What Caplet takes: the conceit's honest case is organisation and voice, not proven persuasion. Few images (at most
  five or six), one definition early, and none where a part has no natural fit.

**Card 2. Early, or not at all (Thibodeau & Boroditsky, 2011; Thibodeau, 2017).**
- What is it? Readers got a crime report that called crime a "beast" or a "virus" and chose policies. In one
  experiment the metaphor was moved to the last sentence. A later reanalysis of a larger replication tested extension
  and recall.
- Did it work? Early metaphors shifted choices by 17-18 points (74% against 56%); in the last sentence, no effect
  (p = .59). Readers rarely noticed the metaphor (3%) and credited the statistics. In the reanalysis, consistent
  extensions raised support slightly (r = .105) and did not improve recall (84.6% either way); a frame "should be
  presented early in a stream of processing" because frames do not reshape views already formed.
- How is the report's case like it? 3.0 sets the rule, then 3a-3e give figures it is meant to organise.
- How is it not? These are one-paragraph texts; a report is pages long and its "start" is a section, not a sentence.
  Experiments 4 and 5 compared a short report's first sentence with its last; nothing compares a part's first line
  with its third.
- What Caplet takes: the definition goes in 3.0, before any figure it frames, and nothing in the comics domain comes
  before it (so not 2.1). Inside a part, the image comes before or with its number, and may open its paragraph.
  "Never the last line" is evidence; "never a part's first line" is a voice rule (round 1), not evidence.

**Card 3. One mapping, extended the same way (Thibodeau, 2016; Gentner et al., 2001).**
- What is it? Two tests of what happens when a metaphor is carried on. Thibodeau gave 1,000 readers ten issues with a
  framing metaphor and a closing sentence that extended it, switched to a different metaphor, or said nothing more.
  Gentner and Boronat timed the reading of a passage's last sentence when it continued or switched the passage's
  metaphor.
- Did it work? Consistent extensions raised frame-consistent choices (54.9%, and 58.9% for specific ones, against
  51.3% with none); an inconsistent extension wiped the effect out (50.1%). Switching metaphors slowed reading, but only
  for novel metaphors; conventional ones (stock phrases) were read word by word with no switching cost.
- How is the report's case like it? "Pencil and ink" applied to Treasuries is novel, so readers build a mapping, and
  each image sentence is an extension of it. The plan's 5b parallels are not all the same mapping: ranks 1, 2, 4, 5 and
  12 extend "owned or not yet owned"; rank 3 (trim, bleed, safe area) maps the range onto a page's layout; the two-thirds
  reduction maps a size ratio; ranks 10 and 11 map ink onto the team's own records.
- How is it not? Thibodeau's inconsistent extensions came from an unrelated source domain (a virus passage ending with
  a beast); trim and bleed are from the same world of printed comics, so the cost may be smaller. Untested.
- What Caplet takes: every image sentence answers one question, "owned in Treasuries yet, or not?" Drop 2.1's image and
  3f's; in 3c prefer the inking dates (bottom January 2028, top before the meetings) and keep trim, bleed and safe area
  only if it wins the blind test. Conventional finance metaphors (ladder, floor) do not clash and need no policing.

**Card 4. Ordinary English reads "inked" as "signed" (Bowdle & Gentner, 2005; Gentner et al., 2001; Spiro et al.,
1989).**
- What is it? The "career of metaphor": a novel figure is understood by comparing two things; a conventional one by
  pulling a stored meaning off the shelf. Spiro's list of analogy-induced misconceptions includes the everyday meaning
  of a word overriding the technical one.
- Did it work? Novel figures were read faster and preferred as similes; conventional ones as metaphors; after two
  exposures readers preferred the bare metaphor. Highly conventional metaphors showed no mapping at all in reading
  times.
- How is the report's case like it? Section 5 records that "pencilled in" and "inked a deal" are ordinary English and
  concludes "The conceit needs no explaining". The stored sense of "inked" is "signed a deal": a commitment. The plan's
  sense is a figure already owned in Treasuries that will pay it. A reader who uses the stored sense hears "Laura
  states a figure only once she has committed to it", which is circular and loses the whole point (owned, not
  promised).
- How is it not? "Pencilled in" (tentative) does match the plan's pencil. Only "ink" points the wrong way, and only
  slightly (signing is also a kind of finishing).
- What Caplet takes: 3.0 states the comparison once in full, as a comparison ("as in a comic, where only the inked
  line prints, ..."), and says "owned in Treasuries that will pay it"; later uses can be the bare verb with its date.
  The repeat-back test scores "owned" and "barring a US default"; "committed" or "promised" scores as a miss.

**Card 5. Caveats fade, the core stays (Spiro et al., 1989; Glynn, 1994; Harrison & Treagust, 2006).**
- What is it? Three accounts from teaching science with analogies: a catalogue of how analogies mislead advanced
  medical students; a six-step method drawn from 43 textbooks and 10 teachers; a teaching guide (FAR) that asks for
  each analogy's LIKES and UNLIKES.
- Did it work? Spiro found caveats do not stick ("The analogical core is what is retained") and recommends warnings
  built into the use and integrated analogies that patch each other. Harrison and Treagust report that stating each
  analogy's conditions "reduced the incidence of alternative conceptions". Glynn's step 5 ("indicate where the
  analogy breaks down") came from practice; his method's outcome claims (Glynn, 2008) were not read at source.
- How is the report's case like it? Guard 5 makes "barring a US default" and the date travel with every "inked";
  section 8 lists where the image is not honest; guard 11 drops an image that needs explaining twice.
- How is it not? The studies teach new science to students who must later use it; judges read once and judge.
- What Caplet takes: keep guards 5 and 11 and section 8; put each limit in the sentence that uses the image, never in a
  separate caveat paragraph; the patch images ("painted over, the correction stays"; "one sealed envelope per payment")
  are the right kind of second analogy because they correct the first rather than start a new one.

**Card 6. An expert notices the difference that matters (Gentner, 1983; Gentner & Markman, 1997).**
- What is it? Structure-mapping: an analogy maps relations, and the more closely two things align, the more readers
  notice the differences that sit inside the shared structure. Inferences carried across are "only guesses".
- Did it work? It is the standard account of analogy, with studies showing people list more than twice as many
  aligned differences for similar pairs (9.0 against 3.9) and rate as most apt the analogies they read most
  relationally.
- How is the report's case like it? The conceit's core relation is permanence: ink cannot be taken off cleanly. The
  aligned difference a finance judge will see at once: a Treasury can be sold tomorrow. The plan's permanence comes
  from holding each Treasury to its payment date (buy-and-hold), not from the bond.
- How is it not? A judge may also simply read the image as a rule about statements, not holdings, and see no conflict.
- What Caplet takes: section 8 gains one limit ("ink stays because the plan holds every Treasury to its payment date;
  a Treasury could be sold, and the plan does not sell it"), said where the definition is given. Every candidate
  inference a reader might draw (ink fades, ink is guaranteed, ink is in WInS) is already closed by guards 5 and 8;
  this one is not.

**Card 7. Metaphors in market talk move expectations; numbers damp them (Morris et al., 2007).**
- What is it? Experiments and archive studies of end-of-day market commentary: "agent" metaphors ("the Nasdaq
  climbed") against "object" metaphors ("was pushed higher") and plain description.
- Did it work? Agent metaphors raised readers' expectations that the trend would continue, by changing what readers
  thought the commentator believed; the effect appeared when prices were shown as a graph and vanished when they were
  shown as a table of numbers.
- How is the report's case like it? It is the nearest test of metaphor on a financial reader, and it shows readers take
  a metaphor as the writer's conviction.
- How is it not? Student readers, a one-day forecast, a stock index; the conceit describes holdings, not price moves.
- What Caplet takes: guards 1 and 2 are backed (the number in the sentence; never in tables); "inked" will be read as
  the team's confidence, so it carries only what NUMBERS supports; no agent verbs for markets in an image sentence
  ("shares will climb", "rates fight back").

**Card 8. Expert readers: the device is redundant, and can cost (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Donnelly & McDaniel, 2000;
Steen et al., 2014; Ottati et al., 1999).**
- What is it? Four lines of evidence on readers who already know the subject or care about the source domain.
- Did it work? Help built for novices can hurt experts (expertise reversal, many replications); analogy hurt
  knowledgeable learners when the material engaged what they knew (abstract only); metaphor matters only when needed to
  understand the topic (Steen's termination hypothesis); a sports metaphor sharpened scrutiny in sports fans and dulled
  it in others (abstract only).
- How is the report's case like it? Wharton's judges include finance professors and practitioners who know Treasuries,
  default risk and buy-and-hold better than any image can explain them.
- How is it not? None of these studies used expert judges scoring a written entry; the conceit's job is to state the
  team's own rule, which no judge knows in advance. That is new information even to an expert.
- What Caplet takes: the image may name the team's rule and its dates; it never explains a finance concept (what a
  Treasury pays, what a default is, why rates move). Every image sentence must pass a deletion test: strike the image
  word and the sentence still states the number and the rule (guard 1's "inside a sentence that already says what it
  means", made checkable). "Patronising" stays unverified; no study was found.

**Card 9. Readers cannot tell whether an analogy helped them (Wiley et al., 2018).**
- What is it? Students read science texts with or without analogies, judged how well they understood each, and took
  tests.
- Did it work? Analogies sometimes raised test scores, but readers who judged by surface cues became worse at telling
  which texts they understood.
- How is the report's case like it? Section 10 plans a blind read on Mon 16 Nov in which readers "say which they
  understood better". That is exactly the judgment analogies distort.
- How is it not? The readers were students learning science, not readers judging a plan; the effect is on
  self-assessment, not on understanding.
- What Caplet takes: score understanding, not preference. Ask each reader, closed book, to restate the rule and answer
  one applied question (for example: in 2031 the Treasuries she owns pay $110,000 by 2033; can Laura tell co-sponsors
  $120,000?). Count correct answers with and without the conceit.

## (d) Verdict

- **Keep:** one conceit, two carrying words, one definition in 3.0, the exception inside each use, no images in tables
  or in Laura's words, never the last line, the limits stated where the image is used, Johnson's test.
- **Kill:** 2.1's two-thirds image; any image in 3f; "The conceit needs no explaining" (section 5).
- **Change to:** at most five image sentences, all extending "owned in Treasuries or not yet"; 3.0's definition as one
  explicit comparison saying "owned"; the image before or with its number; trim, bleed and safe area only if it wins the
  blind test; one more limit in section 8 (ink holds because the plan holds each Treasury to its payment date); the
  deletion test in guard 1; scored understanding, not preference, on Mon 16 Nov.
- **For the lead to decide:** whether "never the first line" stays as a voice rule (no evidence for or against at this
  scale), and whether 3c keeps trim, bleed and safe area before the blind result is in.

## (e) Gaps

- No study found of expert finance readers, competition judges or written investment reports; claim 4 rests on
  learners and on attitude studies. "Experts find analogies patronising": no evidence found either way (UNVERIFIED).
- Almost every persuasion study measures attitude after a short message, not understanding or memory of a rule; the
  repeat-back test is the only direct evidence the team will have.
- No evidence at sentence or paragraph scale on first against second sentence.
- Read as abstracts only: Donnelly & McDaniel (2000), McDaniel & Donnelly (1996), Ottati et al. (1999), Christmann &
  Göhring (2016); their numbers are not in this card. Glynn's (2008) claim that his method raised learning (Glynn,
  Duit & Thiele, 1995; Paris & Glynn, 2004) is UNVERIFIED.
- Reijnierse et al. (2015) not read; its data are cited only through Thibodeau's (2017) reanalysis.
- Whether trim and bleed (same craft, different mapping) cost as much as a switch to an unrelated domain is untested.
