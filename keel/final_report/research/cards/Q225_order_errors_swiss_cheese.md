- **Report parts it serves:** 2.5 (how the portfolio was bought: the one-trader rule, one clause), 3f (the Team Reflection: how it is told, no outside parallel in its prose, D16), References (Reason 2000; SEC Rule 15c3-5 if cited), Table 3 (none).

- **Not repeated:** Citibank and Revlon (RANKING_4 Moment 3, store); store H4-002, H4-056 and H5-033 (Swiss cheese as common-cause counting for the plan's layers: this card uses it only for the buying night); Q88-021 (Rule 15c3-5 read as the plan's stop rules: row 1 below adds the price-parameter link to the 25-basis-point rule and the access clause); H9-183 (a "four-eyes check" as a "now what": row 27 tests it).
- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. (a) "A single owner for any action that cannot be undone, and a check before every order, is how markets themselves answer entry errors."
  2. (b) "The team's mis-click was a slip, not a mistake; its lesson is a procedure, not a change of strategy."
  3. (c) "Its cost was small because a layer was already in place."
- **Problem class:** an irreversible action entered through an interface that lets a wrong keystroke or click through; where to put the checks (before entry, at the gate, after entry) and who may act. **Field that solved it first:** safety science (aviation, nuclear power, medicine: Reason) and securities market regulation (SEC, TSE, Korea's FSS and FSC); banks' internal-control standards (Basel Committee) for the four-eyes principle.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim (a): CHANGE.** Markets answer entry errors with checks in four places, and none of the texts read says "one owner for what cannot be undone".
  - **Before the order leaves:** automatic rejection of orders outside "appropriate price or size parameters" (SEC Rule 15c3-5(c)(1)(ii), in force 2011; the SEC's own example is a manual slip, "2,000 shares at $2.00" entered as "2 shares at $2,000.00").
  - **At the gate:** access "restricted" to "persons and accounts pre-approved and authorized" (15c3-5(c)(2)(iii)). This is the market form of the team's one-trader rule, but it limits who may send an order; it is not a check on the order.
  - **A second person:** Knight's SEC order names the missing second technician; the Basel Committee names the "four-eyes principle" as two people. The team's rule is one person, so the team must not borrow the bank phrase (row 27).
  - **After the order:** a working cancel path and an emergency stop. Mizuho's cancel failed on the exchange's side; Korea's regulator then required an "emergency button" and caps near 5% of shares outstanding.
  - **What the four failures had in common with a wrong click:** an error went through because no layer stood between the person's hand and an action that was hard to take back. **What they did not:** each was huge and out of range (610,000 shares for one; 2.8 billion shares against about 89 million issued; 397 million shares in 45 minutes), so a size or price limit would have stopped it. The team's cancel was a normal-sized, plausible order action on a real order, so only a confirmation, an undo or a restriction on hands could have changed it.
  - **Say instead:** "markets check an order before it leaves and limit who may send it; we did both in small form: a price check before every bond order and one person at the keyboard." Never "how markets answer entry errors" as if one rule did it.
  - **Judge's note:** the standard repair after a fat-finger loss was a limit on size and price, set before the order, and the team's 25-basis-point rule is that kind of check (Keel reading), made on 29-30 September, a week before the click.
- **Claim (b): CHANGE.** The shape fits a slip, with two cautions.
  - **Reason's terms (1990, chapter 3; 2000):** slips and lapses are failures of execution where the action deviates from the intention; mistakes are failures of the plan. A click that lands on the wrong row while the intention was right is the first kind (Keel classification of the record).
  - **Caution 1:** "slip" is a label for the event's shape (intention right, action wrong), never a claim about who or what made the click. The report keeps Caplet's own sentence, "a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled", and leaves the safety-science word in the notes.
  - **Caution 2:** the missing layer is better named undo than confirmation. Raskin (2007) argues that people click through "Are you sure?" boxes by habit and that "the solution to our warning woes is undo". WInS offered neither.
  - **Lesson:** a procedure, yes (the HSE says slips happen to "the most experienced, well-trained and highly-motivated people" and answers with design and a chance to "detect and correct"). Nothing in it touches the strategy. The report states it that way: one clause, no blame.
  - **Judge's note:** Reason's line, "the important issue is not who blundered, but how and why the defences failed", is also why 3f can name the rule and leave the click nameless.
- **Claim (c): KILL, with an honest replacement.** No layer was "already in place" for this error; the record names the absence ("no confirmation step"), and the rule came afterwards, the same day.
  - **What kept the cost small, on the record:** the cancel fell between 11:13 and 15:22 AEDT on Wed 7 Oct (an inference from the EDT time stamps), and the order was re-placed at 15:22, about nine hours before New York's open at 00:30 AEDT on Thu 8 Oct (Keel arithmetic). An unfilled order can be placed again; a filled one cannot.
  - **Honest version:** "the click came hours before the open, when a cancelled order can be placed again; once the market opens, nothing can be re-placed, so from that day only the team leader touched an order." Whether anything was lost is not recorded; every order filled by 8 Oct.
  - **Correct a loose phrase:** RANKING_4 says the rule gave "any action that cannot be undone" one owner. This cancel could be undone. Say "any action on an order".
  - **Judge's note:** the luck was the clock, not a safeguard, and saying so is the more credible telling.
