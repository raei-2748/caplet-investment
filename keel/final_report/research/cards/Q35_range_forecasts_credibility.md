**The plan being tested** (NUMBERS 4 and 8l, MODEL). In 2031, before the meetings, Laura states the range the rule
gives that day: each end is what Treasury funds she already owns will pay if no more interest is earned, less 0.5%,
rounded down to $5,000. Centrally about $95,000 to $115,000 on today's market rates. The bottom is the floor, fixed
in January 2028; the top adds the shares moved into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. The facility contribution
is the lower of what the funds pay and the stated top; anything above the top joins her flexibility. The contribution
equals the stated top in every simulated future and every starting month tested (8l (iii)); it falls inside the range
barring a US default, and reaches the top unless the funds' running costs pass the 0.5% cut (at 0.75%, short by at
most $429). Two funders who read the plan (SKELETON 3d) each said they would count the bottom ($95,000).

**Claims tested.**
1. "Readers judge a range forecast by whether outcomes land inside it, and they penalise a miss below the range more
   than they reward an outcome at or above the top; so a range whose bottom is owned is the most credible kind."
   Keep, kill or change (Hirst, Koonce and Miller 1999; Libby, Tan and Hunton 2006; Han and Tan 2010; Bartov, Givoly
   and Hayn 2002).
2. "If outcomes always land at the top of a stated range, readers learn to treat the top as the forecast, so
   co-sponsors will in time count the top, not the bottom." Keep, kill or change (Ciconte, Kirk and Tucker 2014;
   Matsumoto 2002; studies of how readers learn a forecaster's bias). What it means for a speaker who states the
   range once; whether the report should say plainly that she intends the top, as SKELETON 3d already does.
3. "A narrow range signals confidence and a wide one signals honesty; the right width is the one the forecaster can
   defend." Keep, kill or change (Cheng, Luo and Yue 2013; Yaniv and Foster 1995, with its retraction status). Laura's
   width is $20,000 and is set by the rule, not chosen: is that worth saying?

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** Stating a one-time bounded figure to readers who will check it once,
when the speaker has a reason to overstate. Two fields have worked on it. Accounting research on management earnings
guidance studies the form, width and track record of range forecasts, in archival and lab studies. Judgment and
decision-making research studies interval estimates and advice: accuracy against informativeness, and confidence
against calibration. The disanalogy runs through every row: guidance is repeated every quarter, priced by a market,
and about a figure the firm does not yet have; Laura states her range once, to a few funders, about money she owns.

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1: CHANGE.** The first half holds: inside the range is how a range is judged (Teigen et al., Q19 r25; Han
   and Tan 2007: a result low in a range is read more kindly than the same result against one figure). The second
   half does not hold for markets: per unit, the reward for a beat exceeds the penalty for a miss (Bartov et al. H3,
   Q25 r7), and repeated beats earn a larger premium, not a smaller one (row 7). The asymmetry belongs to promises
   (Gneezy and Epley, Q25 rows 1-4), and for Laura nothing can land above the top anyway. "Most credible" is earned
   by two things the plan has, not by an owned bottom alone: the bottom can be checked (Rogers and Stocken: readers
   discount optimism they cannot check; Hutton et al., Q18 r25), and the outcome will match the words once, in
   December 2032, when calibration beats confidence (Sah et al.: confident advisers lost 1.77 points of
   credibility when shown wrong, unconfident ones 0.83; Tenney et al., Q18 r24). **Libby, Tan and Hunton
   (2006) is RETRACTED (2015): KILL as a source.** Its co-authored companion, Tan, Libby and Hunton (2002), is
   retracted too.
2. **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Readers do treat the top as the forecast, but by convention, not by learning one speaker's
   record. Across 30,106 company range forecasts (1996-2010), results landed at or above the top 57.6% of the time,
   and investors priced the top, not the midpoint (Ciconte et al.). Professionals barely learn: analysts undid 9.1% of
   that bias (Ciconte et al.), and their shift after a year that ended near the top was weak (t = 1.73; Tang et al.).
   Analysts put 73% to 77% of the weight on the bottom, more when ranges are wider (Tang et al., 10,989 annual
   forecasts), which is what both funders did. Laura states once, so co-sponsors cannot learn her record: they bring
   one of two conventions. **Yes, say plainly she intends the top** (SKELETON 3d already does), beside the bottom as
   the figure to count. It is true by construction after the move (NUMBERS 8l (iii)), so a co-sponsor who counts the
   top is right barring the two named exceptions; and a kept stated intention beats a met expectation (Gneezy and
   Epley, Q25 r3). Lands: 3d, existing clause; no new words.
3. **Claim 3: CHANGE.** Narrow does signal confidence (Jerez-Fernandez et al.: d = 0.34), but only pays when the record
   is accurate (Hirst et al.) or the setting is certain (Rupar: point beats range on credibility 4.98 to 4.43 when
   uncertainty is low; range beats point 4.40 to 4.08 when it is high). Wide does not signal honesty: 80% preferred a
   narrow range that missed to a wide one that held (Yaniv and Foster, full paper; no retraction or correction), and
   wide ranges are read as less certain (Løhre et al., Q19 r26). Width is suspected of being chosen to suit the
   speaker when readers cannot see its basis (Cheng et al.). Laura's width is about 0.19 of its midpoint, against a
   corporate median of 0.053 (Keel arithmetic on Cheng et al.), which pushes professionals further to the bottom (Tang
   et al.). **What is worth saying is what the width is, not that it was not chosen:** the bottom is the floor, the
   top adds the shares moved into Treasuries (CANON D1), so after the move the width is owned money, not a margin of
   error. Lands: the rule's one sentence in 3c (each end named by what pays it) and Table 2's note; no separate
   "not chosen" clause.

## (b) Cards

**B1. Professionals count the bottom (rows 18-21).**
- *What is it?* Tang, Zarowin and Zhang regressed analysts' revisions on both ends of 10,989 annual company ranges
  (1996-2011). Analysts put 73% to 77% of the weight on the bottom. The lean grew with doubt: earlier in the year,
  wider ranges, more disagreement among analysts, more volatile shares, and a range wider than the year before.
- *Did it work?* Yes, for accuracy: revisions made with perfect foresight of the actual result also leaned on the
  bottom. Learning from a company's past ranges was weak (t = 1.73).
- *How is Laura's case like it?* Both funders who read the plan picked the bottom, $95,000, for the board paper
  (SKELETON 3d). Her range is wide by company standards (row 29), the condition under which the lean is strongest.
- *How is it not?* Analysts revise an earnings estimate many times a year; a co-sponsor commits money once. Laura's
  contribution does not fall short of the top, so for her the bottom is a floor, not a likely outcome.
- *What Caplet takes.* Evidence for an existing note: 3d's "the one figure to count (the bottom, $95,000, owned since
  January 2028)". No new words. Store only; if 3c argues why co-sponsors count the bottom, one APA cite fits there
  (3d is in Laura's voice and carries none).

**B2. Investors read the top, by habit; Laura gives no record to learn from (rows 13-16, 21, 24, 25).**
- *What is it?* Across 30,106 company ranges, results landed at or above the top 57.6% of the time (61.6% after 2001),
  and the price response fits investors reading the top, not the midpoint. Analysts undid only 9.1% of that bias.
  Bartov et al. found no discount for habitual beaters (row 7).
- *Did it work?* As a habit, yes: it is the norm investors priced. As learning, barely: professionals did not adjust.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* The contribution equals the stated top in every simulated future and every starting
  month tested (NUMBERS 8l (iii)). A co-sponsor who reads the top as her real figure is right, barring a US default
  and the funds' running costs passing the 0.5% cut (then short by at most $429 at 0.75%).
- *How is it not?* Laura states once, so claim 2's "in time" never comes; co-sponsors bring a habit from company
  guidance or from grant practice. Her top is the most she gives, never a bar to beat.
- *What Caplet takes.* 3d keeps both clauses already planned: the bottom as the figure to count, and the top as what
  she intends. That tells each reader what each end means, so neither habit has to be guessed. Before the move nothing
  is stated (NUMBERS 4; row 16). No new number in prose.

**B3. A named figure inside a range; matching the form to what is still uncertain (rows 4, 5, 27).**
- *What is it?* Han and Tan (2007): knowledgeable investors judge a range against its ends and its implied midpoint;
  a stated best figure inside a range becomes a further benchmark. Rupar: one figure is more credible when nothing is
  uncertain, a range when much is.
- *Did it work?* In the lab, yes: both effects were significant (Rupar's interaction p = 0.018). The published Rupar
  (2017) was not read.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* After the move nothing about either end is uncertain, so a bare range is the
  mismatched form. Naming the end she intends turns it into a range with a best figure, the form van der Bles et al.
  recommend (Q18 r20).
- *How is it not?* The case requires a range and a confidence (case l.115-118), and IPS l.15 speaks of a band she
  chooses within; one figure is not open to Caplet. Lab participants judged earnings, not pledges.
- *What Caplet takes.* 3c's existing "why a range" clause carries it: the case asks for a range, and the two ends say
  different things. Store one risk: once the top is named as intended, a shortfall under it (at most $429 at fund
  costs of 0.75%, NUMBERS 8l) reads as a miss of the named figure even inside the range.

**B4. Width: what it is beats why it is that size (rows 26-32).**
- *What is it?* Yaniv and Foster: 80% preferred a narrow range that missed to a wide one that held; people weigh
  accuracy against informativeness. Cheng et al.: managers pick precision to suit themselves where readers cannot see
  the basis. Jerez-Fernandez et al.: precise numbers read as confident.
- *Did it work?* The preference for narrow ranges replicates across studies (Du et al., Q11a r33; Løhre et al., Q19
  r26). Cheng et al. is archival evidence over 10,799 forecasts.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* Her central width is about 0.19 of the midpoint, against a company median of 0.053
  (Keel arithmetic). Judged as a forecast, it is wide and uninformative.
- *How is it not?* After the move it is not a forecast. The bottom is what the floor pays; the top adds what the
  shares moved into Treasuries pay (CANON D1). The width is owned money.
- *What Caplet takes.* 3c's one sentence for the rule names each end by what pays it, and Table 2's note can show the
  two sources. No separate "set by the rule, not chosen" clause: the rule already shows it, and a stand-alone line
  would be a slogan flip. The width's figures ($20,000; $10,000 to $35,000) stay in Table 2.

**B5. What a reader can check is what a reader believes (rows 9-11, with Hutton et al., Q18 r25).**
- *What is it?* Rogers and Stocken: the market discounted good-news forecasts by the optimism it could predict, and
  managers biased more where bias was hard to detect. Mercer: credibility comes from incentives, record, assurance and
  the statement's features.
- *Did it work?* For good news, the market's discount was complete at once; bad news was taken at face value (the
  2002 working paper; the 2005 version says efficiency "varies with the forecast news").
- *How is Laura's case like it?* She wants co-sponsors to join, so any forecast of hers would be discounted. The
  bottom is not a forecast: it is a Treasury fund she owns from January 2028, and a custodian statement shows it.
- *How is it not?* Firms forecast earnings they do not control; Laura states holdings she owns. Mercer was read at
  abstract level only.
- *What Caplet takes.* 3d's checklist already has "proof on request (a custodian statement)". No new words; the
  evidence goes to the store.

**B6. Two retracted guidance studies, and where claim 1's asymmetry really lives (rows 1, 2, 7, 8).**
- *What is it?* Libby, Tan and Hunton (2006) and Tan, Libby and Hunton (2002) are retracted (2015, Retraction Watch
  records 16323 and 5444). Bartov et al.: per unit, the market rewards a beat more than it penalises a miss, and does
  not discount habitual beaters.
- *Did it work?* The retracted studies cannot be used. Bartov et al.'s premium held over 64,872 firm-quarters.
- *How is Laura's case like it?* Not at all on the upside: no result can land above her top. On the downside the
  promise logic of Gneezy and Epley (Q25 rows 1-4) applies: the bottom must not be missed.
- *How is it not?* Markets reprice every quarter; co-sponsors judge one founder once.
- *What Caplet takes.* Store only. REFERENCES_APA gets neither retracted paper; any note citing Han (2013) for
  "a wide range softens a miss" cites Han and Tan (2007) instead.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1: CHANGE.**
- Kept: readers judge a range by whether the result lands inside it. Teigen et al.'s boundary effect (Q19 r25) and Han
  and Tan (2007, row 4) agree that inside, even low inside, is read as met.
- Changed: "they penalise a miss below more than they reward a result at or above the top" is wrong for markets.
  Per unit, beats earn more than misses cost (Bartov et al. H3, Q25 r7), and habitual beaters earn more, not less
  (row 7). What costs is the fact of a miss (Skinner and Sloan, Q25 r9). The strong asymmetry is in promises: Gneezy
  and Epley found exceeding a promise earned no credit while breaking it cost a great deal (Q25 rows 1-4). For Laura
  the upside case cannot occur, since the contribution is never more than the stated top. So the bottom is treated as
  a promise, and the top as a stated intention kept.
- Changed: "an owned bottom makes it the most credible kind" overreaches on one cause. The evidence gives two:
  - Checkability. Readers discount optimism they can predict and cannot check (Rogers and Stocken, rows 9-10). Hutton
    et al. found good news believed only with verifiable support (Q18 r25). A custodian statement is the check.
  - Calibration at the one moment of feedback. Without feedback confidence wins; with it, confident-and-wrong loses
    most (Sah et al. row 12: 4.00 to 2.23 against 3.67 to 2.84; Tenney et al., Q18 r24). Co-sponsors get one piece of
    feedback, in December 2032, and the contribution matches the top then.
- Killed as a source: Libby, Tan and Hunton (2006), retracted; the brief also had its journal wrong.
- Density: store only. 3c already carries the confidence in one sentence, and 3d the custodian statement.

**Claim 2: CHANGE.**
- What holds: readers do take the top as the forecast. Investors priced company ranges at their top, and 57.6% of
  results landed at or above it (Ciconte et al., row 13).
- What fails: "readers learn ... in time". It is a habit across companies, not a lesson from one speaker. Analysts
  undid 9.1% of the bias (row 14). Learning from a firm's own past ranges was weak (row 21). Repeated beats were not
  discounted (row 7). Laura speaks once. Matsumoto (row 22, abstract only) shows how firms walk expectations down
  over repeats; with one statement there is nothing to walk down.
- What co-sponsors will actually do: bring a habit. Investors' habit is the top (row 13). Analysts' is mostly the
  bottom, 73% to 77% of the weight, more for wide ranges, and that was the accurate weighting (rows 18-20). Both
  funders who read the plan chose the bottom.
- For Laura both habits are right after the move: the bottom is owned from January 2028, the top from the move, and
  the contribution equals the top in every simulated future and starting month tested (NUMBERS 8l (iii)).
- The lead's question, answered: yes, the report says plainly that she intends the top (3d, as SKELETON already plans;
  3b already names about $115,000 as the recommended contribution), beside the bottom as the figure to count. Saying
  both:
  - removes the guess each habit makes;
  - makes the top a kept stated intention, which beats a met expectation (Gneezy and Epley Experiment 4, Q25 r3);
  - gives knowledgeable readers a named figure, so they do not fall back on the midpoint ($105,000; row 5).
- One risk to store: the named top becomes a benchmark. A shortfall under it (at most $429 at fund costs of 0.75%;
  NUMBERS 8l) reads as a miss of the named figure even inside the range (row 5).
- Density: 3d's existing clause; no new number in prose.

**Claim 3: CHANGE.**
- "Narrow signals confidence": KEEP in part. Precision is read as confidence (Jerez-Fernandez et al., d = 0.34). But
  it raised investors' confidence only with an accurate record (Hirst et al., row 3) or when uncertainty was low
  (Rupar, row 27).
- "Wide signals honesty": KILL. Wide reads as uninformative (Yaniv and Foster, 80% preferred the narrow miss; Du et al.,
  Q11a r33) and as less certain (Løhre et al., Q19 r26). It also pushes professionals toward the bottom (Tang et al.,
  row 19). Width chosen by the speaker invites suspicion where readers cannot see its basis (Cheng et al., row 28).
- "The right width is the one the forecaster can defend": CHANGE to "the right width is one the reader can see the
  source of". Laura's width is about 0.19 of its midpoint, about 3.6 times the company median (Keel arithmetic, row
  29); defended as a forecast it would lose. It is not a forecast after the move: the bottom is the floor's payout,
  the top adds the payout of the shares moved into Treasuries (CANON D1), both owned.
- The lead's question, answered: "set by the rule, not chosen" is a real advantage (it shuts Cheng et al.'s
  strategic-width reading), but it is shown by the rule's one sentence, not said as a separate line (row 32). What is
  worth saying is what the width is (row 31): each end named by what pays it, in 3c's rule sentence and Table 2's note.
- Retraction status: Yaniv and Foster (1995) has no update notice in Crossref; REFERENCES_APA row 249 already says
  CONFIRMED (no notice).
