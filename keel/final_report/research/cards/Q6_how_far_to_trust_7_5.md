# Q6 research cards: how far can Caplet trust 7.5% for world shares?

Research run, Mon 9 Nov 2026 (Sydney). Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose. Checked against Caplet's
FINAL IPS (`keel/final_report/FINAL_IPS_as_submitted.txt`) and the locked TNA
(`keel/drafts/writing_room_2026-11-03/tna_as_submitted.txt`). The plan is frozen: a "yes" in the Clash column is a
risk to note in the Final Report, never a change to propose.

**Claim tested** (class: forecasting and asset pricing): "7.5% is a reasonable central figure for world shares over
2028-2033, but no one can forecast six-year share returns well, so the plan is right to make the promise independent
of it."

**Verdict: KEEP, with three changes.**
1. "Reasonable central figure" holds only as "J.P. Morgan's figure, at the top of the published range". Eight houses
   with a dated world-share figure run from about 5.8% to 7.5% (median about 6.5%). Only J.P. Morgan and Aon say
   7.5%, and GMO is about 10 points lower. Against history 7.5% is not heroic: developed markets returned 8.5% a year
   in USD from 1900 to 2025, about 5.4% after inflation, while J.P. Morgan's 7.5% is about 5.0% after its own 2.40%
   inflation (rows 2-11).
2. "2028-2033 / six years" is the wrong window. VT is bought in January 2028. The half that sets the top is held about
   three years, to the move before the 2031 meetings; the kept half is held about five years, to 2033. J.P. Morgan's
   7.5% is a 10-15-year average that J.P. Morgan says is not for short-term decisions (rows 1, 29).
3. "No one can forecast well" is supported, but it needs a number:
   - ten-year house forecasts miss by about 4 points a year on average, and in 14 of 15 asset classes the outcome
     fell outside the whole range of forecasts;
   - at three years, starting valuations explain about a tenth of US outcomes, even in flattering in-sample tests;
   - forecasters rank assets far better than they size any one return (rows 13-24).

"Independent of it" is strongly supported, with one precision. The ten payments and the bottom never rest on the
forecast, and the 2031 range is money owned once half of VT moves. What shares decide is the size of the top before the
move, and that is decided by realised returns, not by the forecast. Across 4% to 10%, the choice of forecast moves the
top by $3,784; luck at a fixed 7.5% moves the moved half by about $26,000 (rows 27-28).

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. The houses disagree, and 7.5% is the top of the range
- **What is it?** Eight large houses publish a dated, roughly comparable world-share figure:
  - J.P. Morgan and Aon at 7.5%;
  - Northern Trust 7.1%;
  - AQR about 6.6% (converted);
  - Invesco 6.4%;
  - Janus Henderson 6.2%;
  - Capital Group 6.0% (20 years);
  - Vanguard about 5.8%.

  The median is about 6.5%. In one Florida pension document, three consultants' figures from the same weeks read
  7.50%, 5.70% and 5.25%. GMO, the house whose 7-year horizon is closest to Laura's, implies about -2.7% a year in
  nominal terms. Horizon's 43-adviser survey puts 7.5% near the 75th percentile for the parts of a world fund (rows 3-11).
- **Did it work?** As a description, yes: it is all dated primary material. Dahlquist and Ibert explain the spread.
  Institutions differ more from each other than over time, because some believe price-earnings ratios return to
  normal and others treat them as a random walk (row 12).
- **How is Laura like it?** Her co-sponsors' advisers will each hold one of these beliefs. Any figure read off a single
  house's forecast would be contested in the room.
- **How is she not?** She never needs to pick a house. Her bottom is a Treasury fund, and from 2031 her range is money
  she owns (row 28).
- **What Caplet takes:** call 7.5% "J.P. Morgan's figure, at the top of the published range", and show a low case near
  6% or the median. TNA note 3 cites only J.P. Morgan; naming the spread answers the cherry-picking question before a
  judge asks it.

### Card 2. Ten-year house forecasts miss by about four points a year, in both directions
- **What is it?**
  - Dimensional: ten firms' forecasts for 2014-2023 missed US equities (5.5-7.7% forecast, 11.5% actual), with an
    average equity error a little over 4 points a year.
  - Sebastian: 14 of 15 asset classes landed outside the whole range of the consensus.
  - AQR, on its own forecasts: a 50% chance of missing by more than 3 points a year over ten years.
  - AQR's 2011 forecast of 4.0% real became 14.0% real; GMO's 2012 forecast of 0.4% real became about 11.2% over
    seven years (rows 13-18).
- **Did it work?** As forecasts of the level of a return, poorly. As rankings, better: GMO's equity forecasts
  correlated 0.939 with outcomes in 2000-2008 while still missing the levels, and Research Affiliates' rank order
  held (rows 17, 19).
- **How is Laura like it?** VT's dollar value in 2031 is a level, not a rank, so it is the weak kind of forecast.
- **How is she not?** She is not choosing between assets on forecasts. VT's role is fixed by the plan, so ranking
  skill is irrelevant to her and level error lands only on the top.
- **What Caplet takes:** a sourced error bar: about plus or minus 4 points a year at ten years, and wider at three
  (Keel arithmetic: if the error is mainly return noise, it scales by the square root of 10/3, about 1.8 times). The
  last decade's biggest misses came from the pessimists, so a low forecast is not "safe" either.

### Card 3. Predictability fades as the horizon shortens, and VT's horizons are short
- **What is it?**
  - Goyal and Welch: no academic predictor beat the plain historical average out of sample.
  - Campbell and Thompson: some do, with restrictions, but "the out-of-sample explanatory power is small".
  - Vanguard (2012): P/E explains about 40% of 10-year real returns and almost nothing at one year.
  - Keel's US check: starting CAPE explains about 4% at 1 year, 10% at 3, 17% at 6 and 29% at 10, all in-sample with
    overlapping windows, which flatter the figures (rows 21-24).
- **Did it work?** The finding has held for two decades and was re-checked in 2024 with data to 2021 (row 22).
  Practitioners agree: Vanguard found a naive average beat the standard CAPE regression in real time, and Dimensional
  sees no "compelling evidence" for valuation timing (row 20).
- **How is Laura like it?** The half of VT that sets the top is held about three years, where valuations explain about
  a tenth of the outcome.
- **How is she not?** The kept half runs about five years and is promised to no one. Its uncertainty is her
  flexibility, not a risk to any promise.
- **What Caplet takes:** "no one can forecast" can carry numbers rather than rhetoric: about a tenth explained at
  three years, about a sixth at six. The word "well" in the claim is fair.

### Card 4. Experts' ranges are too narrow, and worst at five years and beyond
- **What is it?**
  - US finance chiefs' 80% ranges for next year's S&P 500 held the outcome 36.3% of the time; their ranges were 14.5
    points wide against 42.2 points in history (Ben-David, Graham and Harvey 2013).
  - Tetlock and Mellers (Wharton): expert forecasts were weakest "for long-term forecasts (often 5 y plus)" and among
    those with strong theoretical priors.
  - Even superforecasters are proven over about a year (rows 30-32).
- **Did it work?** It is one of the best-replicated findings on judgement; the CFO data cover 13,300+ forecasts.
- **How is Laura like it?** If she announced a range built on someone's forecast in 2031, it would likely be too narrow.
- **How is she not?** Her 2031 range is not a forecast: both ends are Treasuries she owns. Miscalibration cannot touch
  a range made of holdings.
- **What Caplet takes:** this is the deepest reason the IPS design is right. It also corrects store H7-032 to the
  published figures (14.5 against 42.2 points).

### Card 5. The range to show around 7.5%
- **What is it?** At J.P. Morgan's own inputs (7.50% compound, 17.88% volatility), the 90% band for VT's average yearly
  return is -7.9% to +25.5% over 3 years and -4.7% to +21.2% over 5. US history since 1871 gives almost the same width
  (-5.8% to +27.1% over 3 years). In dollars, the $33,515 bought in January 2028 is worth about $26.2k / $41.6k /
  $66.3k on 1 Jan 2031 (5th / 50th / 95th percentile) (rows 25-27).
- **Did it work?** The two methods agree on the width. Vanguard warns that models built on history may understate
  extreme losses, so the bands are, if anything, too narrow in the tail (row 25).
- **How is Laura like it?** The top of her range moves with this distribution until the move; NUMBERS' house model
  gives $111k to $171k (1-in-20 each side).
- **How is she not?** The bottom ($115,929 central) has no band once bought.
- **What Caplet takes:** show three things.
  - The 90% band: about -8% to +25% a year over three years.
  - The chance of a loss over three years: about 1 in 5.
  - The chance of landing near 7.5%: also about 1 in 5. Only about 20% of three-year outcomes fall between 5% and 10%
    a year, by both the model and US history.

  This answers CANON A25's request for VT's paths "in good and bad markets".

### Card 6. The forecast barely matters; the design does
- **What is it?** Across 4% to 10%, the choice of forecast moves the top by $3,784. Luck at a fixed 7.5% moves the
  moved half by about $26,000 between 1-in-20 outcomes. If VT went to zero, the payments would be unchanged and the
  gift would be the floor, $115,929 (rows 27-28; NUMBERS section 4).
- **Did it work?** It is what the frozen IPS already says: "markets will be able to increase or decrease the top ... but
  the bottom will remain constant".
- **How is Laura like it?** Her promise to co-sponsors is the owned range, so an error in the forecast cannot break a
  promise.
- **How is she not?** Realised returns still decide how generous the top is, so "independent of markets" would be
  false. "Independent of the forecast" is true.
- **What Caplet takes:** the ratio of about seven to one (the luck spread against the forecast spread) is a compact way
  to say why the team spent its care on the Treasuries and not on the share forecast. It matches TNA l.23: "VT is where
  we allow ourselves to be wrong".

### Card 7. The wrong window: six years versus three and five
- **What is it?** The claim speaks of 2028-2033 and six-year returns. VT is bought in January 2028; the half that sets
  the top is held about three years and the kept half about five. J.P. Morgan's 7.5% is a 10-15-year average and,
  in J.P. Morgan's words, "not designed to inform short term tactical allocation decisions" (rows 1, 29).
- **Did it work?** Using a long-run figure for a strategic holding is normal practice, so it is not a misuse. But a
  10-15-year average includes a slow change in valuations that may happen in any part of the period, so the first three
  years can differ widely.
- **How is Laura like it?** Her horizon to 2033 is six years (FINAL l.9), which is probably where "six" came from.
- **How is she not?** VT's money does not wait six years in shares. The part that matters to co-sponsors waits three.
- **What Caplet takes:** say "about three years for the half that sets the top" and keep "six years" for Laura's own
  wait. J.P. Morgan's caveat becomes a point in the plan's favour, because the plan never uses the figure for a
  short-term promise.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **The forecaster tells you not to bank on its forecast.** J.P. Morgan's own matrix says "an investor should not
   expect to achieve returns similar to the outputs shown herein". Its web page says the assumptions are "not designed
   to inform short term tactical allocation decisions". Most teams quote the 7.5%; few quote the warning beside it, and
   the warning is the case for Caplet's design (row 1).
2. **Professionals 10 points apart on the same asset in the same season.**
   - Within weeks of each other, consultants put world shares at 7.50%, 5.70% and 5.25%.
   - J.P. Morgan says +7.5% a year; GMO's figures imply about -2.7% nominal.
   - Dahlquist and Ibert show this is a lasting difference in beliefs, not noise (rows 4, 10, 12).
3. **"About 7.5%" is rarely what happens.** Over three years, J.P. Morgan's own volatility and 150 years of US history
   agree: only about one result in five lands between 5% and 10% a year, and about one in five loses money (rows
   25-26).
4. **The pessimists were the most wrong last decade.**
   - Every one of ten firms forecast US shares below the 11.5% they delivered in 2014-2023.
   - AQR's 2011 forecast of 4.0% real became 14.0%; GMO's 2012 forecast of 0.4% became about 11%.
   - A low forecast is not the "safe" one; the error is about size, not direction (rows 13, 16, 18).
5. **Wharton's own research points the same way.** Tetlock and Mellers found expert judgement weakest at "5 y plus",
   and finance chiefs' ranges for the market held the outcome barely a third of the time (rows 30-31).
6. **The design swamps the forecast seven to one.** Changing the forecast from 4% to 10% moves the top by $3,784;
   ordinary luck at 7.5% moves it by about $26,000 (row 27).
