### Verdicts on the brief's claims (evidence in the rows named)

1. **"An author's income falls in recessions": CHANGE, stream by stream.** Not one income but five, on different
   clocks. (a) Advances: NEW deals and their size are cyclical (2009 cuts fell on midlist and new authors, rows 6, 7),
   but money already contracted is paid in instalments on milestones the publisher controls, so a downturn mostly shows
   as delay (rows 1-5). (b) Royalties: retail book buying is defensive, and royalties arrive twice a year, three months
   after each half, with reserves held back for up to two years; a recession raises returns and shows up on later
   statements (rows 8, 9). (c) School and library money: falls late and hard, driven by government budgets, not shares;
   the deepest US school falls came in 2011-12 when federal stimulus ran out (el-hi -21.9% in the first half of 2011,
   K-12 -15.9% in 2012), while shares rose (rows 19-23). (d) Speaking and school visits: the most fragile stream, and
   for children's authors often the largest non-book one (rows 24-26). (e) Licensing and film/TV options: paused by
   events that have nothing to do with shares, such as the 2023 writers' strike, when option clocks stopped under force
   majeure in a year the S&P rose 26.06% (rows 27-30).
2. **"Children's and young-adult books held up better than adult books in past downturns": CHANGE, partly true.** At
   the till, yes in 2008-09: US juvenile units +6.2% (2008) and about flat (2009) while adult nonfiction fell 3.9% and
   6.4%; UK children's sales rose 4.4% in 2009 while adult non-fiction fell 8.6% (rows 12, 14). But it was carried by
   hit series (Twilight, Wimpy Kid), the institutional half fell (all-channel juvenile units -1.3% in 2008, row 13) and
   fell again later through schools (rows 19-21). And children's books and graphic novels had their own slump in
   2023-24, during a bull market (rows 17, 18).
3. **"The 2028 deposit's risk is mostly timing, not permanent loss": KEEP for contracted money, CHANGE for the
   portfolio.** Contract law and practice make most of a downturn's effect on money already agreed a matter of delay
   (rows 1-5, 8, 10). But the case fixes the deposit at "the beginning of 2028" and forbids any other money in before
   2033, so for the portfolio a payment that arrives after January 2028 is the same as a smaller deposit (row 31). The
   permanent-loss channels are real but narrower: deals never signed, cancelled speaking, a publisher that fails (rows
   10, 11), and a slump in her own genre (row 18). A late or smaller deposit is not one of the IPS's two reasons to
   change the plan (TWOREASONS; CANON A34, D9).
4. **Surprises that fit her profile:** see section (c).

## (b) Cards: the seven strongest ideas

### Card 1. For the portfolio, late is the same as small
- **What is it?** The case fixes the deposit at "the beginning of 2028" and forbids any other money in before 2033
  (case p.2). Most of what a downturn does to money already contracted is delay (rows 2, 4, 5, 8, 10). Delay past
  January 2028 cannot be made up later, so the plan sees it as a smaller deposit (row 31).
- **Did it work?** In 2020 the biggest publisher "lengthened the period of payment on the first installments" (row 4);
  Simon & Schuster delayed 150 titles in a quarter (row 5); Dorchester's authors waited about two years and were then
  paid (row 10). Delay was the normal outcome, loss the exception.
- **How is Laura like it?** Her deposit draws on advances, speaking, licensing and ventures (case p.2), all paid on
  someone else's timetable.
- **How is she not?** She may have savings outside the portfolio (case p.2 mentions "financial resources outside the
  portfolio" for living costs) and could pay in on time from them; the case does not say she would.
- **What Caplet takes:** "later" and "smaller" are one risk for this plan, not two. CLASH noted: the IPS names a missed
  deposit, not a late or smaller one (CANON A34, D9); the plan's own order (ladder, then floor, then VT) already answers
  it, and the report can show that without adding a rule.

### Card 2. How an advance is paid, and who holds the clock
- **What is it?** Advances come in two to five parts: on signing, on acceptance, on publication, sometimes on the
  paperback (rows 1, 3). Acceptance "is in the publisher's control" (row 2).
- **Did it work?** The spread grew from halves to thirds and fourths between 2005 and 2010, before and through the
  crash (row 3). In 2009 new deals were cut for midlist and new authors, while proven sellers "will still command a
  price" (row 6). In 2020 deal volume held and sizes softened (row 7).
- **How is Laura like it?** Advances are the first item in her list (case p.2). Any 2026-27 deal would pay its later
  parts in 2028 or after, past the deposit date.
- **How is she not?** She is "bestselling" (case p.1): in 2009 that was the protected group. Her contract terms are not
  known.
- **What Caplet takes:** the advance risk to the deposit is mostly a timing risk on contracted money and a size risk on
  new deals; for a bestselling author the second is smaller than the median. CLASH (partly): weakens "smaller" for her
  advances.

### Card 3. The royalty lag: a shield and a delay
- **What is it?** Statements twice a year, paid within three months of each half; a reserve of up to 35% held against
  returns for about two years (rows 8, 9).
- **Did it work?** In the 2008-09 recession returns were forecast to "spike to unprecedented levels" and publishers
  became "bankers" to the shops (row 9), so the hit reached authors on later statements.
- **How is Laura like it?** Royalties are not in the case's list by name, but "publishing advances" earn out into
  royalties. If her periods are calendar halves, sales from the second half of 2027 pay out about March 2028.
- **How is she not?** Period dates vary by publisher; many books never earn out, so royalties may be small next to
  advances.
- **What Caplet takes:** a late-2027 recession reaches royalties only after the deposit; a 2026 to mid-2027 slump
  reaches them before it (row 33). This sharpens "later": royalties trail sales by three to nine months, and reserves
  hold part back for up to two years.

### Card 4. Children's books: steady at the till, late at the school
- **What is it?** Children's books sell into a family market and an institutional market "and those markets do not
  cycle together" (row 15).
- **Did it work?** In 2008-09 shop sales held (US juvenile +6.2% then about flat; UK children's +4.4% in 2009, rows 12,
  14), but all-channel juvenile units fell 1.3% in 2008 (row 13), and the school channel fell hardest in 2011-12
  (el-hi -21.9%, K-12 -15.9%) when federal aid ran out (rows 19, 20). School library medians fell 21% from 2008 to 2012
  (row 21).
- **How is Laura like it?** "Her books are read in classrooms around the world" (case p.1): she sells into both markets.
- **How is she not?** US data; the world's school systems do not share one budget cycle. Twilight and Wimpy Kid
  carried much of the shop resilience (row 12).
- **What Caplet takes:** the brief's claim 2 is true for shops and false for schools. The school half of her income
  falls three to four years after the share trough, which supports "later" and cuts against "during that same period".

### Card 5. Her genre has its own weather
- **What is it?** Graphic novels rose 29% in the 2020 crash year (Q1 row 32), then fell 22.4% in 2023 and 11.5% in
  2024 while the S&P rose 26.06% and 24.88% (row 18). Children's books led the market down in 2023 (row 17).
- **Did it work?** It is a boom and its hangover: kids' books were still above 2019 in 2023 (row 17). Graphic novels
  were about flat in 2025 (+0.7% to early October, row 18).
- **How is Laura like it?** She is a "bestselling graphic novelist" (case p.1) whose breakout came in 2020.
- **How is she not?** BookScan's graphic novel line is an adult fiction subcategory; where her own books sit is not
  checked; her sales are not known.
- **What Caplet takes:** the biggest recent swing in her market ran against shares, not with them. This is the strongest
  find against the correlation half of "career as shares"; it supports the certainty half (payments safe whatever shrinks
  the deposit). CLASH noted.

### Card 6. Speaking and school visits: the stream that breaks first
- **What is it?** Children's authors "often earn up to three quarters of their annual income from readings or
  workshops" (Europe) and "typically subsidise their income with visits to schools" (UK) (row 24).
- **Did it work?** In 2009, 38% of artists had fewer bookings and 44% cut fees (row 25). In April 2020 cancelled
  speaking was the most common loss (232 of 940 authors), far ahead of delayed or cancelled book contracts (52)
  (row 35). Schools were cutting author visits for budget reasons before COVID (row 24).
- **How is Laura like it?** "Educator" and "public speaker", with books in classrooms (case p.1); speaking is named in
  the deposit's sources (case p.2). Graphic novelists earn about half their author income outside books (row 34).
- **How is she not?** Her split between corporate, school and festival events is unknown; corporate fees pay most and
  are most cyclical (row 26, Q1 row 36).
- **What Caplet takes:** speaking is the deposit's most fragile source; it falls both with corporate budgets (fast) and
  with school budgets (late).

### Card 7. The force majeure clock
- **What is it?** Screen options pay about 10% of the purchase price up front and last 18 months to three years (row
  27). In 2023 producers used the writers' strike as force majeure "to stop the option clock" (row 28).
- **Did it work?** After the 2008 recession producers stretched first options from 12 to 18 months (row 27); after the
  2023 strike buyers returned with "pent-up demand" but green-lights stayed slow (row 28).
- **How is Laura like it?** "Licensing" is in her list (case p.2); a bestselling graphic memoir is the kind of property
  that is optioned. Whether she has any screen deal is not known.
- **How is she not?** Licensing for her may mean merchandise or foreign editions, not film; the licensing market as a
  whole recovered within two years in 2020-21 (row 29).
- **What Caplet takes:** a risk to the deposit that comes from a labour dispute, not a market fall; it shows why the
  plan treats the deposit as uncertain without forecasting why.

## (c) What would surprise a judge
1. **Laura's genre fell by about a third in a bull market.** Graphic novels -22.4% (2023) and -11.5% (2024) while the
   S&P rose 26.06% and 24.88%; up 29% in the 2020 crash year (row 18). Her career looks less like a share index and
   more like a single, hit-driven stock with its own cycle. The certainty design still holds; the correlation story
   needs care.
2. **The school money fell hardest when shares were rising.** US school sales fell 21.9% (first half of 2011) and 15.9%
   (2012), three to four years after the share trough, because federal stimulus expired (rows 19, 20). The same kind of
   cliff came again when pandemic school aid ended in September 2024 (row 22), just before Laura's 2026-27 earning
   window.
3. **The deposit date turns delay into loss.** Publishers mostly delay rather than default (rows 4, 5, 10), but the case
   allows no late top-up (row 31). And if living costs come first, a fall in income can hit the deposit by a larger
   share than the income itself (row 32, a Keel inference).
4. **Royalties trail sales by three to nine months, reserves by up to two years.** Sales in the second half of 2027
   pay out about March 2028 (row 8), so a late-2027 recession would reach royalties only after the deposit; one in
   2026 would reach them before it (row 33).
5. **Advances are paid in up to five parts over three years,** a habit that predates the 2008 crash (rows 1-3). In
   2009 the cuts fell on midlist and new authors; bestsellers kept their price (row 6).
6. **A strike, not a crash, stopped the option clocks** in 2023 (row 28), and the world's main children's rights fair
   was cancelled in 2020 (row 30).
