## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Two kinds of corporate cash, two halves of Laura's plan.**
(a) Damodaran's paper on corporate cash says a firm with "large but predictable investment needs can line up external
funding" in advance, while a firm with "both substantial expected investment needs and high uncertainty about the
magnitude of these needs" keeps money on hand, and keeps more when it must "make significant investments when their
operating cash flows are low" (Acharya, Almeida and Campello, as he cites them).
(b) Laura has both needs: the ten payments are large and known, so the plan buys them in advance; the building is
large and unknown, so half the shares are promised to no one; and the building's bad years are likely to be the years
her own advances shrink (IPS l.5-9), which is the third case, the one where a firm keeps the most.
(c) It breaks on what the money is held in: a firm's precautionary cash sits in Treasury bills, while her flexibility
sits in VT, so it is about a third smaller in the years shares fall (NUMBERS 8o), and the break teaches the honest
limit for 3e: her flexibility is there for uncertainty in its purpose, not in its form.
- Decision served: the half promised to no one (IPS l.15-16) beside the ladder (l.11-12); 2.3's career argument.
- Report part: 3b and 2.2 (one clause), 3e (the limit).
- Source: Damodaran (2005), the motives section and its footnote 7; Acharya, Almeida and Campello (2005) as cited
  there (original not read: UNVERIFIED).
- Pedigree or aha: **aha candidate.** A finance judge knows the precautionary motive; seeing the IPS's two halves as
  the textbook's two motives, with Laura's career as the hedging motive, is new, and the break is the question a
  judge would ask.

**2. A Treasury is a zero-NPV purchase: certainty costs Laura expected return, never value.**
(a) Damodaran writes that money in Treasury bills is "a zero net present value investment, earning exactly what it
needs to earn, and thus has no effect on value", and shows how an analyst who discounts a firm's riskless interest at
the firm's risky rate turns $200 million of cash into $90 million on paper.
(b) The same slip makes Laura's ladder look expensive: at market prices a dollar of Treasuries and a dollar of VT are
each worth a dollar, so the Treasuries give up only the extra return shares are paid for their lower lows (all
Treasuries for the 2028 deposit, option A: a median $186,000 left to her and $159,000 at the 1-in-20 low; all VT,
option E: $201,000 and $134,000; NUMBERS, simulated futures), which is why 3a can call the price of certainty "closer
to a deposit than a fee".
(c) Zero NPV holds for an owner whose other wealth is spread across the market; Laura's career already rises and falls
with shares (IPS l.9), so the Treasuries take away risk she carries anyway, and for her the trade is better than even:
the IPS's opening argument is exactly where the market's zero stops applying.
- Decision served: IPS l.11's "one step further" and 3f's price of "none" (about 1 in 31 against none, NUMBERS 8s).
- Report part: 3a (one clause beside "a deposit, not a fee"), 3f.
- Source: Damodaran (2005), the "Operating versus Non-operating (Excess) Cash" section and Illustration 1.
- Pedigree or aha: zero NPV is pedigree; **the reframe is an aha candidate**: the market charges nothing for the
  Treasuries today, and her career makes them worth more to her than to the market.

**3. One missed January would cost the years after it, and doubt costs before it.**
(a) Damodaran argues that valuations overstate firms that may not survive, because "a firm that becomes distressed in
year 3 loses its cash flows not just in that year but also in all subsequent years", and because the perception of
distress drives off customers, staff and lenders, costing "10-25% of firm value" in the studies he cites.
(b) The residency is a going concern, so its ten payments are a chain, not ten separate bets: a missed 2036 payment
could end 2037-2042 as well, and doubt about the money could cost artists, staff and co-sponsors before any January
is missed; "none" protects the chain and the residency's standing, not one $50,000.
(c) It breaks because a distressed firm's lenders can force a sale, while Laura's ten are owned whatever happens to the
residency (card Q77), so the ladder can stop the chain breaking for lack of money but cannot stop it breaking for any
other reason; that limit belongs to the operators and co-sponsors, and 3e can say so in a clause.
- Decision served: why "none" rather than 1 in 31 (IPS l.11; 3f), and why 3d says the ten are owned to 2042.
- Report part: 3f (the price of "none"), 3d (one half-clause), 3e.
- Source: Damodaran (2006), pp. 2-5 and the "Dealing with Distress Separately" section.
- Pedigree or aha: indirect costs of distress are pedigree (a finance judge knows them); a missed payment as a
  truncation rather than a shortfall is an aha candidate of middle strength.

**4. The textbook waives its own second test; a payer cannot.**
(a) Damodaran's riskfree paper says a true riskless rate needs one zero-coupon government bond for each year's cash
flow, then admits this "may not be worth the effort in mature markets" because the effect on present value is small,
and that banks found matching each cash flow "difficult to put into practice", so they match average duration instead.
(b) Laura's plan takes the purist route the textbook skips: one Treasury maturing before each payment, plus extra
Treasuries sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, which is what honouring his "second condition
... that is often forgotten" costs when the zero-coupon bonds themselves (STRIPS) are banned.
(c) His shortcut is right for a firm with endless, uncertain cash flows, where the error is a small percentage in a
model; Laura has ten known dollar amounts, so the purist route is cheap and her error would be a short cheque on a
date, which shows why a plan with ten fixed payments can do what the textbook calls ideal.
- Decision served: the extra Treasuries and the payments' standard (NUMBERS 2).
- Report part: 3a.
- Source: Damodaran (2008), pp. 6-10.
- Pedigree or aha: the two conditions are pedigree (already in the store); **the waiver and why Laura cannot take it is
  new**, middle strength.

**5. Ink is a currency: the Taiwan dollar's price for 2033 would use up about all her flexibility.**
(a) Damodaran teaches that a riskfree rate "is currency-specific", that across currencies "the only significant factor
that can cause differences is expected inflation", and that where rates do not match inflation, "interest rates will
have to rise at some point to correct for this divergence".
(b) Her Treasuries are riskless in dollars, the currency of the ten and of her stated range, but the building is priced
in Taiwan dollars; Taiwan's 5-year government yield was 1.78% and its 10-year 1.88% on 18 Nov 2026 against the US's
4.64% and 4.86% (6 Nov), and that gap compounded to January 2033 gives about 27.8 Taiwan dollars per US dollar against
33.0 today (Keel arithmetic), about 1% short of the strongest rate in 25 years (27.52, Jan 2022); if the rate went
there, the $115,000 top would need about $21,500 more, about the size of her flexibility ($21,179) and three-quarters
of her flexibility plus anything above the top ($28,682).
(c) A forward is a price for removing the risk, not a forecast, and long Taiwan-dollar forwards barely trade (card Q161:
hedges run one year at a time); his own inflation test fails here (Taiwan 2.33% in October 2026 against the 2.4% US
assumption), so his theory says the gap must close by Taiwan's rates rising (the 10-year rose from about 1.31% to
1.87% in a year) or by the Taiwan dollar rising, and the break teaches the plan to watch Taiwan's rates beside the
exchange rate, not to hedge.
- Decision served: no currency hedge and no Taiwan-dollar holdings; her flexibility's job "a stronger Taiwan dollar"
  (IPS l.15); Table 3 row 7.
- Report part: 3e (one limit), Table 3 row 7's note.
- Source: Damodaran (2008), pp. 10-13 and 20-22; Damodaran (2025 slides); Cbonds (2026a, 2026b); CBC (2026);
  Chunghwa Post (2026); repo Treasury curve (6 Nov 2026).
- Pedigree or aha: covered interest parity is pedigree to a finance judge; **"the gap's price for 2033 sits at the
  25-year strongest Taiwan dollar and equals about her whole flexibility" is an aha candidate**, partly overlapping
  card Q161's "a hedge costs about 3% a year".

**6. Which tool for which risk: Damodaran's table sorts 3e.**
(a) Damodaran's chapter on probabilistic methods puts discrete risks that come in steps into a decision tree, discrete
risks that come together into scenarios, and continuous risks into simulations, and judges a hedge by simulating how
often a fatal "constraint" is broken with and without it.
(b) Laura's plan has all three: the three dated steps and the 2028 deposit are a tree with known nodes; a Taiwan shock
that hits the exchange rate, building costs and co-sponsors at once is a scenario; shares and waiting cash are
continuous and simulated; and the 27 Sep check priced the ladder exactly as a constraint test, about 1 in 31 against
none (NUMBERS 8s).
(c) In Damodaran the simulation produces the decision's number; in Laura's plan the stated figures come from owned
Treasuries, so simulation only tests the top's level and her flexibility, never the stated range, which heeds his own
warning that "simulations yield great-looking output, even when the inputs are random".
- Decision served: the IPS's two reasons as named branches, not percentages; 3c's "certain" after the move.
- Report part: 3e's order (one line), 3c.
- Source: Damodaran (2007), "Comparing the approaches", Table 6.4, "Simulations with Constraints".
- Pedigree or aha: pedigree (a method), useful for structure, not an aha.

## Best three for the report

1. **Row 5 with rows 6-7 (idea 2): a Treasury is a zero-NPV purchase, and Laura's career makes it better than zero
   for her.** It gives 3a's "a deposit, not a fee" its textbook reason and turns the IPS's opening argument into the
   exact place the market's zero stops; a judge who expects "safety costs return" meets "safety costs nothing at the
   price, and her career makes it worth more than the price".
2. **Rows 1-3 (idea 1): the textbook's two motives for corporate cash are the IPS's two halves.** Known needs are
   bought in advance, unknown needs keep money back, and money is kept most when the need arrives in lean years, which
   is Laura's career; the break (her flexibility sits in shares) is the honest limit 3e must state.
3. **Rows 18-20 (idea 5): the US-Taiwan rate gap, compounded to 2033, prices the Taiwan dollar near its 25-year
   strongest and would use up about all her flexibility.** It tests IPS l.15's "a stronger Taiwan dollar" with a
   number from today's markets, explains why the plan neither hedges nor forecasts, and gives Table 3 row 7 a second
   thing to watch (Taiwan's own rates).
