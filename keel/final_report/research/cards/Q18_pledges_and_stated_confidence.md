## Verdicts

1. **Claim 1 (accounting and campaign counting): CHANGE.** The exact standard wording (FASB, ASU 2018-08, ASC
   958-605):
   - 25-5A: "A donor-imposed condition must have both" a barrier to overcome and a right of return or right of
     release; 25-5B: "it must be determinable from the agreement (or another document referenced in the agreement)".
   - 25-5C: "A probability assessment about whether the recipient is likely to meet the stipulation is not a
     factor." This replaced superseded 25-12, under which a conditional promise "is considered unconditional if the
     possibility that the condition will not be met is remote."
   - 25-5E: a promise "containing stipulations that are not clearly unconditional shall be presumed to be a
     conditional" promise.
   - 55-17: "A challenge (matching) grant is a common form of a conditional promise to give."

   Four corrections to the claim:
   - (a) The standard governs the recipient's books (the residency entity), not a co-sponsor's finance office.
     Co-sponsors read her statement in due diligence and may write her minimum into their own grant as a condition
     (B6a Q2).
   - (b) A market-set "up to" is not a condition in this sense: it has no barrier for the recipient and no right of
     release. It is an amount not yet promised, and it is booked as nothing.
   - (c) The bottom is booked only if it is documented, stated apart from the top (25-5E) and written in commitment
     words ("promise", "pledge", "agree", not "plan" or "intend"; SFW). It is then booked at fair value, discounted
     for the years to 2033. Campaign counting is kinder: CASE counts "Pledges and planned gifts ... at face value",
     the whole multi-year pledge since the 2024 update, but "Unspecified or undocumented pledges should not be
     included".
   - (d) After the 2031 move both ends are owned (A32), so "bottom firm, top conditional" describes the range only
     before the move. The kept half is never an "up to" (A30).
2. **Claim 2 (campaign practice): KEEP, with a change.**
   - What holds: a founder's committed money is the base that makes a project fundable. It signals viability when
     it is documented, binding, a specific amount with a date, and large against the goal. At public launch,
     campaigns typically hold "about half of their goal" (Marts & Lundy).
   - The change: some challenge funders do not count the founder's own money as the match. Knight: "Can the
     matching funds be my own money ...? No. We're seeking new money for the arts".
   - Some public facility funders count only legally binding pledges or cash in hand. Mass Cultural Council:
     "Pledges that are not legally obligated do not fulfill the matching requirement".
   - So her floor unlocks other money, but it is not necessarily the match. With strict public funders it counts
     only when it is paid in 2033.
3. **Claim 3 (stating uncertainty): KEEP, with a change.** The evidence says yes:
   - In van der Bles et al. (2020), numeric uncertainty cut trust in the number by d = -0.15 and verbal uncertainty
     by d = -0.55. On trust in the source the effects were d = -0.03 (not significant) and d = -0.21.
   - Meta-analyses find quantified ranges have null or small effects; disagreement framing is the costly kind.
   - The change: co-sponsors will see the 2033 result, and once outcomes are known, calibration beats confidence
     (Tenney et al. 2007; Sah, Moore & MacCoun 2013). A stated range also cushions trust when results move
     (Dries et al. 2024).
   - The useful form is therefore:
     - a numeric range with a central figure;
     - a number beside any likelihood word;
     - the named factors that could move it (the PSLRA "meaningful cautionary statements" analogy);
     - figures stated below the modelled payout, which the plan already does (NUMBERS s4 margins).
4. **Claim 4 (assurance): CHANGE.** The claim puts on one line two things that are separate: capacity (does the
   money exist?) and obligation (is she bound to give it?).
   - Owned Treasuries prove capacity but create no obligation and give no one else control.
   - A written binding pledge creates obligation but proves no capacity, and whether it binds varies by place
     (Restatement 90(2) against Kadimah).
   - Escrow or a control account gives both (Little Island). A bank standby letter of credit moves the risk to a
     bank (ISP98). A donor-advised fund route is the weakest, because DAF sponsors will not pay binding pledges.
   - Ranking: intention or DAF route < owned assets alone, or a binding pledge alone < a binding written commitment
     plus disclosed holdings < escrow or control account < standby letter of credit.
   - The case bars transfers before 2033, so Laura's honest position is a written commitment backed by disclosed
     Treasury holdings in her own name (B6a point 6). The language must say whether it is binding or a statement of
     intent (MA Council of Nonprofits), and must not call it escrow.
5. **Claim 5 (real founders' phrasing): KEEP as evidence, with one finding.** Read at source: Little Island, Ucross,
   Clarion West, Delfina Foundation and the Eric Carle Museum, plus Newberry, FIU, Florida Studio Theatre, the Lucas
   Museum and Black Rock Senegal as contrasts.
   - No founder or lead donor read today states a range with a likelihood.
   - They state an exact sum ("$100,000", "a $1.175 million gift"), a floor ("more than $113 million"), or a
     need-based cap ("up to $25 million ... as may be necessary").
   - Some state no amount at all (Carle, Delfina's founder family, Black Rock).
   - Laura's range-with-confidence therefore borrows its form from company guidance and risk communication, not
     from philanthropy (row 39).

## What the co-sponsor language must contain (notes, not wording)

1. Two separate statements: the owned minimum on its own, then the range. No single sentence that blends them
   (958-605-25-5E presumes ambiguous wording conditional).
2. A commitment verb for the minimum ("promise", "pledge", "commit"); "plan" or "expect" only for the range. The IPS
   already splits it this way ("the bare minimum you'll be able to promise", "your planned band").
3. A plain statement of whether the minimum is a binding commitment or a statement of intent (MA Council of
   Nonprofits; Gallet Dreyer & Berkey).
4. What backs it and where it sits: a Treasury fund in her own name maturing late 2032. Owned, not escrowed, not
   "set aside for" anyone (the case bars transfers before 2033).
5. For the minimum: an exact amount, the date (January 2033), the currency (US dollars) and the receiving entity
   (NEH: "a specific amount of money"; CASE: documented pledges only).
6. The range in numbers, with a central figure, and the confidence as a number or as "certain barring a US default"
   once both ends are owned (van der Bles; Gaertig & Simmons).
7. The basis of the confidence: owned Treasuries after the move, a model before it, said as "we modelled", not
   "we judge".
8. The specific factors that could move each end. Before the move: share prices, which set the top only. After the
   move: a US default, plus small interest on cash, already deducted. Specific factors, not boilerplate (PSLRA
   analogy).
9. The date the range becomes fixed (the move, before the 2031 meetings) and what can still change after it.
10. Nothing about the kept half: it is not in the range, not in the confidence, not an "up to" and not a backstop
    offered to co-sponsors (A30, A31, D12).
11. The minimum is not offered as a match on co-sponsors' money (55-17; Q11a). Co-sponsors may cite her minimum in
    their own conditions, which puts her money first in the counting chain (CASE).
12. Calibrated figures, stated below the modelled payout as NUMBERS s4 already does, because the 2033 result will be
    checked against them.
13. Timing and form stated once and kept; no later restructuring once others rely on it (FIU and Wertheim).
14. No claim of legal force that the team cannot show (Taiwan law is out of scope).

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 1-4 (how a promise is booked).** The bottom counts only when it is documented, stated on its own and
   written in commitment words. Likelihood never turns the top into a promise: "A probability assessment ... is not a
   factor" (25-5C), and ambiguous wording "shall be presumed to be a conditional" promise (25-5E). The IPS's verbs
   already fit ("promise" for the minimum, "planned band" for the range).
2. **Rows 27-29 (capacity is not obligation).** "Money you already own" proves she can pay. Whether she is bound is a
   separate fact, and the material must say which. Little Island shows the step above (a control account the
   partner runs), which the case rules out for Laura before 2033.
3. **Rows 20, 23 and 24 (numbers, then calibration).** Numbers cost little trust (d = -0.15 against d = -0.55 for
   words). Once 2033 arrives, calibration decides credibility. The plan already states both ends below the modelled
   payout (NUMBERS s4), which is calibration built in.
4. **Rows 9-11 (founder money is the base, maybe not the match).** Knight wants "new money"; NEH wants "a specific
   amount"; Massachusetts wants the match "in hand". Laura's minimum makes the residency fundable, but strict public
   funders will count it only as cash in 2033.
5. **Row 19 (FIU and Wertheim).** A $20 million lead pledge collapsed when its timing changed after a state match
   relied on it. Others will build on Laura's 2031 statement, so its date and form must hold.
