## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1, "the 2031 range is like a tolerance on an approved proof, not a forecast": CHANGE. The mapping breaks in three places; one part holds.**
  - What a contract proof is, in the standards' words: a print "intended to simulate a printing condition defined by a set of characterization data" (ISO 12647-7:2016, abstract). It is "the absolute reference against which the finished production is compared", and the cheaper validation print is "not intended to replace 'contract proofs' for predicting colour on production printing devices" (ISO 12647-8:2021, introduction). Who signs: the print buyer approves "validation prints and proofs", "OK sheets", "the print run" and "the finished job" (ISO/TC 130 guidelines, 2019 and 2024). Its binding force comes from the order, not the name (proof-drucken.de).
  - What tolerance means: the proof against its data, ΔE00 ≤ 3.0 for paper white, ≤ 2.5 on average and ≤ 5.0 at worst across the wedge, ≤ 3.0 for the primary solids (ISO 12647-7:2016, bvdm 2018 Table 30). The press against the proof: the OK sheet within ΔE*ab 5 of the proof, and production sheets within 4 (yellow 5) of the OK sheet "for at least 68 % of the prints" (ISO 12647-2:2004 and Amd 1:2007, 4.3.2.3).
  - **Break 1:** a contract proof is itself a prediction, made before the run on another machine. In this conceit it is the report's modelled figures ("about $95,000 to $115,000 on today's figures", NUMBERS 4), never what Laura states after the move.
  - **Break 2:** a print tolerance is two-sided and statistical: about a third of the run may fall outside the variation tolerance and the run still conforms. The ends Laura states after the move are owned in every tested future (200,000 simulated, 1,770 stock windows since 1871, 585 joint windows; NUMBERS 4), and the contribution is "the lower of what the funds pay and the stated top".
  - **Break 3:** the range is not a permitted error around one target. Its bottom is what the floor alone pays and its top what the floor and the moved half pay, each counted below what their bonds pay (less 0.5%, rounded down to $5,000), and the contribution lands on the top in every path and window (NUMBERS 4).
  - **What holds:** both are references agreed before delivery and checked by measurement, and a contract proof must carry its own measured status line (rows 7-8), as a stated figure carries the Treasuries that pay it. So the report never calls the 2031 range "a tolerance" or "a proof". If the trade is used, use the signature (claim 4) and the status line, not the tolerance.
  - **Judge's note:** the print industry's own standard lets about a third of a run fall outside its tolerance and still pass. No tested future falls outside the range Laura states after the move.
- **Claim 2, the over- and under-run allowance: CHANGE. The custom is real and documented; the mapping holds on one side only, and the trade's word must not be used.**
  - Exact wording, 1994 Printing Trade Customs (Printing Industries of America, NAPL, GATF), clause 12: "Over runs or under runs will not exceed 10 percent of the quantity ordered. The provider will bill for actual quantity delivered within this tolerance. If the customer requires a guaranteed quantity, the percentage of tolerance must be stated at the time of quotation." The 1985 text (Graphic Arts Council of North America): over- or under-runs "not to exceed ten percent (10%) ... shall constitute acceptable delivery ... If customer requires guaranteed exact quantities, the percentage tolerance must be doubled." UK, BPIF conditions (2001): "margins of 5 per cent for work in one colour only and 10 per cent for other work ... for overs or shortage (4 per cent and 8 per cent respectively for quantities exceeding 50,000) the same to be charged or deducted"; the 2004 text: 5% "unless otherwise agreed in writing". Why overs exist: spoilage, "a printer must start with many more copies" (printindustry.com, 2005).
  - **What maps, and is true:** a guaranteed count costs extra copies. The 1985 custom doubles the allowance for a guaranteed count; a practitioner's example is "60,000 rather than 55,000 copies on a 50,000 press run". Laura's top is guaranteed the same way: at today's figures the funds pay about $123,476 against a $115,000 top, so about $8,476 lands above it (NUMBERS 4; about 7% of the top, Keel arithmetic).
  - **Where it breaks:** in print the buyer pays for the extra ("bill for actual quantity delivered"). In Laura's plan the contribution is never more than the top, and the about $8,476 joins her flexibility: the extra comes back to her. And the custom runs both ways (over or under, charged or deducted); her range does not. After the move the contribution is the top, never above it, and below it only if the funds' costs beyond their fee take more than 0.5% (at 0.75% the top is missed in about 1 house path in 17, still inside the range; NUMBERS 4).
  - **Where it would mislead:** the IPS already uses the word for building costs ("building costs that overrun", l.15), as do NUMBERS 8j and 8o. A print "over-run" for the money above the top would give one word two opposite meanings in 3b-3c (money left over, money short). Tell the turn without the word (row 20).
  - **Judge's note:** in printing, a buyer who wants a guaranteed count pays for the extra copies. Laura's guaranteed top also produces extra, about $8,500 at today's figures, and the extra comes back to her.
- **Claim 3, the press check as Table 3's turn: KEEP IT OUT. Registration marks stay; two plain facts from it move into 3e.**
  - Craft facts, read at source: the signed sheet "becomes the standard that the printer is to match throughout the run" (US Government Publishing Office, GPO); "the operator will monitor the run to ensure consistency with your signed sheet" (Friesens, 2026); sheets are pulled on a schedule fixed by run size, every 500 sheets for 5,001-25,000 (Public Services and Procurement Canada, 2011); "A PSI is not a quality monitoring of the entire run and therefore does not constitute approval of the entire run" (GPO).
  - What it would add: checks dated in advance against a signed reference, and the point that signing the reference does not approve what follows.
  - Why it stays out as an image: (1) it is a second image for the job registration marks already do ("outside the page, checked each pass", V2.2 3e); (2) its signed sheet makes a reader ask "what is the reference?", and the nearest answer, the IPS, would become a proof (V2.4 guard 3); (3) a press check is where the buyer adjusts colour, which reads as adjusting the plan, when a fall in the market is not a reason (IPS l.17).
  - Plain use in 3e, with no image: Table 3's checks are dated in advance (written confirmation of the deposit by 1 Dec 2027; nothing by 31 Mar 2028; the trade days; the notice watch in September-October), and owning both ends in 2031 does not end the watching: fund events and a Treasury payment more than five business days late are watched to 2042.
- **Claim 4, "OK to print" and "stet": KEEP the OK, which has a true job in 3d; CHANGE "hers to keep"; stet goes to the store only.**
  - The OK, at source: proofs come back "marked 'OK', 'OK with corrections', or 'Revised proof required' and signed by the customer", and "The provider will not be responsible for undetected production errors if ... the work is printed per the customer's OK" (1994 customs, clause 9). UK: "the printer shall incur no liability for any errors not corrected by the customer in proofs so submitted" (BPIF 2001). The GPO tells its own inspectors: "Do not sign 'OK TO PRINT.' If you sign 'OK TO PRINT,' you are taking responsibility for items such as margins, image placement, and pagination, which you most likely did not check." They sign "OK FOR COLOR" only.
  - **The job:** whoever signs carries the error. In 2031 Laura is the one who signs, and the case says that if she "promises more than she can ultimately contribute, she could damage her credibility". So she signs for what she has checked: the two ends she owns. She never signs for her flexibility, J.P. Morgan's 7.5% or a median. And she signs later than the trade does. A printer's OK comes before the run, which then varies within tolerance; Laura names her range only after the move, when nothing in the market can move either end (NUMBERS 4). A 40% fall after the move changes only her flexibility (NUMBERS 8p).
  - **Change:** after the statement the figure is hers to answer for, not "hers to keep". The contribution goes to the facility; what is hers to keep is the money above the top and her flexibility.
  - **Where the OK breaks:** in print the signer approves someone else's work and frees the printer. Laura signs for her own money and frees nobody, so the parallel is the discipline (sign only what you checked), not the role.
  - **Stet** ("let it stand": "to direct retention of (a word or passage previously ordered to be deleted or omitted from a manuscript or printer's proof)", Merriam-Webster) is true but fails three ways. It lives on a proof, so it would call the owned range provisional. It restores, by a proofreader's choice, what the range keeps by construction. And pointed at the plan, it makes the IPS a proof (guard 3).
  - **Judge's note:** the US government's own printing office trains its inspectors never to sign for what they have not checked. That is Laura's rule, with money.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 32, "Do not sign 'OK TO PRINT'" (3d, the line before Laura's words).** The US government's printing office trains its inspectors to sign only for what they checked. It gives the co-sponsor passage its reason in one dissimilar fact: Laura signs for the two ends she owns, never for her flexibility or a forecast. It pairs with row 34 (she signs after the ink is dry).
2. **Row 4, "at least 68 % of the prints" (3c, the confidence).** The print standard's tolerance lets about a third of a run fall outside it. The ends Laura states after the move are owned in every tested future. It is the clearest evidence for why the report says "certain" and never borrows tolerance or confidence-interval language (with Q74).
3. **Rows 13 and 19, a guaranteed count doubles the extra copies, and the buyer pays (3c-3b).** Guaranteeing the top also produces extra, about $8,476 at today's figures, and the extra comes back to her. Told without the word "overrun" (row 20). It competes with dot gain for 3c's one turn; it adds who keeps the margin, which dot gain does not.
4. **Row 26, "no longer correct to judge the press sheet ... against the original" (3c or 3e).** After the move the top is judged against what the owned Treasuries pay, not against J.P. Morgan's 7.5%. It answers a judge who asks what happens to the forecast after 2031.
5. **Row 6, tolerances are "a reasonable balance between customer expectations ... and production costs" (3b-3c).** Printing gives up some certainty to save cost; Laura's plan gives up size to keep certainty ($33,150 of floor payout to the payments' safety; about $5,300 of what she keeps for each $5,000 more of top). It reframes the half kept as the price of a sure figure, not a hedge.

Runners-up: row 2 (ISO's own word for a contract proof: "predicting"), row 7 (a contract proof carries its own status line: 3d's basis), row 23 (eight exits against two), row 27 (signing the standard does not approve what follows: 3e).
