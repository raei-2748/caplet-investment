- **Problem class:** a solvent holder of safe bonds forced to pay before its bonds do (a funding or liquidity mismatch, as against a solvency loss). **Fields that solved it first:** central banking (the lender and buyer of last resort, from Bagehot to the Bank's "backstop pricing" and the Fed's term lending), deposit insurance law (the FDIC's least-cost rule and its systemic-risk exception), pension regulation (the collateral buffer sized in basis points and in days) and the bond desk's dedicated, cash-flow-matched portfolio, which is what Laura's ten Treasuries are.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. In 2022 the pensions held; the borrowing on top of the gilts broke

- (a) British pension schemes had put their matching money into funds (LDI funds) that bought government bonds (gilts) and then borrowed against them, through repo loans and interest-rate swaps, to buy more; when 30-year gilt yields rose 160 basis points in a few days after 23 September 2022, the lenders called for cash (over £70 billion, Bank staff estimate), and the funds sold gilts into a falling market, slowly and with the Bank of England as buyer after 28 September. The pensions were still paid: the regulator told Parliament on 10 October that it was "primarily an issue about short term pressure on liquidity (rather than a funding problem)", and most schemes ended better funded because the value of their promises fell further than their assets.
- (b) So the danger in a matched book is not the bonds but a claim on the holder that can fall due before the bonds pay; Laura buys every Treasury outright with money she already has, so a move the size of 2022's would mark her ten holdings down about $37,000 on paper ($234 a basis point, NUMBERS 1, times 160; a parallel, straight-line estimate, Keel arithmetic) and ask her for nothing, because she sells none of them before its date.
- (c) It breaks on the attack's own proposed fix, "no one can force a sale": no lender or depositor can, but one outside party can turn a holding of hers into cash before its date, a fund's sponsor ending an iBond fund early, and that is the one route where the 2022 lesson still applies (an IBTM end in 2031 with cash waiting at zero leaves the stated top up to $4,589 short, NUMBERS 3; Laura herself can also sell, card Q3b row 33). The honest sentence is therefore "no lender and no depositor can make her sell", with the sponsor's early end named in Table 3 row 4.
- **Decision it serves:** the payment money goes "straight into Treasuries" bought outright (IPS l.11, l.13), with no borrowing anywhere in the plan.
- **Report part:** 3e, the reserve "Nobody can run on the plan", reworded as in "Best three" below.
- **Source:** The Pensions Regulator (2022), Q1 and covering paragraph; Bank of England (2022), section 5; NUMBERS 1 and 3.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to a UK bond judge, who sat through it; what lands is getting it right (the regulator's own "liquidity, not funding") and naming the one route left, which the judge expects the team to miss.

### Idea 2. SVB's depositors were paid in full, by money that was never the bank's

- (a) Silicon Valley Bank held long bonds the Fed's review calls "low risk from a credit perspective", but 94% of its deposits were uninsured and could be withdrawn on request; over $40 billion left on 9 March 2023, the bank closed on 10 March, and its depositors were paid in full only because two-thirds of the FDIC's board and two-thirds of the Fed's board recommended, and the Treasury Secretary approved after consulting the President, a "systemic risk exception" to the usual law, under which uninsured deposits would have shared in the losses.
- (b) The cost of that exception, about $16.7 billion for SVB and Signature Bank together on the FDIC's current estimate, is being charged to about 114 other banking organisations through a special assessment, while SVB's shareholders and "certain unsecured debtholders" were not protected; so the promise was kept, but with other people's money, which is exactly what Caplet's IPS rules out for Laura ("none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price, or on anybody else's money", l.11), and the case gives her no one who could step in.
- (c) It breaks because a deposit can be withdrawn whenever the depositor asks, while each of Laura's payments falls due on a date the case fixed years ahead, so she faces no run; what carries over is the question "who keeps the promise if the portfolio cannot?", and for Laura the answer has to be "nobody needs to", which is why each payment sits in a Treasury that pays out before its date (NUMBERS 8h).
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.11's standard ("on anybody else's money") and the payments' dated Treasuries.
- **Report part:** 3e (SVB named once at most, pack C's guard); one clause available to 3a.
- **Source:** Board of Governors et al. (2023a); 12 U.S.C. 1823(c)(4)(G); FDIC (2023b; 2026); Board of Governors (2023c), SVB's failure section; card Q3b row 11 for the 94%.
- **Pedigree or aha:** the run is pedigree; the bill is not. Few readers know that other banks are still paying about $16.7 billion for it, and that turns the attack's correction ("the promise was kept") into the plan's strongest reason.

### Idea 3. A pension promise "does not depend on the performance of the fund"; Laura's does

- (a) Asked in the Lords whether the crisis put pensions at risk, the UK government answered on 14 November 2022 that "Individual member benefits were not at risk", because "The DB pension promise is ultimately the responsibility of the sponsoring employer and does not depend on the performance of the fund."
- (b) That is the comfort Laura's plan does not have: after the 2028 deposit no employer, sponsor or lifeboat stands behind her ten payments, so the portfolio is the whole promise, and that is why the report holds the payments to a test stricter than any pension's funding measure, paid in full even if cash waiting earns nothing (NUMBERS 2; card Q2a claim 2: "a pension needs less from its assets because it has more around them").
- (c) It breaks in Laura's favour on one point: a pension needs an employer behind it partly because its promises run for decades, depend on how long members live and keep growing while members work, whereas Laura's are ten fixed sums on ten known dates, the one kind of promise a set of Treasuries can carry alone.
- **Decision it serves:** the zero-interest standard for the ten payments and the extra Treasuries bought in January 2028 to meet it (NUMBERS 2, 4).
- **Report part:** 3a (the standard, one clause) or 3e.
- **Source:** UK Parliament (2022), written answer HL3123; The Pensions Regulator (2022), Q4 and Q7.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The government's own sentence says the pension promise survived because it did not rest on the fund; Laura's rests on nothing else, and the plan was built for exactly that.

### Idea 4. Two central banks sold time; Laura bought hers when she chose the dates

- (a) Both crises ended when a central bank sold time to holders of bonds that would pay in full later: the Bank of England bought £19.3 billion of gilts over 13 working days, on purpose "only ... at relatively distressed prices", to "buy time for LDI funds", and the Federal Reserve lent banks money for up to a year against Treasuries "valued at par", that is at the sum they repay at maturity, "eliminating an institution's need to quickly sell those securities in times of stress".
- (b) The Fed's rule counted a Treasury the way the report's conceit counts ink, at what it will pay on its date rather than what it would fetch today, but the Fed could count that way only because it was lending the time to wait; Laura's plan already owns that time, because every holding pays out half a month to 10.5 months before the payment it is for (NUMBERS 8h).
- (c) It breaks on price: the rescuers charged differently for time (the Bank a distressed price, the Fed a loan at par), and Laura pays neither, but her time is not free, since money held in dated Treasuries cannot also be in shares; the IPS pays that cost only on promised money ("only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares", l.11).
- **Decision it serves:** each Treasury matures before its payment (IPS l.13: "each maturing just before it's needed").
- **Report part:** 2.2 or 3a, one clause; the par point could serve the conceit's definition of ink once (CONCEIT V2.2).
- **Source:** Bank of England (2023b), sections 1, 2 and 5; Board of Governors (2023b).
- **Pedigree or aha:** BTFP's par valuation is known to US finance judges (it was argued over as a subsidy); the link to how Laura's plan counts its ink is new.

### Idea 5. The 2022 funds broke when yields rose; Laura's plan can be hurt only by yields falling, and only before she buys

- (a) Borrowed pension funds lost cash when gilt yields rose, because a rise cut the value of the gilts they had borrowed against, and since March 2023 the Bank of England's policy committee has asked them to stay able to absorb a sudden rise of "at a minimum, around 250 basis points" without selling.
- (b) A rise does nothing to Laura's payments, because she has borrowed nothing and only loses value on paper on bonds she will hold to their dates; the direction that can hurt her is a fall in yields before she buys, because then the ten cost more (a fall of one point before 1 January 2027 lifts their cost from $302,287 to $334,092, NUMBERS 1), and the 2028 deposit pays the difference (all of it only after a fall of 3.89 points).
- (c) It breaks after January 2028: once the ten and the extra Treasuries are bought, neither direction touches the payments, so the plan needs no buffer in basis points at all; it needs only the two other things the regulators added (cash in the right place on time, and a tested route for every step), which Table 3's dates and the fund-payout watch already carry.
- **Decision it serves:** the January 2027 purchase and the 2028 deposit as the plan's only top-up (IPS l.13: "we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit").
- **Report part:** 3e (the one rate risk the plan carries, and when it ends); 2.5 if the WInS fills are told.
- **Source:** Bank of England (2023a); Bank of England, Financial Policy Committee (2023); Bank of England (2022), section 5.4; NUMBERS 1.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A judge who expects "rates up" to be the bond plan's danger finds that the plan's only rate risk runs the other way and ends on a known date.

## Best three for the report

**Attack 5 (fixed-income PM): it stands, and the claim must change.** Every fact in it checks out at source, with three refinements. (1) The government told Parliament that "Individual member benefits were not at risk", and the regulator called the crisis liquidity, not funding; no source counts missed payments, so the report says "not at risk", never "every member was paid". (2) The funds did not stop selling on 28 September. They kept selling until 14 October (about £14bn by schemes and £23bn by funds), into the Bank's backstop, and none went below zero because the Bank bought them time. (3) The judge's proposed fix, "so no one can force a sale", is one step too strong itself: a fund's sponsor can end an iBond fund early (NUMBERS 3, $4,589 at worst on the top), and Laura herself can sell. **What the report should then say (3e, notes for Fri 27 Nov, not prose):** in 2022 and 2023 the promises held and the borrowing broke. Pension funds that had borrowed against their gilts faced over £70 billion of calls for cash, and a bank whose depositors could leave in a day lost over $40 billion of them in one day. Pensions were still paid and depositors were made whole, by employers, by the Bank of England and by other banks. Laura borrows nothing and takes no deposits, so no lender and no depositor can make her sell; and because nobody would rescue her, each payment sits in a Treasury that pays out before its date. SVB is named once; Orange County never.

1. **Q207-028 with Q207-002 and Q207-018 (ideas 2 and 3), 3e (one clause in 3a).** These promises survived because something stood behind the portfolio: in the UK government's words, the pension promise "does not depend on the performance of the fund", and about 114 other banks are paying about $16.7 billion for SVB's and Signature's depositors. Nothing stands behind Laura's portfolio, and her IPS already promised that nothing would need to ("on anybody else's money", l.11). The judge's correction becomes the plan's reason, which is the aha.
2. **Q207-001 and Q207-027 (idea 1), 3e.** The regulator's own "liquidity (rather than a funding problem)" settles the history in five words, and naming the one route left, a fund sponsor's early end in Table 3 row 4, shows a bond judge that the team knows exactly where the 2022 lesson still applies to Laura and where it does not.
3. **Q207-026 (idea 5), 3e.** 2022's borrowed funds broke when yields rose; Laura's plan can be hurt only by yields falling, and only before she buys (one point lower before January 2027 costs $31,805 more, NUMBERS 1, paid from the 2028 deposit). After January 2028 neither direction touches the payments, so the plan needs no buffer in basis points at all. It inverts what a judge expects, and it can be checked against the register.

Where it lands: 3e, the reserve "Nobody can run on the plan": it explains why the payment money is held in Treasuries bought outright, with no borrowing, each maturing before its date (IPS l.11, l.13), by showing that the safe-bond portfolios which broke in 2022 and 2023 broke on money that could be called early and were rescued by others, which the case never offers Laura.
