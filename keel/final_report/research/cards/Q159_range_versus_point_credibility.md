- **Problem class:** saying a quantity as an interval to someone who must act on it. Two questions: which value the listener books, and how width trades against being right.
- **Field that solved it first:** judgment research on interval estimates (Yaniv and Foster 1995, 1997; Teigen's group in Oslo). The largest field test is management earnings guidance in accounting (Pownall et al. 1993; Baginski et al. 1993; Hirst et al. 2007). Negotiation research (Ames and Mason 2015) shows how a range offer is read.

## Verdicts in one screen

### Claim 1: CHANGE. A listener books the end the speaker names. With no end named, which end is heard depends on who listens.

- **The funder test (16 Nov, four simulated funders).**
  - Top readers booked $115,000 and range readers $95,000.
  - One range reader booked $95,000 "+$20,000 'expected'".
- **Accuracy scoring.** A listener scores a range against "the judge's best point estimate which is sometimes explicitly indicated by the judge or, otherwise, the midpoint" (Yaniv & Foster 1997). Unnamed, Laura's range is judged at $105,000, a figure she never means (Keel arithmetic).
- **Investors read up.** Of managers asked where their "very likely" internal figure sits, 23.9% said above the top of the range they publish and 36.1% in its upper half; under 7% said below the middle (Call et al., Table 13).
- **Budgeters read down.** Analysts put most of the weight on the bottom (Tang, in store).
- **Negotiators are pulled toward the second figure.** A range whose second figure is lower than the one meant (a "backdown" range) lowered the counterpart's assumed walk-away price from $77.01 to $68.15 (Ames & Mason 2015, Study 1).
- **Fundraisers expect the bottom:** "they will almost always choose the lowest amount" (Eisenstein).
- **Lay valuers of a vague gain lean up:** "vagueness seeking for positive outcomes" (Budescu et al. 2002).
- **New wording of the claim:** "listeners book the end Laura names; a bare range is booked low by co-sponsors, who budget."
- **What this means for option (b).** With the top as Laura's figure, the bare range is in form a backdown range, so naming the top is required, not optional. The only range form that raised the anchor puts its extra figure above the one meant, and Laura can never state a contribution above the stated top.
- **Judge's note:** a careful team's instinct is to add the safer lower figure. In negotiation that instinct costs about a tenth of the deal, and in fundraising practice the giver's bottom is what gets booked.

### Claim 2: CHANGE. A range is believed when each end is backed and shown, not because it is a range.

- **Showing the parts earns belief.** "disaggregated forecasts are judged to be more credible than aggregated ones" and offset a speaker's motive to overstate (Hirst, Koonce & Venkataraman 2007). Analysts react more to forecasts that come with their parts (Merkley et al. 2013).
- **Width is read as doubt.** "precision signals to recipients the magnitude of the error they might expect" (Yaniv & Foster 1997). A range is the expected form only where the outcome is uncertain: "Incongruous forecasts ... may be perceived as non-credible" (Du et al. 2011).
- **Company widths show chosen caution.** Width mostly reflects the confidence a speaker chose, "as opposed to uncertainty" (Hribar et al. 2025).
- **Company ranges miss.** In 1979-87, "Actual EPS fell outside the forecasted bounds in more than 50 percent" of range forecasts (Pownall et al. 1993); about 31% held in a 2021 survey (in store).
- **Laura's range after the move:**
  - It holds by construction (NUMBERS 4).
  - Its width is money, not doubt (store Q35-031).
  - Read with company habits, a bare range will still be heard as "a margin for error" (funder reader 2).
- **The gap in the evidence.** No study compares a range whose ends are owned with a point. The verdict rests on disaggregation and congruence.
- **Judge's note:** the stated ranges finance readers know best miss more often than they hold. Laura's is one where, after the move, the outcome cannot fall outside, but only if 3c says why.

### Claim 3: KEEP, with its limit stated.

- **Where it holds.** People take a narrow range that misses slightly over a wide one that holds. 90% chose 140-150 United Nations members (truth 159) over 50-300, and 93% chose $12-14 million (truth $15 million) over $1-20 million (Yaniv & Foster 1995, Table 2).
- **Where it fails.** A narrow range that misses badly loses: 87% chose 140-180 rainy days (truth 130) over 160-165.
- **When both hold,** the narrower wins: 97% chose 37,000-40,000 over 30,000-45,000.
- **In finance,** ratings peak at a narrow but non-zero width, ±10-20% (Du et al. pilot). Laura's is ±9.5% (Keel arithmetic).
- **For Laura,** every honest wording holds after the move, so the most informative wording wins: the top named as her figure.
- **Judge's note:** "being 'precisely right' is rarely an available option" (Yaniv & Foster 1995). After the move Laura's figure is that rare case: money her Treasuries pay even if waiting cash earns nothing, with $2,142 to spare above the $115,000 top (NUMBERS 4, central, on the Treasury curve of 6 November 2026).

### Contradictions with the plan

None overturns option (b). Four cautions:

1. Fundraising practice is split on range asks, and the bottom of a range also moves what people give (rows 34-35).
2. Lay valuers lean up on vague gains, so "listeners hear the top" is true of some listeners (row 23).
3. Laura's width is about 3.6 times the median company range (row 32).
4. The case asks for a range, so the plain figure alone does not answer it (row 10).

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 29 (with rows 30-31), for 3d. Ames and Mason's backdown range.** A range whose second figure is lower than the one meant pulls listeners down. So Laura states the top as her figure and says the bottom once as its history. This is the plainest reason that option (b) is right, and the instinct it corrects is the one a careful team has.
2. **Rows 7-8, for 3c. Yaniv and Foster 1997.** Listeners judge a range against the figure the speaker names, or else its middle, and they hear its width as the error to expect. So 3c names the top and says what the width is: money moved into Treasuries, not doubt.
3. **Row 15 (with rows 9 and 17), for 3c. The judge's aha.** The stated ranges finance knows best miss more often than they hold, and they are set as cushions below what managers expect. After the move Laura's stated range holds by construction and has no cushion in it.
4. **Row 20, for 3c and 3d. Hirst, Koonce and Venkataraman 2007.** A forecast shown in its parts is believed more, and showing the parts offsets the speaker's motive to overstate. Laura's two parts with their two dates are exactly that.
5. **Row 6, for 3c's judge's note.** "being 'precisely right' is rarely an available option." After the move Laura is in the rare case where it is, which turns the textbook trade-off into her advantage.

## Gaps

- **Du et al. (2011):** the three experiments' numbers are paywalled; only the abstract, introduction and pilot were read.
- **Budescu et al. (2002) and Budescu and Templin (2008):** abstracts only. No effect sizes for vagueness seeking on gains.
- **Teigen, Halberg and Fostervold (2007a)** on how one-sided bounds widen implied intervals: only a snippet was seen, so it has no row.
- **Hirst et al. (2007; 2008) and Merkley et al. (2013):** abstracts and synopsis only. No effect sizes.
- **No study of a giver stating a range of what she will give.** All the fundraising evidence found is about asks to donors. How a foundation or corporate sponsor books a pledge stated as a range was not found (CASE reporting standards not read here; card Q147 covers gift offices).
- **No study compares the credibility of a range whose ends are both owned with a point.** The nearest evidence is disaggregation (rows 20-21).
- **Nelson, Partelow and Schlüter (2019, Journal of Environmental Management):** abstract read. The 48%, 35% and 20% response rates appear only in a secondary summary (MarketSmart) and are UNVERIFIED, so there is no row.
- **Seen only as cited, not read:** Weyant and Smith (1987); Lansford et al. (2012); Hribar et al. (2021); Russo and Schoemaker's managers' 90% intervals (38-58% held, cited in Yaniv and Foster 1997).
