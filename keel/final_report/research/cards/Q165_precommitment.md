  (Britain's Fixed-term Parliaments Act, 2011-2022).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### 1. The rope and the oath: the same crew kept one promise and broke the other

- (a) In Odyssey 12 the same crew makes Odysseus two promises: the rope that ties him to the mast holds even while he
  begs to be untied, but their oath not to eat the Sun's cattle lasts only "so long as my men had grain and red wine",
  and breaks after a month of wind keeps them on the island and the food runs out, while he is asleep.
- (b) Laura's plan puts every figure she states on rope, not on an oath: the ten Treasuries and the late-2032 fund pay
  on their dates with nobody awake and nothing left to decide, while the written rule (IPS l.17) is only an oath, so
  the plan never asks the rule to carry a promise; and the 2031 move turns the top from oath into rope before she names
  it ("so whatever figure you choose is money you already own", l.15).
- (c) It breaks because Laura's rope can be untied any day (a Treasury can be sold, Q7 row 11); what makes it rope is
  not a knot but that keeping it takes no act, since the oath needed hungry men to say no every day for a month and a
  held Treasury needs no one to do anything; the break teaches that the plan's strength is not willpower but that,
  after each of its three dated steps, doing nothing keeps every stated figure (Table 3's watch aside).
- **Decision it serves:** held to the end, no sale on price (IPS l.13, CANON A8); the 2031 move made before she speaks
  (l.15, 1 January 2031, NUMBERS 8r); the two reasons (l.17) carry no promise. **Part:** 2.0 or 3c in plain words
  ("Laura states a figure only when keeping it needs nobody to do anything"); 3e for "the rule is not what keeps the
  figures". The Odyssey itself stays out (Q7b row 42) unless the lead reopens it.
- **Source:** Homer, Odyssey 12.36-54, 12.192-200, 12.260-352 (Murray's translation), read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Every finance reader knows Ulysses at the mast (pedigree, store H2-052); almost none knows
  that the same book holds a second promise by the same men, an oath, that broke, or that the man who tightened the
  ropes (Eurylochus) led the breach. Set side by side, the two promises are the difference between ink and a rule.

### 2. Circe's choice: a certain small toll or a timing gamble

- (a) Circe tells Odysseus he must pass either Scylla, who will surely take six men, or Charybdis, who swallows the sea
  three times a day and destroys everything only if you are there at the wrong moment, and she tells him to take the
  six, because "it is better far to mourn six comrades in thy ship than all together".
- (b) Buying the bottom in January 2028 rather than at the 2031 meetings is the same choice: it costs about $7,400 in
  the middle and leaves FINAL behind the rival in 59.8% of house paths (Scylla's toll, paid in most worlds), while the
  rival is under $95,000 in 16.4% of house paths and 9.4% of history windows, and from a July 1929 start leaves $22,573
  against FINAL's $105,843 (Charybdis, which takes everything only if you arrive as she drinks; NUMBERS 8c).
- (c) It breaks because Scylla's six die for certain, while Laura's toll is a smaller middle that she does not pay in
  about 40% of paths and that takes nothing she has stated; and because Odysseus hid Scylla from his crew so they would
  keep rowing, while the report can name its toll (8c: "the price of FINAL"); the break teaches that the plan, unlike
  Odysseus, can afford to state its cost, because no promise rests on anyone's nerve.
- **Decision it serves:** the bottom bought in January 2028, three years before the meetings (IPS l.15); 3c's "held
  since January 2028". **Part:** 3c (why the bottom is bought early), 3e (the cost, stated). Never "3.2% fails 'high'"
  if the first plan's one date (8s) is set beside Charybdis (row 7).
- **Source:** Odyssey 12.101-126 and 12.222-233, read at source; NUMBERS 8c (MODEL).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. "Between Scylla and Charybdis" is a phrase every judge knows; Circe's actual advice (take
  the certain smaller loss, refuse the gamble on timing) is not, and it is 8c's trade almost line for line.

### 3. Strotz's two answers: bind the promised money, and let the rest be a plan she will want to follow

- (a) Strotz (1955-56) showed that a person who knows his wishes will change has two answers: tie his future self now
  ("either irrevocably or by contriving a penalty"), or choose "the best plan among those that he will actually follow".
- (b) Laura's plan uses both and splits the money between them: promised money is tied now (the ten Treasuries bought in
  January 2027 and completed in January 2028, the late-2032 fund in January 2028), while the 2031 move is a plan she
  will want to follow, because under "state only ink" the only top she can name in 2031 is one she has moved into
  Treasuries, and co-sponsors count the top (funder test, 16 Nov); half the shares are left wholly to her, which is
  Thaler and Shefrin's "Partial Rules": "discretion over a limited domain of decisions".
- (c) It breaks because Strotz's planner fears his own wishes and Laura's plan fears markets, and because the 2031 move
  rests on her wanting to name a top, so "state only ink" is the one rule the plan cannot turn into Treasuries; the
  break shows where Elster's warning (you can bind yourself to a mistake) is allowed to bite: only on figures she has
  not stated, such as the middle (about $7,400, 8c) or a top after a 40% fall before the move ($95,000 to $105,000, with
  the bottom and every payment unchanged, 8p), and never through the tie itself on a stated one (the one known way a
  stated top comes up short, an early end of the late-2032 fund with waiting cash earning nothing, is a fund risk of at
  most $4,589, NUMBERS 3, not a binding mistake).
- **Decision it serves:** which money is bound and which is free (IPS l.11, l.15); half the shares promised to no one;
  the 2031 move. **Part:** 3b (her flexibility as the free domain), 3c (why the move happens), 3e (where a binding
  mistake would land).
- **Source:** Strotz (1955-56), pp. 165, 173-174, read at source; Thaler and Shefrin (1977, rev. 1978), working-paper
  table, read at source; NUMBERS 3, 8c, 8p.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Strotz is known to economists and named in the store (H3-049), but not his two answers;
  that Laura's plan uses the first for promised money and the second for the top, so that the cost of any binding
  mistake falls only on pencil, is new and answers Elster's warning directly.

### 4. Britain's Fixed-term Parliaments Act: IPS l.17's shape, and why its weakest part carries no promise

- (a) In 2011 Britain fixed its general elections at five-year intervals with exactly two ways out (a vote by two-thirds
  of all seats, or a no-confidence vote not reversed in 14 days); the law produced one full five-year term, its
  two-thirds door opened in 2017 by 522 votes to 13, the door failed three times in autumn 2019, Parliament then passed
  a one-off Act by a simple majority, and the law was repealed in 2022.
- (b) IPS l.17 has the same shape (dated steps, two named reasons, "A fall in the market is not one of them"), and the
  British story shows why Laura's plan must not, and does not, rest its promises on that sentence: by the time any
  figure is stated, Treasuries she owns will pay it on their dates whatever the sentence says.
- (c) It breaks in two places, both in Laura's favour: Britain's exits were votes by the very people the law bound, so
  the exits became the decision, while Laura's two reasons are events nobody chooses, and the deposit reason closes by
  31 March 2028, before anyone relies on a figure (Q7b row 33); and like Parliament, Laura can rewrite her plan any
  day, but a rewrite cannot reach a figure already carried by owned Treasuries unless she sells them, which the plan
  never does on price; the break teaches that a list of exits is the weakest part of any precommitment, as Elster had
  warned in 1995 of a two-thirds rule set in a statute that "can be changed by simple majority".
- **Decision it serves:** the two reasons (IPS l.17) and the order of the plan: own first, then state. **Part:** 3e (one
  clause: a true fact behind the working words "the two reasons"); 3c. Never "only two things can break the plan"
  (Q109: the reasons to change and the ways a promise fails are two lists).
- **Source:** Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 s.2 (as enacted); House of Commons Library (2020); Institute for
  Government (2017); Heaton-Jones (2024); Elster (1995) n.42; all read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge will not know the Act, and its shape matches l.17 exactly: fixed dates and
  two named exits. Its history shows in one paragraph what Laura's plan does differently and why it matters.

### 5. Dated locks against amount locks: the Philippine field test

- (a) When a Philippine bank offered savers a locked account in 2003-04, savers who locked until a date mostly reached
  it (110 of 147 within a year, and 109 of them rolled the money into a new lock), while only 6 of 62 who locked until
  they reached an amount got there; and the account's three exits (a hospital bill, a death certificate, a document
  proving a move) were not used once in six months.
- (b) Laura's plan locks by date, never by amount: its three steps fall on 1 January 2027, 2028 and 2031 whatever prices
  do, so each one always arrives, and its two reasons, like the bank's exits, are events that can be checked on paper
  (a bank record, a Treasury that does not pay), not judgements anyone can argue about.
- (c) It breaks because the savers were binding themselves against spending, not against markets, and because the
  authors "conjecture that the amount-based goal is a stronger device" (it keeps people depositing), which Laura does
  not need since the case fixes her deposits; the paper also gives 140 date-based accounts on one page and 147 on
  another; the break teaches that a date lock suits Laura because her problem is arriving on time, not saving more.
- **Decision it serves:** the three dated steps (GOLDMINE 27: each buys a promise before it is stated); the two
  reasons as checkable events. **Part:** 2.2 (dated steps), 3e (the reasons).
- **Source:** Ashraf, Karlan and Yin (2006), pp. 638-639 and 657, read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree with one niche fact. The study is known (Q7-012 holds its headline); the split between
  date locks and amount locks, and the unused documented exits, are not in the store and do a job for 2.2 and 3e.

### 6. Christmas clubs: the oldest consumer commitment, and the price Laura does not pay

- (a) The first Christmas club (Carlisle, Pennsylvania, December 1909) took weekly pennies and paid them out "the week
  before Christmas, just when a little cash is most welcome", and for many years such clubs paid little or no interest,
  which Thaler and Shefrin called "a real puzzle for economists" and explained, as Strotz had, as the price people pay
  to stop themselves spending.
- (b) Laura's ladder keeps the same calendar (IPS l.13: "each maturing just before it's needed"), but not the same price:
  holding a Treasury to its date is how it pays its yield, so binding the payments costs her nothing beyond owning the
  Treasuries, and the plan's price of commitment is paid elsewhere, in the about $7,400 lower middle of buying the
  bottom early (8c).
- (c) It breaks because a club's saver was binding against her own spending while Laura's plan binds money against
  markets, and because the 1911 club rules punished a missed deposit ("Members neglecting to make their weekly payments
  will receive only the principal paid in"), while Laura's plan only says what comes first (the ladder is completed
  first, l.13); the break teaches that among commitment devices Laura's is unusual: tying the promised money costs no
  return that the same money, held the same way, would otherwise earn.
- **Decision it serves:** each Treasury maturing just before its payment (l.13); held to the end. **Part:** 3a (one
  clause at most), 3e. The opportunity cost of Treasuries against shares is the price of certainty (3b), not of binding.
- **Source:** Gardner Library, "The Topless Bathing Suit Revisited" (Cumberland County History); Bankers' Service Corp.
  v. Landis Christmas Savings Club Co. (M.D. Pa. 1920); Strotz (1955-56) p. 173; Thaler and Shefrin (1977, rev. 1978);
  all read at source.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for the clubs (behavioural economists know them); the advert's echo of IPS l.13 and the
  zero price of Laura's tie are small ahas.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 4 with rows 23-26 (3e, one clause): Britain's Fixed-term Parliaments Act had l.17's exact shape and lasted
   one full term.** A judge has not seen it. In one clause it shows that a list of exits is the weakest part of any
   precommitment, and that Laura's plan puts no promise on its list: every stated figure is owned in Treasuries first.
2. **Idea 3 with rows 11-14 (3b, 3c, 3e): Strotz's two answers, split by money.** Promised money is tied now, and the
   2031 move is a plan Laura will want to follow, because she can name only a top she owns. It answers Elster's warning
   head on: a binding mistake can cost only figures she has not stated.
3. **Idea 1 with rows 1-6 (2.0 or 3c, in plain words): the rope and the oath.** It explains why ink means owned rather
   than promised. A rule is an oath, and an oath breaks after a long bad spell while its keeper sleeps. The plan states
   a figure only when keeping it needs nobody to do anything. If the lead reopens Q7b row 42, Circe's choice (idea 2,
   rows 7-8) is the one image that maps a register figure (8c).
