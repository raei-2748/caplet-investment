- **Problem class:** when does money set aside for a debt count as having paid it? Accountants call this derecognition (taking a liability off the books). **Field that solved it first:** the law of performance (Taiwan's Civil Code book of obligations, 1929: lodgement with a court office), then US bond indentures (a bond "deemed paid" once Treasuries are deposited with a trustee), then US accounting: FASB Statement 76 (1983) said yes for companies, FASB Statement 125 (1996) said no, and governments kept the yes (GASB Statements 7 and 86).

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Accounting tried "set aside means paid" for thirteen years and gave it up, for the reason the IPS already respects.**
- (a) In 1983 America's accounting standard-setter, by four votes to three, let a company treat a debt as gone once Treasuries matching every payment sat in an irrevocable trust; Exxon had already done this in 1982 and added $132 million to one quarter's profit. In 1996 the rule was withdrawn, because the company is not released and, if the trust falls short, "the debtor must make up the difference".
- (b) Laura's IPS makes only the claim that survived: none of the ten "will depend on a share price, or on anybody else's money" (l.11), never that they are paid. Its two reasons to change (l.17) are the two ways her set-aside could be missing: the money never arrives (the 2028 deposit, whose first $39,162 completes the ten) or the US does not pay (a default).
- (c) It breaks because Laura has no creditor to release her and books no gain; Exxon's gain came from retiring old cheap debt, which the standard-setter later called "an economic loss to shareholders". The break gives the report its verbs: "set aside" and "does not depend on", never "paid" or "settled" before each January.
- Decision served: IPS l.11's wording and l.17's two reasons. Report part: 3a (one sentence), 3e. Sources: rows 1-2, 8-9, 11-14. **Pedigree or aha:** aha as a synthesis (the two reasons turn out to be the cases accounting names); the FASB history alone is pedigree for an accounting judge and news to most finance judges.

**2. Ink has two halves, and accountants wrote the second half in 1983.**
- (a) Property law decides whether a Treasury is "owned" (card Q157). The 1983 rule decided whether set-aside money "will pay it on its date": only "monetary assets" risk-free "as to the amount, timing, and collection" of payments, "denominated in the currency in which the debt is payable", with payments that "approximately coincide, as to timing and amount" with the debt.
- (b) That is ink's definition clause by clause: the T-bonds pass every clause; VT is owned but fails "monetary", because its dollars are not fixed, so it is pencil. The currency clause shows why a figure in US dollars still needs the half kept back for "a stronger Taiwan dollar" (IPS l.15): a US-dollar set-aside cannot cover a cost priced in Taiwan dollars.
- (c) It breaks on the iBond funds: a fund share is not on the rule's list, and the issuer can end a fund early (IBTA ended 15 days early). So the five fund-held payments meet the rule's reason (the extra Treasuries pay them even if early cash earns nothing) but not its letter. The 1996 rule adds a third verdict the conceit must keep: ink is set aside, not paid.
- Decision served: the conceit's definition; Table 1's T-bond and fund split; the half kept. Report part: 2.0, 2.2, 3c. Sources: rows 3-4, 6, 32. **Pedigree or aha:** aha for the conceit ("a startling new perspective": the line between pencil and ink was drawn by a standard-setter forty years earlier, for the same reason).

**3. A city may call the debt met; a company may not; the difference is what each reader needs to know.**
- (a) Since 2017 a US government that puts Treasuries bought "with only existing resources" (money it already has) into an irrevocable trust stops reporting the debt, because it "has sufficiently met its obligations" (GASB 86). A company doing the same keeps the debt, but discloses the set-aside, because that shows "what resources are unavailable to general creditors and for general operations" (FASB 125 and 140).
- (b) Co-sponsors read Laura's figures the way citizens read a city's books: they want to know what is spoken for and what is still free. So 3d works best when Laura tells them which dollars already sit in Treasuries for their building (the figure she states) and which stay hers (her flexibility), not how likely a figure is.
- (c) It breaks because GASB requires an escrow agent and an irrevocable trust, and when nothing forbids swapping the Treasuries for riskier assets the city must say so. Laura has no agent and no legal bar, only IPS l.17, so 3e owes the reader GASB's disclosure in one sentence: her own rule is the only thing that stops a swap.
- Decision served: 3d's words to co-sponsors; 3e's limits. Report part: 3d, 3e, 3a. Sources: rows 15, 19-22. **Pedigree or aha:** niche; an aha for a public-finance judge, because GASB 86 describes Laura's case exactly (her own money, open-market Treasuries).

**4. Bond law's order is money, then proof, then the word.**
- (a) When New York's Dormitory Authority set aside money for bonds on 6 October 2026, the Treasuries went to the trustee first and an accountant verified they paid "without regard to any reinvestment". Only then were the bonds "deemed to have been paid", and only then was notice published twice in newspapers and mailed to holders.
- (b) The conceit's rule, that Laura states a figure only once it is inked, is the bond market's own order. So the move before the 2031 meetings is the sequence every defeasance follows, not caution for its own sake. Whatever is left over goes back to the issuer "free and clear", as what comes back from the extra Treasuries goes back to Laura.
- (c) It breaks because the bond law needs a trustee, a lawyer's opinion and an accountant's report, and Laura has none. So the report never calls her holdings escrowed or defeased (WORDS; store H1-015, Q152-036); her proof is the plan's test (paid even if cash waiting earns nothing) and the holdings themselves.
- Decision served: the 2031 order (shares moved before the meetings); the conceit's 3c turn. Report part: 3c, the conceit. Sources: rows 23-24. **Pedigree or aha:** "deemed paid" is pedigree for a bond judge; the order (deposit, proof, announcement) as the conceit's rule is a modest aha.

**5. In every system read, "settled" means the risk has moved off the payer.**
- (a) Under Taiwan's Civil Code a debtor whose creditor will not take payment may lodge it with a court office, and from then the risk of loss is the creditor's (arts. 326, 328). A bond "deemed paid" moves the risk to the escrow, and US company accounting says it never moves without the creditor's release.
- (b) Laura's ten are settled under none of these, because the risk stays with her. The one risk she keeps is the smallest there is, the US Treasury paying, which is why the IPS names a Treasury default as a reason to change instead of pretending the payments are made.
- (c) It breaks because her ten have no creditor yet: the residency's operations begin in 2033, and in Taiwan's law a debt owed to oneself ends by merger (art. 344). The lesson is that "a high degree of certainty" was always about keeping a tiny risk well, not about passing it on.
- Decision served: how 3a frames certainty; 3e. Report part: 3a, 3e. Sources: rows 25-29. **Pedigree or aha:** niche, and Taiwan's own law; a modest aha.

**Verdict:** by every test about the money, the ten are set aside from January 2028 (not 2027): fully for the T-bond payments, in reason for the fund payments. By every test about the wall, they never are. Under company accounting since 1996 they could not be "paid" even with a wall. The wall the IPS supplies is a rule in words (l.17), and the report should say so plainly.

## Best three for the report

1. **Row 13 (with rows 11-12): the two reasons to change are the cases accounting names.** A judge who knows FASB withdrew in-substance defeasance sees that the IPS never claimed what was withdrawn, and that l.17's two reasons are the standard-setter's own reason, met by name. It serves 3a's one sentence on certainty and 3e's limits, and needs no new figure.
2. **Rows 3-4 and 32: ink's second half was written by a standard-setter in 1983.** "Essentially risk free as to the amount, timing, and collection", in the debt's own currency, is ink's "will pay it on its date"; it shows at once why VT is pencil (not a monetary asset), why the T-bonds and funds differ (the asset list), and why a dollar figure needs the half kept for a stronger Taiwan dollar (the currency clause). That is the conceit's "startling new perspective" with a source behind it.
3. **Rows 15 and 19: GASB 86 is Laura's case, and FASB says what co-sponsors need.** A government using its own money to set aside matched Treasuries may call the debt met; a company must instead disclose "what resources are unavailable". For 3d this turns Laura's words to co-sponsors from "how likely" into "what is already spoken for, and what is still free".
