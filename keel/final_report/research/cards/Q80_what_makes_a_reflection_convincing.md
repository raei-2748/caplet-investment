## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE (keep the core; say "changed or tested and kept", and add "checkable").**
  - The shape is old and sound. Dewey's complete act of thought runs from "a felt difficulty" to "experimental
    corroboration, or verification, of the conjectural idea" (1910, ch. 6). Mezirow puts reflection on premises at the
    top: "Reflection on presuppositions is what we mean by critical reflection". REFLECT's own scored example asks for
    "Exploration and critique of assumptions ... and the consequences of action" and credits "commitment to applying
    the understanding in the future" (Wald et al. 2012, supplement). Description sits at the bottom of every scheme
    (card Q27 rows 9-13, not repeated).
  - Two changes. First, a view tested and kept also scores at the top (card Q27 rows 16-17); the copout is that kind.
    Second, assessors cannot check most reflections. They see "interpretative descriptions", and that dependency "is a
    serious threat to the validity" of the mark (Koole et al. 2011). Their remedy is to judge "generic process skills"
    and to know the "triggering situation". A dated trigger and a register figure make Caplet's moments checkable,
    which most reflections are not.
  - New wording: "Assessors score highest the reflection that shows a belief, a dated trigger, and what was changed or
    kept, with the reason and a figure the reader can check; an account of events scores lowest."
  - **Judge's note:** the weak point of reflective writing, for the people who mark it, is that they cannot check it. A
    reflection that carries dated figures removes the marker's main doubt.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE (keep "few, in depth"; drop any magic number).**
  - Readers average rather than add. Mildly favourable items added to strong ones lower the overall judgement, and
    presenters do not foresee it (Weaver, Garcia and Schwarz 2012, seven studies, abstract). A weaker fourth moment
    would pull the three down. In RANKING_5 terms, adding the reserve event (20.3) or buying it (17.7) to the three at
    20.7-21.7 is what the finding warns against, and RANKING_5 already moved buying it to 2.5.
  - The "rule of three" is contested. Shu and Carlson (2014) found three claims beat four when readers know they are
    being persuaded. A test with 198 students found four positive claims liked more than two, and no rise in scepticism
    with more claims (Nordmo and Selart 2015).
  - Three is right for 3f for other reasons. 600 words hold three moments, each told with a before, a dated test and an
    after. And the three already cover all six words of Wharton's criterion (row 12).
  - New wording: "A few moments, each told with its belief, its dated test and what it changed, beat many told briefly,
    because readers average; the number is set by the words and by Wharton's list, not by a rule of three."
  - **Judge's note:** adding a good but lesser example lowers a reader's overall judgement, though writers expect it to
    help.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE.** An admission raises trust under four conditions, each tested at source.
  - It comes from a writer already shown to be able. A spilled coffee made the 92% quiz contestant the most liked of
    four and the 30% contestant the least (30.2 against -2.5; Aronson, Willerman and Floyd 1966). 3f comes last, after
    the plan has shown its skill.
  - It is a mistake of skill, not of honesty. After a competence lapse, owning up beat denial on rated integrity (5.32
    against 4.27); after an integrity lapse, the reverse (2.73 against 4.39; Kim et al. 2004).
  - It is owned as the writer's own decision and told first. Firms whose shareholder letters blamed their own decisions
    had higher share prices a year later, appearing "more in control" (Lee, Peterson and Tiedens 2004, via the
    university's release). Managers rated employees higher when they disclosed a mistake first and took responsibility
    rather than "blaming others" (Guchait et al. 2023, abstract).
  - It matches what the record later shows. A response later found untrue does more damage (Kim et al. 2004, Study 2),
    and negative statements are read harder than positive ones (Nordmo and Selart 2015).
  - Where it fails: a token flaw helps only readers who skim (Ein-Gar, Shiv and Tormala 2012), and a judge does not
    skim. Self-criticism written for an assessor is "two sides of the same coin" with self-praise (McGarr and
    O'Gallchoir 2020). An average performer's slip lowers liking (Aronson et al.).
  - New wording: "Admitting one real mistake of judgement, owned as our own decision, with its dated cost and the rule it
    produced, raises a careful reader's trust once the report has shown its competence; a confession made for its own
    sake does not."
  - **Judge's note:** owning up to a lapse of skill raised rated integrity from 4.27 to 5.32; owning up to a lapse of
    honesty cut it from 4.39 to 2.73. Which kind of mistake it is matters more than whether it is admitted.
- **Claim 4: KILL as a description of investors; KEEP as the standard this reader is told to use.**
  - Investors, professionals included, mostly judge by outcome. 3,400 US plan sponsors (1994-2003) hired managers after
    large excess returns that did not continue, and the managers they fired would have done about as well as their
    replacements (Goyal and Wahal 2008, abstract).
  - Those who do judge by process did no better. Investment consultants weigh managers' "non-performance attributes"
    far above past returns, yet the funds they recommended trailed the rest by 1.12% a year, equal-weighted: "We find
    no evidence that these recommendations add value" (Jenkinson, Jones and Martinez 2016).
  - Neither rule alone is safe. Process judging "can readily ossify into bureaucratic rituals", and outcome judging
    breeds "risk-averse consensus forecasts"; Tetlock and Mellers (2011) favour hybrids of "rigor and accuracy". The lab
    result they cite for outcome pressure did not replicate (row 30).
  - Wharton is the exception that matters. Its evaluators are told that "WInS gains and losses are not treated as
    evidence of the quality of the strategy". Its Guide says a strategy "may evolve as you learn more" but must not be
    rewritten "because markets move or hindsight reveals a different outcome".
  - New wording: "Good decision-makers try to judge a decision by its process; most investors do not, and process judging
    pays only when the process was written down before the result. Wharton tells its judges to judge the process, so a
    change of mind is told by its dated reason, and every good result is checked for luck."
  - **Judge's note:** the professionals who judge fund managers by process rather than returns recommended funds that
    trailed by 1.12% a year. Process judging pays only when the process was written down before the result, like a bar
    set before the numbers ran.
- **Map to RANKING_5's three moments (which rows help tell each, and how; no report wording).**
  - **The knife edge (K):** the trigger is a dated fact, Dewey's "felt difficulty" (rows 1-2); the moment can be
    checked, which markers rarely get (rows 7-9); the fills near the year's cheapest close are told as timing, which
    is Wharton's own rule for its judges (row 31) and the opposite of what plan sponsors do (row 24); the dropped
    headline is a lapse of skill, owned as the team's own (rows 15, 18).
  - **Too bold, then too timid (T):** the first plan's failure is the one admission in 3f, and it meets all four
    conditions of claim 3: it comes late in a report that has already shown skill (row 13); it is a lapse of skill
    (rows 15-17); it is owned as the team's own decision and told before a judge could find it behind IPS l.9 (rows
    18-20); its figures are the dated model's, exact and checkable (rows 17, 21). The "too timid" verdict is Mezirow's
    trigger (row 4) and a question about the plan's premise (row 3).
  - **"A copout" (C):** a question about the premise (row 3), answered by a standard written before anyone's view was
    known (rows 26, 28), with the dissenter treated as Tetlock and Mellers ask (row 29); the room 220 words give it is
    what lets it carry the erudition (row 10); at most one sentence on what switching without new evidence costs
    investors (row 27), told as what the team understands now.
  - **The set:** three, not four (rows 10-12); the closing paragraph says which recommendation each changed or held,
    which is REFLECT's "commitment" (row 6) and Wharton's "may evolve as you learn more" (row 32).

## Best five for the report

1. **Rows 31, 24 and 26: Wharton tells its judges to do what most investors do not.** Part 3f, the knife edge's luck
   line and the copout's bar. Why: a judge expects a team to credit its results; a reflection that names the luck of
   the 7-8 Oct fills and shows a bar written before the numbers ran meets Wharton's own instruction ("not treated as
   evidence") while professionals who claim to judge process picked funds that trailed by 1.12% a year.
2. **Row 15: which mistake, not whether.** Part 3f, too bold then too timid. Why: the first plan's failure is a
   lapse of skill, the kind an admission repairs (integrity 5.32 against 4.27), so it is told plainly as the team's own
   decision; nothing in 3f may read as hiding, the kind of lapse an admission cannot repair.
3. **Row 10: readers average.** Part 3f's set. Why: it is the evidence for three moments and for moving buying it
   to 2.5; a fourth, weaker moment would lower the judge's view of the three.
4. **Row 7: assessors cannot check most reflections.** Part 3f, every moment. Why: each moment carries a date and
   a register figure the judge can check against the IPS and TNA, which removes the assessor's main doubt; it is the
   reason RANKING_5 asks for "a single number a reader can carry away".
5. **Row 32: learning, not results.** Part 3f, the closing paragraph. Why: all three moments changed or held the
   plan before any order, the change Wharton's Guide invites ("may evolve as you learn more") and not the one it bars
   ("after observing the results"); the dates say so without a word of argument.
