## Verdicts in one screen

1. **"An act, not an amount; its job is to stop the same thing twice": CHANGE.**
   - True of election ink, and stronger than stated. The UK marks the register "to denote that a ballot paper has been
     received but without showing the particular ballot paper" (RPA 1983 Sch. 1 r.37(1)(d)); India's law exists "with
     a view to preventing personation" and refuses a ballot to anyone who "has already such a mark" (RPA 1951 s.61;
     CER 1961 r.37(2)). In India the ink goes on *before* the ballot is handed over, and an inked elector "shall not be
     forced or compelled to record the vote" (ECI handbook 2023), so the mark proves the entitlement was used, not that
     a vote was cast (rows 4-6).
   - The map holds with three exact limits (row 34): the late-2032 fund serves two audiences (TNA note 2, "IBTM does
     two jobs"), kept apart only by its lots; the extra Treasuries are one reserve shared by all ten payments, sized so
     every gap is paid at once; and her flexibility plus anything above the top now backs the letter's make-good (at
     most about $4,600 in paths the model leaves out, NUMBERS l.583) beside three uses never promised.
   - Where it stops, and why it is never a turn for "ink": an election mark hides the amount and the choice; Laura's
     ink *is* an amount with a date, meant to be read. And India's ink is followed by a signature in Form 17A, so the
     image leans toward "signed", the reading the definition with "owned" keeps out (V2.1).
   - **Judge's note:** "No dollar does two jobs; one fund does two, and keeps them in two records." A finance judge
     reading "no dollar does two jobs" (store H1-082) beside the Trading Note's "IBTM does two jobs" sees a
     contradiction; this sentence resolves it.

2. **"The box, not the ink, made a ballot final": KEEP, with two corrections.**
   - UK polling stations give pencils because ink is the riskier mark: it "may cause some transfer ... when they fold
     it, potentially leading to their vote being rejected if ... it looks like they have voted for more candidates"
     (Electoral Commission handbook 2026). A spoilt paper is swapped only before it is cast: "Once a spoilt ballot paper
     has been placed in the ballot box, a replacement ballot paper cannot be issued." Boxes are sealed "so as to prevent
     the introduction of additional ballot papers", and agents may add their own seals (r.43; handbook). Finality is
     custody, witnesses and law (rows 10-12).
   - Correction 1: in the box is not counted. A cast ballot is still rejected at the count for want of the official
     mark or for too many votes (r.47), as a held fund's payout "is not fixed" (TNA l.13), which is why the top is stated
     below what the held fund is counted to pay ($117,142 stated as $115,000, NUMBERS 4) (row 13).
   - Correction 2: the ballot leaves the voter; Laura's Treasuries stay hers. What keeps the top in place after the
     move is the IPS's rule (two reasons to change, l.17) and the signed letter (C3), so never "cannot move" or "locked
     in the box" (row 14).

3. **"When the ink failed, the safeguard was the register": CHANGE.**
   - The register is the primary safeguard everywhere; ink is the back-up where registers can be duplicated. UNDP: ink
     "complements primary safeguards, such as voter registration and ID verification"; IFES: ink is for when "the
     register still contains duplicates"; Ferree et al.: ink suits "countries with unreliable voting records". The UK
     uses no ink at all and recorded 7 polling-station personation allegations in 2024, 0.000017% of votes cast
     (PACAC 2025) (rows 16-17).
   - Malaysia 2013 fits the claim inside one roll: when its ink (about 1% silver nitrate against 10-18% norms) washed
     off, the Election Commission's defence was that names "had been marked off" (rows 19, 21). But critics said the ink
     had been demanded against one person "voting multiple times under false identities", which no roll can see
     (row 20). Afghanistan 2004 had both a duplicated register and misapplied ink; the fix was procedure, and the outcome
     was found unaffected (row 23).
   - Map: the ledger (lots by purchase date, the second account of C11) is what keeps a dollar to one promise, and
     Laura's money also carries a mark a voter lacks: each Treasury's own maturity date (row 18).

4. **Our own: "A mark that cannot tell two acts apart needs a second place to mark": KEEP.**
   - India inks the middle finger at a re-poll ("the marking ... during original poll should be ignored") or when a poll
     was held within two months, and an authorised companion on the right index finger (ECI handbook 2023, 4.4.2,
     5.2.4). The one exception, one mark for two simultaneous polls (CER r.49K(3)), works only because the mark records
     that a person came through, not how much (rows 7-8).
   - In Laura's plan the ten payments are told apart by their own dates. Only the late-2032 fund holds money for two
     audiences under one date: the 2033 payment's lot and the building's two lots. Read as one line it overstates the
     building's money by $20,000 to $27,000 (NUMBERS 8y C). The fix is the election official's: a second place to mark,
     the second account at the same broker (C11), with two orders in January 2028 (row 23).
   - **Judge's note:** the only instrument that serves two audiences is the only place a statement can count a dollar
     twice; the plan's answer is a second record, not a second fund.

**Also found (row 4):** election law puts the mark on *before* the act it guards (India "before delivery" of the
ballot; the UK "immediately before delivery"), so no gap opens between act and record. It is a strikingly dissimilar
fact behind the spine "Laura buys first and states second", never a new turn.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 18 (with rows 7-8): the one fund with two audiences is the one place a statement can count a dollar twice.**
   3d's one clause on the statement and Table 3's statement line. A finance judge has not seen election law's answer
   (a second finger for a second act) set beside a bond fund's lots; it explains C11's second account in one breath and
   reconciles "no dollar does two jobs" with the Trading Note's "IBTM does two jobs".
2. **Row 4: election law marks before the act it guards.** A fact behind the spine "Laura buys first and states second"
   and 3c's "inked before the 2031 meetings": two unrelated systems close the gap between act and record by putting the
   record first, as the plan puts the holding before the statement (IPS l.15). Strikingly dissimilar, true, one clause.
3. **Row 27: a visible mark lets others act on it.** 3b. Ferree et al.'s finding gives a reason, from outside finance,
   why her flexibility is never shown: what co-sponsors can see, they book (card Q147), so the statement shows the
   building's money alone.
4. **Row 13: in the box is not counted.** 3c. The UK's own rule that a cast ballot can still be rejected at the count is
   the plain picture of why a held fund's payout "is not fixed" (TNA l.13) and why the top is stated below the count.
5. **Row 2: "indelible" means dated.** A writers' guard for 3.0 and 2.0: the world's most famous indelible ink is built
   to last one poll and grow out, the true fact that kills "ink is permanent" and supports "certainty has dates".
