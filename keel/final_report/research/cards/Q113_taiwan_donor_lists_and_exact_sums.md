- **Problem class:** how the size of a gift is made public (a ledger of exact sums, or an honour roll of names and

  building accounts of Qing-era Taiwanese temples. Their 題捐碑 carved each giver's name and sum, then the accounts.
  A historian calls most of the 1871 stone in row 13 "徵信" (public accounting). Charity law later turned this into

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **"Exact sums by name, so say which figure she gives": CHANGE (the conclusion holds for a sharper reason).**
   - Taiwan has two habits, not one:
     - **The ledger.** Temple stones from at least 1871 (rows 13-16, 18), and since 2006 the half-yearly 徵信 lists
       the law requires (rows 1-6). They print each giver's name, the date, the exact sum and the use.
     - **The wall or thank-you list.** Cloud Gate's hinoki wall for 4,155 gifts and its yearly lists, the national
       venue's sponsor page, the national award and the university tiers (rows 19-24, 27, 32). These print names
       only, sorted by tier, by stroke count or by date.
   - Both lists record money that has arrived. None of the 30-odd lists read prints a pledge or a range (row 39).
   - So what a Taiwanese ledger will one day print is one exact figure, and Laura's 3d words should name it. The plan
     already does: in every modelled path the 2033 contribution equals the stated top, $115,000 centrally (NUMBERS
     4). That is the figure co-sponsors count (row 40).
   - **Judge's note:** people picture Taiwanese giving as names and sums on a temple wall. Taiwan's best-known modern
     wall, Cloud Gate's, kept the temple tradition and dropped the sums; the sums moved to a ledger the law requires
     twice a year. In 1949, as the old currency collapsed, Tainan's Tiantan temple dropped the sums from its stone too.
     Its first giver's 140 million old dollars were NT$3,500 at the reform rate (Keel arithmetic, rows 16-17). A sum
     is worth carving only in money that holds its value. That is why Laura states US dollar figures owned in
     Treasuries and gives the NT$ figure as a translation dated by its rate.
   - **Also:** in 1871 a Chiayi village temple carved its own shortfall into the stone: 2,169.18 dollars spent,
     2,028 given, "still short 141.18" (row 13). Taiwan's oldest building accounts publish the gap, not a promise.
2. **"Disclosure of solicited gifts is required by law and shapes how a founder's share is shown": CHANGE.**
   - The law binds the receiver, not the giver. It applies to gifts received, not pledges, and to all gifts, not
     only solicited ones.
   - The 公益勸募條例 (promulgated 17 May 2006) and its rules cover public schools, public bodies, public-interest
     associations, foundations and government agencies. Each must publish every gift at least every six months:
     the giver's name, the date, the use and the exact amount (row 1). This includes gifts nobody asked for (row 2).
   - Its central authority is the Ministry of Health and Welfare (since 2013), not the Interior (row 10).
   - A giver may hide the name but not the sum (rows 3-5).
   - Foundations publish names and sums every year unless the giver objects in writing beforehand (row 11).
   - Religious fundraising is outside the Act (row 9).
   - So the law would shape how Laura's paid gift appears, if it passes through such a body. It does not shape how
     her 2031 range appears: nothing in it publishes a promise.
   - The case puts Taiwan legal questions out of scope, so the report uses this only as context for 3d.
   - **Judge's note:** on a Taiwanese gift list the name is optional and the sum is not. A NT$1m gift appears as
     "○奇○○○○股份有限公司" with "100 萬元" printed in full (row 4). The figure is the public part, which is why
     3d leads with one figure.
3. **"Tiers and naming, with wording a founder could follow": KEEP, with sources and two twists.**
   - The national Wenxin award paid out in four money tiers (NT$0.5m, 1m, 5m and 10m). It was repealed on 8 Sep 2025,
     so it is history (rows 24-25).
   - The university rules supply ready wording (rows 27-33):
     - money tiers;
     - more than half the cost to name a building;
     - a naming term set by the gift's share of the cost;
     - "a name gives no ownership".
   - Museums themselves use the ledger more than tiers (rows 5-6, 23).
   - **Twist one:** every tier table read is wider than Laura's range. NT$3.11m and NT$3.77m (Keel arithmetic at
     32.768, Q11e claim 4) fall in the same tier in all five tables (rows 24, 27). Taiwanese recognition cannot tell
     her bottom from her top.
   - **Twist two:** every naming rule read gives a building's name only for more than half its cost. If co-sponsors
     together pay more than half, these norms would offer them the name. So 3d should say what recognition is
     offered for what share (row 28).
   - **Judge's note:** a 2026 Taiwanese university rule already draws the conceit's line. For pledged or instalment
     gifts, thanks "可依累積金額或實際到位金額分段對應" (may step up with the cumulative or actually-arrived amount)
     (row 30). Recognition follows money in hand, and Laura states only figures she already owns.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 3 (with row 4), for 3d.** On a Taiwanese gift list the giver can hide the name but not the sum. This is the
   plainest reason, from Taiwan's own law and practice, for 3d to lead with one figure.
2. **Row 40, for 3d and 3c.** In every modelled path the top Laura states in 2031 is the exact figure she pays
   (NUMBERS 4). It is the line a Taiwanese ledger would later print, so the figure co-sponsors count and the figure
   that gets published are the same.
3. **Row 24 (with row 27), for 3d and 3b.** Both ends of her range (NT$3.11m and NT$3.77m) fall in one tier in every
   Taiwanese recognition table read. The width of the range costs her nothing in how Taiwan would thank her.
4. **Row 30, for the conceit's turn beside 3d.** A 2026 Taiwanese rule thanks a pledge only by the money that has
   arrived. It is Taiwan's own version of the line the report draws between pencil and ink.
5. **Row 16 (with row 17), for 3e and 3d's NT$ line.** In 1949 a Tainan temple stopped carving sums because the money
   had melted: 140 million old dollars were NT$3,500 (Keel arithmetic). This is a vivid case for stating US dollar
   figures owned in Treasuries, with the NT$ figure dated by its rate. Runner-up: row 13, the 1871 stone that carved
   its own shortfall.
