- **Problem class:** how much reserve of strength must sit between what is promised and what will be there, and whether to state it as a multiple or as a deduction. **Fields that worked it out first:** structural engineering (Tredgold and Rankine in the 19th century: the factor of safety as breaking load over working load; then limit-state and partial-factor design, in America the AISC's load and resistance factor design of 1986, in Europe the Eurocodes) and bond analysis (Graham and Dodd, 1934 and 1940: the coverage of fixed charges by earnings).

## The short answer to the question asked

- **Graham's bond margin is a multiple.** A railroad's earnings should cover its fixed charges "better than five times" (1973), twice in 1940. Graham says the same idea can be stated as the share by which earnings may fall before interest is missed: five times covered means earnings may fall 80% (Keel arithmetic, 1 - 1/5). Its job is to protect against a fall in an income nobody can forecast.
- **Laura's plan carries no single multiple.** It counts each source of money at its own worst value: the Treasuries' own interest and principal in full; each iBond fund's payouts less 0.5%; interest earned by cash waiting at nothing; shares at nothing for any promise. Then it asks only that what is left be zero or more. Engineers call this partial-factor (load and resistance factor) design; NASA's "margin of safety" is what is left after the factors, and zero passes.
- **Its visible margins are deductions.** The 0.5% margin and the rounding down to $5,000 together make the room under the top: 2.32% centrally if cash waiting earns nothing ($2,731 under $117,731), and 3.16% under the bottom (NUMBERS 3). The extra Treasuries are not a margin (WORDS never calls them one): they replace the interest the ladder would have needed with Treasuries she owns, sized so nothing is spare in the zero-interest test. Read afterwards as a multiple, the reserve holds about $1.12 for each $1 the payments need at today's rates ($445,655 against $399,089, NUMBERS 8h), a figure nobody chose.
- **Why it matters.** (1) A single multiple charges the safest money for the doubt of the least safe, so it costs more for the same safety. (2) A deduction covers only the cost it was sized for, so it must come with a list of what it does not cover (NUMBERS 8u; Table 3 row 4); that is the Comet's lesson. (3) A deduction can be small where the thing it guards is measured: Laura's room is thinner than the margin Buffett called cutting it close, yet 8.6 times the costs measured (NUMBERS 8u).
- **Where Graham's margin and the plan's part.** Graham's margin "is available for absorbing the effect of miscalculations or worse than average luck". The plan does not keep a margin for misjudging value; it removes the judgment (whatever it cannot measure counts at nothing) and keeps margins only for costs it can measure.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

**1. Buffett's "cutting it close" is the size of Laura's margin, and for her it is wide (the strongest).**
- (a) In 1984 Warren Buffett explained Graham's idea by saying you never buy a business worth $83 million for $80 million, a margin of under 4% (Keel arithmetic: $3 million of $83 million is 3.6%), because "you insist it can carry 30,000 pounds, but you only drive 10,000-pound trucks across it".
- (b) Laura's stated top sits only 2.32% under what the late-2032 fund pays on its two purchases behind it if cash waiting earns nothing ($115,000 under $117,731), and her bottom 3.16% under the floor's ($95,000 under $98,096), thinner than the margin Buffett turned down; yet each is 8.6 and 11.7 times the costs it guards against, because the only thing that can make a Treasury fund pay less than its holdings is fund costs beyond its fee, about 0.27% in the five ended funds (NUMBERS 0, 3, 8u).
- (c) The comparison breaks where Buffett's $83 million breaks: his figure is "a very general estimate" of what a business is worth, which can be wrong by far more than 4%, while hers is a sum of Treasury payments read from holdings she owns; the lesson is that a margin is sized against the error in what it measures, not against the size of the figure, as Graham himself wrote of the bond buyer: "if he were sure of that, the margin demanded might be small".
- Decision served: the range rule's 0.5% margin and rounding down, and why co-sponsors can count the top. Report part: 3c. Sources: rows 2, 13, 16; NUMBERS 3, 8u. **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Every finance judge knows the bridge line as an argument for wide margins; here it shows why a margin of about 2% can be the wider one.

**2. Graham's bond test, run on Laura's ten: covered exactly once by money that cannot fall, with the money that can fall counted at nothing.**
- (a) Graham judged a bond by how many times the borrower's earnings covered its interest, because that margin protects the holder "in the event of some future decline in net income"; he wrote that the steadier the income, the "larger the portion of the supposed normal earning power which may be consumed by interest charges", and that a borrower's ability to pay "should be measured under conditions of depression rather than prosperity".
- (b) Laura's ten payments are her fixed charges, and the money that pays them is of two kinds: the Treasuries' own interest and principal, which cannot fall, and the interest those payments earn while they wait, which can fall all the way; so the plan counts the first in full and the second at nothing, which is Graham's depression test taken to its end, and in January 2028 it buys the extra Treasuries ($36,774) so the ten are covered exactly once, with nothing spare (NUMBERS 2).
- (c) The parallel breaks on how the margin is sized: Graham sized his from "the margin shown in the past record" and trusted that a depression would not go far below it, and the plan's own first sizing of Mon 9 Nov did the same (reinvestment 3 points below today's market rates) and would have left the ten short in 35 of 585 starting months since 1962, so the plan swapped that margin for a bound, because interest counted at nothing needs no guess about how far it can fall.
- Decision served: the size of the extra Treasuries, the test IPS l.11 is held to. Report part: 3a. Sources: rows 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 34. **Pedigree or aha:** Graham's coverage test is pedigree to a value investor; reading the zero-interest standard as his test run on a borrower whose earnings cannot fall, with the only earnings that can fall counted at nothing, is an aha, and gives 3a a sentence a bond judge recognises.

**3. Neither a multiple nor one margin: a factor for each source of money, then nothing spare (the engineers' answer).**
- (a) Engineers began with one factor of safety, the breaking load divided by the working load (Rankine: 3 for iron under a steady load, 6 under a moving one), and later moved to a separate factor for each load and each material, because one factor charges the well-known parts for the doubt about the poorly known: America's steel code switched in 1986 to "factoring both loads and resistance", Eurocode 3 counts steel's yield strength with a factor of 1.00 and Eurocode 2 counts concrete with 1.5, and a lower factor is allowed "if justified by measures reducing the uncertainty".
- (b) Laura's plan is built the second way: each payment is fixed in dollars (factor 1.00), the Treasuries' own payments count in full (1.00, like steel), each iBond fund's payouts count less 0.5%, and interest on cash waiting and shares count at nothing; after those factors the ten must have zero or more left over, which is NASA's pass mark for a margin of safety ("with zero as acceptable"), so "nothing spare" in the zero-interest test means the design passes, not that it is thin.
- (c) It breaks on calibration: engineering sets its factors so that failure is rare in probability terms, and the 1986 code was "calibrated" to match the older designs, while the plan's 1.00 and nothing are bounds, not probabilities, and only the 0.5% is calibrated (to five ended funds); that is why the report can call the stated range certain and prints no failure probability for the ten, and why the plan's one multiple, about $1.12 of reserve for each $1 needed at today's rates (NUMBERS 8h), is a result nobody chose.
- Decision served: why the extra Treasuries are sized to leave exactly nothing spare in the zero-interest test, and why WORDS never calls them a margin; the range rule's structure. Report part: 3a (one clause in 3c). Sources: rows 3, 18, 21-24. **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree to an engineer; to a finance judge, "a load-and-resistance-factor design that passes at a margin of zero" is new, and it answers the bond reader's question "why is nothing spare?".

**4. The Comet: a large multiple guarded against the wrong failure.**
- (a) De Havilland built the Comet's cabin to a multiple of its working pressure above any country's rules, believing, with the regulator, that a cabin which survived a test "to double its working pressure, 2 P, would not fail in service under the action of fatigue"; two Comets broke up in 1954 from fatigue cracks after about 1,290 and 900 pressurised flights, while the test section that had survived 16,000 cycles had first been loaded to twice its pressure, which strengthened the very corners that cracked.
- (b) Each of Laura's margins is sized against one named thing (the 0.5% against fund costs beyond the fee, the zero-interest test against cash waiting earning too little, her flexibility against the building), so the plan keeps a list of what no margin covers (NUMBERS 8u); one item on it is a fund event: if the late-2032 fund ended early in 2031, the interest its holdings still had to pay would be lost, and the 0.5% does not pay it (the top up to $4,589 short if cash waiting earns nothing, nothing short at today's market rates, NUMBERS 3).
- (c) It breaks because the Comet's failure was physics nobody had yet measured, while a fund's early end is a decision its board must announce, so the plan's answer is a watch and an act (Table 3 row 4), not a bigger margin; the teaching is the Cohen Court's, that a structure with "an ample reserve of strength in its early life" can still fail by fatigue: a margin covers its own failure, never a different one.
- Decision served: Table 3 row 4 and 3e's fund events; why the plan lists its exceptions instead of enlarging the 0.5%. Report part: 3e, Table 3. Sources: rows 27-31. **Pedigree or aha:** the Comet is pedigree to engineers and to many general readers; that a margin is a margin against one named thing, and that the plan prints its exceptions, is a modest aha for 3e.

**5. Quebec, 1907: a thin margin is safe only on a weighed load.**
- (a) The Quebec Bridge fell while it was being built because its engineer allowed working stresses "higher than any established by past practice" and the designers never re-weighed the bridge after lengthening its span, although their own specification said the dead load used "shall not be less than the actual weight of the structure when completed"; the stresses came out "fully 10 per cent" above those calculated, and the thin margin was gone.
- (b) Laura's room under the top is thinner still (about 2%), and it holds for the reason Quebec's failed: the figure she states is computed on the trade day from the holdings she has bought, each fund's published coupons and principal added up (NUMBERS 0, "How Laura measures an end on the day"), never from the model's $95,000 to $115,000, which on the 13 Nov curve was already $105,000 to $125,000 (NUMBERS 4).
- (c) It breaks because a bridge's weight must be estimated before the steel exists, while a Treasury's payments are printed before she buys it, so Laura can always do the step Quebec skipped; and the Commission's warning against "applying the precedents of practice to a structure that was entirely outside the range of experience" is a second reason, beside the conceit's ink, that the plan states no figure before it is owned (IPS l.15).
- Decision served: the rule that measures each end on the trade day from the holdings owned; nothing stated before the move. Report part: 3c. Sources: rows 20, 32, 33. **Pedigree or aha:** the collapse is pedigree to engineers; its link to "own, then state" is an aha for 3c.

**6. Where Graham and the plan part: his margin is for being wrong; hers are for costs.**
- (a) Graham wrote that a margin of safety "is available for absorbing the effect of miscalculations or worse than average luck", and that a true one is shown "by reference to a body of actual experience"; the engineers who fixed the aircraft factor of 1.5 in the 1930s said it "could not be substantiated statistically" and kept it because aircraft built to it "were flying safely", and their successors at NASA say it does not cover "Analysis or modeling errors".
- (b) Laura's plan keeps no margin for misjudging value, because whatever it cannot measure it counts at nothing (interest on cash waiting for the ten; shares for the bottom, so VT at zero still leaves the $95,000 bottom, NUMBERS 4; Trading Note 3 refuses "the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), and the only margin left is for a cost with a record: the 0.5% against the about 0.27% the five ended funds show.
- (c) It breaks on experience itself: Graham's coverage tests were "at bottom arbitrary" but rested on decades of bond defaults, while the plan's record for interest over 16 years is about four independent rate eras since 1962 (NUMBERS 7), too short to size a margin, which is why interest gets a bound and fund costs get a margin: where there is a body of experience, a margin; where there is none, count it at nothing.
- Decision served: why the payments rest on a bound and the range carries a deduction. Report part: 3e (assumptions and limits); one clause available to 3c. Sources: rows 11-13, 25, 33. **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A judge who knows Graham expects a margin of safety to absorb error; the plan refuses the error instead, which is the IPS's closing line ("With these promises, ours is to take none") in Graham's own terms.

## Best three for the report

1. **Idea 1, row 16 with rows 2 and 13: Buffett's "cutting it close" is Laura's margin, and hers is wide.** The margin Buffett refused (under 4%) is wider than Laura's room under the top (2.32%), yet hers is 8.6 times what it guards against, because a Treasury figure can be wrong only by fund costs beyond the fee. A judge who knows the bridge line sees in one sentence why about 2% is safe for her and not for a stock buyer. It serves 3c.
2. **Idea 3, rows 22 and 24: a factor for each source of money, then nothing spare, which is NASA's pass mark.** It answers the question a bond reader asks of 3a ("why is nothing spare?"): the safety sits in how each dollar is counted (Treasuries in full, fund payouts less 0.5%, cash waiting at nothing), and a margin of zero after those factors is how engineers pass a design. It also answers this card's question: the plan carries neither one multiple nor one margin.
3. **Idea 2, rows 1, 5 and 6: Graham's coverage test on a borrower whose earnings cannot fall.** Covered exactly once by Treasury payments, with the only income that can fall counted at nothing, is Graham's "depression rather than prosperity" taken to its end, in his own words ("if he were sure of that, the margin demanded might be small"). It gives 3a a sentence of pedigree that is also an argument.

Runner-up: idea 4 (the Comet) for 3e and Table 3 row 4: a margin covers its own failure, never a different one, which is why the plan prints its exceptions.
