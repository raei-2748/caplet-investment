**Claims tested**

3. Keep, kill or change: "Under-promise the commitment and hold a contingency outside it: project finance and public

**Problem class and the field that solved it.** Claim 1 is a decision under uncertainty with an act that cannot be
undone and news still to come: the economics of irreversible investment solved it (Arrow and Fisher 1974 on
quasi-option value; Bernanke 1983 on the "bad news principle"; McDonald and Siegel 1986 on the hurdle; Dixit and
Pindyck 1994, already held), and venture finance applied it as staged commitment (Sahlman 1990; Gompers 1995).
Commitment against flexibility for one person who expects news is a mechanism-design problem (Amador, Werning and
Angeletos 2006). Claim 2 is a campaign-design problem: capital campaign practice (Klein 2001; Kihlstedt 2016, 2018)
holds the know-how; the experimental literature on seed money and matches is already in the store (Q11a). Claim 3 is
the reserve-ownership problem of large projects: NASA's confidence-level budgeting (NPR 7120.5E; NAII 7120.1; GAO
reports) and the UK's Crossrail (NAO) show who holds the reserve and how often it is drawn; Norway and the Green Book
are already held (Q5).

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. Waiting is for bad news, and only one direction can be fixed later (Bernanke 1983; Arrow and Fisher 1974;
rows 1-5).**
- What is it? Two classic results on choices that cannot be undone. Arrow and Fisher show that even a planner with no
  fear of risk should hold back when the act is irreversible and the future will teach something: "underinvestment
  can be remedied before the second period, whereas mistaken overinvestment cannot". Bernanke turns it into a rule:
  commit now only if the cost of waiting beats the chance the commitment proves a mistake times its size. Only bad
  news matters ("only the left side of the outcome distribution is relevant").
- Did it work? It became the basis of the field (Dixit and Pindyck 1994 build on both; already held, Q5 row 30) and
  of how economists explain why investment stalls when uncertainty rises. These are theory papers; no field test.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her promise is the act that cannot be undone (case l.114). Each IPS use is bad news:
  an overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short. News on all three arrives after she states her
  figure in 2031 (case l.113). She can always add more from her flexibility; she cannot take back a figure.
- How is it not? Their investor chooses when to build; Laura must state a figure in 2031 whatever happens, and her
  money (unlike a wilderness) can be sold. The theory values waiting; it does not say what share to keep. And the
  option sits in world shares, smallest when shares fall (row 13; NUMBERS 8f).
- What Caplet takes: the reason for her flexibility in one line of economics, "a figure can be raised later, not
  lowered". Arrow and Fisher's sentence is the cleanest evidence for 3b. Use it once; never "real option" as jargon.

**Card 2. The price of waiting, staging, and a floor with freedom above it (McDonald and Siegel 1986; Sahlman 1990;
Amador, Werning and Angeletos 2006; rows 6-11).**
- What is it? McDonald and Siegel: because building is irreversible and waiting is not, wait until benefits are well
  above costs (about twice, for their parameters); the hurdle falls when waiting is costly. Sahlman: venture
  capitalists "rarely, if ever, invest all the external capital that a company will require", keeping the right to
  invest more. Amador, Werning and Angeletos: when a person values both commitment and news, the best simple rule is
  a floor with "complete flexibility otherwise".
- Did it work? Staging is the standard practice of venture finance (Sahlman's survey; Gompers's 794 firms). The
  floor-plus-freedom rule is a theorem, not a field result.
- How is Laura's case like it? The IPS is a committed stage (floor plus moved half, owned before 2031) plus a kept
  right to add (her flexibility). Waiting is costly for her (a "meaningful" figure, l.111), so she promises most
  and keeps back half the shares only, as the lower hurdle predicts.
- How is it not? Staging keeps the right to abandon; Laura's stated bottom cannot be abandoned (C5). The
  floor-plus-freedom commitment is against one's own temptation, not a promise to partners. And Sahlman warns that
  holding back can signal doubt to other funders (row 8).
- What Caplet takes: the shape (a firm figure and freedom above it) has a theory behind it; the signal risk is
  answered by naming the flexibility as hers (SKELETON 3b), not by hiding it.

**Card 3. Lead donors who come back at the end, and the rule not to count on them (Klein 2001; Kihlstedt 2016, 2018;
rows 14-20).**
- What is it? Campaign practice knows two kinds of late money from a lead donor. The announced kind, the "bookend":
  "I will give the first and the last $100,000"; or a $25,000 gift plus $25,000 pledged "once they reached the last
  $100,000". The unannounced kind: a donor "decides later to give another gift" when the campaign is "falling short
  of its goal" or "cost overruns have made the goal too low". Klein's rule for the second: "you should not count on
  this in your planning."
- Did it work? In Kihlstedt's account the donor who made the very first gift offered $30,000 to close the last
  $30,000; "His generosity sparked others to increase their gifts" and he did not have to pay. Practitioner stories,
  not studies.
- How is Laura's case like it? Two of the IPS's three uses, overruns and others' shortfall, are exactly what lead
  donors cover later of their own accord. "Promised to no one" is Klein's "do not count on this" seen from the
  donor's side. The IPS chose the unannounced form.
- How is it not? The announced forms are promises; copying them would break A30 and SKELETON 3d ("never a backstop").
  Klein's donors give from wealth outside any plan; Laura's flexibility is a defined half of her shares. Donors cover
  only a group that is "a little short", and large gaps are met by a new round and a match (Kihlstedt 2018), which
  3d never offers. No study gives a typical share (row 20).
- What Caplet takes: the strongest outside support for "promised to no one" in the words co-sponsors use. In 3b the
  flexibility can be described as what campaign practice already knows (a lead donor may help later, and no one
  plans on it); in 3d it is named as hers and never offered. No share is quoted.

**Card 4. The owner's reserve held above the project (NASA NAII 7120.1, NPR 7120.5E, SMD policy; rows 21-24).**
- What is it? NASA promises Congress a cost at 70% confidence, gives the project manager a budget at no less than
  50%, and keeps the difference ("Unallocated Future Expenses", "equal to the ABC minus the MA") above the project.
  The science directorate's rule: the reserve "will not be provided to the project ... without approval". The CFO
  will not let the same money "protect against multiple commitments".
- Did it work? It is NASA's policy since 2009 and the practice GAO audits each year; how well it holds is Card 5.
- How is Laura's case like it? The money that may meet an overrun is held one level up by the owner, outside what
  the builder or co-sponsors can plan on, and released only by her choice: D12's "Laura's separate choice, outside
  the announced range".
- How is it not? NASA's reserve sits inside its outside promise; Laura's sits outside hers, because what she
  promises is already owned (C5). NASA sizes the reserve by confidence levels; the case forbids sizing a contingency
  fund (l.106-107). And NASA forbids one pot backing several commitments, while the IPS gives one pot three uses
  (A31; card Q24): a size risk to record, not a broken promise, since none of the uses is promised.
- What Caplet takes: the word "under-promise" is wrong for Laura; "she promises what she owns and keeps the
  project's reserve above the project" is right. The NASA structure (Norway's P50/P85 is the same shape, Q5 rows
  18-21) shows the arrangement is normal for careful owners.

**Card 5. Held reserves are drawn, often (GAO 2019, 2025; NAO 2019, 2021; rows 25-26).**
- What is it? Of 53 major NASA projects since 2009, 23 (43%, Keel arithmetic) overran their baselines by 15% or more
  despite 70% budgets and headquarters reserves. Crossrail's builder exhausted its own contingency (£1,857 million to
  £84 million) and forecast needing the £600 million TfL held; funding still rose from £14.8 billion to £17.6 billion.
- Did it work? The reserves did their job, but were too small for the worst projects; the sponsors paid the rest.
- How is Laura's case like it? Overruns come first in the IPS's list, and the evidence says they are common even
  where reserves are designed with care. Her flexibility may be used.
- How is it not? Space and railway megaprojects are not a residency building; Laura's contribution does not rise
  with overruns (SKELETON 3d), so the risk lands on the project, not on her promise.
- What Caplet takes: one clause in 3e (assumptions and limits): her flexibility may not cover a large overrun, and
  co-sponsors should not plan on it. Percentages only; no facility cost (l.164-165).

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1, real option value: KEEP, with two conditions written in.**
- For: three primary sources read in full (Bernanke's working paper, Arrow and Fisher, McDonald and Siegel's working
  paper) say the value of waiting is real, needs no risk aversion, and comes from bad news. The IPS's three uses are
  all bad news. The asymmetry (a figure can be raised later, not lowered) is case l.114.
- Condition 1 (news must arrive): the option is worth something only if facts arrive between the 2031 statement and
  the money's use. They do: the tender, the Taiwan dollar, the written pledges. The report should say which facts.
- Condition 2 (waiting has a price): McDonald and Siegel's hurdle falls when waiting costs something; for Laura the
  price is a smaller "meaningful" figure (l.111). That is why the IPS keeps back only half the shares, not more.
- Against, recorded: the option sits in world shares and is smallest when shares fall (row 13; NUMBERS 8f), and the
  co-sponsor use is the one most likely to come with a fall (card Q4b, mixed). Withholding can signal doubt (row 8); SKELETON 3b's "name it"
  answers this. The theory never sizes the reserve (Q5 card 7, repeated here only as a limit).
- Report: 3b, one sentence on the asymmetry (Arrow and Fisher); 3e, the two conditions as assumptions.

**Claim 2, last-dollar funding by a lead donor: CHANGE.**
- What stands: campaign practice knows a lead donor who covers a shortfall or overruns late (Klein, Kihlstedt), two
  of the IPS's three uses.
- What changes: (1) Practice has an announced form (the bookend, a conditional pledge) and an unannounced form; only
  the unannounced form fits "promised to no one". Naming Laura's flexibility to co-sponsors as a last gift or
  gap-closer would turn it into a promise (A30, D12, SKELETON 3d). (2) "A typical share" goes: no study measures one,
  the examples run from gaps of about 1-8% of a goal to half of a donor's total, and with no goal in the case no share can be
  computed. (3) Practice covers only a group "a little short"; a large shortfall is met by a new round, which the
  report never offers.
- Against, recorded: Kihlstedt's announced backstop "sparked others to increase their gifts", a reason some would
  name it; the plan forbids that (A30), and the store records the opposite risk of "less need" (Q22-038).
- Report: 3b may say the flexibility does what lead donors in campaigns sometimes do later, and that no one plans
  on it (Klein's rule); 3d names her flexibility as hers and never asked or offered.

**Claim 3, under-promise and hold the contingency outside: KEEP, with a change of words.**
- What stands: owners hold the reserve above the spender and release it only by their own decision (NASA UFE; SMD
  rule; Norway, already held). That is D12's reading of the IPS.
- What changes: "under-promise" is the wrong word. NASA's and Norway's reserves sit inside the outside promise, and
  the promise is set high enough to include them; Laura's stated figure is already owned (C5), and her flexibility
  sits outside it for the project's risks, not for her promise. Say she promises what she owns.
- Against, recorded: held reserves are drawn often (row 25: 43% of NASA's major projects overran by 15% or more;
  row 26: Crossrail), and NASA forbids one pot backing several commitments while the IPS gives one pot three uses
  (row 24; A31; Q24). Both are risks to the project and to the size of her flexibility, not to her promise.
- Report: 3b, one clause on where the money sits (with her, outside the figure); 3e, that it may not cover a large
  overrun.
