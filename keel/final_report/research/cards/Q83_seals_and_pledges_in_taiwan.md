- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "Civil Code art. 3 makes a seal count as a signature, and a registered seal plus its certificate is what binds property and company acts."
  2. "Under art. 408 a gift promise can be withdrawn until delivered unless notarised or made for a moral duty, so a co-sponsor's pledge is pencil." Also: how Taiwanese capital-campaign pledges are written, any court ruling on a pledge to a nonprofit, and what this means for Laura's bottom, owned in Treasuries from January 2028.

  4. "Seal craft or seal law gives the conceit a true, startling turn for 3d."
- **Problem class:** proof of commitment: when does a written, stamped promise to give bind the giver, and what does a stamp prove? **Fields that solved it:** civil and notarial law (Taiwan's Civil Code of 1929, amended 1999; the Notary Act) and, for the stamp itself, Chinese connoisseurship (seals as provenance).

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE (half KILL).** Art. 3 is narrower than claimed. It applies only where a law requires writing, and it makes a seal equal to a signature, never more: 「如有用印章代簽名者，其蓋章與簽名生同等之效力」 (row 1). A sealed private paper is presumed genuine (Code of Civil Procedure art. 358), so a seal proves who signed, not that the signer is bound or that the money exists (row 2).
  - **What a registered seal (印鑑) is for:** proving identity to a registry without turning up in person:
    - land registration takes a seal certificate issued within one year instead of an appearance (Land Registration Rules art. 41(10));
    - a company or foundation disposing of land files 「法人及代表人印鑑證明」 and states its internal approvals were done (art. 42);
    - the land office keeps the legal person's seal and its representative's seal on one card (rows 4-7).
  - **Company seals are fading even at the registry.** Since 2008 a business seal "only" checks who is applying, and in 2026 an online company record without the big and small seals counts the same as a sealed paper one (row 9).
  - **Verdict:** kill "binds"; say "proves who, to an office".
- **Claim 2: KEEP, and it is stronger than the claim.** Taiwan changed the rule on purpose.
  - From 1929 to 1999, any written gift (立有字據) could not be withdrawn, 「俾得確定法律之關係」.
  - The amendment of 21 Apr 1999 took that away, because a written gift is sometimes made in a moment of feeling, and 「凡經過公證之贈與，始不適用」 (rows 10-12). Writing, a signature or a seal no longer makes a promise to give binding. Only a notary's deed, a real moral duty, or the money actually moving does.
  - A notary stamping only the signature (認證) is not enough:
    - in 2014, 52 of 61 notaries voted only to explain the doubt and leave it to a court (row 14);
    - in 2015 the High Court's forum held that an authenticated gift can still be withdrawn (rows 13-15).
  - **The courts agree:**
    - **Keelung, 2011:** a coal-mining association's council and members' assembly approved NT$80m for a named hospital building. It paid NT$40m and then withdrew the rest, and the court upheld the withdrawal. "Whether to give was the association's free choice; that the gift served the public good did not make it a moral duty" (row 20).
    - **Tainan, 2025:** a father had signed and sealed a gift deed, attached a seal certificate and filed it at the land office. He withdrew before the register moved, and the court ordered the title deeds back to him (row 19).
    - The moral-duty exception is narrow: the Supreme Court allows a pledge made in a disaster, if it is proportionate to the giver's means (row 21). A law firm says charity gifts are "mostly" moral duties, against Keelung (row 22, CONFLICTING).
  - **How Taiwanese pledges are written:**
    - university gift forms are signed intent or consent forms (意願書, 同意書, 認捐書) giving name, ID and amount, with no notary and no seal field in the forms read; one school issues a receipt only once cash arrives or a transfer is registered (row 26);
    - a bank's lecture hall began as a letter of intent (合作意向書) and ended as money in a dedicated account (row 27);
    - the government's own model founder's pledge asks for the registered seal, and promises to move the money later (rows 24-25).
  - **No notarised pledge was found.**
  - **For Laura:** her bottom is owned in Treasuries from January 2028, so it needs no exception, no seal and no notary; it is held for her. A co-sponsor's sealed but unpaid pledge is pencil in Taiwan's own law.
  - **Guard:** Laura's own 2031 statement is strong because the money is owned, not because of its legal form (row 37).
  - **Judge's note:** a seal looks like the strongest ink in Taiwan. Yet since 1999 Taiwan's own law has said that a written, sealed promise to give is still pencil until the money moves.
- **Claim 3: CHANGE; KILL "only" and "after a board resolution".**
  - The Supreme Court held in 1982 that a chairman signing for a company 「祇須表明代表公司之意旨為已足，非以加蓋公司之印章為必要」 (only has to show he acts for the company; the company seal is not needed) (row 32).
  - The chairman represents a company (Company Act art. 208) and a foundation (Foundations Act art. 43) (row 33).
  - A board resolution is required only in some cases:
    - for a public company: gifts to related parties, and "major" gifts to others (NT$100m, or 1% of revenue, or 5% of paid-in capital) (row 34);
    - for a foundation: drawing on its own fund (基金) needs a special resolution plus the regulator's approval (row 36).
  - Inside public companies, the big and small seals are kept by different people and stamped only after an approved request (row 35).
  - **What a residency would ask for (Keel synthesis, row 37):** the co-sponsor's registration record showing who represents it, plus board minutes where the rules above require them. None of these papers makes an unpaid pledge ink.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, with three true turns found (the lead picks at most one per part).**
  - **(a) Pencil void by rule.** Taipei's and Taichung's arts-grant rules say a receipt is void if 「擦刮挖補塗改、鉛筆書寫、墨跡不勻」: scraped, patched, altered, written in pencil, or in uneven ink (row 29).
  - **(b) The ink line.** A Taiwanese notarial deed fills every blank 「以墨線」 (with an ink line), so nothing can be pencilled in later (row 16).
  - **(c) What a seal proves.** In Chinese painting a seal proves who stamped, not what stands behind it: genuine seals turn up on fakes (row 39).
  - **Taiwan's companies, too:** the seal is the last step of an approval chain, stamped when the work is finished (row 35). This is the same rule the calligrapher keeps (Q47 A15).
  - **Judge's note:** the reader expects the seal to be the promise. In Taiwan's own law and craft the seal only says who, and what makes Laura's figure good is that the Treasuries are already owned.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 11 (the 1999 change: written to notarised)** for 3d. Since 1999, Taiwan's law has said that a written or sealed promise to give is pencil. The judge learns why the report counts only what Laura owns, from the residency's own legal system.
2. **Row 29 (pencil receipts void in Taipei's and Taichung's arts-grant rules)** for 2.0 or 3d, in the team's sentence. Taiwan's arts funders already work by the conceit: a figure in pencil does not count. It is a true and startling turn, and it does an investing job by naming what co-sponsors count.
3. **Row 20 (Keelung, 2011: half of a NT$80m building pledge withdrawn)** for 3c or 3e. A pledge approved by resolution was paid half and withdrawn half, and the court upheld it. This is why the board paper uses the owned bottom alone.
4. **Row 35 (the big and small seals, stamped only after approval)** for 3d. In Taiwan's companies, as in calligraphy, the seal is the last step, stamped when the work is finished. It ties the craft image (Q47 A15) to how co-sponsors themselves work.
5. **Row 16 (the notarial ink line)** for 2.0, as the alternative to row 29: Taiwan's binding deed fills its blanks with ink so nothing can be pencilled in. Use at most one of rows 16 and 29, and keep row 39 (genuine seals on fakes) as the guard beside the 3d turn.
