**Claims tested**
1. Funders decide whether to take a meeting on a few checkable things: what the founder has already committed and in
   what form (owned, escrowed, pledged), dates, what the funder is asked for and what is not, who else is in, what
   happens if costs overrun, and who runs the place.
2. Proof a co-sponsor can check (a custodian statement, a segregated account, a dated maturity) is worth more to a
   funder than words.
3. The passage should open with the ten-year operating commitment already owned, then the facility contribution (the
   co-sponsor judge's claim).
4. A passage of about 250-300 words is the right length.

**Problem class:** a disclosure written for a screener. One reader, one decision (take the meeting or not), little
time, and a file the reader must fill later (secured, expected, conditions, dates). Prior art from five fields: public
and private capital-grant guidance (what screeners ask for), finance-office verification (mortgage and match-funding
proof), campaign forms and gift policies (how a promise is worded and counted), real announcements and founder texts
(order and length, measured), and plain-language document testing (the reader test).

**Already held, not repeated:** Q18's fourteen "must contain" notes (wording, verbs, binding or intent, calibration,
no match, no legal claim); Q18 rows 27-29 (capacity is not obligation), row 19 (FIU), row 35 (Delfina); Q11b verdicts
(the full case opens with mission; announcements lead with committed money; CASE splits firm and conditional money),
rows 1-11 (AFP, Fund Raising School, Campbell, CCS, MacDowell, Banff, Yaddo, Skowhegan) and 22-25 (Kresge reserves and
"less need", GIA); Q11a row 11 (Gneezy overhead letter, cited again here only for order); Q4b (co-sponsors in
downturns); Q9 (what makes a residency last). This card adds what those did not have: real screening checklists from
public funders in England, Wales, the US and Taiwan; what a finance office accepts as proof; Taiwanese corporate
sponsors' own criteria; measured lengths; and a test protocol.

## (b) Cards: the six strongest ideas

**1. Screeners file money by status, date and what happens if it fails (rows 1, 6, 8, 18).**
- What is it? Public capital funders in England, Wales and the US ask the same three things about every source of
  match money: is it secured or only expected; when will it arrive; what happens if it does not. Wales adds who manages
  it. The NEA asks applicants to star the money that is "committed or secured".
- Did it work? These are working rules: the money is checked at application and again before contracts are signed (row 5).
- How is Laura's case like it? In 2031 she is the first source on the residency's funding table. Every co-sponsor will
  ask these questions about her money before asking anything else.
- How is it not? These funders judge organisations with boards. Laura is a person, and until January 2033 the money is
  hers, not the residency's.
- What Caplet takes: the passage answers status, date and "what if" for her money in plain words. The one "what if"
  left by 2031 is a US default (row 39).

**2. Proof shows capacity; a stated commitment shows obligation (rows 17-21).**
- What is it? Finance offices accept a dated statement from the institution that holds the money: custodian, holder,
  holdings, balance, recent. Proof-of-funds practice calls such a letter "a statement of capacity, not a loan commitment
  or a guarantee". Whether the giver is bound is a separate line on a pledge form.
- Did it work? Yes, as routine: lenders and match-funders verify this way every day.
- How is Laura's case like it? She can show Treasury fund units and bonds in her own name, the funds' public end dates
  and the bonds' maturities. These are things a co-sponsor's finance office can check without trusting her.
- How is it not? A co-sponsor's first meeting is not an audit. Documents are asked for at the award stage, so the
  passage offers them "on request" and does not attach them.
- What Caplet takes: claim 2 holds, with a change. Proof replaces adjectives ("without doubt", CANON C5), and one
  sentence says whether her statement binds (most likely intent until the residency entity exists). No escrow,
  segregated-account or DAF claim (Q18).

**3. Funders will not cover overruns, and they look for who does (rows 3, 38).**
- What is it? "We are unable to provide additional capital ... if the project costs increase"; contingency must match
  "the degree of certainty on the costs".
- Did it work? It is a standing rule of a national capital programme. It pushes overrun risk onto the applicant.
- How is Laura's case like it? Co-sponsors will want to know whether Laura's figure grows if the building costs more.
  The IPS's answer is in two parts: her contribution is the stated range, and the kept half is her separate flexibility
  (A31, D12).
- How is it not? The case says teams need not size a contingency fund or the facility's cost, so Laura cannot be the
  one to say how big the project's contingency is.
- What Caplet takes: the passage says her contribution does not rise with costs. It names the kept half as her own
  flexibility for the project's risks, outside the range and not promised. It leaves "a co-sponsor falling short" out of
  her words to co-sponsors, so that the cushion does not invite them to give less. The report's 3b says why.

**4. Real texts lead with what is being funded; operations covered still helps (rows 24-26, 29).**
- What is it? Combined announcements put the building first and operations last: Clyfford Still in 2008, the Met in
  2026. Artpace's founder text gives the building, then 12 years of operating costs. Gneezy's letter test found that
  saying the running costs were already covered raised giving the most.
- Did it work? The announcements are real; Gneezy's result was replicated (Camerer et al., 2018, via FLoRA).
- How is Laura's case like it? The case asks for materials "describing this potential contribution", which is the
  facility contribution. Her ten owned payments are the "running costs covered" that make the rest worth funding.
- How is it not? No real text found opens with the founder's operating commitment and then states a facility range.
  The judge's order is untested.
- What Caplet takes: claim 3 changes. Operations come first only as a one-clause credential with dates, and the
  facility contribution follows in the same opening paragraph.

**5. In Taiwan, other money must be named, and companies budget by date and amount (rows 11-16).**
- What is it? Taiwan's grant rules require every funder and amount to be listed (concealment means the grant is
  revoked). The national arts foundation weighs the applicant's finances and other funding, and refuses exclusively
  sponsored plans. Firms want a written plan, check the amount and timing against their budget, and weigh who else has
  backed the project. Public subsidies are annual and can be cut, and even pledged corporate money can slip a year.
- Did it work? These are current rules (2021-2026), plus a 2007 survey of 242 large firms.
- How is Laura's case like it? Her figure will be copied into Taiwanese funding tables. Its date is set by a Treasury
  maturity, and her ten payments are fixed while public money is not.
- How is it not? The survey is old and covers sponsorship with returns, not gifts. No Taiwanese corporate foundation's
  own criteria were found.
- What Caplet takes: one range, in one stated currency, the same every time. Her money is the first named source,
  others will be listed with their amounts, and no exclusive partner is sought.

**6. One paragraph that works alone (rows 7, 27, 30, 31, 37).**
- What is it? Funders' first reads are short: Wales caps a first outline at 150 words, guides say the ask cannot wait
  for page two, and real lead paragraphs about committed money run 74-123 words.
- Did it work? Good Hart's 77-word paragraph holds owned money, conditional money, its condition, its date and
  progress.
- How is Laura's case like it? The judge's "thirty seconds" is that paragraph.
- How is it not? Laura's passage must also carry what cannot change, proof and the kept half, which a challenge page
  does not.
- What Caplet takes: about 250 words in all, with an opening paragraph of about 75-125 words that a funder could stop
  after (see the length guidance in (c)).

**When (Sydney time first).**

  the no-prose rule.

  zones, or test a US reader on Wed 25 Nov with the Stage 1 list only.

**Stage 1 (10 minutes, checklist only).**
1. "Here are twelve things Laura could tell a co-sponsor about her own money. Which would you need before agreeing to
   meet her?" Record the items marked "need", "nice" and "no".
2. "What is missing?" Record verbatim.
3. "In what order would you want them?" Record the reader's first four items.

**Stage 2 (15-20 minutes, the drafted passage on paper, Stop Here marks after each paragraph).**
1. Cold read of the opening paragraph only, timed (record seconds). Then: "Would you take the meeting? Why or why not?"
   This is the one yes-or-no question. Record yes, no or maybe, and the reason verbatim.
2. Paraphrase at mark 1, with no correcting (row 35). "In your own words: what has Laura already done, how much is she
   offering, when, how sure is it, and what is she asking of you?" Score each of the five as right, partly right or
   wrong against the intended meaning.
3. Read on to marks 2 and 3, putting a plus or minus beside any line (row 36). At each mark: "What would you do next if
   this were on your desk?" and "What would your finance office ask to see?"
4. After the whole passage:
   - "What would make you doubt her?"
   - "What do you think the money she keeps back is for?"
   - "Would a figure for it change your view?" (This is the test for checklist item 9: two readers asking for a size
     means the report gives one, from NUMBERS 8f, MODEL.)
   - "Was it too long, about right or too short?"
   - "Which word or number did you not trust?"
5. Order check: give one reader the opening with operations first, another the opening with the facility first (same
   words, reordered). Record which reader paraphrases the contribution correctly and which says yes to the meeting.

**What to record (one sheet per reader).**
- Role and experience.

- The five paraphrase scores.
- Plus and minus marks by line.
- The finance-office answer.
- The doubt answer.
- The kept-half answer.
