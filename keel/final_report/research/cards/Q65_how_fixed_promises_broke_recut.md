## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP for property/casualty insurers; CHANGE for life insurers.**
  - **Property/casualty.** A.M. Best (1969-2002) found a primary cause for 562 of 871 impaired insurers:
    - deficient loss reserves or inadequate pricing 37.2%;
    - rapid growth 17.3%;
    - alleged fraud 8.5%.
    - It was 88% of the cases in 2002 alone.
    - The IAIS, on Best's data to 2009, puts it at 38.1%.
  - **Life and health.**
    - The IAIS reads Best's 1969-2009 data as "deficient loss provisioning and inadequate pricing – the most important causes in both segments", with investment losses "clearly more important" for life insurers.
    - Since 2000 the leading named causes of Best's 198 life/health impairments are different: Affordable Care Act co-ops 19, fraud 15, rapid growth 13.
  - **Europe.** Of 20 EU insurers wound up in 1996-2001 (17 of them non-life), underpricing and mispricing caused 8, assets 2, reinsurance 1, and 6 had several causes (Sharma). Under every one of the 21 case studies sat poor management.
  - **Laura's form of it.** The ten as bought in January 2027 are sized at forward rates. They are $54,595 short if cash waiting earns nothing ($55,920 with the bill fund's fee; NUMBERS 8h). The extra Treasuries ($35,894, January 2028) close the gap. After that the ten are paid even if every waiting dollar earns nothing and pays the fee, and they are 111.4% funded at forwards on 1 Jan 2033.
  - So under-reserving is carried for one year. It is the same failure as the payer-in: the 2028 deposit buys the extra Treasuries.
  - **Judge's note:** insurers fail most often not because their assets crash but because they promised more than they set aside. Laura's reserve is sized on the one assumption that cannot flatter it: every waiting dollar earns nothing and still pays the fund's fee.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. It began as a time mismatch; it became a credit and fraud crisis because the insolvent were left open.**
  - **Phase 1, 1979-82.**
    - Industry tangible net worth fell from 5.3% of assets (1980) to 0.5-0.6% (1982).
    - At the end of 1982, 415 S&Ls holding $220bn of assets (13% of S&Ls, 32% of assets) were insolvent on tangible net worth.
    - Closing them then would have cost about $25bn, four times the insurer's $6.3bn of reserves.
    - Officials thought the losses were only "on paper".
  - **Phase 2, 1982-89.**
    - Net worth rules were cut (5% to 4% to 3%), and the insolvent were allowed to grow and "substitute credit risk for interest-rate risk".
    - Development loans were "most likely the principal cause for thrift failures after 1982" (FDIC).
  - **The bill.** 1,043 thrifts with $519bn of assets failed in 1986-95. The cost was $153bn, of which taxpayers paid $124bn.
  - **Fraud.** The Commission judged that fraud "probably accounted for only 10 to 15 percent of total losses", and that fraud "was not the cause".
  - **Laura's time gap runs the safe way.** Each Treasury pays half a month to 10.5 months before its payment (NUMBERS 8h). Money waits for the payment; the payment never waits for money. The cost of waiting is counted at zero in the standard.
  - **Judge's note:** in 1982 officials called the S&L losses "only on paper"; they became real because deposits could leave. Laura's price falls really are only on paper, because nothing she owes can be called before its date.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with the time it took.**
  - Executive Life was conserved on 11 Apr 1991 with $8.8bn of assets.
  - Its 44,000 pension annuitants were paid 70% for almost 13 months (GAO 1993; card Q2a row 9).
  - Aurora took over the policies in September 1993.
  - Distributions ran to 2007. Holders recovered 86% (Auditor) or 90% (Department) of their policy values.
  - The estate closed on 15 Aug 2022, 31 years after the seizure (NOLHGA).
  - Mutual Benefit Life, failed the same year, was in rehabilitation from July 1991 and dissolved in June 2001.
  - **Judge's note:** a fixed annuity from a failed insurer took two years to restart and 16 years to settle. A Treasury held to its date takes zero.
- **Claim 4: KEEP, sized both ways. Segregation turned broker failures into delays. Theft turned them into losses.**
  - **Delays only.**
    - Lehman's US customers: days (card Q2a row 22).
    - MF Global: all customers 100%, $6.7bn to 26,000 of them, with the final distribution in April 2014.
  - **Losses.**
    - Madoff: statements showed $64.8bn that never existed. $15.509bn has been recovered (to 23 Oct 2026), or 72.015% of each allowed claim after 17 distributions.
    - Peregrine: more than $215m taken from more than 24,000 customers over two decades, hidden by bank statements made "using Photoshop and inkjet printers". Customers "lost most".
  - **The rules.**
    - Rule 15c3-3 makes a broker keep "possession or control" of customers' fully paid securities and a "Special Reserve Bank Account for the Exclusive Benefit of Customers", computed weekly.
    - SIPC covers $500,000 a customer, $250,000 of it cash (store Q26-022).
    - The 1940 Act s.17(f) puts a fund's assets with a bank, an exchange member or the fund itself under SEC rules, never with the adviser.
  - **The fund sponsor.** No case was found of an ETF holder losing value because the sponsor failed (UNVERIFIED absence). What is documented is the fund board's power to end, extend or merge a fund (Q36 rows).
- **Claim 5: KILL "none of the first two"; rebuild the map as nine patterns (row 38).**
  - **Leverage and collateral: none.** Card Q2a stands.
  - **Someone else's promise: one, chosen and named.** The ten and the floor rest on the US Treasury's promise. The IPS names that promise itself ("barring a US default"), and a Treasury default is its reason 2.
  - **The keepers.** Around that promise stand keepers who promise nothing about value: the iShares funds (adviser BlackRock Fund Advisors; each fund a series of iShares Trust with its own board), the funds' custodian bank, and Laura's broker. Their failures cost time where assets were kept apart and money where they were stolen.
  - **The largest unsized exposure is custody.** Everything sits at one broker: $589,655 at the end (NUMBERS 8k) against SIPC's $500,000.
  - **Judge's note:** apart from one 1979 delay, every broken promise read here broke somewhere between the Treasury's payment and the person owed, or before the money was ever in Treasuries.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 12 (S&L losses "only on paper").** Part 3a or 3e. The single sharpest aha: the S&Ls' losses became real because depositors could leave. Laura's price falls stay on paper because nothing she owes can be called before its date.
2. **Rows 1 and 9 (under-reserving).** Part 3a. Wharton asks how certain the commitment is. The commonest way an insurer breaks is setting aside too little. Laura's reserve is sized on cash waiting earning nothing and still paying the fee, and the one year when it is not is named (until January 2028).
3. **Row 38 (the nine-pattern map).** Part 3e and Table 3. It does the job Wharton gives 3e ("assumptions and limits"): it states each exposure with a case and a number, and gives the cold readers' three patterns their place.
4. **Row 32 (custody: $589,655 at one broker against $500,000).** Part 3e and Table 3. This is the plan's largest unsized exposure, and stating it beats a judge finding it. History gives the honest size: days or years of delay where assets were kept apart, and losses only through theft.
5. **Row 37 (where the losses sat).** Part 3a. One true sentence of certainty without over-claiming: every broken promise read here broke between the Treasury's payment and the person owed, or before the money was in Treasuries.
