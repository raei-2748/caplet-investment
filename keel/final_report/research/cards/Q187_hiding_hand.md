## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. Hirschman's own conditions: ignorance pays only where there is another way round, someone stuck with the project, and money to finish

- (a) Read whole, Hirschman's 1967 essay says that ignorance of a project's difficulty paid off only when three things held: there turned out to be another way round the trouble (when the bamboo feeding a Pakistani paper mill flowered and died, the mill turned to village bamboo, other timber and new species), the people who had started were "caught" by the money, time and prestige they had already spent, and, in his summary of his source John Sawyer, success came "once the necessary funds were secured and the project was brought to completion".
- (b) The ten payments fail the first and third conditions under the case's own rules, because each is a fixed $50,000 that may not rely on "co-sponsors, grants, program fees, or other outside funding" (case l.90-92); a figure Laura states fails the second, because a co-sponsor misled by it is not stuck and can withdraw (case l.114-115); and the building meets all three, because its design can change, Laura as its founder will be stuck with it, and her flexibility is money kept back to finish it.
- (c) It breaks on the odds: Hirschman himself wrote that "one has to be rather lucky", and Flyvbjerg and Sunstein found the rescue in 5 of the 18 large buildings they could test, so even where all three conditions hold the building cannot count on creativity, which is why the third condition is met by money set aside in January 2028 and not by hope.
- **Decision it serves:** the split between promises owned in Treasuries and her flexibility (IPS l.11, l.15); stating only owned figures.
- **Report part:** 3c (why ignorance is refused for every promise); 3b (what her flexibility is for).
- **Source:** Hirschman (1967), parts I-III and VI; case l.90-92 and l.114-115; Flyvbjerg and Sunstein (2016), Table 2. Keel arithmetic: 28% of 18 is 5.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge who knows the Hiding Hand knows it as a slogan for starting in ignorance; that Hirschman's own essay sets these conditions, and that the case's rules switch two of them off for the payments, is new.

### Idea 2. The plan is not a Hiding Hand: it is what Hirschman hoped would replace one

- (a) Hirschman called the Hiding Hand a "crutch" for people who do not yet trust their own creativity, and hoped they would drop it quickly ("the shorter the transition and the faster the learning, the better") and learn to "differentiate between acceptable and non-acceptable risk".
- (b) Caplet's plan hides nothing from Laura or her co-sponsors, since the case itself says the building's cost "has not been determined", and it draws Hirschman's line in the open: it takes the building's risk in the half of the shares promised to no one ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong", TNA l.23) and takes none with the payments or any figure she states (IPS l.19).
- (c) It breaks on who does the learning: Hirschman's learners were governments that could get into trouble, get out, and do better on the next project, while a promise Laura breaks to a co-sponsor costs her credibility (case l.114), so the only place she can afford Hirschman's kind of learning is her flexibility.
- **Decision it serves:** the closing line, "With these promises, ours is to take none" (IPS l.19), and the split it closes.
- **Report part:** 3e (what the plan accepts and what it refuses), with one clause available to 3.0.
- **Source:** Hirschman (1967), parts III, V-VI; case l.164; TNA l.23; IPS l.19.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Read through Hirschman, the IPS's last line stops being a slogan and becomes the step he said planners should take once they no longer need to be tricked into acting.

### Idea 3. When to state: as soon as each end is owned, because ten owned years of operations are Hirschman's own cure for trouble that shows early

- (a) Hirschman warned that trouble which shows early gets projects dropped (the East African Groundnut Scheme, a quick-yielding farm project, was abandoned within a few years, while the Owen Falls hydroelectric station, a permanent structure with the same early money troubles, was kept going and found new customers), and his remedy was a backer "having a long-term commitment to the success of the projects", starting small "in the style of a pilot project".
- (b) By January 2028 Laura owns all ten years of the residency's operations, to 2042, and no building cost can reach them (a building overrun is not one of IPS l.17's two reasons to change the plan), so she is that backer, and she can show the architect and co-sponsors her limit as soon as it is owned (the bottom from January 2028, the top from the move on 1 January 2031) without the residency being given up when the building's real cost appears.
- (c) It breaks on the word "owned": a top given to an architect before the move is pencil, and the move would buy less than a top stated a year early in 10.6% of house paths (NUMBERS 4), so a design drawn to it could meet the shortfall only after the architect was stuck with it, which would make the plan its own malevolent Hiding Hand; the order has to be own, then state, then design.
- **Decision it serves:** the 2031 timing and the move on 1 January 2031 (NUMBERS 8r); stating only owned figures (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3c (when each end can be stated); 3d (what an owned figure lets the residency do).
- **Source:** Hirschman (1967), part III; NUMBERS 4 ("When each end is fixed"; "Why nothing is stated before the move"), 8u (the payments' break set); CONCEIT 3c ("inked in January 2028").
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The store's case for stating early rests on the co-sponsors (SD-1, SD-3) and the architect (Q172 idea 4); this adds the reason stating early cannot hurt the residency itself, from the author most quoted on the other side.

### Idea 4 (the best companion). The other side of "caught": the founder is the one who throws good money after bad, so the plan sets her limit before she is caught

- (a) Hirschman's rescue works because people who have already spent money and prestige on a project fight to save it, and in 1976 Barry Staw showed the same force going wrong: in a role-play, business students who had chosen a failing investment themselves put the most money back into it, while for students who had inherited the choice the bad news made no significant difference.
- (b) In the residency the person most caught will be Laura, its founder, so the plan decides in advance what being caught can reach: her flexibility, which IPS l.15 names first for "building costs that overrun", and never the ten payments, because a building overrun is not one of the IPS's two reasons to change the plan (l.17).
- (c) It breaks because Staw's students moved play money in a classroom, and because the fence around the payments is a written rule, not a lock (Treasuries can be sold), so the break teaches that her flexibility is exactly the money Hirschman's rescue and Staw's escalation both reach for, and the limit on everything else holds only while Laura keeps to her own policy.
- **Decision it serves:** IPS l.15 (her flexibility's first named use) and l.17 (only two reasons to change the plan).
- **Report part:** 3b; 3e.
- **Source:** Staw (1976); Sleesman, Conlon, McNamara and Miles (2012); Hirschman (1967), part III.
- **Pedigree or aha:** escalation of commitment is pedigree to anyone who has read management research; the aha is that Hirschman's rescue and Staw's escalation are one behaviour seen from two ends, and the plan prices it before 2031.

### Best companion, in one line

Staw (1976) is the companion: it shows Hirschman's "caught" from the opposite side and predicts something checkable in the plan, that the founder is the person most likely to pour more in, which the IPS's own words limit (l.15, l.17). The runner-up, Harrington, Morgenstern and Nelson (1999), shows where the benevolent pattern does have a record (a fixed requirement with the means left free: costs came in under the estimate for 12 of 25 rules and over for 6), which is the shape of an owned figure handed to an architect (row 17).

### What the fuller reading says, in one paragraph (for the lead, not prose)

**When does not knowing help, and when does it ruin?** Hirschman's own answer, read whole, is narrower than his reputation: ignorance helps only where there is another way round the trouble, where the people who started are stuck with the project when the trouble shows (and the trouble does not show too early), and where money can be found to finish; he called it a crutch, said one must be "rather lucky", and conceded that survival is not vindication. Flyvbjerg and Sunstein add the measured odds (the rescue in about one project in five, about one building in four) and the reason ignorance ruins: it hides "the barriers to creativity itself", most of all where creativity "cannot possibly save the day". Laura's ten payments and any figure she states are that case: a fixed $50,000 has no other way round and may not use outside money, and a misled co-sponsor is not stuck but leaves. **When to state:** Hirschman's timing rule (hide the limit until people are stuck) is exactly what the case forbids for her figure, and his cure for early trouble (a backer with a long-term commitment) is what her ten owned years of operations make her; so each end can be stated as soon as it is owned (the bottom from January 2028, the top from the move), never before, and the one person the plan must protect from being caught is Laura herself, which IPS l.15 and l.17 do by naming in advance the only money an overrun can reach.

## Best three for the report
