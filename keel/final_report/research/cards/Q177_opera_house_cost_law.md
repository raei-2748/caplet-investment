# Q177. Why did the New South Wales Parliament write a cost cap into the Opera House Act, how did the cap fall, who paid the gap, and what does that say about stating a cost against stating money already owned?

## The parallel's structure, tested

The parent card's relation was: a promise about a building's cost failed even in statute; a promise about money owned cannot fail that way. Read whole, the record keeps the relation and changes its cause. The clause did not fail because Parliament broke a solemn vow. It failed because a figure about other people's prices was written by a body that could rewrite it, and it was rewritten each time the prices moved. The Act's own text shows a second relation the parent card did not have: the order. Section 1(2) deems the sanction to have begun on 1 January 1957 and s 5 ratifies contracts already signed, so the law came after the commitment. Laura's order is the reverse: she owns first and states second.

## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. The strongest words were the cheapest: "under any circumstances" was the template, and its author could rewrite it

- (a) The sentence that said the Opera House's estimated cost "shall not, under any circumstances, be exceeded by more than ten per centum" was not written for the Opera House: the same words stand in New South Wales Acts for a country railway (1923), a pedestrian subway costing £23,000 (1950), an £11 million dam (1951) and a harbour wall later in 1960, and they grew from a 1912 rule that a Minister's contracts must not pass the estimate by more than ten per cent.
- (b) The clause worked as a tripwire, not a vow: every larger rise had to go back to Parliament, and Parliament, which could rewrite its own Act, raised the figure in 1963, replaced the clause in 1966 and set a new maximum in 1969, so the words had nothing behind them, while Laura's stated figure needs no strong words because Treasuries she owns stand behind it.
- (c) It breaks because the tripwire did its job (each rise did go back to Parliament, which is why the architectural writer Peter Murray says the Act made the building "a political football"), and the break teaches that a written limit on a cost cannot stop the cost; it only decides who must answer for it, which for Laura's building is the residency and its funders, not her stated figure.
- **Decision it serves:** stating only money owned (IPS l.15); the range's wording in 3c.
- **Report part:** 3c (one clause); 3e (the plan states money, never a cost).
- **Source:** Sydney Opera House Act 1960, s 2(4), and the bill as first printed (22 March 1960); Wyalong towards Condobolin Railway Act 1923 s 4; College Street Pedestrian Subway Construction Act 1950 s 4; Blowering Dam Act 1951 s 4; Evans Head Harbour Works Act 1960 s 4; Public Works Act 1912 ss 34, 37; Acts 1963 No 6, 1966 No 45, 1969 No 23; Murray (2004).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha, and a correction. No finance judge knows that the Opera House's "under any circumstances" was boilerplate shared with a £23,000 subway. It turns the parent card's line ("not even an Act of Parliament could hold a cost") into a sharper one: the strength of a promise is in what pays it, not in its words.

### Idea 2. The law spent nine years learning to stop stating a cost; Laura starts where it stopped, one step further on

- (a) In 1960 and again in 1963 the Act stated an estimated cost that "shall not ... be exceeded"; in 1966 it stopped estimating and only authorised spending up to $37.5 million "towards" the building; in 1969 it set "the maximum amount to be expended" at $85 million, which was the latest estimate; and in 1974 the Minister announced a final cost of $102 million.
- (b) Laura's plan starts where the law ended, because it states money and never what the building will cost, and it takes the step the law never took, because Parliament's $85 million was permission to spend lottery money not yet raised (the lottery ran until 1986), while every figure Laura states is money already in Treasuries she owns.
- (c) It breaks because a government can keep paying after its figure fails (from 1976 the law paid the building "out of money provided by Parliament" and swept the lottery account into general revenue), and Laura cannot, which is why her plan needs both of its halves: a stated figure that is money owned, and money promised to no one for whatever the building costs beyond it.
- **Decision it serves:** the range stated only once owned; her flexibility kept outside it (IPS l.15).
- **Report part:** 3c; 3b.
- **Source:** Acts 1960 No 29, 1963 No 6, 1966 No 45 (and its explanatory note), 1969 No 23, 1975 No 89 (new s 4A and s 6(3)); Murray (2004) for the 1967 estimate of $85 million; Museums of History NSW (n.d.) for May 1974's $102 million; Muhlen-Schulte (2024) for the lottery to 1986.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. The statute's own wording moves from "estimated cost" to "maximum amount to be expended": the law itself discovered, over nine years, that it could limit money but not a cost. Nobody has told that as a lesson about promising.

### Idea 3. The Opera House had the money; its stated figure stopped it

- (a) By mid-1965 the Opera House's lottery account held £5,151,288 and was taking in about £3 million a year, yet first one Minister and then the next would not go back to Parliament to raise the legal figure, so the second took control of the architect's payments, and Utzon resigned in February 1966.
- (b) What failed was the figure, not the cash, because the only room the law allowed (ten per cent) sat above an estimate and inside the stated figure; Laura's plan puts its room the other way round: about 2% under each stated figure, below money she owns (the room under the top), and her room for the building, her flexibility, outside every figure she states, so an overrun can be met without any stated figure changing.
- (c) It breaks because the Opera House's money was a stream of ticket sales rather than money owned, and because her flexibility is finite (with anything above the top it covers a rise of about 25% in a building budget set on her top, if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed, NUMBERS 8u), so room outside the figure answers the ordinary overrun while a far larger one would still fall on the building's size, funders or timetable.
- **Decision it serves:** half the shares "promised to no one" (IPS l.15), and the 0.5% margin and rounding under each stated end (WORDS).
- **Report part:** 3b (where her room sits); 3c (the room under the top, one clause).
- **Source:** Murray (2004) ("the lottery income was keeping pace with costs"; the 1964-65 balance; Ryan and Hughes unwilling to return to Parliament); Acts 1963 No 6 and 1966 No 45; Museums of History NSW (n.d.) for the resignation date; WORDS and NUMBERS 8u for the plan's figures.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Everyone knows the Opera House "ran over budget"; almost nobody knows its account was in credit when the crisis came. A figure set below the need, with no room outside it, broke the project while the money was there.

### Idea 4 (the companion). Two "shall not"s in one fortnight: the one that held asked nothing of the world

- (a) On 19 April 1960 the Opera House Act's "shall not, under any circumstances" became law, and on 20 April the same Parliament sat jointly on a bill to abolish its upper house, which a 1929 section said "shall not be abolished" without a referendum; the courts had upheld that section in 1930-32, and in April 1961 the voters refused abolition by 1,089,193 votes to 802,512.
- (b) The upper-house section held because it governed only an act of Parliament's own (presenting a bill for assent) and could be changed only with the voters' consent, while the cost clause governed prices nobody in Parliament set and could be changed by Parliament alone; Laura's stated figure has the first property, because it asks nothing of builders or markets, only that she keeps holding Treasuries that pay it.
- (c) It breaks because Laura's own rules (only two reasons to change the plan, IPS l.17) are self-made, like the cost clause, so her figure holds not because a rule binds her but because nothing outside her has to behave for it to be paid; and that predicts something checkable which does hold: in the register's table of how each promise breaks (NUMBERS 8u), no building price appears under the ten payments, the stated bottom or the stated top, and building costs appear only under her flexibility, which "cannot break a promise".
- **Decision it serves:** stating only money owned; the two lists kept apart (the ways a promise breaks against IPS l.17's two reasons, NUMBERS 8u).
- **Report part:** 3e (what can break a stated figure, and what cannot); 3c.
- **Source:** Constitution Act 1902 (NSW) s 7A as quoted in *Trethowan* (1931, 1932); *NSW Legislative Council Practice* (2nd ed.), ch. 2; Dicey (1915), ch. 1; Act 1960 No 29; NUMBERS 8u (checked Thu 19 Nov 2026: the word "building" appears in no break row but the flexibility row).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for a lawyer (Dicey and *Trethowan* are classics), aha for a finance judge. Its use is the test, not the colour: a limit holds when it governs only its maker's own act; a limit on someone else's prices is a guess with a deadline.

### What the whole record says, in one paragraph (for the lead, not prose)

**Why Parliament wrote it:** because every Act that sanctioned a public work in New South Wales carried that sentence, and because the 1960 Act's purpose was control (it moved the work under the Minister for Public Works and named the architect and engineer in statute); the cap made each rise a matter for Parliament. **What ministers said:** the 1960 debate was not reached; in March 1963 the Minister carrying the new figure told the Assembly the cost "could exceed" it and called it "unreasonable" to say it would not, while the Act he carried kept "under any circumstances". **How it fell:** 1963 (£12.5 million, room used up the day it passed), 1965 (pounds to dollars only), 1966 (an authority to spend $37.5 million "towards" the work, the cost words gone), 1969 ($85 million maximum, equal to the estimate), 1974 ($102 million spent). **Who paid:** ticket buyers until 1986; the State's general budget from 1976; the architect (payments controlled, resignation) and the design (the Schedule's "generally in accordance with the design prepared by Joern Utzon" rewritten in 1966); and, from 1965, the lottery account paid the State's budget too. **The lesson for Laura:** a stated cost is a guess that someone must later answer for; a stated sum of money owned needs no one to answer for it. She states the second kind only, and keeps her room for the first kind outside every figure she states.

## Best three for the report

1. **Rows 1-3 with row 12 (idea 1), 3c.** The words "shall not, under any circumstances" were the template of every New South Wales public-works Act since at least 1923, and the Minister who carried the 1963 figure told the Assembly it "could" be exceeded. A judge who knows the overrun learns that the promise had nothing behind it but its words; Laura's figure has no strong words and needs none, because money she owns pays it.
2. **Row 24 with rows 18, 20 and 21 (idea 2), 3c.** Over nine years the statute itself moved from "estimated cost ... shall not ... be exceeded" to "the maximum amount to be expended", and then, in 1975, to "money provided by Parliament". The law learned to limit money rather than a cost but never got to money already owned; Laura's plan starts there. It is the parent card's aha, made true to the record.
3. **Rows 15 and 26 (idea 3), 3b.** The Opera House's account was in credit when the crisis came: the stated figure, set below the need with no room outside it, broke the project while the money was there. Laura's plan keeps its room the other way round: a little under each figure she states, below money she owns, and her flexibility outside every figure.
