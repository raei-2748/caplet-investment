# Q115. Is moving half the shares into Treasuries on one day better or worse than averaging out over a year?

- **Date:** Tue 17 Nov 2026, evening, Sydney. Sources read Tue 17 Nov 2026.
- **Report parts:**

  - 3e, what to watch as 2031 approaches: the date, never a share-price level.
  - Table 3, row "Trades in 2027, 2028, 2031" ("Changes: the date, never past the 2031 meetings").
  - MOVE2031 throughout. Most rows are for the store only.
- Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.

- **Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):**
  1. "A one-day move takes risk off sooner, so on average it beats averaging out, at the price of more regret if that day is a bad one."
  2. "Averaging out would leave part of the top of the range not yet owned for longer, which the IPS's rule ('whatever figure you choose is money you already own') does not allow before the meetings."
  3. "A funded-level trigger (move when the shares reach a level) would add a reason to change the plan beyond the IPS's two (a missed deposit, a Treasury default)" (IPS l.17).
- **Problem class:** when to carry out a switch from shares to safe assets: one trade on one day, a set of equal trades over a period, or a trade when a level is reached.
  - **Field that solved it first:** portfolio theory on dollar-cost averaging. Constantinides (1979) showed that a fixed schedule is beaten by a policy that can use later information. Rozeff (1994) did the mean-variance version.
  - **Borrowed from other fields:**
    - pension de-risking (funding-level triggers);
    - target-date fund design ("to" and "through" glide paths);
    - grain marketing (selling the crop in equal parts);
    - public share sales (trading plans).
- **The Keel arithmetic in this card.** A stylised model, not register figures:
  - only the timing of the moved half varies;
  - the floor is held at the register's central $98,874 of zero-interest value (NUMBERS 4), and yields at today's forwards;
  - a part moved t years before 1 Jan 2031 earns about 1.070113 + 0.0483 t per dollar at zero interest;
  - the top is less 0.5% and rounded down to $5,000;
  - VT follows the house model's 7.5% and 17.88% (200,000 paths, seed 2031) or US stocks with dividends (Shiller monthly, 1,782 windows bought Jan 1871 to Jun 2019).
  - The scripts were run from the scratchpad and are not in the repo: `averaging.py`, `averaging2.py`, `trigger.py`.
  - Check: its central path gives $118,072, the register's unrounded top. Its US-history stated top for a 1 January move matches 8r's $110,000 / $115,000 / $130,000.
  - Its house-model 1-in-20 low ($110,000) is above the register's ($95,000), because the register also moves the floor and yields.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE. "Takes risk off sooner" is backwards for any averaging the IPS allows. The rest holds, and the gap is small.**
  - **The deadline.** l.15 moves the half "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031". The case lets the first meeting come at the start of 2031, so the register moves on 1 January (8r, point 1). Any averaging out must therefore end by then: twelve monthly sales across 2030, say.
  - **The flip.** Against that schedule, the one-day move on 1 January 2031 keeps the half in shares longest: on average 5.5 months longer. It takes risk off later, not sooner.
  - **The mirror of the lump-sum result.** Lump sum beats averaging in because the money spends longest in shares. Selling to a deadline, the same premium favours the trade that waits:
    - Vanguard (2012): lump sum won in 67% of rolling ten-year periods (US 60/40, 1926-2011), by 2.3% on average. Vanguard (2023): 68% (MSCI World, 1976-2022).
    - Rozeff (1994): lump sum won "in 40 of 65 years" on the S&P 500.
    - Keel arithmetic: the one-day move gives the higher unrounded top in 54.6% of house futures and 63.3% of US windows since 1871. The average gain is $407 (house) and $526 (history), under a tenth of one $5,000 step. The stated top is the same in 68.7% and 71.0%.
  - **The regret.** The one-day move states a step lower than averaging out in 12.0% of house futures and 9.4% of US windows; two steps lower in none of the house futures and in 0.1% of windows. It states a step higher in 19.2% and 19.6%.
    - Where VT falls more than 10% in 2030 (16.1% of house futures), the one-day move states the lower top in 38.0% of them.
    - Named case: VT bought January 2006, moved January 2009. One day states $105,000; averaging out over 2008 states $110,000.
  - **The cost of the steadier top.** Averaging over 2030 narrows the top's spread by about 13% (standard deviation $6,553 to $5,695, house).
    - Rozeff's own answer is that "risk reduction" is done "more effectively by lowering the fraction of funds invested in the risky asset and investing them all at once".
    - On Laura's numbers: moving 11/24 of the half on 1 Jan 2030 and the rest on 1 Jan 2031 gives the same mean as monthly averaging (-$406 against -$407) with a narrower spread ($5,608 against $5,695; history $5,092 against $5,184).
    - So averaging is a weak dial, and the difference is tiny either way.
  - **What to keep in words:** one day on 1 January 2031 keeps the half in shares as long as the IPS allows. On average it states a slightly higher top. In about one future in ten it states one $5,000 step less than spreading the sale across 2030 would have.
  - **Judge's note:** when you sell to a deadline, the lump-sum rule turns round. The single trade is the one that waits, and the averaging schedule is the one that takes risk off early.
- **Claim 2: KILL as worded. l.15 sets a deadline, not a method.**
  - **Averaging inside the deadline.** A schedule that ends by 1 January 2031 owns each part of the top sooner, not later. When the last part moves, the whole top is owned, exactly as with one day. Such a schedule meets l.15.
  - **What l.15 does rule out.** Any schedule that runs into the meeting year, where the top cannot be stated until the last sale. Monthly sales from 1 Jan to 1 Dec 2031 would fix the top only in December.
    - Keel arithmetic: that schedule adds $401 to the mean top (house) and widens its spread to $7,024.
    - It is 8r's later-date bet with twelve dates instead of one: a later date lowers the stated top in about 1 house path in 8 (April) or 1 in 6 (July).
  - **Two reasons the register gives that hold** (8r points 1-3):
    - a move at the start of January is the only one before every possible first meeting;
    - a later date gains almost nothing and adds a bet.
  - **One the claim missed.** The range rule is written for one day ("the moved half's on the same basis on the move day", NUMBERS 4). Averaging would need the rule restated as a sum of twelve purchases, each at its own yield.
  - **Wording.** Never say "not yet owned for longer" or "not yet owned in" a share of paths. Before the move no part of the top is owned in any path (NUMBERS 4, coherence reader 7).
  - **Judge's note:** the IPS says when the half must be in Treasuries, not how to get it there. One day is a choice inside l.15, made for one figure worked out once, not something the rule forces.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, sharpened.**
  - **Not a third reason.** A trigger written into a plan in advance is a rule, not a reason to change it. So it is not literally a third item on l.17's list.
  - **But a rule the frozen IPS lacks.** It would make a market move the reason to act. l.15 sets a date. l.17 says "A fall in the market is not one of them", and a rise is a market move too.
  - **Pensions use triggers widely.** At the end of 2016, 30% of US corporate sponsors had a formal de-risking glide path, and 72% of those were based on funded status alone (CEM, 2018).
  - **Aon's back-test** (750 five-year periods, 1992-2011): triggers left a higher funding level in about 70% of periods, had no effect in about 10% (never hit) and left it lower in about 20%. "In periods that are plain awful, triggers do not help, and neither do they hinder."
  - **On Laura's numbers** (Keel arithmetic): move the whole half the first month the unrounded top reaches $120,000, otherwise on 1 January 2031.
    - The trigger is hit in 58.5% of house futures.
    - The chance of stating at least $120,000 rises from 38.0% to 59.2%.
    - The 1-in-20 high falls from $131,112 to $121,595, and the mean falls by $1,092.
    - The 1-in-20 low does not move ($110,174 against $110,249).
    - A trigger buys a likelier middle figure with the upside, and does nothing for the bad end, which is the end co-sponsors need protected.
  - **Disanalogy.** A pension's liability moves every day with rates, and it de-risks toward an insurer's price at any date. Laura's top matters on one known date.
  - **Judge's note:** a trigger would have helped in about seven pension periods in ten, by capturing gains that later fell back. On Laura's plan it gives up the top's upside and leaves the bad end exactly where it was.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 24 with row 2 (3c, MOVE2031).** It turns the textbook round: the lump-sum literature says the single trade wins about two-thirds of the time because it holds shares longest. Selling to a deadline, the single trade is the one that waits. On Laura's numbers it is worth under a tenth of a step on average.
2. **Row 29 (3c).** It kills a tempting wrong reason. l.15 sets when, not how. One day is a choice for one figure worked out once (and keeps "two inkings, two dates", row 40). The case's possible January meeting fixes the end.
3. **Rows 31-32 (3e, TWOREASONS).** Pensions' own back-test and Laura's numbers agree: a level trigger catches gains that later fall back and never lifts the bad end. That answers "why no trigger?" with evidence, not with l.17 alone.
4. **Row 11 (3b or 3c, HALFKEPT).** Statman's regret cure for selling is to sell only part. The half kept already does that: a rally after the move still lifts her flexibility. Say it does this too, never that it was built for it (its purpose is flexibility, IPS l.15).
5. **Row 37 (3e or Table 3 note).** Institutions sell in tranches because of their size (15% of volume, the Lloyds plans). Laura's roughly $18,500 has no such reason, so one day costs her nothing in market impact.

## Gaps

- Constantinides (1979) and Rozeff (1994) full texts were not read (paywalled). Constantinides' argument comes from four secondary accounts that agree; Rozeff's from his SSRN abstract.
- Statman (1995) was read in abstract only; his 2018 article was read in full.
- Milevsky and Posner (2003), Trainor (2005), Thorley (1994), Williams and Bacon (1993) and Knight and Mandell (1992/93) were read only through others' summaries.
- Aon's "Do Funding Triggers Work?" was read only in excerpts; its site blocks a full fetch. The section "Does it matter how quickly you act?", the closest evidence on phasing a trigger-led sale, was not read.
- Morningstar's target-date landscape on "to" and "through" was not read; the SEC testimony describes the two without the labels.
- Basu, Byrne and Drew (2009/2011) on performance-triggered lifecycle switching: no abstract reached.
- Estrada (2014) was read through IESE's write-up only.
- No study was found of de-risking toward a single dated, stated figure. The nearest are target-date funds, pension buy-outs and fixed-date grain sales. A search of the insurance pricing literature (structured settlements, lottery annuity purchases) might find one.
- VT's daily volume, to size a one-day sale against the market, was not looked up (card Q45 has its spread).
- Tools: Consensus paper search was out of its free monthly usage, Firecrawl was out of credits, the shared WebFetch budget ran out several times, and Parallel's free tier was rate-limited, so several fetches were partial.
