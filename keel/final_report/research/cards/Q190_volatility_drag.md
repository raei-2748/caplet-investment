## The ideas, each in three plain sentences

### Idea 1. One run of years: the middle of Laura's futures grows at the compound rate, and most futures never reach the average

- (a) In 1959 Henry Latané showed that money reinvested year after year ends up, almost certainly over many years, close to what its steady compound rate predicts, so he judged an investment by that rate (the "geometric mean") and not by the plain average of its yearly returns.
- (b) Laura's shares live through one run of years, from the purchase in January 2028 to the move on 1 January 2031, so the middle of her possible futures grows at J.P. Morgan's compound 7.5% a year (the house model's median for VT), while compounding the plain average, 8.94%, gives the mean of all possible end values, which a few very good futures pull up: only about 44 in 100 three-year paths end above it (43 in 100 over five years; Keel arithmetic, lognormal on J.P. Morgan's figures).
- (c) It breaks on the length of the run: Latané himself wrote that his rule "would not apply to unique choices", and three years are one short run, so the compound rate tells the report where the middle of Laura's futures lies but not that she will land near it (half of three-year paths average between +0.9% and +14.5% a year, card Q6 row 25), which is why she states a range only once it is owned and never a forecast.
- **Decision it serves:** the central top ($115,000) and Table 2's note rest on VT's median path, J.P. Morgan's compound column.
- **Report part:** 3c (what "central" means); 3e (which figure is assumed).
- **Source:** Latané (1959), pp. 144-147 (the subgoal section and the central fact); J.P. Morgan 2027 USD matrix, row "AC World Equity 7.50 8.94 17.88"; NUMBERS section 0 ("VT median growth 7.5% a year"). Keel arithmetic below.
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree for the rule (every finance judge knows compound is below arithmetic); the aha is the plain count, that only 44 in 100 three-year futures reach the average, so a top built on 8.94% is a figure most futures miss, and that Latané fenced his own rule off from "unique choices" like Laura's.

### Idea 2. Ink has no drag: the gap between the average and the middle exists only for money she does not yet own

- (a) J.P. Morgan's world figures lose 1.44 points a year between the plain average (8.94%) and the compound rate (7.50%), because ups and downs of the same size leave money behind (a 20% rise and then a 20% fall leaves $96 of every $100), and J.P. Morgan's handbook says the gap "grows with higher volatility".
- (b) Over the three years to the move that gap is about 4% of VT's value, so on the $29,594 of VT bought in January 2028 the mean path ends about $1,500 above the middle path ($38,193 against the register's central $36,698), which through the range rule's $0.53 per dollar moves the unrounded top by about $790 (from $117,142 to about $117,930), under a sixth of one $5,000 step, so the stated top is $115,000 whichever figure is used (Keel arithmetic on NUMBERS 4).
- (c) The idea stops at the Treasuries: a Treasury held to its end pays a fixed sum, so its average outcome and its typical outcome are one number and it has no drag at all, which means the drag can move only pencil figures (the top she will be able to state, and her flexibility, about $1,460 on 1 January 2033), and the move on 1 January 2031 switches it off for the half of the shares that becomes her top.
- **Decision it serves:** the move before the 2031 meetings ("whatever figure you choose is money you already own", IPS l.15); the payments and the bottom in Treasuries (IPS l.11).
- **Report part:** 3c, with one turn available to the conceit (CONCEIT version 2): only pencil has two averages.
- **Source:** J.P. Morgan (2025, handbook FAQ); J.P. Morgan 2027 USD matrix; Chambers and Zdanowicz (2014), whose limit case is a fully diversified portfolio that "becomes a riskless asset" whose arithmetic and geometric returns are equal; NUMBERS 4 (+10.6%, $0.53 per $1, $36,698) and 8u ($28,682, 24.9%).
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. A finance judge knows volatility drag; the new step is that the plan is built so that the one number drag can blur, the share forecast, never becomes a stated figure: what she states has no ups and downs left in it, so its average and its middle are the same.

### Idea 3. A world fund raises the typical future, not the average one, and Laura lives one future

- (a) Booth and Fama (1992) showed that a mix of assets compounds faster than the average of its parts' compound rates, because mixing smooths the ups and downs without lowering the plain average, and Chambers and Zdanowicz (2014) pointed out the catch: this "does not generate higher expected wealth", it lifts only the middle of the outcomes.
- (b) That catch is what Laura needs, because her top is set on the one path she lives and the middle path is the one she is as likely to beat as to miss: in J.P. Morgan's 2027 table, Hong Kong shares have the world's plain average (8.92% against 8.94%) but compound 0.6 points a year slower (6.90% against 7.50%) because they swing more (21.27% against 17.88%), and China's domestic shares have the highest plain average of any equity row (10.84%) yet compound slower than the world (7.40%).
- (c) It breaks twice: J.P. Morgan expects Japanese shares to swing less than the world's (16.89%) and compound faster (9.10%), so the report says "less drag than most single markets", never "any"; and VT is never rebalanced, so its edge over its parts comes, in Willenbrock's (2011) words, from "the assets that perform the best" becoming "a greater fraction" of it, the same skew that left the median US stock 2.29% down over its listed life while the whole market beat Treasury bills (Bessembinder 2018).
- **Decision it serves:** one world fund of about 10,000 companies for the top (IPS l.15; TNA l.21 and l.23).
- **Report part:** 3e (the assumption is for a world fund, and why that matters for one path); one clause for 3f's copout moment.
- **Source:** Booth and Fama (1992), abstract; Chambers and Zdanowicz (2014); J.P. Morgan 2027 USD matrix (twelve equity rows read); Willenbrock (2011); Bessembinder (2018), pp. 7, 17, 33 of the accepted manuscript.
- **Pedigree or aha:** aha. Judges know diversification lowers risk; fewer know it leaves the average untouched and raises the middle, which is the outcome that matters for one path, and J.P. Morgan's own table shows it: the market with the richest average future (China's: a three-year mean 5.3% above the world's) has a poorer typical one (0.3% below; Keel arithmetic).

### Idea 4. The median cannot see a crash, so the range has two ends of different kinds

- (a) Markowitz in 1959 preferred the mean to the median for judging a portfolio, because the median "is insensitive to changes, even to unambiguously good or bad changes": the worst outcomes can be made much worse and the middle does not move.
- (b) The central top is a median, so on its own it cannot see a crash, and the plan answers Markowitz's objection with a second end of a different kind: the bottom ($95,000) is owned in Treasuries from January 2028 and holds even if VT goes to zero (NUMBERS 8p), while Table 2 shows market luck moving the top from $95,000 to $140,000.
- (c) It breaks on purpose: Markowitz was choosing one portfolio from many over a single period, where the mean rightly rewards every improvement, whereas Laura's range chooses nothing and only describes, so the report uses the median for the middle, the owned bottom for the tail, and has no use for the mean of the shares at all.
- **Decision it serves:** the range rule's two ends: the bottom set in January 2028 on the late-2032 fund alone, the top only after the move.
- **Report part:** 3c.
- **Source:** Markowitz (1959), chapter III, p. 55 (archive.org text of the 1970 printing); NUMBERS 4 and 8p.
- **Pedigree or aha:** niche pedigree. The line is in the founding book of portfolio theory and few judges will have read p. 55; it explains why a single central figure would not do.

### Idea 5. The right column for the right job: a plan that never blends needs no portfolio return

- (a) J.P. Morgan's methodology handbook says the compound return is "most relevant to investors for a single asset", and that the plain average should be used only to blend assets into a portfolio, because plain averages add up across a mix and compound rates do not.
- (b) Laura's plan never blends: each dollar has one job (the ten payments, the bottom, the top, her flexibility), each job is valued on its own holding, and only one holding, VT, needs a forecast, so the report quotes one figure for one asset in exactly the column J.P. Morgan says fits, and never needs an expected return for "the portfolio".
- (c) It breaks on the horizon: J.P. Morgan built the figure for "a 10- to 15-year investment horizon" (card Q6 row 1), while VT's run to the move is three years, so the right column does not turn it into a forecast for 2031, and the report quotes it as an assumption, never as what VT will do.
- **Decision it serves:** Table 2's note and 3e's one share assumption; the split of every dollar by job (IPS l.11-15).
- **Report part:** 3e.
- **Source:** J.P. Morgan (2025), Methodology Handbook, FAQ "What is the difference between arithmetic and geometric (compound) return?"; J.P. Morgan LTCMA web page (horizon).
- **Pedigree or aha:** pedigree with a point: many plans quote a blended expected return; this one has none to quote, because nothing in it is blended.

## Best three for the report

1. **Rows 13, 8 and 31 (Idea 2), ink has no drag:** a judge who knows that 7.5% and 8.94% are two answers to one question sees why the plan never has to choose between them for anything Laura states: only money not yet owned has two averages, and the move ends that for her top.
2. **Rows 16-18 (Idea 3), a world fund raises the typical future, not the average:** J.P. Morgan's own table shows the market with the richest average future (China's) having a slower typical one, and Hong Kong with the world's average compounding 0.6 points a year slower, so one world fund is the choice that serves a single path, and the team's "price" of picking winners gets a number.
3. **Rows 10 and 2 (Idea 1), only about 44 in 100 three-year futures reach the average:** a top built on the plain average would be a figure most futures miss, and Latané, who founded the compound-rate rule, wrote that it "would not apply to unique choices" like Laura's single run to 2031, which is why she states a range only once owned.

## Keel arithmetic (method, so the figures can be re-run)

Lognormal yearly returns. From J.P. Morgan's plain average A = 8.94% and simple spread S = 17.88%: log variance s² = ln(1 + S²/(1+A)²) = 0.02658 (s = 16.30%); middle growth (1+A)/exp(s²/2) - 1 = 7.50%. Mean end value per $1 after T years (1+A)^T; middle (1.075)^T; chance a path ends above the mean path 1 - Φ(s√T/2). House model as coded: log mean ln 1.075, log spread 0.1788, so plain average exp(ln 1.075 + 0.1788²/2) - 1 = 9.23% and simple spread 19.69%. Dollar figures apply VT's 0.06% fee per year and NUMBERS 4's $0.53 of unrounded top per $1 of VT at the move.
