- **Problem class:** learning from a rare outcome that cannot be repeated (a sample of one), without being fooled by luck in either direction: reading a success that was nearly a failure as safety, or fleeing a risk too far after a fright.
- **Field that solved it first:** aviation and spaceflight safety, through near-accident reporting. Organisation theory (March, Sproull and Tamuz 1991) named the methods. Decision research (Dillon and Tinsley, from 2005) measured the bias, and learning theory (Denrell and March 2001) measured over-correction.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: KEEP the finding; KILL "the 1 in 31 is a near-miss"; CHANGE to "the model showed the near-misses before any were lived".**
  - **The measured effect.** In the NASA study (24 NASA managers, 95 MBA students and 83 undergraduates), a project manager whose mission survived only by luck was rated almost the same as one whose mission went to plan. Competence was 4.4 against 4.7 out of 7, and 3.9 after a failure. Promotability was 3.5 against 3.9, and 2.7 after a failure. The authors found "no significant differences between the miss (success) and near-miss condition", and the NASA managers' trend went further: they rated the near-miss manager higher (Dillon, Tinsley and Rogers 2006, read in full).
  - **Choices move, estimates don't.** In a drilling game (134 students paid on results), the engineers' figure was a 40% chance of catastrophe. Players' own estimates of the danger did not differ by condition (48%, 48% and 46%), yet those told of a near-miss with no visible damage drilled more (χ² = 12.087). Of the 34 who drilled into the first storm, 65% of the resilient group drilled again, against 35% of the vulnerable group (Tinsley, Dillon and Cronin 2012, read in full).
  - **Expertise is no cure.** Emergency managers with 13.6 years' experience showed the same pattern (p < .005 against the control; p < .001 resilient against vulnerable; same paper).
  - **Why a modelled 3.2% is not a near-miss.** In their terms a near-miss is an event that "by chance" did not end in disaster; resilient and vulnerable describe how that event is framed. The 27 Sep model's 3.2% is a probability that was described, not lived.
  - **What the model did instead.** It held the near-misses. In 1 market in 20 the first plan either missed or left less than $19,990, under half a payment, above the ten (NUMBERS 8s, "What 'none' cost Laura"). So about 1.8% of markets (3,547 of 200,000; about 1 in 56) paid all ten with less than $20,000 to spare (Keel arithmetic on 8s). A team living one of those paths would have had a near-miss and, on the evidence above, would probably have called it a success. In two experiments, the near-miss bias "goes away" once probability information is made salient and used as the basis for the decision (Dillon and Tinsley 2005, abstract).
  - **Judge's note:** NASA's own managers scored a mission saved by luck as highly as one that went to plan. A plan that pays in 30 markets of 31 is exactly where a team learns the wrong lesson. The first plan's miss would have been settled on a single day, 1 Jan 2033, with no money left to come. So the only time to learn was before the first trade, and the model let the team see the 31st market before living any of them.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE. Over-correction is real, but the team's turn was not a hot stove; the "too timid" pull is the danger the research names next.**
  - **What the hot stove is.** Learning that "reproduces successful actions" biases a learner against "alternatives that initially may appear to be worse than they actually are" (Denrell and March 2001). The bias needs a learner who stops sampling after a bad result. "Information about foregone payoffs increases risk taking" (Denrell 2007), and complete feedback "controls the hot stove effect" (Fujikawa 2009).
  - **Why the team's turn was not one.** The team never touched the stove: no money had moved and no loss had happened (the change came on 27 Sep; trading opened on 28 Sep). The change also came with its price. In the 27 Sep model's own terms, the Treasury-first plan left about $19,000 less at the median above the ten (8s). Before the lock on 1 Oct, the team also priced the other side: each $25,000 less floor added about $2,000-3,000 in the middle and cost about $9,000-10,000 in a bad market (29 Sep model; PEOPLE_IN_THE_ROOM B). That is foregone-payoff information, the hot stove's cure. And the after-state kept shares for money not yet promised (IPS l.15).
  - **Over-correction measured elsewhere,** for the writers' context and not for 3f:
    - flood insurance take-up "peaks at 9% the year after a flood", and the effect is gone after nine years (Gallagher; 10,841 communities);
    - an estimated 1,500 Americans died on the roads after 9/11 avoiding flying (Gigerenzer 2006);
    - people who lived through low stock returns take less financial risk decades later (Malmendier and Nagel 2011).
  - **The opposite pull ("too timid").** Success breeds "fine-tuning": "Fine-tuning reduces probabilities of success, and it continues until a serious failure occurs" (Starbuck and Milliken 1988). Tetlock and Gardner: "It is not enough just to avoid the most recent mistake."
  - **Small steps.** The most accurate forecasters "made frequent, small updates", and low-skill ones made "infrequent, large revisions" (Atanasov et al. 2020; over 400,000 forecasts). Disanalogy: the team's turn was one large step on the payments. But it was a design choice made before any money moved, not a revision of a forecast, and the small-step edge "came from superior accuracy in their initial forecasts" (Atanasov's own summary).
  - **Judge's note:** a fright teaches too much only when you never see the road you did not take. The team priced that road, about $19,000 at the median in its own model's terms, before choosing.
- **Claim 3: KEEP, with a new "before" from the team's own record.**
  - **The before.** The team's notes of Sun 27 Sep give the first plan ("full license" for an "equity-heavy portfolio", about 75% shares). Under "Entry 0 pre-mortem" they list risks to avoid. Two of them are the two errors this card studies: "(1) abandoning the growth clock early after a volatile week" (over-correction) and "(5) letting good early results loosen risk discipline" (the near-miss and fine-tuning error).
  - **The trigger and the after.** The same day the team dropped the growth clock, but for neither error. No week had passed and no result had come in. The cause was how a writer is paid (IPS l.9; the same notes already call her income "lumpy"). The check came second and priced the change both ways (8s).
  - **How to stay clear of a moral:**
    - Give the turn a reason, a dated before, the price of the road not taken, and luck named as chance.
    - Mellers et al. (2015): the best forecasters attributed close calls "to coincidence" and were "least likely to invoke destiny" (effect size .16 against .40).
    - March et al.: "the near-histories of genius and foolishness are more similar than their realized histories". That supports round 8's concession ("and for most clients it would be").
  - **Judge's note:** the team's own notes warned against abandoning the growth plan "after a volatile week". It abandoned it before any week at all, for a reason about the client. Tell this flatly, never as a twist.
- **Claim 4: KEEP.**
  - The ten cost less than $300,000 on only 37 of 214 closes, and the orders filled within two trading days of the year's cheapest close, "luck of timing, not skill" (8b).
  - The team's notes had kept "Catch 1: it's very tight" from the start. The IPS gave the 2028 deposit its first job (l.13), and the ten cost $301,544 at the freeze.
  - Airlines learn from near-misses "where the possible dangers are salient" and not from those with "no clear warnings" (Madsen, Dillon and Tinsley 2016). The about $1,900 of room on 16 Oct was a salient warning, and the team wrote a rule for it rather than taking the lucky fills as proof.

## Best five for the report

1. **Row 21 (3f moment 2, the before).** The team's own notes of 27 Sep named both errors this research studies, over-reacting "after a volatile week" and letting good results "loosen risk discipline". It then dropped the growth clock for neither, for a reason about the client. That is learning shown in the team's own words, with no moral.
2. **Rows 6-7 (3f's order; Articulation and Investment Strategy).** In the drilling study the danger estimate did not move (48%, 48%, 46%) but the choices did. Bare probabilities "may be insufficient for producing action". This supports CANON A3's order with evidence: the reason first, the check second.
3. **Rows 12-13 (3f moment 2 or 3e, one clause).** Every miss in the first plan would have been settled on a single day, 1 Jan 2033, with no money to come, so there was no near-miss to learn from later. March's "samples of one or fewer" names the tool the team used: hypothetical histories, run before the first trade.
4. **Row 23 (3f closing paragraph or 3b).** A fright over-teaches only when the road not taken goes unseen. The team counted it: about $19,000 at the median in its own model's terms (8s), plus the price of smaller floors before 1 Oct. Its caution is priced, not a flinch.
5. **Rows 19 and 32 (3f moment 1).** The knife edge is the team's real near-miss, and it was read the vulnerable way: a rule was written for the days the plan did not fit, and the fills were told as timing. The best forecasters read close calls as "coincidence", never destiny, which is the stance that keeps 3f free of a moral.
