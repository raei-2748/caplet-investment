# Q9b research cards: how residencies keep running money, pay for buildings, and outlive a founder

Research run, Fri 13 Nov 2026 (Sydney morning). Report parts: 3a (commitment and operating reserve), 3d (words for

Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose.

Claims tested (each to keep, kill or change):
1. "The residencies that lasted kept operating money apart from capital (an endowment, a board-designated reserve or a
   dedicated public line) and never paid for a building out of operating money." Result: CHANGE.
2. "A residency funded by one founder fails when that founder stops giving (Jentel), so funders read a founder's money as

Problem class: liquidity and the separation of running money from capital in a mission organisation, plus key-person

board-designated and donor-restricted net assets, FASB ASU 2016-14), endowment law and practice (UPMIFA spending rules),
and estate planning in philanthropy (a founder's money placed by deed, will or estate before the founder leaves).

## Verdicts in one screen

1. **Claim 1, CHANGE.** The separation is real but soft, and buildings do reach running money.
   - The richest residencies separate money mostly by the board's own label, which the board can lift. Yaddo's
     board-designated endowment is $22,554,625 of $32,402,204 (69.6%, 2025), and "The board has the authority to modify or
     remove designations" (row 1).
   - Buildings reach the operating statement everywhere: Yaddo's depreciation plus repairs are 22.9% of 2025 expenses
     (row 3). MacDowell bought a building half on a 25-year mortgage (row 6) and drew a line of credit secured on its
     investments (row 7).
   - The clean versions exist. MacDowell's written rule pays capital from a board-designated building reserve or gifts
     given for it (row 5); Djerassi's capital fund came from a one-time easement sale (row 12); Yaddo's capital work
     was paid from campaign gifts (row 4).
   - The one clear breach in the filings read is Headlands in 2023. It spent $757,854 on campus work while operations
     used $474,399 of cash, and was left with about 8 days of available money. In January 2024 it cut programmes and
     paused the work (rows 8-9).
   - Better claim: residencies that last keep capital money apart by source and by written rule; where capital work and
     programmes share one pool of cash, programmes are what gets cut. Laura's plan keeps the two apart by instrument (the
     operating reserve, IPS l.17; the facility money, IPS l.15; NUMBERS 8h: "the facility money ... is separate").
2. **Claim 2, KILL as stated; CHANGE.** Jentel did not fail.
   - Neltje died on 30 April 2021 and "gifted her estate to the University of Wyoming", which includes the residency
     (row 19). The university added $1 million (row 20), and in December 2025 the residency hosts six residents a
     month (row 21).
   - The Jentel Foundation's $0 contributions (year to June 2023) are the residency moving to the university, not
     failing (row 22). Q32 row 31's "fragile" reading needs a note (Gaps).
   - Pattern (Keel inference, small sample): survivors had the founder's money or property placed by deed, will or estate
     (Jentel, Albee; Yaddo and MacDowell in Q9 and Q32). Near-closures and break-ups had the place but no running money
     placed: Dorland in 1985, two years after its founder left (row 24); Byrdcliffe after 1929 (row 26); Black Mountain's
     last two years (row 25).
   - Bamboo Curtain ended for site and pandemic reasons, not money, in the source now read (row 27), so the claim cannot
     lean on it.
   - Better claim: a founder's money keeps a residency alive past the founder only when it is placed before the founder
     leaves. Placing the land is not enough; the running money must be placed too. Laura's ten payments, owned in
     Treasuries and dated to 2042, are running money placed before the doors open, for one decade (the case's horizon).

## (b) Cards

### 1. Jentel: the founder's estate carried the residency to a university (rows 19-22)
- **What is it?** A Wyoming residency founded in 2001 and funded almost wholly by its founder (Q32 row 31). She died in
  April 2021 and left her estate, including the residency, to the University of Wyoming. The university added $1 million.
- **Did it work?** Yes. In December 2025 the residency has a new manager and hosts six residents a month. Her own
  foundation's filings show $0 contributions only because the residency had moved.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** One founder funds the running costs. The money is in place before the founder stops,
  so a second funder (here a university) builds on it, not in place of it.
- **How is it not?** Jentel's money arrived by estate, at death, and is meant to last for good. Laura places hers in
  2027-2028, while working, for ten years only. Her funders are co-sponsors, not a university.
- **What Caplet takes.** Report part 3d: at most one evidence clause behind SKELETON 3d's "Ten years of operations owned,
  dated to 2042", with no new number. Otherwise store only. Correct Q32 row 31's "fragile" reading before any part of the
  report cites Jentel.

### 2. Headlands 2023: campus work and programmes shared one pool of cash (rows 8-10)
- **What is it?** A residency on National Park land. In 2023 it spent $757,854 on campus work and design while
  operations used $474,399 of cash. Money available within a year fell to $89,341, about 8 days of expenses.
- **Did it work?** No. In January 2024 it cut programmes, paused the capital work, and cut stipends by more than a third.
  Its 90-day reserve, held at about 1.2 months in 2020, no longer appears in the 2024 audit.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** It is the failure claim 1 warns of: building money and running money in one pot.
  Laura's plan keeps them in different instruments (operating reserve, IPS l.17; facility money, IPS l.15; NUMBERS 8h).
- **How is it not?** Headlands raises its running money every year; Laura's is bought. Its building belongs to the Park
  Service; Laura's facility cost is outside the case and is never estimated.
- **What Caplet takes.** Store only. If 3a needs one piece of outside evidence for keeping the reserve apart, this is the
  cleanest, with no number in prose. No exhibit.

### 3. A board-designated reserve is a label the board can lift (rows 1, 5, 10, 11)
- **What is it?** Yaddo's board designates 69.6% of its endowment and can "modify or remove designations". MacDowell's
  board can draw past 5% when deficits are larger. Headlands' 90-day reserve was 1.2 months and then disappears from the
  accounts. Djerassi never designated one.
- **Did it work?** As a habit, mostly yes for the richest. As a hard wall, no: in each case the board can release it.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her operating reserve is her own money, not a donor's restricted gift. What holds it
  in place is a written rule: only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan (IPS l.17, CANON A34). That
  rule is narrower than a board's power to undesignate.
- **How is it not?** The ten Treasuries are dated and sized: each pays out before its payment. What keeps the payments
  safe even if interest earns nothing is the top-up, which is two things: $2,388 to complete the ladder (IPS l.13) and
  $35,894 that sizes the operating reserve under IPS l.11 (NUMBERS 2).
- **What Caplet takes.** A wording guard for 3a and 3e: say "owned and dated" and "held under the two reasons"; never say
  "restricted", "locked" or "irrevocable". Store otherwise.

### 4. Residencies hold weeks to months of spendable money; the richest add market-priced years (rows 13, 15-17, 30)
- **What is it?** Months available within a year, from four audits: Headlands 0.25-4.1, Djerassi 7.1-8.7, Yaddo 18.6-19.4,
  MacDowell 21.4-34.8. In 2020, 9% of 358 residency organisations had closed indefinitely.
- **Did it work?** For the richest, yes, but their months move with markets. MacDowell lost 13.4 months of cover in one
  year; Djerassi's endowment fell 38.7% in 2022 while paying 30% of its costs.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Her operating reserve answers the same question: how long running money lasts without
  new gifts.
- **How is it not?** Hers is 120 months of her own $50,000 a year, not of a whole budget (H9-119). It is dated and ends in
  2042; theirs is perpetual and priced daily.
- **What Caplet takes.** Store only. The density cut leaves 3a with one headline number, and that is not this one. If a
  figure note wants context, use words with no new number: "residencies' reserves are counted in months and move with
  markets". Keep the 120-months framing out of prose unless "her share only" sits beside it.

### 5. The place was placed; the running money was not (rows 24-26, 28)
- **What is it?** Dorland's founders gave their land to the Nature Conservancy in the early 1970s. Two years after the
  founder left, the colony talked of closing for "lack of funds". Black Mountain's last two years brought rejected grants,
  rejected loans, an alumni appeal that raised $250 of $25,000, and a council that "boosted credibility but did nothing to
  boost credit". Byrdcliffe's family sold land to live after 1929.
- **Did it work?** Dorland survived by cutting staff to part-time and charging residents. Black Mountain closed. Byrdcliffe
  was split up.
- **How is Laura's case like it?** Co-sponsors will ask whether the running money exists before the doors open. Laura's
  answer is that it does, in Treasuries she owns.
- **How is it not?** These are older, smaller and poorer cases, with no audited figures. Laura's case is a ten-year
  commitment, not a perpetual institution.
- **What Caplet takes.** 3d: Black Mountain's "credibility but not credit" is the nearest outside echo of "money you
  already own" (IPS l.15). At most one clause, and only if the lead wants an outside voice there. Store otherwise.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1, CHANGE.**
- What holds: the residencies with the deepest pockets do separate building money from running money.
  - MacDowell by policy, with a board-designated building reserve or gifts given for the purpose (row 5).
  - Djerassi by source: a 1999 easement sale created its land and buildings fund (row 12).
  - Yaddo by campaign gifts (row 4).
- What fails:
  - "Never paid for a building out of operating money" is false everywhere. Upkeep and depreciation are 22.9% of
    Yaddo's 2025 expenses (row 3).
  - Buildings were also bought on debt (MacDowell's mortgage, row 6), and short gaps were bridged with a line of credit
    (row 7).
  - The separation is a label the board can lift (row 1).
- The breach that shows why it matters is Headlands in 2023 (rows 8-9). Campus work and programmes shared one cash pool.
  The pool fell to about 8 days, and programmes and stipends were cut.
- Revised claim: residencies keep capital apart by source and written rule, and when they do not, programmes pay.
- For Laura: the facility money (IPS l.15) and the operating reserve (IPS l.17) are different instruments. The reserve
  changes only for the two reasons. That is a firmer separation than any board designation found, though it is still her
  own rule, not a legal restriction.

**Claim 2, KILL as stated; CHANGE.**
- Jentel is the opposite of the claim. The founder's estate moved the residency to a university, the university added
  $1 million, and the residency runs at full size in 2025 (rows 19-22).
- The pattern that survives (Keel inference, small and hand-picked):
  - Founders whose money or property was placed by deed, will or estate left residencies that continued: Jentel, Albee,
    and in other cards Yaddo and MacDowell.
  - Where only the place was placed, the residency nearly closed or was sold off: Dorland, Byrdcliffe. Where nothing was
    placed, it closed: Black Mountain, after its founder-treasurer left (Q9 row 2).
- Bamboo Curtain does not fit either way. In the source read, it closed for site and pandemic reasons, and its
  founder's death disrupted the wind-down (row 27).
- The half of the claim about funders stands as a Keel inference, not a finding. No source read states how funders score
  a founder's money. Black Mountain's rejected asks (row 25) and Res Artis's 61% without emergency funding (row 18) are
  the nearest evidence that outside money does not arrive when it is needed.
- Revised claim: a founder's money keeps a residency going past the founder only when it is placed before the founder
  leaves, and it must include running money, not only the place. Laura's ten payments are that, for one decade.

**Claim 3, CHANGE.**
- "Months" is right for spendable money. Headlands and Djerassi held from about 8 days to 8.7 months (row 15), the
  arts-wide median is about 1.5 months (Q9 row 13), and a 2020 shock closed 9% of responding residency organisations
  (row 17).
- "Not years" is wrong for the richest. Yaddo's endowment is about 6.9 years of expenses (row 30) and MacDowell's
  available money was 34.8 months in 2021 (row 15). But that money is perpetual, pays about 5% a year, and moves with
  markets: MacDowell lost 13.4 months of cover in one year (row 16), and Djerassi's endowment fell 38.7% (row 13).
- Revised claim: residencies hold weeks to months of spendable money; the richest add perpetual, market-priced
  endowments. Laura's operating reserve is different in kind: 120 months of her own $50,000 a year, owned and dated
  (NUMBERS 8h, MODEL), covering her share and not a whole budget (H9-119).
- Size of the gap (Keel arithmetic, with years named): her reserve covers her share for 120 months. The best-covered
  residency read held 34.8 months of its whole budget (MacDowell, 2021), and the median small one about 1.5 months
  (2019).
