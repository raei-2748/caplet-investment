**The problem.** The case says "A meaningful personal contribution may signal that the residency is financially viable,
demonstrate Laura's commitment to the project, and make potential co-sponsors more willing to contribute" (case l.110-112).
Two of three blind judges said the plan never argues that her facility contribution is meaningful. The plan's figure is
$115,000 at today's market rates, the top of a stated range of $95,000 to $115,000; the bottom is owned from January 2028
(NUMBERS 4). The usual yardstick, a lead gift's share of the campaign goal, cannot be computed: "The total cost of the
facility has not been determined. Teams are not expected to estimate that cost ... [or] determine the project's total
funding gap" (case l.164-166). Laura's living costs are met "by income and financial resources outside the portfolio"
(case l.61-63), so the case does not give her total means either.

**Claims tested.**
1. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE: "Cost and timing make a founder's contribution credible, more than its dollar size; so about
   $115,000, a quarter of all she puts in and owned before she asks, is meaningful." Is the share-of-means signal
   evidence or only theory?
2. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE: "Campaign practice judges a lead gift by its share of the goal (top gift 10-20% or more), so
   without a goal Laura cannot call her contribution meaningful." Which signals do funders actually ask for?
3. KEEP, KILL or CHANGE: "For an individual founder of a small arts institution, a contribution large against her own
   means is the norm and is read as commitment."

**Problem class and the fields that solved it.** Proving commitment to outsiders who cannot see your books or your
project. Public economics solved it in theory and in the lab: the informed person gives first, visibly, at a cost
(Vesterlund; Andreoni; Potters, Sefton and Vesterlund; Levati et al.), and in the field the lead donor's identity did
the work (Karlan and List). Nonprofit governance solved it in practice with a separate yardstick for insiders' giving
("according to their means", 100% participation; BoardSource, Kresge, GAR). Entrepreneurial finance calls the same thing
founder money as a commitment signal ("skin in the game"; Conti et al.). The founding histories of artist residencies
show what founders actually put in.

## Verdicts in one screen

- **Claim 1: CHANGE.**
  - Keep the mechanism. Theory and lab agree that a lead contribution persuades through its cost to the giver and its
    timing (first, announced, provable):
    - "it is the leader's sacrifice that makes the signal credible" (Andreoni, p. 20); a charity can "collect the money
      and provide proof" or require "a legally binding contract" (p. 17);
    - an informed leader moving first raised total giving about 50% (117.25 against 76.75 per session), but only when
      followers could not judge the project themselves; with full information, no gain (80.75 against 86.50; Potters et
      al., 2007).
  - Change the size claim. "Share of her means" is theory plus a practice norm. No field test read isolates it, and the
    one lab test that varies means cuts the other way:
    - followers copied the leader's amount, not her share: when leaders' means were halved they gave a larger share,
      yet followers gave significantly less (Levati et al., 2007);
    - with means unknown, a leader added almost nothing (10.86 against 10.42 without a leader); with means known, a
      leader lifted giving from 9.78 to 14.38.
  - In the field, the lead donor's identity and expertise did the work (+26% in the giving rate, +42% in money per
    letter; Karlan and List, 2020), not her sacrifice.
  - Changed claim: the contribution reads as meaningful when co-sponsors see three things: the dollar figure, what it is
    measured against, and that it is owned and comes first. "$115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in" (Keel sum 25.6%) is
    the right form. "A quarter" is not: WORDS reserves "about a quarter" for the top-up.
- **Claim 2: CHANGE.** Practice has two yardsticks, and the case uses the second.
  - The lead gift is judged by its share of the goal: top gift "at least 20%", "In most campaigns ... 20% or 25%"
    (Capital Campaign Pro; Q11b r30). Laura cannot claim that without a goal, and the case rules the goal out (l.164-166).
  - Insiders' gifts are judged against the giver's own means: "a meaningful personal contribution according to their
    means"; "By making a personally meaningful gift, each board member demonstrates their commitment ... [and can]
    function as a more credible fundraiser and inspire other donors" (BoardSource). The case's sentence (l.110-112)
    matches this nearly word for word: "meaningful personal contribution", "demonstrate ... commitment", others more
    willing. So Laura can call it meaningful without a goal.
  - What funders actually ask for (six funders: five read at the source, one at a secondary source; none asks for a
    founder's share of her means as a number):
    - a share of the goal already raised before they will look: Kresge 20% to 50%, Mabee 40%, Hearst 50%, Boettcher
      one-half to two-thirds (secondary source);
    - 100% of insiders giving: GAR requires it; BoardSource: "Funders often ask";
    - the form of the money: Kresge counts "written pledges or cash"; the Massachusetts Cultural Facilities Fund counts
      only "a secured cash match" or legally obligated money, not pledges;
    - who gave and for what: Mabee wants donor names and does not count "internal or reserve funds" or money not
      designated to the project;
    - the giver's own history: "Are these the largest gifts they have ever made to your organization?" (Kresge).
- **Claim 3: CHANGE (mostly KEEP).** In nine real residencies, the founder gave first and gave the place or most of the
  early money:
  - MacDowell (deed transferred 1907), Yaddo (fortune and estate), Ragdale (house, barn and five acres, 1986), Millay
    (the Barn, 1976), Djerassi (land and buildings, 1999), Jentel (one contributor, the founder, gave all $579,500 of
    2018's contributions), Mesa Refuge ("largely self-funded" 1997-2013), Arvon (Ted Hughes's house leased 1975, sold
    1989), Tyrone Guthrie (house bequeathed to the state).
  - Changes:
    - most of these founders were wealthy, so "large against her means" is shown for one founder only: Marian
      MacDowell, who "did not have the financial resources" of the big patrons;
    - only MacDowell's founder says why owning first persuades: "there had to be something tangible for people to have
      back of a venture so that they could believe in what we were trying to do";
    - founder-only funding is fragile: Jentel's contributions went from 97.8-99.5% of revenue each year (2011-2022) to
      $0 in the year to June 2023, after the founder died. Laura's plan (her figure first, co-sponsors from the start)
      is the sturdier pattern.

## (b) Cards

**Card 1. The case's word is the board-giving yardstick (rows 14-19)**
- What is it? Nonprofit governance judges an insider's gift against the giver's own means, and asks that every insider
  give. BoardSource's practice text says a "meaningful personal contribution according to their means" lets a board
  member show "commitment and trust" and act as "a more credible fundraiser and inspire other donors".
- Did it work? It is a practice norm, not a tested effect. Funders do enforce it: GAR requires 100% board participation,
  and BoardSource says "Funders often ask". Kresge asks whether insiders' gifts are "the largest they have ever made".
- How is Laura's case like it? The case's sentence (l.110-112) uses the same three parts: "meaningful personal
  contribution", "demonstrate ... commitment", co-sponsors "more willing". So "meaningful" is measured against her own
  means and commitment, which needs no facility cost.
- How is it not? A board member's yearly gift is not a founder's capital contribution. The case gives no total for her
  means (l.61-63), so only the portfolio can be shown.
- What Caplet takes: 3b, one clause: the yardstick (her means, not a share of a goal), cited to BoardSource. Drop "lead
  gift" from SKELETON 3b's note (row 17): it means the top gift by share of goal, and WORDS bans "gift" for her money.

**Card 2. Show the amount and what it is measured against (rows 7-9, 35)**
- What is it? A lab public-goods game with unequal endowments. A leader raised others' giving when everyone knew the
  endowments, and barely at all when they did not. Followers copied the leader's amount, not her share.
- Did it work? Yes, in the lab: with known endowments, giving rose from 9.78 to 14.38 with a leader; with unknown
  endowments, 10.42 to 10.86. When leaders became poorer and gave a larger share, followers gave significantly less
  (p < 0.02). Glazer & Konrad's donor lists show the same habit outside the lab: people read round dollar amounts.
- How is Laura's case like it? Co-sponsors will copy a dollar figure and judge it only if they see what stands behind
  it. SKELETON 3b's "$115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in" does both.
- How is it not? Lab tokens, students, small stakes. Laura's co-sponsors can ask her questions. Her total means are not
  given, so "her means" here is the portfolio she puts in, not her wealth.
- What Caplet takes: 3b keeps "$115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in", with the payments named in words ("beside ten
  years of operations already bought") and no second figure; the share (25.6%) stays in the store. 3d keeps the one
  figure to count, $95,000.

**Card 3. The informed founder goes first, visibly (rows 3-6)**
- What is it? When one donor knows a project's value and others do not, the informed donor's announced choice is
  copied. Theory says she must give more than she otherwise would; the experiments measured it.
- Did it work? Yes, in two experiments: followers copied a contribution 85% of the time; giving more than doubled when
  donors chose the order themselves and rose about 50% when it was imposed. With full information there was no gain.
- How is Laura's case like it? In 2031 Laura knows the residency; co-sponsors do not. The case names this effect first:
  "signal that the residency is financially viable" (l.111). The papers' advice to fundraisers, "making the informed
  donors move first and announcing their contributions", is the order of SKELETON 3d.
- How is it not? Two-player games with a known menu of values. Foundations that do their own checks are close to full
  information, and for them the signal fades; what they count is owned money (Card 4).
- What Caplet takes: 3d already says "her figure first and unconditional". The evidence for "first" is here; store the
  numbers. No new clause in 3b.

**Card 4. What funders count: written, owned, named, designated (rows 2, 20-24)**
- What is it? Six capital funders' published rules (five read at the source, Boettcher at a secondary source). They ask for a share of the goal raised before they look (20% to
  two-thirds), 100% of insiders giving, and money in a form they can count.
- Did it work? These are gates, not tested effects. Andreoni (p. 17) gives the logic: proof or "a legally binding
  contract" stops fake announcements, and "This is, in fact, the primary practice among charities".
- How is Laura's case like it? Her bottom is owned from January 2028; in 2031 it can be pledged in writing (Kresge
  counts "written pledges or cash"); it is named and designated to the facility (Mabee). The operating reserve is a
  separate fund and is never counted as money raised for the building (Mabee excludes "internal or reserve funds").
- How is it not? A strict matching funder counts only "secured cash" (Massachusetts), so her figure counts only once
  the funds pay out in late 2032. These are US funders; Taiwan funders are in Q11d.
- What Caplet takes: 3d, one clause: "in writing" beside "her figure first and unconditional", for the bottom. Store
  the funder list. 3e or Q11c: a matching funder may count her figure only in December 2032.

**Card 5. Real founders gave first; one said why (rows 26-34)**
- What is it? Nine artist residencies read at their own pages or filings. In each, a founder or family gave the place
  or most of the early money before others joined.
- Did it work? The residencies lasted (MacDowell since 1907). MacDowell's founder gave the deed first so that people
  "could believe in what we were trying to do", then raised close to $100,000 by 1930 from concerts and clubs. Founder-
  only funding was fragile: Jentel's contributions fell to $0 after its founder died; Mesa Refuge is moving "from a
  founder-supported organization".
- How is Laura's case like it? An author founding a residency and putting in her own money first is the common pattern.
  Her plan adds co-sponsors from the start and owns ten years of operations, which the single-founder cases lacked.
- How is it not? Most founders gave property and were wealthy; Laura gives cash from a dedicated portfolio, and her
  money stays hers until late 2032. Only MacDowell shows a founder of modest means.
- What Caplet takes: store. The MacDowell sentence is the one precedent worth a clause, in 3d or not at all; density
  allows one. Never present these founders' acts as Caplet's experience.

## (c) Verdicts

**Claim 1 (cost and timing, not size; "a quarter of all she puts in"): CHANGE.**
- What holds. Theory makes a lead contribution credible through cost and timing:
  - cost: "the leader's sacrifice" (Andreoni, p. 20); the leader "will clearly be worse off" than as a follower (p. 11);
  - timing: the informed first giver's announced amount is copied (Vesterlund; Potters et al., 2005, 2007);
  - proof: collected money or a binding contract (Andreoni, p. 17).
- What fails. The share-of-means signal is theory and practice norm only:
  - no field test read isolates a giver's share of her means;
  - the only lab test with unequal means found followers copy amounts, and an example fails when means are unknown
    (Levati et al.);
  - the one field test of a lead donor found the effect ran through identity and expertise (Karlan & List);
  - entrepreneurial finance finds founder money matters to some investors (angels) and not others (venture
    capitalists), and, in one abstract, no link to long-term outcomes (Conti et al.; Busenitz et al., UNVERIFIED).
- Changed claim. Her facility contribution reads as meaningful when co-sponsors see the dollar figure, what it is
  measured against, and that it is owned and comes first. Form: "$115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in" (SKELETON 3b).
  Not "a quarter" (WORDS l.48 reserves it for the top-up).
- Size check. $115,000 is 25.6% of the deposits; the bottom, $95,000, is 21.1% (Keel sums). With the ten payments, she
  commits $615,000 nominal from $450,000 (Q25 r19); 87.2% of all the money is promised on 1 Jan 2033 (Q25 r26).

**Claim 2 (no goal, so not meaningful): CHANGE.**
- What holds. Lead gifts are judged by share of goal (top gift 20% to 25%; Q11b r30 and row 17), and Laura cannot
  claim that: the case rules out a facility cost (l.164-166).
- What fails. The conclusion. Practice judges insiders' gifts against their own means, and the case's sentence is that
  yardstick nearly word for word (row 14). So the argument for "meaningful" needs no goal.
- What funders ask for, in order of how often among the six:
  - a share of the goal raised before they look (Kresge, Mabee, Hearst, Boettcher, GAR's "near completion");
  - insiders' participation (GAR; BoardSource);
  - the form: written pledges or cash (Kresge), secured cash only (Massachusetts);
  - names and designation, with internal or reserve funds excluded (Mabee);
  - the giver's own record ("the largest gifts they have ever made", Kresge).
- None asks for a founder's share of her means as a number. So "first, in writing, owned, named" is what funders can
  check; "meaningful" is what the case asks Caplet to argue.

**Claim 3 (founders give much against their means, read as commitment): CHANGE, mostly KEEP.**
- What holds. Founders gave first and gave much in all nine cases read (rows 26-34), and in Q11b's two closest cases
  (Fogo Island, Bamboo Curtain, Q11b r14-15).
- What changes:
  - "against her own means" is shown for one founder only (MacDowell); the others were wealthy or gave inherited
    property;
  - "read as commitment" is stated by one founder, not measured;
  - single-founder funding is fragile (Jentel, Mesa Refuge).
- Changed claim. A founder's own money first is the norm for small residencies. What makes it persuade is that it is
  real before the ask (MacDowell's deed) and that others are brought in. Laura's plan does both, and owns the operating
  years as well.

**Where each strong idea lands (density rule: one answer and one headline number per part)**
- 3b, one clause: the yardstick, her means not a goal (BoardSource), and the denominator "$115,000 of the $450,000 she
  puts in" (Levati et al.). Replace "announced as a lead gift" with "announced first" (row 17 risk).
- 3d, one clause: her figure first, in writing, for the bottom she owns (Potters et al., 2005; Andreoni p. 17; Kresge).
  Already in SKELETON 3d except "in writing".
- Store: the funder gates (rows 20-25), the founder cases (rows 26-34; MacDowell's sentence is the one candidate
  clause), Karlan & List's identity effect, Conti's angels, Glazer & Konrad.
