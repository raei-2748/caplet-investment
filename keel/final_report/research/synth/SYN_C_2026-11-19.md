## SC-1. The executives who forecast badly still keep their dividends: her top does not need the forecast to be right

**Parts**

**Q6-030.** US finance chiefs gave 80% intervals for the next year's S&P 500 return. The realised return fell inside them only 36.3% of the time. Their intervals averaged 14.5 points wide, against 42.2 points between history's 10th and 90th percentiles.
- Source: Ben-David, Graham & Harvey (2013), "Managerial Miscalibration", *QJE* 128(4): 1547-1584.

**H7-060.** Lintner: firms raise dividends only to levels they can keep. Executives see "huge penalties for cuts", and 65% would raise new funds rather than cut.
- Source: Lintner, *AER* 46(2) 1956 (via a secondary); Brav, Graham, Harvey & Michaely, *JFE* 2005 (CFA digest 2006); Damodaran packet 2.

**H6-141.** Markets punish a dividend cut far more than they reward a raise: about +0.8% for a raise against about -6% for a cut (1962-74, read from a chart).
- Source: Damodaran P2 S151, S184.
- Never quote the -6% about donors.

**NUMBERS 4 and 8v (the plan).**
- The stated top is $115,000 for any return on VT from 4% to 10% a year.
- At about 5% a year it is still $115,000.
- At 3.1% it is one step lower, $110,000.

**The new thing**

Two studies that share two authors (Graham and Harvey) show one profession at its worst and at its best:
- As forecasters of the share market, US finance chiefs are badly overconfident: their 80% intervals held only about a third of the time.
- As makers of a public money promise, the dividend, they are reliable. They set it only at a level they expect to keep, and they treat a cut as the costliest thing they can do.

Together the two findings show that a promise's reliability comes from what it is set on, not from how well its maker forecasts. Laura's figure is set on what the move has already bought. So the 7.5% decides which range she is likely to state. It does not decide whether the stated range holds.

**The aha, in one sentence**

The profession whose 80% market forecasts held only about a third of the time keeps its dividend promises because it sets them where they can be kept, and Laura's top works the same way: it is $115,000 at any share return from 4% to 10% a year.

This surprises a judge who expects a range's confidence to stand or fall with the return forecast. It also surprises a reader who has just read 3e's forecast assumptions.

**Builds on**
- AHA_REGISTER row 7 (3c: the forecast barely touches the promise; speaking early does). SC-1 adds human evidence that forecasting skill and keeping a promise are separate things.
- GOLDMINE nugget 31 (Lintner, still "to place"). SC-1 sets Lintner beside forecast evidence, not beside donor trust.
- BOLD thesis 3 already covers the trust side (Lintner, Damodaran, Gneezy & Epley, Barrett & Dannenberg). SC-1 does not rebuild it.

**Why it fits Laura**
- TNA l.23: "a forecast return of about 7.5% a year (as per JPMorgan)" (CANON B15).
- TNA l.21: "They only set how far her gift rises above her promised minimum" (CANON D1).

- The case asks the team to "state how confident they are that her 2033 contribution will fall within that range" (CASE_MAP A5).
- It also asks for assumptions on "investment performance" (CASE_MAP A10).

**The five questions**

*What is it?* Two findings about US financial executives, paired:
- they are poorly calibrated when they forecast markets (Ben-David et al.);
- they have a strong habit of setting dividends only where those can be kept (Lintner; Brav et al.).

*Did it work?*
- The calibration finding rests on 13,300+ forecasts over ten years (QJE, read at source).
- The dividend habit is one of corporate finance's oldest findings (Lintner 1956). A survey found it again half a century later (Brav et al. 2005, read via a CFA digest).
- Markets react more to cuts than to raises (H6-141; approximate, read from a chart).

*How Laura's case is like it.*

*How it is not.*
- A dividend repeats every year, and its signal works through a share price. Co-sponsors have no market price (H7-060's own critique).
- Lintner's managers do forecast. They move slowly toward a target payout of earnings, so "set on cash in hand" overstates them. Laura's top, set on owned Treasuries, is the stricter of the two.
- The forecasts studied are executives' one-year S&P forecasts, not J.P. Morgan's 10-15-year house assumption. The pairing never says the 7.5% is wrong.

## SC-2. Counted once sold: the underwriter's rule and the gift office's rule cut her shares where IPS l.15 does

**Parts**

**H7-067 (with H6-001).** "With a bought deal, the investment bank buys your shares." In a best-efforts deal the bank "doesn't promise anything".
- Source: Shiller, ECON 252 (2011), lecture 19, "Investment Banks", ch. 1 (about 00:05).

**Q43-039.** Gifted shares are "considered available for the project upon receipt" and "sold upon receipt". The one exception is a sponsor who "is willing to accept the financial risk or reward of holding a security".
- Source: University of Idaho (2008), APM 40.06.

**Q67-043.** A gift officer cannot count a pledge that hangs on "some future event over which neither the university nor donor may have control".
- Source: Eastern Washington University Foundation (2022); UVM Foundation (2024).

**Q11b-032.** CASE's counting standards leave out conditional pledges "until such time that it is anticipated the conditions will be met". They also ask institutions to set separate goals for revocable money.
- Source: CASE, "FAQ for Standards" (2024 update).

**The new thing**

The two sides of a promise paid in shares follow rules written for different jobs:
- the seller's side, the underwriter;
- the receiver's side, the gift office.

Yet the rules agree. Both count a share only once it has been turned into something whose value is fixed. Both name one exception: a party who chooses to carry the price risk. For the underwriter that is the bank that buys the deal; for the gift office it is Idaho's sponsor who keeps the security.

IPS l.15 cuts VT along exactly that line, and says which half is which:
- The promised half moves into the late-2032 Treasury fund before any figure is spoken. That is the bought deal, and the gift office's "sold upon receipt" done before receipt.
- The half kept is the exception. Laura carries its risk, and no one counts it (CANON A30).

No part alone shows that one plan stands on both sides of the line at once.

**The aha, in one sentence**

Before any co-sponsor hears a figure, Laura has done what an underwriter does in a bought deal and what a gift office does the day shares arrive: she has turned the promised half into Treasuries. The half she keeps is exactly the exception both rules name, money whose owner chooses to carry the price risk, which is why it never counts in her range.

This surprises a judge who reads the 2031 move as caution or market timing. The move is the counting rule of both sides of the table, and the same rule explains the half kept.

- Card Q67 (Q67-043 already supports 3c's rule).
- The new parts are the receiver's rule and its exception. Together they explain why the half kept sits outside the range.

**Why it fits Laura**

*Did it work?* Both are standing practice, not experiments:

The evidence is that these practices have lasted, not a measured outcome.

*How Laura's case is like it.*

## SC-3. Taiwan's own insurers carry her currency risk, and their regulator chose money set aside over a full hedge

**Parts**

**Q10b-020.** Taiwan's life insurers paid NT$1.6tn to hedge from 2019 to October 2025. That is more than their NT$1.4tn of after-tax profit over the same years.
- Source: FSC (2025d).

**Q10b-023.** The regulator (the FSC) answered with reserves, not full hedges:
- 2025 temporary measures: 30% of 2025 pretax profit into a currency reserve;
- on 12 Feb 2026, three reserves: a volatility reserve, a fixed reserve capped at 10% of net exposure, and a strengthening reserve.
- Source: FSC (2025e, 2025d, 2026); FSC Insurance Bureau (2025).

**Q10b-025.** Taiwan Ratings, as quoted by the central bank: the reserves "would not reduce the underlying risks arising from currency mismatches".
- Source: CBC (2026), Financial Stability Report.

**Q69-011.** The US Defense Department budgets exchange rates "one to two years ahead". It is barred from options and derivatives ("prohibited by law"), and it holds an account that "buffers the variability".
- Source: Gardner, Ritschel, White & Wallen (2017), *Journal of Public Procurement* 17(3).

**The new thing**

Laura's exposure runs one way: she holds US dollars that must later pay NT$ costs, so only a stronger Taiwan dollar hurts her (Q10b-031). Two very different institutions have the same exposure, and neither fully hedges it:

| Who | Exposure | Why no full hedge |
|---|---|---|
| Taiwan's life insurers | US dollar assets against NT$ promises | the hedge cost more than they earned |
| The Pentagon | dollar budgets against foreign costs | derivatives are barred by law |
| Laura | dollars to be spent on NT$ costs | derivatives are barred by the competition's rules |

All three answer with money set aside ahead. Taiwan's rating agency adds the honest limit 3e needs: money set aside absorbs a move; it does not remove the risk.

**The aha, in one sentence**

The risk IPS l.15 holds money back for cost Taiwan's own life insurers more than their after-tax profit to hedge in under seven years. Their regulator, like the Pentagon, answered it with money set aside rather than a full hedge. So Laura's flexibility is the answer Taiwan's own supervisor chose, with the limit its own rating agency named.

This surprises a judge who expects a student team to say "hedge the currency". It also surprises anyone who thinks a plan without a hedge has ignored the risk.

## SC-4. Her flexibility holds what a builder's contingency leaves out by definition

**Parts**

**Q5-006.** In cost engineering a contingency "usually excludes ... c) Management reserves; and d) Escalation and currency effects", and it "is expected to be expended". Money "for discretionary management purposes outside of the defined scope" is a management reserve.
- Source: AACE RP 10S-90, Cost Engineering Terminology.

**Q5-023.** Taiwan's public-works cost manual prices escalation (物價調整費) separately from contingency. It lists exchange rates (總.4.10) and fundraising (總.4.14) among the causes of cost change.
- Source: PCC 總則篇 (2018), chapter 4.

**Q11e-016.** Taiwan's Ministry of Culture grant rules say of overruns: 「應自行籌措財源配合，不得要求追加補助數額」 (the grantee raises any shortfall itself and may not ask for more).
- Source: 文化部 (2021).
- With Q5d-028: the Ministry's local venue programme leaves anything over the approved budget to the local government.

**Q5d-008.** England's arts lottery building programme: supplementary grants met about 35% of overruns, so about two thirds fell on the organisations.
- Source: HC 253 (2004).

**The new thing**

The profession that writes building budgets defines its contingency narrowly:
- AACE puts escalation and currency outside it by name;
- Taiwan's own manual lists exchange rates and fundraising as causes of cost change, priced outside it;
- the funders who might help send what is left back to the owner: Taiwan's Ministry of Culture by rule, England's lottery in practice.

So most of what IPS l.15 names is, by the builders' own definitions, money that no building budget's contingency and no grant will carry. It is the owner's own. That covers building costs that overrun through escalation, a stronger Taiwan dollar, and a co-sponsor falling short.

Her flexibility is therefore not the contingency fund the case tells teams not to size. It is a different kind of money, the kind the profession keeps apart from contingency.

**The aha, in one sentence**

A judge may read Laura's flexibility as the contingency fund the case says not to size. Yet the cost engineers' own definition says a contingency "usually excludes" escalation and currency effects, and Taiwan's culture ministry says a grantee "may not ask for more". So what IPS l.15 holds back is the money no building budget and no grant was ever going to carry.

This surprises a judge who assumes the building's own budget covers these risks and so reads the half kept as double counting.

**Builds on**
- AHA_REGISTER row 6 (3b), at risk at 3.0. SC-4 is a candidate for 3b's aha slot if row 6 stays at 3.
- SKELETON 3b's PCC manual line (Q5-023, causes of cost change). SC-4 combines it with AACE's exclusions and two funders' overrun rules into one claim.
- It is not in GOLDMINE or BOLD_THESES.

**Why it fits Laura**
- IPS l.15: "That is your flexibility – for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short".
- The case: "Committing all remaining assets could limit her financial flexibility as the project develops" (CASE_MAP A4).
- CANON A31: discuss each use without sizing a contingency fund (case l.106-107) or estimating the facility's cost (case l.164-165).

**The five questions**

*What is it?* Cost engineering's split of project money (contingency, escalation, currency, management reserve), and two funders' rules on overruns.

*Did it work?*
- AACE's terminology is the profession's standard (read online).
- The PCC manual is Taiwan's public-works standard (read).
- The Ministry of Culture rule is in force in its 2021 text (read in full).
- England's arts lottery buildings show what happens in practice: the programme's 5% contingency had to be raised to 7%, and two thirds of overruns fell on the organisations (Q5d-008).

*How Laura's case is like it.* She founds a residency in Taiwan with co-sponsors and perhaps public grants. She is the owner these rules send overruns back to.

*How it is not.*
- Laura is not the builder, and AACE's terms come from industrial projects.
- IPS l.15's "building costs that overrun" also covers estimating error, which a contingency does cover.
- IPS l.15 gives one pot three jobs that AACE would keep apart (Q5-006's clash note).

*What Caplet takes.*
- One sentence in 3b that answers "isn't the building's own budget enough?" and sizes nothing: a builder's contingency leaves out escalation and currency by definition, and Taiwan's grant rules send overruns back to the grantee.
- The name stays "her flexibility". AACE's "management reserve" may be quoted once at most, for judges, because "reserve" is the operating reserve's word.

**Against the frozen work**

None, on four conditions:
- the name stays "her flexibility";
- nothing is sized;

## SC-5. Three one-sided bets around her top (built, then dropped)

**The new thing**

Three asymmetries point the same way:

- a top set low can be topped up later, while one set high cannot be taken back;

With the trade near one-for-one (8o), every asymmetry favours the side that holds money back.

**The aha, in one sentence**

Every asymmetry around her top points to holding back: beating a promise earns nothing, a low promise can be topped up but a high one cannot be taken back, and small buildings overrun far more often than they come in under.
