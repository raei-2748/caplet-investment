## SD-1. Her figure is their target: why co-sponsors get one owned figure, not a margin

    Standards" (2024 update), https://www.case.org/faq-standards

## SD-2. The best-tested fundraising letter is the plan's dullest promise

- **Parts:**
  - **Q22-029** (18, keep; paper read at source): in a 40,000-letter field test, the letter saying a donor had already
    given a grant "to cover all of the overhead costs" drew 8.55% of recipients, against 4.75% when the same lead gift
    was announced as seed, 4.41% with a 1:1 match and 3.36% for the control. Gneezy, Keenan & Gneezy (2014), Science
    346(6209): 632-635, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1253932 (one successful replication recorded, Camerer et al.
    2018).
  - **Q9-016** (25, keep): private art museums that closed had been open a median of ten years, and closure was
    "usually related to financial difficulties, rather than the founder's death". Velthuis and Gera (2025),
    International Journal of Cultural Policy 31(6): 805-818.
  - **Q140-026** (22, keep): UK museums are "far more likely to close before they are ten years old than at any other
    point". Mapping Museums Lab (2025).
  - **Q11b-023** (22, keep): Nonprofit Finance Fund's evaluation for Kresge: facility grantees did not plan "for the
    costs associated with operating new or expanded facilities"; flexible money raises best "packaged alongside exciting
    facility projects". Francis & Johnson (2015), Nonprofit Finance Fund.
- **The new thing:** the field test's lesson is not only that a lead gift helps, but that the same lead money drew far
  more givers when it was described as having paid the costs givers least like to pay. Laura's plan offers both letters
  at once: running money for ten years and a facility figure. The running money covers the first decade, when such
  places are most likely to close, and the costs campaigns forget. So the first sentence of 3d's settled order (the ten
  payments owned, dated to 2042) is its strongest fundraising fact, not a preamble before the figure.
- **The aha, in one sentence:** The plan's least exciting promise is the one the best-known fundraising experiment
  rewards most: told that a lead donor had already paid the running costs, 8.55% of people gave, against 4.75% when the
  same gift was called seed money, and Laura's ten payments are running money owned for the residency's first decade,
  when such places are most likely to close. Surprising to a judge who sees the ten payments as the settled, dull half
  of the case and the facility figure as the whole fundraising story.
- **Why it fits Laura:** IPS l.11 ("none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price, or on anybody else's
  money"); IPS l.17 ("From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve"); case l.83 ("reliable support for its early
  operations"), l.85-86 ("any operating support beyond Laura's commitment, may come from co-sponsors"), l.110-111
  ("signal that the residency is financially viable").
- **The five questions:**
  - *What is it?* Overhead aversion: givers avoid paying for running costs, and telling them someone else has paid them
    lifts giving.
  - *Did it work?* The 2014 field test, read at source, with one replication; the closure records are read at source
    (Q9-016, Q140-026); NFF found campaigns forgot operating money (Q11b-023).
  - *How Laura's case is like it:* $50,000 a year of running money is paid before anyone else is asked, and co-sponsors
    are asked for capital and yearly programme money (SKELETON 3d's two-part ask).
  - *How it is not:* the test's givers were small mail donors to a water charity, and "overhead" is administration, not
    a residency's operations; co-sponsors are institutions; $50,000 a year is "a strong base, not a whole budget"
    (H9-119: small US residencies spent about $111k to $599k), so "running costs covered" overclaims; the test never
    tried seed and running money together; and Taiwan's village grant does pay a year's running costs (Q100-028's note),
    so never say Taiwan's public money will not.
  - *What Caplet takes:* keep 3d's order; the team's sentence gives this fact as the reason; in Laura's words the
    payments are the base the residency starts with, "the first $50,000 of every year for ten years", never "the
    running costs are covered".
- **Against the frozen work:** none (CANON A33). Never "endowment" for the reserve (WORDS).
- **Its weakness:** a nonprofit finance director: "co-sponsors will still be asked for running money from 2033, so the
  overhead-covered frame flatters" (true; the claim is a base owned for ten years). A scientist: one field experiment

- **Where it lands:** 3d, the team's sentence that says why the passage opens with the ten payments; or 3.0, beside
  goldmine 15's placed clause (one of the two, never both). Client Knowledge; Investment Strategy.

## SD-3. First money in a field of last money

- **Parts:**
  - **Q11c-019** (20, keep): Arts Council England withholds its first capital payment until 100% of the partnership
    funding is "confirmed in writing": "No, 100% of the funding must be confirmed before the project can begin". Arts
    Council England (n.d.), Creative Foundations Fund Round 1 guidance.
  - **Q11c-021** (18, keep): Washington State's Building for the Arts pays at most a third, by reimbursement, and "you
    may start drawing down funds only after all other money needed to complete the project has been raised".
    Washington State Department of Commerce (2022).
  - **Q11a-004** (22, keep): with a high fixed cost, giving in sequence raised the rate at which the project was provided
    from 40% to 76%; with a low fixed cost the order did not matter. Bracha, Menietti & Vesterlund (2011), Journal of
    Public Economics 95(5-6): 416-427, https://sites.pitt.edu/~vester/jpube2011.pdf
  - **Q11a-002** (21, keep): seed money announced at 67% of the goal, against 10%, raised contributions "nearly
    sixfold" for a good worth nothing until fully paid. List & Lucking-Reiley (2002), Journal of Political Economy
    110(1): 215-233.
  - Linked by **Q11c-026** (20, keep; Keel inference from the rules): two funders cannot both be last money, so
    conditional pledges need one figure that asks nothing and one close date.
- **The new thing:** the public capital funders a building campaign most needs are written to pay last, after every
  other pound or dollar is confirmed; the experiments say a lumpy project is built far more often when someone goes
  first. So someone must be unconditional, and for most givers that is exactly the over-promise the case warns about
  (l.114). Only money already owned can go first without that risk. The IPS's move before the meetings is what lets
  Laura be the campaign's first money, and the December 2032 condition lets every other pledge stay conditional without
  the funders waiting on each other.
- **The aha, in one sentence:** Every large capital funder's rulebook is written so that it pays last, and two funders
  that each insist on paying last wait for each other forever; a building gets built when someone goes first (seed money
  announced at two-thirds of the goal drew a "nearly sixfold" rise in gifts), and the only figure that can go first
  without conditions and without risking a broken promise is one already held in Treasuries. Surprising to a judge who
  thinks the hard part of the 2031 meetings is persuading co-sponsors to give: the harder part is that their own rules
  stop them giving first.
- **Why it fits Laura:** IPS l.15 ("whatever figure you choose is money you already own"); IPS l.11 ("or on anybody
  else's money"); case l.110-112 and l.114-115; SKELETON 3d ("Laura's figure first and unconditional"; "Laura first
  among named sources").
- **The five questions:**
  - *What is it?* Last-money rules in public capital grants, and the economics of giving in sequence to a project with a
    high fixed cost.
  - *Did it work?* The two rules are read at source; the lab result (40% to 76%) is read in full; the sixfold field
    result is read in full.
  - *How Laura's case is like it:* a residency building is the high-fixed-cost case; a campaign for it will meet funders
    who come last by rule (Kresge's challenge "is paid in full only when the campaign succeeds completely", Q11c-016;
    Taiwan's venue grant leaves overruns to the owner, Q11d-007).
  - *How it is not:* England and Washington are not Taiwan, and Taiwan's village grant sets no ratio for a private
    applicant (Q9c-003), so never "match" or "anchor" for Taiwan's funders; the deadlock is Keel's inference, and no
    source describes one; in List & Lucking-Reiley the seed came from the charity, not a founder; Laura's share of the
    goal cannot be stated (case l.164-166).
  - *What Caplet takes:* in the team's sentence before Laura's words: her figure is unconditional because it is owned,
    and the December 2032 condition is said of the co-sponsors' pledges only (Q147-024, placed); she never conditions
    her own figure and never offers a match.
- **Against the frozen work:** none; it is IPS l.15 read from the co-sponsors' side. Never "backstop", "anchor" or
  "match" for co-sponsors (WORDS; BOLD_THESES rejected list).
- **Its weakness:** a fundraiser: lead donors often pledge without conditions from future income (Kresge counts written

- **Source chain:** Arts Council England guidance -> card Q11c row 19 -> store Q11c-019; Washington State Department of
  Commerce (2022) guidelines -> card Q11c row 21 -> Q11c-021; rows 19, 21, 22 and card Q11b row 32 -> Q11c-026 (Keel
  inference) -> "two cannot both be last"; Bracha, Menietti & Vesterlund 2011 (pitt.edu PDF) -> card Q11a row 4 ->

- **Where it lands:** 3d, the team's sentence on why her figure comes first and without conditions (3b already carries

## SD-6. Her word takes the Treasury's credit, not a writer's

    installments of significant advances". Publishers Weekly, 10 Apr 2020.

- **The new thing:** a pledge carries the credit of whoever stands behind it: Marvel's pledge carried Marvel's. A writer's
  pledge from future income would carry her career's, which IPS l.9 says comes "later and smaller" in the very downturn

  way to swap a promisor's credit for the Treasury's: set aside Treasuries that pay the promise, and the promise is then
  rated as the United States, not as the city. The IPS's move does the same for Laura's word: after it, a co-sponsor

  payments only: it also hedges what co-sponsors have to believe.
