## SA-1. A career's risk cannot be sold, passed on or insured, so the portfolio is the only place to counterbalance it

**Parts:**
- Q134-030, Q134-031, Q134-032. Fantex is the one firm found that floated shares in people's earnings. It postponed
  its first offer after the player's "season-ending back surgery" (12 Nov 2013) and floated one in April 2014. Its
  insiders bought about half of its last offering (July 2016), and it deregistered on 27 Jan 2017. Sources: Fantex
  S-1, 8-K, DEF 14C and Form 15 (sec.gov); NYT DealBook, 12 Nov 2013.
- Q134-027. US regulators treat income share agreements, a sold share of future pay, as credit: "regardless of the
  name on the label, these products are credit" (CFPB consent order 2021-CFPB-0005, 7 Sep 2021). Purdue paused new
  agreements in June 2022 (Q134-028).
- Q134-034. No market insures a trade's income. Shiller's "livelihood insurance" was only a proposal (Taipei Times,
  29 Mar 2006), and "Because no such market existed, the government acted instead as social insurer" (Abramowicz,
  *University of Chicago Legal Forum*).
- Q134-029 and Q134-025, on why. Adverse selection "has unraveled private markets for these products" (Herbst and
  Hendren 2024, *AER*, read through Microeconomic Insights). And a career "would not be comparable" to a bond because
  it depends on "the co-operativeness of the original borrower" (Friedman 1955).
- Q1-022. Households that moved into volatile-wage industries cut their share of risky assets "by up to 35%"
  (Betermier, Jansson, Parlour and Walden 2012, Riksbank WP 255). The lever people do pull is what they hold.
- Q134-001 and Q134-040, the honest edge. Finished books' royalties do sell, at about 5.5 years' income (Royalty
  Exchange, 20 Dec 2021). What cannot be sold is work not yet done.

**The new thing.** Q134-040 sorted out what can be sold. This asks the next question: how could a career's risk be
moved off its owner? Three routes exist: sell shares in it, sell a slice of future pay, or insure it. The attempts at
the first closed, the second was ruled credit, and the third was never built. If the risk cannot leave her, the only
lever left is what she holds, and real households pull it. That turns IPS l.9's "one you cannot sell" from a phrase
into a finding. It also shows why the pitch's "So we hedge it!" (IPS l.3) can only mean l.9's counterbalance, since
nothing on sale pays out when authors' income falls.

**The aha, in one sentence:** Markets have tried three ways to take a career's risk off its owner (shares in a
person's earnings, a sold share of future pay, insurance on a trade's income), and the attempts found closed, were
ruled credit or were never built, so the one place left to counterbalance Laura's career is what her portfolio holds.
A finance judge knows human capital as theory (Mayers's "nonmarketable assets", Q134-035) but rarely knows that a
market in it was tried and shut within four years. A lay judge learns that "a stock she can't sell" is literally true.

**Why it fits Laura.** IPS l.9: "In effect your career is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell. And
so, we believe, your portfolio should be a counterbalance to that". IPS l.3, quoted as the IPS's own (CANON A1). Case
l.44-45: the 2028 deposit comes from "publishing advances, speaking engagements, licensing, and other entrepreneurial
ventures", mostly work not yet done. Case l.64-65: "she will neither add to nor withdraw from the portfolio before
2033".

**The five questions:**
- *What is it:* the market's own record on shedding a career's risk, read from securities filings, a consumer-finance
  order, an insurance proposal and household data.
- *Did it work:* none of the routes has lasted. Fantex deregistered in 2017 (Q134-032). Income share agreements are
  treated as credit, and Purdue's programme paused (Q134-027, Q134-028). Livelihood insurance was never built
  (Q134-034). The portfolio lever does work for households: up to 35% fewer risky assets (Q1-022).
- *How Laura is like it:* her next books, talks and licences pay the 2028 deposit, and nobody will buy them, lend
  against them on fair terms or insure them. She also moved from a salaried job to working for herself (case p.1;
  Q1-022's use).
- *How she is not:* Fantex's careers were athletes', whose risk is injury, not the economy (Q134-030 critique).
  Income share agreements are education finance. Her finished books' royalties can be sold (Q134-001), so the
  unsellable part is future work, which is most of what pays the deposit but not all of it (Q134-040 note). "Never
  built" holds only as of the sources' dates, and new attempts were tried in 2024 and 2026 (Q134-040 note).
- *What Caplet takes:* the counterbalance has to sit in what she holds, and the plan puts it there: promised money in
  Treasuries (IPS l.11), and shares bought only with money not yet promised.

**Against the frozen work:** none. It supports CANON A1-A2. Guards: "hedge" stays the pitch's quoted word, never
Keel's (SKELETON 2.3; Q134-034 note). Never "the only legal way to sell a career's future income is to borrow against
it" (Q134-027 note).

**Its weakness.** A judge can answer that she has no need to sell the career, because being able to work more is
itself insurance, so the textbook says she should take more risk (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson, Q39-003). The reply is
the case's own rule: no money goes in or out before 2033 except the two deposits (case l.64-65; Q1-047), so working
more after a bad market cannot reach the plan before 2033. A second weakness: the evidence on the closed routes comes
from US athletes and students, not from authors.

## SA-2. IPS l.9 is a pension regulator's covenant test, with Laura as her own sponsor

**Parts:**
- Q7-023. The UK funding code for defined-benefit pensions allows "more risk ... if a scheme is a long way from
  reaching the relevant date", but only within a "reliability period", while trustees "can be reasonably certain of
  the employer's cash flow" (The Pensions Regulator, "Journey planning").
- H2-013. The regulator says the sponsor "underwrites investment risks and funding risk". It warns that comparing
  sponsor and investments "may show that there are common drivers of both" (its example is a house builder whose
  scheme holds property), and it tests a downturn "also on the support available from the employer covenant" (TPR,
  *Integrated risk management*, Dec 2015, Example 8; DB funding code 2024, para 28).
- Q7-022. UK law's end state: assets "highly resilient to short-term adverse changes in market conditions so that
  further employer contributions are not expected to be required" (SI 2024/462, reg 5).
- Q100-017, Q100-030 and Q100-041. Taiwan's founder-run museums ran on the next year's income. Hong-Gah's founder
  said 「我一年一定要去賺一千四佰萬來給這邊用」 ("I must earn NT$14m a year to give here"; 陳嬋娟 2003). IT Park
  went on "rest" in October 2025 after 37 years, when digital photography ended the trade that paid for it (ARTouch).
  Four of the thirteen institutions found stopped when the income behind them stopped (cases found, not a census).
- Q134-039 and Q7-031. The plan relies on her career once, at a known date, and after January 2028 a missed deposit
  can no longer happen.

**The new thing.** The regulator works with two dials: time, which allows risk, and the sponsor, which limits it.
Read with those two dials, IPS l.9's opening is the whole covenant analysis. Its first sentence turns the dial that
allows risk: "6 years to wait" is the time to the relevant date. The next sentences turn the dial that refuses it: a
sponsor whose cash comes "later and smaller" in a recession, "during that same period" that shares fall, which is the
regulator's "common drivers". The sponsor is Laura. Taiwan's founder-run museums show the other design, a scheme with
no fund that calls on its sponsor every year. Laura's plan calls on hers once, in January 2028. From then on it holds
what UK law calls the end state for the ten.

**The aha, in one sentence:** IPS l.9's opening sentences are the two questions Britain's pension regulator asks
before it lets a scheme take risk (how long until the payments, and whether the sponsor's cash falls in the same
downturn as the assets), with Laura as her own sponsor; Taiwan's founder-run museums called on their sponsors every
year, and her plan calls on hers once, in January 2028. A pensions or LDI judge finds a regulator's covenant reasoning
in the first paragraph of a student's letter. A lay judge finds her career standing in as the "employer" behind her
own plan, and in Taiwan's record a founder's earnings really were a museum's income.

**Why it fits Laura.** IPS l.9 in full. IPS l.11: the money for the payments "will go straight into Treasuries". IPS
l.13: the 2028 deposit completes the ten. IPS l.17: a missed deposit is one of the two reasons. Case l.44-45: the
deposit comes from her earnings. Case l.64-65: no other money goes in or out before 2033. Case l.69-73: "willing to
take thoughtful risks", and "an appropriate balance between pursuing growth and protecting the capital required for
her goals".

**The five questions:**
- *What is it:* the UK's integrated risk management for defined-benefit pensions. Covenant, investment and funding
  are judged together, with risk allowed by time and limited by how far the sponsor can be relied on.
- *Did it work:* it is current law and code (SI 2024/462 in force 6 Apr 2024; the code applies to valuations from 22
  Sep 2024, Q7-022), and the warning about common drivers is the regulator's own worked example. Taiwan's record shows
  the other design failing: four of the thirteen founder-run institutions found stopped when the income behind them
  stopped (Q100-041), IT Park the latest (Q100-030).
- *How Laura is like it:* a fixed schedule of payments (the ten), a sponsor whose next contribution is earned rather
  than owned (the 2028 deposit), and a sponsor whose cash can fall in the same downturn as shares (IPS l.9; cards Q1,
  Q1b, Q12).
- *How she is not:* a UK sponsor can be asked for more, and the code trusts employers for three to six years
  (H2-013 critique). The case allows Laura no more money (Q7-023 critique), so the regulator's reasoning flatters her
  plan. These are UK employer schemes, not a US individual, so the analogy is a standard of reasoning and never a
  legal claim. Taiwan's cases are the ones found, not a census, and two of the closures had other causes too.
- *What Caplet takes:* the plan's dates are a covenant decision. Most of each payment is bought in January 2027 with
  money already owned (the first deposit). The rest is bought in January 2028 with the deposit's first dollars (the
  top-up and the extra Treasuries), after which the ten need no further deposit whatever markets do. Read as a
  sponsor, her career is called on once, at a known date and for a known size.

**Against the frozen work:** none. IPS l.9's own reason (a writer's income, CANON A3) stays first. The regulator is a
lens on that reason, never a replacement for it. Neither reserve row is rebuilt: the regulator's test is added as the
reason, and l.9 as its text.

**Its weakness.** A pensions judge will say the code's reliability period is measured in years and concerns a
company's solvency, not the timing of one person's income. A strong covenant would let a scheme take more risk, and a
bestselling author might claim one (Q12-006: in 2009 the cuts fell on midlist and new authors, not proven sellers).
The reply is that the plan needs her covenant to be uncertain at one date, not weak, and IPS l.9 is the team's
reason; the regulator's dials only show that it is the reasoning a regulator would ask for. The cold-test record is a
second weakness: "Taiwan's founders" fell from 3.7 to 3.0 once its claims were qualified ("The careful hedging made me trust it, but not
lean on it"), so Taiwan stays one clause.

## SA-3. Pooling leaves the author holding the recession

**Parts:**
- Q127-012. A publisher can promise floors because publishing is "a 'portfolio business'"; "only 35 out of 100 books
  turn a profit" (US v. Bertelsmann opinion, pp.5-6, justice.gov).
- Q1-030. In 2009 UK advances were "reduced to a quarter" (one example: £120,000 to £30,000) while sales were "only
  down by about 5 per cent" (Times Online, 11 Jun 2009, via hnn.us).
- Q12-004. In 2020 Penguin Random House "lengthened the period of payment on the first installments of significant
  advances" (Publishers Weekly, 10 Apr 2020).
- H6-028 and H6-009. Insurers exclude correlated risks ("they have to exclude it, because those are correlated
  risks"), and pooling works "only because they don't all burn down at the same time" (Shiller, ECON 252).
- H1-081 (spine row 2). Ten promises, each 99% safe and from separate issuers, would miss at least one about 1 time
  in 10, so the ten share one issuer on purpose.
- Q12-006, the edge. In 2009 the cuts fell on new deals for midlist and new authors, not on proven sellers.

**The new thing.** The store already says that a publisher can promise floors because it pools, and Laura cannot
(Q127-012; 2.3 reservoir item (1); the AHA candidates). Set beside insurance, the pool also explains the shape of the
risk left with her. A pool absorbs the risk that one book fails. It cannot absorb the risk that the economy does, so
it passes that part to authors: in 2009 as cuts (sales about 5% down, new advances down to a quarter), and in 2020 as
delay. The part of a writer's career the pool hands back is therefore the economy-wide part, the same part that moves
share prices. IPS l.9's "large holding of shares" is what remains after her own trade's pooling, and no pool will take
it back. That is why Laura's promises are owned rather than pooled.

**The aha, in one sentence:** Her publisher can carry a flop but not a recession, so in 2009 UK sales fell about 5%
while new advances fell to a quarter: pooling leaves an author holding exactly the part of her career that moves with
the economy, the kind of risk insurers exclude, and that is the "large holding of shares" IPS l.9 counterbalances. A
publishing judge sees the advance as the pool's shock absorber. A finance judge sees an author's income as the
undiversifiable residue of a diversified firm.

**Why it fits Laura.** IPS l.9: "In a recession they come later and smaller", and "a large holding of shares, and one
you cannot sell". IPS l.11: no promise depends "on anybody else's money". Case l.44-45: the deposit comes from
advances, speaking and licensing.

**The five questions:**
- *What is it:* how pooled institutions (a publisher, an insurer) price risk and pass it on.
- *Did it work:* for the pool, yes. Publishers stay solvent through flops (35 of 100 books profitable; "the top 4
  percent of profitable titles generate 60 percent of profitability", Q127-012), and in a downturn they protect
  themselves by cutting and delaying new advances (Q1-030, Q12-004).
- *How Laura is like it:* her income arrives as advances on contract dates (Q12-001), set by a pooled payer.
- *How she is not:* Laura is "bestselling" (case p.1), and in 2009 proven sellers were protected (Q12-006). Signed
  advances were stretched, not cancelled (Q12-004). The 2009 contrast rests on one UK article. Her deposit also draws
  on speaking and licensing, whose payers are not publishers.
- *What Caplet takes:* why the ten and the bottom are owned in one issuer's bonds rather than spread across promises
  (spine row 2), and why the career's risk reaches the plan through one dated deposit.

**Against the frozen work:** none. It supports IPS l.9's "later and smaller". Guards: say "in the same downturn",
never "together" or "in the same months" (card Q50; Q1b-028). Never "the link is strong for top earners" of authors.

**Its weakness.** One article carries the contrast between 5% and a quarter, and the finding cuts against the case's
own word for her ("bestselling"). A publishing judge will add that a firm with 35 profitable books in 100 is also
exposed to the economy and did cut its own costs.

**Where it lands:** 2.3 (Investment Strategy; Client Knowledge), as the reason behind spine row 2, one clause at

**Figures:** none of the plan's. Store facts: about 5% and a quarter (Q1-030); 35 of 100 (Q127-012). Spine row 2's

## SA-4. Illinois's three ways to fail, set against Laura's plan: from January 2028 none of them can break a promise she has made

**Parts:**
- Q126-028. College Illinois wrote that its promise rested on "(i) continued Contract sales within projections; (ii)
  achieving a projected annual net return on Fund investments; and (iii) actual tuition/fee increases being within
  projected amounts" (Illinois Auditor General, FY2011 audit).
- Q126-020. Washington's plan "depends on people continuing to purchase GET units" (OSA 2009), so new money was part
  of the promise.
- Q126-019. Washington's actuary set a correlation of -0.45 between returns and the next year's tuition, because they
  "tend to go the opposite direction": in 2008 the S&P fell 37.0%, and tuition rose 13.1% in 2009 (OSA 2009).
- Q126-005. Alabama's share-led plan went from 97.6% funded (Sep 2007) to 62.3% (Sep 2009) (PACT reports).
- NUMBERS 8u (card Q109's reverse stress test): for each promise, how it breaks, what would drive it, and the size
  needed.
- Q7-031. After January 2028 a missed deposit can no longer happen.

**The new thing.** The store already applies Illinois's list to the ten alone: "the ten rest on none of the three"
(3a reservoir item (3)). The register already holds Florida's rule as a reserve for 3a. Set against 8u's table,
promise by promise, the same three exposures turn out to exist in Laura's plan: new money is the 2028 deposit, from
her career; returns are the shares; and the bill is the price of the ten before they are bought, plus building costs.
Each lands somewhere different:
- Returns never reach a promise. No fall in any price is in any promise's list of breaking events (8u).
- The price of the ten can break their standard only before they are all bought, and new money only until January
  2028.
- Building costs reach only her flexibility.

So from January 2028 none of Illinois's three can break a promise she has made. What remains is a US default and fund
costs beyond the margin, and neither is on Illinois's list. The state plans could not close their exposures, because
they sold new promises every year and their bill was tuition (Keel inference from Q126-045).

**The aha, in one sentence:** Illinois's prepaid tuition plan wrote down the three things its promise rested on (new
money arriving, investment returns, a bill that could rise), and they stayed open for as long as it sold contracts;
Laura's plan carries the same three, but returns never reach a promise she has made, and the other two close for good
in January 2028, when the last Treasuries behind the ten are bought. A judge who expects a share fund behind a fixed
promise to be the danger (the state plans' story) finds it is the one exposure that cannot break a promise here. The
lay reader of 3e gets one list with dates in place of "some twenty ideas".

**Why it fits Laura.** IPS l.11: none of the ten "will depend on a share price, or on anybody else's money". IPS
l.13: the 2028 deposit completes the ten. IPS l.15: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own", and her
flexibility is for "building costs that overrun". IPS l.17: "A fall in the market is not one of them". Case l.90: each
payment is "a fixed $50,000 and is not adjusted for inflation". Case l.97-98: identify "the assumptions supporting
their recommendation". Case l.147: inflation on "facility costs".

**The five questions:**
- *What is it:* state plans that sold families future tuition for money paid now, invested mostly in shares
  (Alabama's target was 72% shares, Q126-003) and refilled from new buyers.
- *Did it work:* not when the plan was led by shares. Alabama fell from 97.6% to 62.3% funded in two years and cut
  what it paid to Fall 2010 rates (Q126-005, Q126-013). Washington's bill rose 13.1% the year after shares fell 37%
  (Q126-019). Illinois moved to Treasuries matched to its payments in 2024 (Q126-031). Florida, which limited shares
  to its surplus from June 2006, cut no promise (Q126-036, Q126-037; AHA reserve).
- *How Laura is like it:* a fixed promise, money paid in early from earnings, and a share fund in the plan.
- *How she is not:* her bill is fixed in dollars (case l.90), while the prepaid bill was tuition. She makes all her
  promises on known dates and has no new buyers. The state plans were pooled, and some were state-backed (Q126-022,
  Q126-043). Her building costs are a real exposure, but only to her flexibility, which is promised to no one.
- *What Caplet takes:* 3e's three notes can be read as one checklist with dates: which exposure, which promise, and
  until when. It tests l.17's "A fall in the market is not one of them" from the failure end (8u).

**Against the frozen work:** none. Guards:
- The ways a promise breaks and IPS l.17's two reasons to change are two lists, never merged (card Q109; 8u, "The
  two lists"). Never "only two things can break the plan".
- A deposit smaller than the first slice, or a fall of 3.57 points before the ten are bought, breaks the payments'
