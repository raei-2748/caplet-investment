# Reader N

Role: the finance director of a mid-sized nonprofit arts organisation (I work outside fixed income).

## 01, item K

1. Assumption: that a ten-year plan's watch list exists to spot the causes of trouble early (a debt-ceiling fight, her income dropping, a crash) and act on them. Sure: 70.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the useful trigger is the cause; the lines that actually change the plan should be the dated, yes-or-no symptoms (money not received, payment not paid), because those cannot be argued over at a board meeting.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (to my finance committee, which spends whole meetings debating whether a recession is coming instead of agreeing in advance what number makes us act).
6. What made it land: the closing line, "a payment that has not arrived cannot" be argued about, is exactly the difference between our budget-variance triggers that work and the "economic outlook" discussions that never lead anywhere.
7. Overstated or unclear: "only two of its lines can change the plan" is clear, but it leaves unsaid what the other lines are for if they can never change anything; and a payment "more than five business days late" sounds like a trigger that fires after the damage, so I am not sure it is early enough to matter. The Google parallel (Ewaschuk, 2016) is about computer systems that page an engineer in minutes; I can see the logic carries over, but a reader on a funding committee may find it an odd source for a ten-year money plan.
8. Question: When either line trips, what exactly does she do, and by what date, and is any money still within reach to do it?

## 02, item G

1. Assumption: that "half" was picked because it is round and sounds balanced, and that the choice of split materially changes how much money she ends up with. Sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the split is about the size of the pot; it mostly decides which money is promised and which stays free, and anywhere in a fairly wide band gives the same promise.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe (the line "decides what is promised and what stays free, not how much there is" is a good way to explain a board-designated reserve, but I would not repeat the numbers).
6. What made it land: the plain sentence about promised versus free landed; the numbers around it (44.5% to 57.3%, $365, $5,300 per $5,000) arrived too fast for me to hold.
7. Overstated or unclear: "differs by a median of only about $365" is a median, and over two years of share prices the median difference would be small for almost any split; what I care about as a finance director is the bad case, which is not given, so "changes almost nothing" may overstate it. The flat stretch also seems partly made by the rounding to a whole $5,000, which is a presentation choice rather than a property of the money. "Trades about $5,300 of what Laura keeps for each $5,000 more she could state" is unclear to me: why does it cost more than one for one?
8. Question: In a bad two years for shares before the move, how far apart are one split and another in what Laura has left, not at the median but at the low end?

## 03, item H

1. Assumption: that the plan's strength is its written policy, the rules she has agreed to follow. Sure: 65.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the payments are safe because she will stick to her rules; they are safe because the money for them is already bought, so the rules only matter for the handful of things still to be done.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (to our board chair: people break their own pre-agreed stopping rules about half the time, so buy the thing rather than promise to behave).
6. What made it land: "8 of 17" executives breaking a rule they had agreed to is concrete and uncomfortable, and I have watched boards do exactly that with reserve policies.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Laura's ten payments do not rest on anyone keeping a rule" overstates it: they still rest on one rule, do not sell the Treasuries early, and a panicked owner or an adviser could break that one too. Also 17 executives in an experiment is a small base for "the evidence on rules is weaker than that".
8. Question: What, if anything, stops Laura from selling the bought Treasuries before their dates if she needs money or loses her nerve?

## 04, item C

1. Assumption: that one low-cost world index fund is the sensible default, the thing our own investment committee and most nonprofit policies already do, so not a copout at all. Sure: 80.
2. What it means: nothing new on the choice itself; the new part is the framing that picking is worth doing only where the answer comes back quickly and can be checked, which is why the team picked dates and not stocks.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe (the "pick where the answer can be checked" line is a handy answer to a trustee who wants to pick stocks).
6. What made it land or not: the story of a teammate calling it a "copout" is human and believable, but the turn to matching Treasuries to dates felt like a stretch, because choosing a bond that matures before a payment date is arithmetic, not the kind of judgement the research is about.
7. Overstated or unclear: "there a fixed rule does better" is asserted rather than shown; the research cited is about when intuition can be trusted, not about stock pickers' records, which is the evidence I would expect for that claim. "The team did pick where picking works" puts date-matching in the same category as expert judgement, which I do not think it is.
8. Question: Besides the psychology research, what record of stock pickers or active funds did you look at before deciding a fixed rule does better over three years?

## 05, item B

1. Assumption: that the case was set up so that $300,000 comfortably buys ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out, i.e. that this is normally affordable. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that this is normal; on most days since 2000 the first deposit could not have bought all ten, so the plan only works this neatly because 2026 is an unusual year, and the team has built it so it does not need the lucky day, only the lucky year plus the second deposit.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (the same ten payments would have cost $470,217 in August 2020 and about $288,000 in October 2026; that is the clearest picture I have seen of what interest rates do to the cost of a fixed promise, and it is exactly the conversation we have about funding a named endowed position).
6. What made it land or not: the 2020 figure against both deposits together landed hard; then the paragraph kept going with a dozen more figures and dates, and by the last sentence I had lost the thread.
7. Overstated or unclear: the last sentence, "that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027", has two conditions stacked and I had to read it three times; I think it means the plan fails only if rates drop 3.6 points in the next few weeks, which is very reassuring, but it does not say so. "Commissions came to $200" and "Thursday 19 October 2023" are trivia beside the main point. "Exactly one day" across nineteen years depends on the team's own measure ("always the same distance ahead"), which I cannot judge.
8. Question: If rates had been at 2020 levels in January 2027, how much of the 2028 deposit would the ten have eaten, and what would have been left for the building?

## 06, item D

1. Assumption: that the plan's two reasons to change course are its emergency exits, the conditions under which she bails out. Sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the rule is a way out; by naming only two non-market events, it is really a way of keeping her in, so that neither a crash nor a boom becomes an excuse to tinker.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe (the point that "only" shuts out a boom as firmly as a crash is a good one for our board, which is always tempted to spend into a good year).
6. What made it land or not: the reversal (a rule written against leaving, not against staying) is clean, but I had half of it already from the earlier item about watching symptoms rather than causes, so it felt like the same point from a second side.
7. Overstated or unclear: "staying put costs nothing" is true for the ten payments but not for the shares that have not yet moved: if shares crash before 2031, staying put does cost her part of the top of the range, so the sentence needs "for the payments". "Most written exit rules guard against staying in too long" is a broad claim backed by two examples.
8. Question: If shares fall hard in 2030, just before the move, is waiting to move them allowed, or does the move happen on its date regardless, and who decides?

## 07, item T

1. Assumption: that when a donor signs a pledge and shows the account behind it, the statement will read at least the pledged sum on the day she signs. Sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that a statement below the pledge means a shortfall; the account reads about $3,300 short on the day because Treasuries are priced below what they will pay, and the honest thing is to show both the day's value and what it will pay by the date.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (to our development director: this is the mirror of what we already do when we book a multi-year pledge at less than its face value, and it is the first time I have seen a donor's side explained that way).
6. What made it land: it answers the exact question I would ask on reading her broker statement, "why does this say less than she signed for?", before I ask it, and the side-by-side display is something I can picture in a campaign file.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Nothing is missing" is true only if the fund is held to its end and pays as expected; a fund is not a single bond and can pay out a little differently, so I would want "is never under about $117,700" qualified by who covers any gap. "Passes $115,000 in late August 2031" is more precise than a 2026 projection can be.
8. Question: If the fund pays out less than $115,000 in December 2032, who makes up the difference, and does the signed letter say so in writing?

## 08, item M

1. Assumption: that a share slump and a building-cost spike are fairly independent, and if anything that construction gets cheaper in a downturn because contractors are hungry for work. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the two risks offset or stay apart; after both big share slumps since 2000, a Taiwan building ended up about a quarter dearer in US dollars, so one bad episode could use all of the money she is keeping free.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (to our capital-campaign committee: after both big share crashes since 2000 a Taiwan building cost about a quarter more in US dollars, once through steel and once through the currency, so "the market is down, construction will be cheap" is not something to count on).
6. What made it land: it is dated and specific, and it ends by refusing to call the free money a hedge, which made me trust the rest of it more.
7. Overstated or unclear: the cover of "about 25% ... if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed" is paired with an episode that starts with shares more than 40% down; in exactly that episode the shares would not have earned 7.5%, so I suspect her flexibility would cover less than 25% then, and the item does not say how much less. "Both ... were followed by" rests on two episodes with two different causes, which is a pattern of timing, not a link, and should be said that way. And 7.5% a year looks high to me against the return assumptions our own investment committee uses.
8. Question: In the 2000-03 replay itself, with her shares more than 40% down at the 2031 meetings, what rise in building costs could her flexibility actually cover?

## Items that clash or repeat

- K and D repeat: both explain the same two reasons to change course as dated, non-market events; K calls them symptoms rather than causes, D calls them a rule against leaving rather than a stop-loss. One of them is enough.
- H and D partly repeat: both end on "the payments are already bought, so no nerve is needed".
- D and M sit uneasily: D says "staying put costs nothing", while M shows a share slump before 2031 can eat the money kept free; D needs "for the payments" to hold.
- G and M sit uneasily: G says the split changes almost nothing at the median, while M shows that in a bad episode the free half is what gets used up, so the low end of G's comparison matters more than its median.

## One item for the co-sponsors

T, because it tells co-sponsors in advance why her statement will read under the signed sum and shows them what to look at instead, which is the question their own finance staff will raise first.

## One item for the limits

M, because it names a real, dated sequence (a share slump then a cost burst) that could use up all of her flexibility, which is exactly what a limits section should admit, though its cover figure should be worked out for the slump case rather than at 7.5% a year.
