# Reader X

Role: a programme officer at a foundation that funds arts buildings, who reads founders' pledges before a capital
campaign.

## 01, item T

1. Assumption: a founder's pledge is "backed" when the account statement she shows us is at or above the pledged sum
   on the day she signs; a statement below the pledge is a gap she still has to fill. How sure: 75.
2. What it means: a statement can sit a few thousand dollars under the pledge and still be fully backed, because the
   Treasuries are priced at a discount to what they will pay on their date, so the honest test is what the holding
   pays by the payment date, not what it would sell for today.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes (to colleagues who read pledge statements; we flag exactly this kind of shortfall).
6. What made it land: the concrete $111,700 against a signed $115,000, with the date it crosses ($115,000 by late
   August 2031) and the payout figure ($117,700) side by side, is precisely the page I would otherwise have queried.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Nothing is missing" is said of a fund, not of individual bonds held to maturity; a bond fund
   that ends on a set date pays out what its holdings pay less its costs, so "never under about $117,700" needs the
   word "fund" and its fees beside it, and I would want to know whether $117,700 is net of the fund's expense ratio.
   "What the fund's Treasuries will pay" is not the same as "what the fund will pay her", and a careful reader will
   notice.
8. Question: who states the "will pay by December 2032" figure on the page co-sponsors see (the broker, the fund
   sponsor or Laura herself), and if the fund pays out less than $115,000, who makes up the difference and how is that
   written into the letter?

## 02, item B

1. Assumption: that $300,000 is roughly what ten $50,000 payments "cost" in safe bonds, give or take, in most years,
   so the first deposit simply buys them. How sure: 55.
2. What it means: the first deposit alone buys all ten on only about one day in ten since 2000, so the plan is not
   leaning on a lucky entry price but on the second deposit to finish the job, whatever the price turns out to be in
   January 2027.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe (the $470,217 in August 2020, more than both deposits, is the one I would quote).
6. What made it land or not: the August 2020 figure lands hard, but the paragraph then piles up dates (19 October
   2023, 5 October 2026, May 2002, 27 February) until I lose which number answers my question.
7. Overstated or unclear: the last sentence, "that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields
   fall about 3.6 points before January 2027", stacks two conditions ("even if" and "unless") and I had to read it
   three times; I still am not sure whether 3.6 points is a fall that would break the plan or one it survives. "Built
   to need the year, not the day" reads as a slogan rather than a finding. "Exactly one day ... Thursday 19 October
   2023" is precise trivia that does not change anything for a funder. The $200 of commissions against a $37,625
   swing feels like a point made for a different audience.
8. Question: had 2027 been a 2020-like year, how much of the 2028 deposit would have gone to completing the ten, and
   what would then have been left to put toward the building?

## 03, item D

1. Assumption: that the two named reasons are escape hatches, the conditions under which the founder may walk back
   what she has promised. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had it backwards; the "only two reasons" are written to keep her in, so a market crash (or a
   boom) is expressly not a reason to change course, and the only triggers are money failing to arrive.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes (to a board member who asks whether a founder will reopen her pledge after a bad
   year in the markets).
6. What made it land: the turn "they face the other way", followed by "a rise in share prices as firmly as a fall",
   is exactly the reassurance a funder wants, and it is stated as a rule rather than an intention.
7. Overstated or unclear: "staying put costs nothing" is true for the ten payments, but the half of the shares kept as
   her flexibility still falls in a crash, so something is lost even if nothing promised is. "Most written exit rules
   guard against staying in too long" is a broad generalisation offered without a source. And I am not told what
   happens next if either reason does occur: changing course to what?
8. Question: if one of the two reasons does occur (say the 2028 deposit never arrives), which promise gives way first,
   the ten payments or the building contribution, and is that order written down before the meetings?

## 04, item G

1. Assumption: that "half" was a round number picked for simplicity, and that moving more would mean a bigger pledge
   and moving less a smaller one, in proportion. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: the split does not change how much money Laura ends up with, only how much of it is promised to us
   and how much she keeps free, and a wide band around half gives the same stated top.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe (the near one-for-one trade, about $5,300 of her cushion for each $5,000 more
   stated, is the part I would use when a campaign chair asks her to stretch).
6. What made it land or not: the closing trade-off lands for a funder, because her free half is also what covers
   overruns on our building, but the 44.5%-57.3% band reads as a rounding effect more than an insight.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Half sits on a flat stretch" is mostly a consequence of rounding the top down to a whole
   $5,000; the flat stretch is in the stated figure, not in the money, so "moving the line within it changes almost
   nothing" holds for the letter, not for her balance. "Differs by a median of only about $365 between one split and
   the plan's" does not say which other split; I cannot tell if that is 44.5%, 57.3% or every split.
8. Question: from a co-sponsor's side, why should we want her to keep half free rather than state, say, $120,000,
   and what exactly does that free half cover on our building before anyone else is asked for more?

## 05, item C

1. Assumption: that a single broad index fund is the ordinary, sensible default (it is what most endowment committees
   I sit near would do), so keeping it needs no defence. How sure: 80.
2. What it means: mostly nothing new for me; the one reframing is that the team did use judgement, but on the part
   of the plan where the answer can be checked (which bond pays which January), and declined it where it cannot.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe (the line "picked where picking works" is a good answer to a donor who wants a
   founder to be seen managing actively).
6. What made it land or not: the internal disagreement told as it happened makes it human, but the conclusion is the
   one I already held, so it confirms rather than turns.
7. Overstated or unclear: "there a fixed rule does better" is stronger than the research supports; a broad fund does
   better than most pickers after costs, on average, not in every case. Calling the matching of bonds to dates
   "picking" or judgement is a stretch: it reads as arithmetic, and a sceptical reader may say so. Two academic
   citations in a paragraph about a teenager's objection feel heavy.
8. Question: when half the shares move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings, is the date and the amount fixed by
   rule in advance, or will someone be using judgement then, in a falling market?

## 06, item K

1. Assumption: that a long plan's watch list should track the likely causes of trouble (debt-ceiling fights, a slump
   in her income, a market crash) and act on early warning. How sure: 65.
2. What it means: the plan watches causes but lets only two plain facts (money that has not arrived) change anything,
   because a fact can be checked on a date and a forecast can be argued about indefinitely.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe (the Google engineers' "symptoms, not causes" is a parallel I have not heard used
   for a giving plan, and it is easy to retell).
6. What made it land or not: the closing line, "a payment that has not arrived cannot" be argued about, lands; but it
   says much the same as the item on the two reasons to change course, so on its own it adds the outside parallel
   rather than a new fact.
7. Overstated or unclear: the parallel is a stretch in one respect: an alert on a server pages someone to act within
   minutes, while a deposit missing on 31 March 2028 is something Laura would know about well before that date, so the
   "symptom" is not really a surprise to her. More important for me, the risks to the building (costs rising, a
   stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short) are not among the two lines that can change the plan, and the
   item does not say who watches those or what follows.
8. Question: which line on the watch list would tell co-sponsors early that the building's share is under pressure
   (costs or the exchange rate), and when and how would we hear about it?

## 07, item M

1. Assumption: that a founder's free money and a building's cost overruns are roughly independent, so a cushion of
   free shares is a reasonable overrun reserve. How sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had it backwards: the two worst share slumps since 2000 were each followed by a Taiwan building
   about a quarter dearer in US dollars, so the very episode that shrinks her free shares is the one most likely to
   call on them, and one such episode could take all of it.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes (to our capital committee, as the reason we ask about a founder's overrun reserve
   separately from her pledge).
6. What made it land: it is the risk that hits my building, it is told with two dated episodes and two different
   causes (steel, then the currency), and it ends by refusing to call the cushion a hedge, which made me trust it.
7. Overstated or unclear: "covers a rise of about 25% ... if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed" sits awkwardly in a
   paragraph about shares falling more than 40%; in the very scenario described, the 7.5% does not hold, so the 25%
   cover is the figure for the good case, and I would want the figure for the bad one. "Both world-share slumps ...
   were followed by" rests on two episodes with different causes, so it is a warning, not a pattern, and should be
   said that way. "The point that matches Laura's 2031 meetings" is unclear: does the slump fall before her shares
   move, cutting both halves, or only the half she keeps?
8. Question: if a fall like 2000-03 comes just before the 2031 meetings and building costs then rise a quarter, how
   much of that rise could her free money still cover, and what does she tell co-sponsors about the rest?

## 08, item H

1. Assumption: that a written policy with agreed rules is what makes a founder's plan dependable; it is why we ask
   founders for an investment or gift policy before a campaign. How sure: 70.
2. What it means: I had the weight in the wrong place: agreed rules are broken about half the time even by
   executives, so what makes the ten payments dependable is that the money is already bought and held, not that anyone
   has promised to follow a rule.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes (it changes what I would ask a founder for: the holdings, not only the policy).
6. What made it land: the plain count, 8 of 17, with the researchers' own admission that nothing they tried fully
   fixed it, then the short turn to "each is already bought".
7. Overstated or unclear: "Laura's ten payments do not rest on anyone keeping a rule" goes a step too far: Treasuries
   held in her account can still be sold before their date, so the payments do rest on one rule, "do not sell them",
   even if it is the easiest rule to keep. One laboratory study of 17 executives is thin support for "the evidence on
   rules is weaker than that". And the building contribution, which is what I fund, rests partly on the 2031 move,
   which is a rule; the item says so, but only in passing.
8. Question: what, beyond Laura's own intention, keeps the Treasuries for the payments and the building from being
   sold early (a separate account, a letter to the broker, a trustee), and would co-sponsors be told of it?

## Figure recall (from memory)

- B: $470,217, what the ten payments would have cost in August 2020, more than both of her deposits together.
- C: none.
- D: five business days (a Treasury payment that late is one of the two reasons to change course).
- G: about $5,300 of what she keeps for each $5,000 more she could state.
- H: 8 of 17 executives broke the rule they had agreed to.
- K: none of its own (its 31 March 2028 and five business days are the same as D's).
- M: about a quarter dearer in US dollars (about 27% after 2000-03, about 24% after 2008-09).
- T: about $111,700 on the statement against the $115,000 she signs for.

## Two items I would still remember after 40 reports

- M: it is the risk that lands on my building, and it shows the founder's cushion and our overrun risk can be hit by
  the same slump, which almost no founder's pledge I read says out loud.
- T: a statement below the signed figure that is still fully backed is exactly the page that would otherwise stop a
  pledge at our committee, and the item answers it before it is asked.

## Items that clash or repeat

- D and K repeat: both rest on the same two triggers (a missed 2028 deposit, a Treasury payment five business days
  late) and both make the same point that only money failing to arrive can change the plan; K adds only the outside
  parallel.
- D and H pull against each other: D presents the two-reason rule as a strength that keeps her in during a crash,
  while H says written rules are broken about half the time; they are reconciled only if the report says plainly that
  the payments do not depend on the rule.
- G and M sit uneasily: G says half sits on a flat stretch and the split hardly matters, while M says one bad episode
  could use up all of the free half, which makes me ask whether half is enough rather than whether it is optimal.

## One item for the co-sponsors

T: it tells co-sponsors in advance why her statement will read a little under her signed figure and what to look at
instead, which is the one thing that would otherwise make us doubt the letter.

## One item for the limits

M: it names the one limit that matters most to a building funder, that a share slump and a cost or currency burst
can come together and use up her whole cushion, and it refuses to call the cushion a hedge.
