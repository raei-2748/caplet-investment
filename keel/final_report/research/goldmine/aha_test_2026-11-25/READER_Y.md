# Reader Y: a finance professor who teaches fixed income

## 01, item C

1. Assumption: that one cheap world index fund is simply the default for a growth sleeve of this size, not a decision anyone needs to defend. Sure: 85.
2. What it means: nothing I had backwards; it recasts the index fund as a use of judgement (knowing where judgement fails) rather than a lack of it, and recasts the Treasury matching as the place where the team's choices can be graded.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the reframe is neat, but the "picking" on the Treasury side is cash-flow dedication, which any fixed-income student would call mechanical rather than judgement, so the symmetry feels more rhetorical than real.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "there a fixed rule does better": the evidence that most active stock pickers trail an index after fees (the SPIVA-type and Fama-French-type results) would carry this better than Kahneman and Klein, whose work is about when intuition can be trusted, not about stock selection; as written it reads as borrowing a psychology result to make a market-efficiency claim.
   - "a choice whose answer is known and can be checked against printed dates the day it is bought": the maturity dates can be checked, but the real choices on that side (bond vs. date-ended fund, what happens to cash between a December fund end or a November maturity and the January payment, how coupons are reinvested) are not settled by a printed date, so "the answer is known" overstates it.
   - "matched each of the ten Treasuries to the January it pays": a Treasury does not pay in January here; it ends before the January payment, so there is a short reinvestment or holding gap.
8. Question for the team: beyond reading maturity dates, what judgement did the Treasury side actually need (fund versus individual bond, and how the gap between each maturity and its January payment is covered), and how did you check you got it right?

## 02, item H

1. Assumption: that a written investment policy protects a plan mainly because people follow its rules, and that such rules are fragile under stress but still the main defence. Sure: 70.
2. What it means: I had the order of defences backwards for this plan; the payments are protected by what is already owned and when it matures, not by anyone's discipline, so the rules only govern a handful of remaining acts, which is the old dedication-versus-contingent-immunisation point (a trigger rule is exactly what fails) put in behavioural terms.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land or not: the concrete 8 of 17 and the researchers' own admission that nothing fully fixed it make the contrast between "rule" and "already bought" vivid in one line.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "Laura's ten payments do not rest on anyone keeping a rule": they still rest on one rule, do not sell the Treasuries before their dates; it is an easy rule, but in a share boom or a personal cash squeeze it is the one a future adviser or Laura could break, so "do not rest on anyone keeping a rule" is too strong.
   - "pay out before its date whatever anyone then thinks of markets": true of a bond held to maturity; a date-ended fund pays its net asset value at termination, which tracks its Treasuries closely but can come in a little under them (fees, cash drag, early termination), so "whatever" is slightly strong for the fund legs.
   - "8 of 17" is a small sample; "about half" is fair, but it is one lab experiment, not a body of evidence on "rules".
   - Unclear whether the payments need any coupon reinvestment between now and each date; if they do, that is another standing act the item leaves out of "the few acts left".
8. Question for the team: what, structurally, stops the Treasuries from being sold early (a separate account, a written instruction to the broker, a letter to co-sponsors), or is "hold to maturity" itself just one more rule?

## 03, item T

1. Assumption: that a co-sponsor shown a market-value statement would see a holding at or above the promised figure once the money is "moved", or else that the team would simply show the hold-to-maturity figure and not the market value. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: nothing new in the finance (it is pull to par), but it shows the plan has thought about the gap between the two numbers a non-specialist will actually see, and chooses to show both rather than hide the lower one.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes (as a teaching example of how a fully funded dedicated holding can look short on a statement).
6. What made it land or not: the specific numbers ($111,700 on the day, $115,000 by late August 2031, at least $117,700 at the date) make an abstract present-value point concrete for a reader who has never priced a bond.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "Treasuries are priced below what they pay until they pay it": true of the total of a bond's remaining payments when yields are positive, but not of its face value; a premium bond trades above par, so a careful reader will want "below the sum of what they will still pay".
   - "It passes $115,000 in late August 2031": that is the path at today's rates; if yields rise after the move, a later quarterly statement can fall back under $115,000, so co-sponsors could see the shortfall reappear; the item reads as if the crossing is fixed.
   - "what the fund's Treasuries will pay by December 2032 is never under about $117,700": a date-ended fund pays its net asset value, after its fee and whatever it earns on bonds that mature inside the fund before it closes, so "the fund's Treasuries will pay" is not quite what the fund pays the holder; the margin of about $2,700 (about 2.3%) looks ample for that, but it should be said.
8. Question for the team: on the worst rate path you tested between the 2031 move and December 2032, how far under $115,000 does the statement co-sponsors see fall, and have you written the sentence that explains it to them before they ask?

## 04, item M

1. Assumption: that a share-funded buffer for building costs is roughly diversified, because share slumps usually come with weak demand, slower construction and soft input prices, so the buffer and the costs it covers would rarely go wrong together. Sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had the correlation sign wrong for this buffer in US dollar terms; in both deep share slumps since 2000 a Taiwan building got about a quarter dearer in US dollars within two years, so the buffer is exposed to wrong-way risk, worth least just when it is most needed.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land or not: two dated episodes with two different channels (steel, then the currency) make the risk feel real rather than theoretical, and the closing "not a hedge" is honest.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "covers a rise of about 25% in the cost of her contribution if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed": the item's own scenario is shares more than 40% down at the 2031 point, so the 7.5% condition fails in exactly the episode described; in a 2000-03 replay the flexibility would be far smaller and cover well under 25%, so "one such episode could use all of it" understates the problem; it could exceed it.
   - "Both world-share slumps of 30% or more since 2000 were followed by...": two cases with two unrelated causes is a pattern of two, not evidence of a link; the honest claim is that it has happened twice, not that slumps lead to cost bursts.
   - "the point that matches Laura's 2031 meetings": the 2000 start to 2003 window is a three-year hold; Laura's shares are bought in January 2028, so the match is the length of the hold, which should be said plainly.
8. Question for the team: in the 2000-03 replay itself, with world shares down more than 40% at the 2031 point, how many dollars of flexibility are left in January 2033, and what share of a 27% rise in the cost of her contribution do they cover?

## 05, item K

1. Assumption: that a watch list for a Treasury-dedicated plan would act on leading causes such as a debt-ceiling standoff or a sharp rise in Treasury credit spreads, moving early rather than waiting for a missed payment. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: I had the value of early warning backwards for this kind of plan; acting on causes invites discretionary sales on noise (selling Treasuries in the 2011 or 2023 debt-ceiling fights would have been wrong), so tying the only plan-changing lines to things that have plainly happened keeps the plan from being talked out of itself.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land or not: the parallel from site-reliability engineering is unexpected and fits, and the last sentence (a cause can be argued for months, a missing payment cannot) is easy to remember.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "a Treasury payment more than five business days late": unclear whether this means the US Treasury missing a coupon or principal on a bond, or a Treasury fund paying late; the two have very different causes and remedies.
   - The parallel to Google's alerting is weaker than it looks on one point: a symptom alert in a computer system still leaves time to fix things, while a late US Treasury payment may leave no safer asset to move into, so the item should say what the plan does once the line fires.
   - "only two of its lines can change the plan, and both watch symptoms": a fund closing or merging early is also a symptom that forces an act (a fund swap was named elsewhere as one of the remaining acts), so either it is a third plan-changing line or the item should say why it is not.
8. Question for the team: if the Treasury-late line fires, what exactly does the plan do next, and into what, given that in a US payment delay nearly every alternative safe asset is under stress too?

## 06, item B

1. Assumption: that the case's figures were set so that $300,000 roughly buys the ten payments at ordinary rates, so funding them with Treasuries is the obvious, comfortable answer. Sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had the feasibility backwards; dedicating the first deposit to the ten payments only works because 2026 rates are high by this century's standards ($300,000 was enough on about one day in nine since 2000, and in 2020 the ten would have cost more than both deposits), so the plan's core is a bet that is only available in a rare year, and the plan is built so it does not depend on the day within that year.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land or not: the $470,217 in August 2020 against $450,000 of deposits is the single number that makes the point, and the $200 of commissions against a $37,625 swing within one year shows where the real risk sat.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027": unclear what is exposed after January 2027; if the 2028 deposit completes the ten, the part not bought until January 2028 is exposed to a year of rate moves, and the item does not say how large a fall over 2027 the plan can take.
   - "the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance ahead": a constant-maturity measure is the right way to compare levels over 25 years, but it means "10.9% of days" describes how high rates were, not what these ten dates would have cost, which a reader could misread.
   - "So the case is set in a rare year": the rarity is of the rate level, and a fixed-income reader would add that high starting yields cut both ways (a more expensive alternative if rates fall before purchase, and lower mark-to-market risk once bought and held), which the item leaves out.
8. Question for the team: what yield fall between January 2027 and January 2028 would leave the 2028 deposit unable to complete the ten and buy the extra Treasuries, and how likely is a fall that size in one year on your own history?

## 07, item G

1. Assumption: that "half" was a round, arbitrary split that a reviewer could attack as unoptimised. Sure: 70.
2. What it means: I had the stakes backwards; because the stated top is rounded down to $5,000 steps, any split in a band around half gives the same promise, so the choice barely moves total wealth and is really a choice about how much is promised versus kept free.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the 44.5%-57.3% band answers "why half?" with a number instead of a preference, though it rests on rounding more than on economics.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "The split decides what is promised and what stays free, not how much there is": not quite; every dollar moved from shares to Treasuries two years early gives up the expected share premium on it, which the item's own last clause prices (about $5,300 kept for $5,000 more stated); inside the band the effect is small, but it is not zero.
   - "moving anything from 44.5% to 57.3% of the shares gives the same stated top": that band is computed on 6 November 2026 rates and share prices; in 2031 the share level and the rates will set a different band, and half could land at an edge, so "half sits on a flat stretch" is true today, not by design.
   - "differs by a median of only about $365 between one split and the plan's": which split? Unclear whether this is the band's edges, a 10-point move or the average over the band.
   - "trades about $5,300 of what Laura keeps for each $5,000 more she could state": say whether that is a median, a mean or the expected value at the 7.5% assumption.
8. Question for the team: in January 2031, on that day's prices, will you move exactly half or re-choose within whatever flat stretch exists then, and if half sits at an edge of a step, which way do you go and why?

## 08, item D

1. Assumption: that the two named reasons are contingency triggers of the stop-loss kind, ways out if things go badly. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: I had the direction backwards; the clause's real work is the word "only", which forbids changing course for a market move either way, so it protects the plan from being abandoned in a crash or chased in a boom, and both reasons are failures of money to arrive rather than calls on markets.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the reversal (an exit rule that guards against leaving) is crisp, but it covers much the same ground as the earlier items on rules and symptoms, so by the eighth item it felt familiar.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "with the payments already in Treasuries, staying put costs nothing": true for the ten payments, not for the plan; the half of the shares still held before the 2031 move takes the full fall, which lowers the top Laura can state, and her flexibility takes it after the move, so staying put costs her range and her cushion, just not the payments.

8. Question for the team: if world shares fall 40% in 2030, is the 2031 move date fixed or can it wait for a recovery, and what exact words tell co-sponsors that the top they hear is lower because of it?

## Two items I would still remember after 40 reports

- B: the claim that $300,000 bought the ten payments on only about one day in nine since 2000, and would have fallen $20,000 short of both deposits together in 2020, tells me the whole strategy exists because of the rate level, which few teams will have measured.
- M: wrong-way risk named and dated in a student report (the buffer and the cost it covers went wrong together twice since 2000) is rare and is the kind of honesty a fixed-income examiner rewards.

## Items that clash or repeat

- D and K repeat: both rest on the same two triggers and the same point (they watch money that fails to arrive, not judgements about markets); K adds the alerting parallel, D the "guards against leaving" reversal; one of them would do.
