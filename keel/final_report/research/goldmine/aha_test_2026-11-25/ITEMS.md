# Cold aha test 10, Wed 25 Nov 2026 (evening): the items (record copy; the brief tells readers never to open this file)

Notes for the Final Report store, not report prose. Each reader sees these items one file at a time, in the order `KEY.md` gives; no reader file holds more than one item. Listed by letter.

**Item B.** On the plan's own measure (the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance ahead), $300,000 bought all ten on 10.9% of days since 2000, and on exactly one day from August 2007, just after the last run of such days before 2026, to August 2026: Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have cost $470,217, more than both of Laura's deposits together. Monday 5 October 2026, two trading days before the team's orders filled, was the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002. Commissions came to $200, against a $37,625 swing in what the ten cost within 2026 ($325,563 on 27 February, $287,938 on 5 October). So the case is set in a rare year, and the plan is built to need the year, not the day: whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them and buys the extra Treasuries, and that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027.

**Item C.** In October 2026 one team member argued that holding a single world share fund was a "copout" and proposed picking stocks and funds instead. The team kept the one world fund. That can look like declining to use judgement. Research on expert judgement points the other way: skilled intuition develops only where the world is regular enough to predict and people get quick feedback on their calls (Kahneman, 2011; Kahneman and Klein, 2009). Which shares will beat the rest over three years is neither, and there a fixed rule does better. The team did pick where picking works: it matched each of the ten Treasuries to the January it pays, a choice whose answer is known and can be checked against printed dates the day it is bought.

**Item D.** The plan names only two reasons to change course: no 2028 deposit by 31 March 2028, or a US Treasury payment more than five business days late. It is tempting to read these as a stop-loss, rules for getting out when things go wrong. They face the other way. Most written exit rules guard against staying in too long: kill criteria for projects, stopping rules for drug trials. This one guards against leaving a sound plan in a crash. Its word "only" shuts out a rise in share prices as firmly as a fall, and both reasons are moments when money itself fails to arrive, not judgments about markets. So nothing in it needs nerve in a falling market: with the payments already in Treasuries, staying put costs nothing.

**Item G.** Laura keeps half of her shares and moves the other half into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. Half looks like a split down the middle, chosen because it is round. On the plan's own figures, at 6 November 2026 market rates, moving anything from 44.5% to 57.3% of the shares gives the same stated top, $115,000, because the top is rounded down to a whole $5,000; and across 200,000 simulated futures, what Laura has beyond the ten payments differs by a median of only about $365 between one split and the plan's. The split decides what is promised and what stays free, not how much there is. Half sits on a flat stretch: moving the line within it changes almost nothing, and moving it further trades about $5,300 of what Laura keeps for each $5,000 more she could state.

**Item H.** A plan written in advance is often trusted because its rules will keep people on course. The evidence on rules is weaker than that. In an experiment, executives who had agreed in advance to a rule for when to stop backing a failing project broke the agreement about half the time (8 of 17), and the researchers wrote that "none of our proposed solutions fully eliminates the problem" (Boulding, Morgan and Staelin, 1997). Laura's ten payments do not rest on anyone keeping a rule. From January 2028 each is already bought, in Treasuries and Treasury funds that pay out before its date whatever anyone then thinks of markets. Rules matter only for the few acts left: receiving the 2028 deposit, the 2031 move and a fund swap.

**Item K.** A watch list for a ten-year plan might be expected to track what could cause trouble: a US debt-ceiling standoff, a slump in Laura's income, a market crash. The plan's watch list looks at those, but only two of its lines can change the plan, and both watch symptoms: a 2028 deposit not received by 31 March 2028, and a Treasury payment more than five business days late. The engineers who run Google's systems give the same advice for alerts: spend much more effort catching symptoms than causes, and worry only about very definite, very imminent causes (Ewaschuk, 2016). A cause can be argued about for months; a payment that has not arrived cannot.

**Item M.** Laura's flexibility, the half of her shares promised to no one, is kept for building costs that overrun and a stronger Taiwan dollar. One might expect it to be safe because shares and building costs rarely go wrong at once. History says a slump followed by a cost burst can catch it. World shares bought at the start of 2000 were more than 40% down three years later, the point that matches Laura's 2031 meetings; over the next two years Taiwan's building costs rose about 18% in Taiwan dollars and about 27% in US dollars, the sharpest part in one quarter of 2003-04, when world steel prices jumped. Both world-share slumps of 30% or more since 2000 were followed by a Taiwan building about a quarter dearer in US dollars: about 27% after 2000-03, through steel, and about 24% after 2008-09, through the Taiwan dollar. Her flexibility, with anything above the top, covers a rise of about 25% in the cost of her contribution if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed, so one such episode could use all of it. It is money kept for those uses, not a hedge.

**Item T.** In 2031 Laura signs a letter fixing her building contribution at $115,000, paid in January 2033, and co-sponsors see her broker's statement of a second account holding the building's Treasury fund. On the day she moves the money, at 6 November 2026 market rates, that statement would show about $111,700, about $3,300 under the figure she has just signed. It passes $115,000 in late August 2031, and what the fund's Treasuries will pay by December 2032 is never under about $117,700, even if the cash they pay out earns nothing more. Nothing is missing: Treasuries are priced below what they pay until they pay it, so a promise is counted by what its Treasuries pay on their date, not by what they would sell for today. Co-sponsors are shown both figures side by side: the day's value and what the holding will pay by its date.
