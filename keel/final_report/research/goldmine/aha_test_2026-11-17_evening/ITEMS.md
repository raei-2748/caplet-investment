# Items

Read these in the order your brief gives you, not in the order they are printed. The summary in your brief is all you
know of the plan.

**Item A.** The usual story of a founder's museum that closes is that the founder's money ran out: a fortune set aside,
then spent. Thirteen Taiwanese art institutions paid for by their founders (artists, their families, collectors and
companies) were found in museum-management research and the arts press; they are the cases found, not a census. In
every one the founder paid first, before any public or co-sponsor money. Apart from one company's permanent fund, which
may spend only its income, no registered fund found held even a year of running costs (a registered fund is a legal
minimum, not a founder's whole wealth). They ran on the next year's income: a company's profit, sales of the artist's
works, or the founder's own earnings. "I must earn NT$14m a year to give here," Hong-Gah's founder said in 2003. Four
stopped when that income stopped (two of the four had other causes too), the latest in October 2025, when Taipei's
artist-run IT Park went on "rest" after 37 years paid for by its founder's commercial photography. The others survive,
on next year's income as far as the record shows. Laura's ten operating payments are not next year's income: most of
each is owned in Treasuries from January 2027 and the rest from January 2028, five years before the first falls due in
2033.

**Item B.** On the plan's own measure (the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance
ahead), $300,000 bought all ten on 10.9% of days since 2000, and on exactly one day from June 2007, the last run of such
days before 2026, to August 2026: Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have cost $470,217, more
than both of Laura's deposits together. Monday 5 October 2026, two trading days before the team's orders filled, was the
cheapest day for the ten since May 2002. Commissions came to $200, against a $37,625 swing in what the ten cost within
2026 ($325,563 on 27 February, $287,938 on 5 October). So the case is set in a rare year, and the plan is built to need
the year, not the day: whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them and buys
the extra Treasuries, and that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points
before January 2027.

**Item C.** A world share fund holds a little of every market, so a plan to pay for a building in Taiwan might seem
partly covered by one if the Taiwan dollar strengthens. Taiwan is in fact the third-largest country in Laura's world
fund, VT: 3.5% of it on 30 September 2026, ahead of the UK at 3.1%. On today's figures the half of her shares promised
to no one would be worth about $21,300 in January 2033, so its Taiwan slice is about $750. If the Taiwan dollar were 10%
stronger when the building is paid for, that slice would gain about $75, counting the currency alone with share prices
unchanged, while the same building budget would need $11,500 more on a $115,000 contribution. Even if every currency
outside the US rose 10% at once, the fund's whole non-US part would gain about $800. What meets a stronger Taiwan dollar
is how much she keeps, not what it holds: on today's figures her unpromised half covers that $11,500 about twice over
(1.9 times), and 2.6 times with what the funds pay above the stated top.

**Item D.** Each payment rests on its own Treasury, and the ten as first priced, $302,287, reach $500,000 only if every
dollar of interest they pay before the payments is reinvested at today's forward rates: about 5.12% a year on every
waiting dollar. That rate was the one forecast left in the team's own trade notes ("reinvested coupons fund the rest").
Replayed over 585 starting months since 1962, the ten as priced would have fallen short more often than not for start
dates since 1981, and for about 1 start in 6 from 1962 to 1980; when short, by a median $16,055, by $34,555 at 1 in 20
of all starting months, and by $45,266 at worst (a July 2007 start). The IRS once tested charitable trusts by assuming
the rate at their creation would last their whole lives; when rates fell, trusts failed it. So the plan takes the
forecast out: extra Treasuries, bought with the 2028 deposit's first dollars, are sized so all ten are paid even if cash
waiting earns nothing. The price is about $38,000 of the 2028 deposit at today's rates, which also completes the ten.
What comes back is worth about $45,500 in 1 January 2033 money if rates hold, all of it Laura's by the last payment in
2042; if cash waiting earns nothing, none.

**Item E.** A 1992 Baltimore Sun column said that anyone who bought the new 20-year Treasury bonds of January 1982,
paying 14.25%, "would have 'locked in' their higher rate until they mature in 2002". The bond locked in its interest
payments and its face value, not the rate those payments earned while they waited. Held to 2002, $1,000 of it grows to
$15,690 only if every interest payment also earns 14.25%. Rolled over in six-month Treasury bills at the rates actually
paid, it reached $6,144: 9.29% a year, quoted half-yearly like the bond's 14.25% (about 9.5% compounded yearly).
Laura's Treasuries also pay interest for years before the payments they fund, and the competition's rules (Wharton's
fourth weekly email to teams) bar stripped Treasuries, which pay nothing until they end. So the plan assumes waiting cash
earns nothing, the strictest assumption found in bond practice, and the bill fund that would hold that cash is still
charged its 0.15% yearly fee.

**Item F.** By the plan's own premise, a writer's income can shrink in a slump, yet the plan waits on a second deposit
from that writer: $150,000 in January 2028. So the deposit looks like the plan's weak point. The order in which the
money is spent decides where a shortfall would land. The money Laura already has, the first $300,000, buys the ten
Treasuries in January 2027. The 2028 deposit is then spent in a fixed order: its first $38,282 completes the ten and
buys the extra Treasuries that pay all ten even if cash waiting earns nothing, and only what is left buys the Treasury
fund for the bottom of her range and then the shares. So at today's market rates the deposit could arrive about
three-quarters smaller (74.5%) and every payment would still meet that standard, and still more than two-thirds smaller
(69.8%) if yields fell two points during 2027, as yields often do in a slump. What shrinks is the building money: a
deposit a quarter smaller ($112,500) lowers the bottom she could state from $95,000 to $65,000, and one half smaller
($75,000) to $40,000. Only a deposit that never comes, one of the plan's two named reasons to change course, leaves the
payments short of the standard: by $57,455 if cash waiting earns nothing.

**Item G.** Of the $111,718 left in January 2028 once the first $38,282 is spent, the plan puts about three-quarters in
the late-2032 Treasury fund and about a quarter in shares. More shares would be the risky choice. With three-quarters in
shares instead, the bottom of the range Laura could state in 2031 would drop from $95,000 to $30,000, and her
contribution would land under $95,000 in 62 of 100 simulated futures. Fewer shares would leave her less. With everything
in the Treasury fund she would state one figure, $130,000, a higher top than the plan's in about 95 futures in 100; but
she would keep about $9,000 of flexibility at the median instead of about $29,000, and be left with less in total in
about 63 futures in 100.

**Item H.** A lender reads a large company's written word of support as a promise. Kleinwort Benson lent to Malaysia
Mining's tin-trading company against a letter that said "It is our policy to ensure that the business of M.M.C. Metals
Ltd. is at all times in a position to meet its liabilities to you", and a High Court judge awarded the bank £12.26m with
interest. The tin market collapsed in October 1985. Malaysia Mining then wrote that the policy had been "our policy at
that time" and that "no assurance was given that such policy would not be reviewed in the light of changing
circumstances". In 1989 the Court of Appeal set the award aside: the sentence was "a statement of present fact and not a
promise as to future conduct". The fact it stated was the company's policy, and the crash changed the policy. Laura's
2031 figure also states a present fact, but the fact is money. The top, her contribution and the figure co-sponsors
count, is $115,000 on today's figures, held for her in Treasuries and named for the facility before she speaks; the
bottom is the part held since January 2028. The two figures record how long each part has been held, not a margin for
error. No fall in markets can change what either end pays when its Treasuries mature.

**Item I.** The case asks for "a high degree of certainty", not certainty, and the common reading of that phrase is
"very likely": a plan that pays in full in 96.8 simulated markets out of 100 sounds as if it qualifies. The team's first
plan (27 September 2026) held about three-quarters of the money in shares to 2030, gliding to 40% in 2032, and on
1 January 2033 it would have bought all ten payments in Treasuries with whatever the money had grown or shrunk to. The
team gave it up once it thought about how a writer is paid (the premise in your summary). A test run on 27 September,
before any trade, put a price on that change; it was not the reason for it. The first plan fell short in 3.2% of
simulated markets, and each miss would have been final: settled on 1 January 2033, before any payment, with no money
coming after January 2028 to close it. "High" is measured against what the same two deposits can buy. Bought into
Treasuries first and sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, they leave no payment short from
January 2028, once the second deposit's first dollars are in. So 3.2% meant about 1 in 31 against none, with a median
miss of $27,695 in 1 January 2033 money.
