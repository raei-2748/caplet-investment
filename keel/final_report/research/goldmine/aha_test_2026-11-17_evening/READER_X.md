## D

1. Assumption: a ladder of coupon Treasuries does not lock in its funding. Interest paid before the payments carries
   reinvestment risk, and a ladder priced on the curve needs reinvestment at the forward rates. Sure: 90.
2. Meaning: the mechanism was nothing new to me, but I had its size wrong. I would have guessed a few thousand dollars.
   History says a median of $16,055 when short and $45,266 at worst, with shortfalls more often than not for starts since 1981.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 5
5. Would I repeat it: yes.
6. What made it land: it puts a dollar figure on a textbook risk, using the plan's own ladder over 585 starting months.
   The fix costs about $38,000 and comes back as about $45,500 if rates hold, so the insurance is close to free in
   expectation. That $45,500 also sits close to the worst historical shortfall of $45,266. The team should say whether
   that is a coincidence or the evidence that a zero rate is not overkill.
7. Overstated or unclear: "reach $500,000 only if every dollar of interest they pay ... is reinvested at today's forward
   rates" is not quite right. The ladder needs the forward rates on average across the reinvestments, not on every
   dollar: some dollars can earn less if others earn more. "About 5.12% a year on every waiting dollar" squeezes a curve
   of forward rates into one number without saying so. The IRS sentence is unclear. Did new trusts fail the test when
   they were set up at a lower assumed rate, or did existing trusts actually run dry? Those are different points, and
   only the second supports the plan. "Short more often than not for start dates since 1981" mostly reflects forty years
   of falling rates. It is history, not a base rate.
8. Question: Why assume a zero rate rather than a floor taken from history, such as the worst bill path on record, and
   how much of the $38,000 would that free for the building?

## E

1. Assumption: a coupon bond "locks in" its yield only if it is held to the end and every coupon is reinvested at the
   same rate. The high coupons of 1982 earned well below their yield. Sure: 95.
2. Meaning: nothing new to me. It is the clearest public example of the point in D, and the gap ($15,690 at the bond's
   yield against $6,144 actually reached) is wider than I would have guessed.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it: yes, to trustees, who say "locked in" all the time.
6. What made it land: a newspaper getting a famous 14.25% coupon wrong makes a dry point memorable. The arithmetic checks
   out: 1.07125 to the 40th power is about 15.69, and $6,144 over 40 half-years is about 4.64% a half-year, or 9.29%.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the strictest assumption found in bond practice". It is a strict standard, not the strictest.
   Short rates have been below zero: US bills briefly traded there more than once, and Europe and Japan ran negative
   rates for years. The 0.15% fee already takes the plan slightly below zero, and that is the stronger thing to say.
   Rolling coupons in six-month bills is also one choice among several. Rolling into longer Treasuries would have reached
   more, so 9.29% is near the low end, and the item should say so.
8. Question: D and E make the same point. Which one does the report keep, and if both, what does the judge get from the
   second?
