# Reader N

Role: the finance director of a mid-sized nonprofit arts organisation (budgets, grants and donor pledges; I work
outside fixed income).

## 01, item K

1. Assumed: a promise of fixed future payments funded partly from a portfolio that holds shares carries market-return
   risk right through to the last payment, the way our multi-year grant commitments ride on the endowment's spending
   draw. Sure: 60.
2. It shows the risks to a pay-later promise can be closed by a date, not carried for the life of the promise: after
   January 2028 the share market cannot touch any payment or stated figure, where Illinois left all three risks open
   for as long as it sold contracts.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land: Illinois's own three named risks, quoted, laid against hers one by one, is how a board would
   want to see it; I have read pledge-risk disclosures that look exactly like that.
7. Overstated or unclear: "no fall in share prices can break a payment or a figure she has stated" is true only of a
   figure already stated; a fall before 2031 still shrinks what she can state, so the sentence reads stronger than
   it is unless that is said nearby. "New money ... close[s] for good in January 2028" hides that the 2028 deposit
   itself comes from a career that the brief says rides on the share market, so the new-money risk and the return
   risk are partly the same risk until it arrives. "the small margin held for them" is unexplained: how small, and
   held where?
8. Question: if the 2028 deposit arrives late or short, how much of the ten payments does the first $300,000 already
   cover on its own, and which payments are left uncovered first?

## 02, item G

1. Assumed: a bond ending before each payment simply covers that payment, and the interest it throws off along the
   way is a small bonus that gets reinvested at whatever rate is going. I had not thought of the interest as
   something the payments depend on. Sure: 45.
2. It shows the interest is not a bonus but part of what pays the ten, so a plan that does not want to bet on future
   rates must buy about $36,800 more to cover the case where waiting cash earns nothing.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the two dollar figures made it concrete, and "even if all that waiting money earns
   nothing" is the same discipline we use when we refuse to budget interest on restricted cash; the Leibowitz quote
   and "immunisation" I took on trust, and they did not add to my belief.
7. Overstated or unclear: "buying extra ordinary Treasuries is the only way left" seems too strong to me as an
   outsider: could the cash not wait in short Treasury bills, or the holdings be chosen to end closer to each
   January? And "the plan's policy allows no trade for a market move" is the team's own rule, so "closed to Laura" is
   partly a choice, not a fact of nature. Unclear why $36,800 bought fixes a $54,600 shortfall; a board member would
   ask that in the first minute.
8. Question: what does the $36,800 take away from her building contribution, and how much of it comes back to her in
   2033 if waiting cash earns anything at all?

## 03, item H

1. Assumed: from the summary I already took it that half the shares move to Treasuries before 2031 and so fix the
   top; I assumed this was a sensible precaution, not a principle with a name. Sure: 70.
2. Nothing new in substance: it gives a familiar reason (a share is worth nothing certain until it is sold) for a
   step I had already accepted, and shows the kept half is left out of the range on purpose, not from timidity.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the gift-office half landed because it is my own world (we sell gifted shares on
   arrival, and I would much rather hear a pledge in cash than in stock), while the bought deal is a world I do not
   know and its mapping to Laura took me two reads.
7. Overstated or unclear: "shares given to it count at what they fetch when sold" is not how my auditors or our gift
   counting work: a gift of shares is booked at its market value on the day we receive it, and the sale shows a
   separate small gain or loss; the office sells fast to avoid that gap, not because the gift counts at sale price.
   "That half is the bought deal" is muddled: in a bought deal the bank carries the risk, so the bank is more like
   the half she keeps, and the moved half is the issuer taking a fixed price.
8. Question: will the late-2032 fund pay out before the building needs the cash, and in what form will the
   co-sponsors receive her commitment (a signed pledge, an escrow, a letter)?

## 04, item L

1. Assumed: her 2031 range is a disclosure exercise, a careful forecast told to co-sponsors so she does not
   over-promise; I had not seen it as the move that unlocks the other funders. Sure: 55.
2. It shows the range is a lead gift, not just a forecast: because her top is already held in Treasuries she can go
   first, which is the one thing last-dollar funders need and the one thing most founders cannot safely do.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land: I have sat in exactly this deadlock, a state capital grant and a foundation each waiting for the
   other's letter, and the item names it and then shows how she breaks it.
7. Overstated or unclear: "someone has to go first without conditions" and, two sentences later, "Her condition
   then lets every other pledge stay conditional" read as a contradiction until I worked out she goes first with an
   all-or-nothing condition; say that plainly. The experiment (List and Lucking-Reiley, 2002), as I remember it, was
   small gifts by mail to a university appeal, not public programmes or foundations with written rules, so "a lumpy
   project ... gets built far more often" claims more than a mail test can carry. "her $115,000 is already held" is
   the top of a range that is "about $95,000-$115,000" on today's rates; the item should not read as if the figure
   is fixed now.
8. Question: if the building's total is not pledged by December 2032, what happens next: does she extend the date,
   scale the building down, or walk away, and has any public arts funder confirmed it would count her
   all-or-nothing pledge as the confirmed money its rules ask for?

## 05, item B

1. Assumed: $300,000 buying ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out is simply what money costs, and the case's
   figures were set to fit any ordinary year. Sure: 35.
2. It shows the case only works because 2026 is a rare year of high rates (one day in nineteen years before it when
   $300,000 was enough), and that the plan leans on the 2028 deposit to finish the job rather than on luck in the
   day the first deposit is spent.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land: "$470,217 on 4 August 2020, more than both of Laura's deposits together" is a figure I can
   say to my board and they would understand at once why the timing of this gift matters.
7. Overstated or unclear: "keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points
   before January 2027" is hard to parse (an "even if" and an "unless" in one clause); I read it three times. The
   item mixes the team's own competition orders ("two trading days before the team's orders filled ... the cheapest
   day ... since May 2002") with Laura's January 2027 purchase; that line sounds like a boast about luck in a plan
   that says it does not need luck, and an outsider cannot tell which purchase is the real one. "the case is set in
   a rare year" may be the case writers' design, not a discovery.
8. Question: if yields did fall sharply before January 2027, how much of her flexibility or her building
   contribution would go into completing the ten, and would she be told before or after the 2028 deposit is spent?

## 06, item A

1. Assumed: a funder who hears a range plans on its bottom, so the bottom is the number that matters to co-sponsors
   and the top is mostly for show. Sure: 65.
2. It shows the number co-sponsors really act on is not hers but theirs, the remainder left to raise, and a figure
   that could still move would move every other funder's target with it; so the top, once held, is the useful number.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land: turning her figure round into "what is left to raise" is how every co-sponsor in a campaign I
   have run actually thinks, and the lab result (a known threshold met 18 times in 20, an unknown one missed 16 times
   in 20) gives that instinct a number.
7. Overstated or unclear: "every co-sponsor's target is exact: the residency's total less $115,000" overlooks that
   the building's total is itself uncertain (the summary sets aside her flexibility for building costs that
   overrun), so her figure removes one moving part, not all of them; that is exactly the threshold uncertainty the
   study says does harm. "She states her range ... and names the top as her figure" left me asking what the range is
   for if both ends are held and the top is the figure. "Fundraising's own counting rules already refuse to count a
   pledge whose conditions may fail" is right as I know it, but it cuts against her own all-or-nothing condition
   (see item L).
8. Question: if both ends are held in Treasuries and the top is the figure she names, what does stating the bottom
   add for a co-sponsor, and why not state one number?

## 07, item E

1. Assumed: the plan is cautious in a general, temperamental way, the way a nervous board is. Sure: 55.
2. It shows the caution is a rule I already live by, book likely costs early and gains only when realised, applied
   consistently in three named places, so it reads as accounting discipline rather than nerves.
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the rule is my own daily rule (we never book a conditional pledge, we always book a
   probable liability), so it landed as recognition, not surprise; the three places made it concrete, but the old
   bookkeeping manual and the ink line were decoration I skipped.
7. Overstated or unclear: "lenders, whose claims are capped, are the reason the rule exists (Watts, 2003)" states one
   writer's explanation as settled fact. "the likeliest gain of all, her shares' growth" is an odd thing to call the
   likeliest when shares are the part that can fall. "about 3 futures in 100" cuts both ways: many funders would
   take a 97-in-100 pledge, so the item needs to say why 3 in 100 is too many for her and not just a small cost of
   stating earlier.
8. Question: do any of the co-sponsors need her figure earlier than January 2031 for their own budget or grant cycles
   (many foundations and public programmes plan 12-18 months ahead), and what does she lose if she waits?

## 08, item D

1. Assumed: payments ten years and more away can afford more investment risk, the long-horizon logic our endowment
   committee uses every year. Sure: 60.
2. It shows the licence to take risk on far-off payments comes from someone who can pay in more if things go wrong,
   and Laura's only such someone is her own income, which falls with shares and is called on once, so the plan
   rightly stops taking risk on the payments once that money is in.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land: the regulator's own house-builder example maps onto a writer cleanly, and "she has only one call
   on that sponsor" is the line that turned "long horizon, so take risk" round for me; the last sentence, owning the
   step as the team's own, made me trust the rest.
7. Overstated or unclear: "from then on they need nothing more from her" is true from January 2028, but for the
   year before it the payments still lean on a deposit from a career the item says can come "later and smaller", and
   the item says nothing of what happens if it does. It also repeats item K's point that new money closes in January
   2028, in a different costume.
8. Question: if the January 2028 deposit comes in smaller than $150,000, which comes first: completing the ten, the
   extra Treasuries, the late-2032 fund or the shares, and what would she tell co-sponsors then?

## 09, item M

1. Assumed: the half she keeps back is in effect a second contingency fund, sitting on top of the building budget's
   own contingency line, and so partly double-counted. Sure: 60.
2. It shows the building's contingency was never going to carry price rises or currency swings, and grant makers will
   not top up an overrun, so the half she keeps is the owner's share that nobody else's budget holds, not a duplicate.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land: "about two-thirds of overruns fell on the organisations" in England's lottery programme, and a
   culture ministry saying the grantee "may not ask for more", are what I have seen on my own capital projects, and
   the item shows the kept half answers exactly that gap.
7. Overstated or unclear: AACE says contingency "usually excludes" escalation and currency, so "leaves outside
   contingency by definition" in the last line overstates its own source. The argument covers two of the three uses
   (building costs and the Taiwan dollar) but not the third, "a co-sponsor falling short", which is not escalation or
   currency at all. And it treats Laura as the building's owner: if the residency is its own organisation, the
   overrun falls on that organisation, and her kept half is a voluntary backstop, not an owner's share.
8. Question: who will own the building and carry an overrun in law, Laura or the residency's own entity, and if the
   kept half is used, does it go in as a gift, a loan or a guarantee?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- L: it turns her range from a careful forecast into the lead gift that breaks the last-dollar deadlock, which is the
  real problem in every capital campaign I have run.
- M: grant makers will not top up an overrun and two-thirds of overruns fall on the owner, so the kept half has a job
  no one else's budget does.
- B: $470,217 in August 2020 against $300,000 now shows at once that this plan is possible only in a rare year.

## Items that clash or repeat

  pledge an all-or-nothing condition (nothing paid unless the total is pledged by December 2032), so by A's own rule
  co-sponsors' counting would not treat her figure as confirmed either.
- K and D repeat: both say the new money from her career closes in January 2028 and nothing rests on her after it,
  one through Illinois and one through the UK pension regulator.
- G and E repeat: E's second "place" (every payment sized as if waiting cash earns nothing) is G's whole item, and B
  ends on the same test.
- H and E overlap: shares count only once turned into Treasuries, told once through a gift office and once through
