## 03, item D

1. Assumption: the plan is a plain cash-flow match of nominal liabilities with Treasuries, the kind of thing a pension does at buyout-level funding; the 2028 deposit is just a top-up, and the career-equity link is a client-profile nicety rather than a structural reason. Sure: 55.
2. What it means: little new to me on the framework (covenant reliance and covenant-asset correlation are daily work), but it shows the plan reaches a pension's "low dependency" end state in one step, the moment the only sponsor contribution arrives, and that the sponsor here is correlated with equities, which is the textbook case for not taking risk against it.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the mapping is accurate and the house builder example is the right one from the regulator, so it gives the plan instant credibility with a pensions reader; it did not surprise me because it is the standard covenant argument, carried over honestly.
7. Overstated or unclear: "By the regulator's logic she should stop leaning on her sponsor as soon as its money is in": close to circular, since once the only contribution is paid there is no sponsor left to lean on; the real covenant exposure is the year before, 2027, when the ten payments are not yet fully bought and depend on the 2028 deposit. "the plan takes none": none on the matched payments in nominal terms, fair, but Treasury bond funds that end on a set date still carry small vehicle risks (cash drag on coupons, fees, an early or late wind-down) that a direct bond ladder does not, so "none" is strong unless those are sized.

## 04, item E

1. Assumption: a student dedication portfolio would be priced on central estimates, reinvesting coupons and maturities at forward rates, with perhaps a small round buffer on top. Sure: 50.
2. What it means: the plan is priced to the escrow and defeasance standard rather than to central estimates: costs are taken early, reinvestment is assumed to earn nothing, and equity is given no value until it is converted, so the stated figures are lower bounds rather than best guesses.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the zero-reinvestment sizing and the haircut set above the observed cost are exactly what I would want to see, and the accounting asymmetry gives a lay reader a name for it; the bookkeeping-manual detail is decoration and added nothing for me.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the likeliest gain of all, her shares' growth": share growth is the largest expected gain, not the likeliest; interest on waiting cash is far likelier to be positive than shares over three or four years. "a 0.5% margin for fund costs beyond their fee": 0.5% of what (the stated range, each payment, the fund's value) and over what period, once or per year? "about 0.27%" from the funds of this kind that have already ended sounds like a small sample; the worst case matters more than the average. "about 3 futures in 100" for a top stated one month early only makes sense if the top is set with the same round-down rule; with no cushion a month of share moves would break it far more often, so the rule behind the 3 in 100 should be stated.
8. Question: Of the bond funds of this kind that have already ended, what did the worst one cost beyond its fee, and is your 0.5% applied once to the end value or every year?

## 09, item G

1. Assumption: sizing every payment to be met with zero reinvestment of waiting cash is the standard defeasance and escrow test, and a careful team would apply it; I took it as given rather than as a choice with a price. Sure: 70.
2. What it means: little I had backwards, but it puts a price on the test (about $36,800 of extra Treasuries, against a $54,600 shortfall without them) and shows why the competition's ban on zero-coupon Treasuries and the policy's no-trading rule make that extra purchase necessary here.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the Leibowitz citation is the right one and the constraint logic is tidy, but the zero-reinvestment test is my everyday standard, so the news is only the price tag, and the arithmetic between the two figures is not explained.
7. Overstated or unclear: "buying extra ordinary Treasuries is the only way left to pass the old test": not the only way; the usual third route is to choose the lowest-coupon issues available at each maturity (deep-discount bonds issued in the low-rate years 2020-21 behave much like zeros and leave far less coupon cash waiting), which would shrink the extra purchase even if it cannot remove it. "about $36,800 of extra Treasuries" against "about $54,600 short": the reader is not told why $36,800 closes a $54,600 gap (presumably the extra bonds' own coupons and maturity value, or the top-up counted separately), so the two figures look inconsistent at first sight. "It looks like an amateur's caution": to anyone who has run a defeasance it looks like the opposite, so the setup undersells the reader.
8. Question: Did you compare buying the lowest-coupon Treasury at each maturity with buying extra ordinary Treasuries, and how much of the $36,800 would that have saved for her range?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- B: a measured history of the plan's own liability with dated days ($470,217 in August 2020, one cheap day in nineteen years) shows the case sits in a rare rate year, which almost no team will have measured.
- L: it turns the Treasury lock from a defensive device into a lead-gift strategy that breaks a funders' deadlock, which is the most original use of the structure.
- A: the finding that uncertainty about the threshold, not about the loss, is what sinks group funding is a clean reason to state one owned figure rather than a probabilistic range.
