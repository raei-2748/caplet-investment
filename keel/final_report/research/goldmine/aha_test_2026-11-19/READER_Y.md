# Reader Y: a finance professor who teaches fixed income (and asset-liability management)

## 01, item A

1. Assumption: that the 2031 range is a confidence statement about Laura's own outcome (an interval with a stated probability, the usual honest way to quote an uncertain liability), and that its job is to protect her credibility, not to help the co-sponsors coordinate. How sure: 65.
2. What it means: I had the range's audience backwards: its main reader is the co-sponsor whose own target is "the total less her figure", so uncertainty in her pledge becomes uncertainty in everyone else's threshold, which the lab evidence says is what breaks collective giving.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the threshold-uncertainty versus loss-uncertainty split (18 of 20 against 16 of 20 failing) is a crisp, transferable result, but it is a climate-negotiation lab game with student subjects, so it lands as an analogy, not as evidence about arts co-sponsors.
7. Overstated or unclear: "so every co-sponsor's target is exact: the residency's total less $115,000." The building's total is itself uncertain (building costs are the very risk her unpromised half is kept for), so the co-sponsors' target is not exact; the plan removes her part of the threshold uncertainty, not all of it. Also unclear: if both ends are owned Treasuries and she "names the top as her figure", what is the bottom of the range still for? A range whose two ends are both locked reads as a commitment of the top with a hedge the reader cannot price. Minor: a target-maturity Treasury fund's payout at its end is close to fixed but not to the dollar (reinvested coupons, the last months in bills), so "owned" is right but "exact" is a shade strong.
8. Question: If both ends of the range are already held in Treasuries by the 2031 meetings, why quote a range at all rather than commit $115,000, and what exactly would make her pay less than the top?

## 02, item D

1. Assumption: that Laura's writing income matters to the plan only as a background "human capital is equity-like, so hold more bonds" argument (Bodie, Merton and Samuelson), not as a sponsor whose strength sets how much risk the portfolio may run against the payments. How sure: 70.
2. What it means: Framing her income as a pension sponsor with a single, one-time call (January 2028) shows the covenant horizon is one year, so the right move is to stop relying on it the moment its cash lands, which is exactly what completing the ten Treasuries first does.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the regulator's own house-builder-holding-property example maps cleanly onto "writer's income and shares fall together", and the "one call, none after it" point is the part I had not framed; the rest is a known ALM idea in a new costume.
7. Overstated or unclear: "By the regulator's logic she should stop leaning on her sponsor as soon as its money is in" is close to circular: once the only contribution is paid there is no covenant left to lean on, so this is a fact, not a choice the plan makes. The real covenant question is the year before, January 2027 to January 2028, when the payments are bought with the first deposit and still need part of the second to be complete; the item does not say how big that dependence is. "The plan takes none" is right for nominal price and default risk on the payments, but a ladder partly made of target-maturity funds still carries fund-structure risk (an early close, costs beyond the fee), so "none" is a shade strong for the funds.
8. Question: In January 2027, how many dollars of the ten payments are not yet covered by what the first deposit buys, and what does the plan do with the payments if the 2028 deposit comes late or smaller?

## 03, item E

1. Assumption: that a dedicated Treasury ladder is sized the usual way, at the curve's yields with a modest reinvestment assumption and fees deducted, and that the share half enters the range at an expected or median value, not at zero. How sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had the sizing posture backwards: the plan applies accounting's imparity rule forward (losses booked when likely, gains only when realised), so the zero-reinvestment assumption, the cost margin above the observed 0.27%, and counting shares at nothing until they are converted are one deliberate habit, not three unrelated cushions.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the KPMG line and Watts's lender explanation give a principled name to what fixed income people call "sufficient without reinvestment" and actuaries call a margin for adverse deviation, and the one-month figure makes the last point concrete; it lands as an elegant frame for practice I already know rather than a new fact.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the likeliest gain of all, her shares' growth" mixes expected value and probability: over three years shares rise in perhaps three futures in four, which is likely, but a 0% reinvestment rate on waiting cash is beaten with near certainty whenever bill rates are positive, so the interest is the likelier gain. "a 0.5% margin" does not say 0.5% of what, or whether it is once or per year; a reader cannot tell if 0.5% against 0.27% is a large or small cushion. "about 3 futures in 100" needs its model named (monthly volatility, fat tails?), since with normal returns a one-month fall big enough to beat the rounding would be rarer than that, and with real tails it could be more. The old-ledger detail is charming but does no work.
8. Question: What is the 0.5% a percentage of, and is it taken once or per year, so I can compare it with the 0.27% the ended funds actually cost?

## 04, item B

1. Assumption: that buying the ten payments outright with $300,000 is something an investor of this size could do in most rate environments, so the plan's design is general rather than a creature of the 2026 curve. How sure: 50.
2. What it means: I had the base rate backwards: on a constant-horizon basis $300,000 covered the ten payments on only about one day in nine since 2000 and on a single day between mid-2007 and 2026, so full dedication is a high-rate-year luxury, and the plan's real safety device is the 2028 deposit acting as rate insurance for whatever January 2027 costs.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: the $470,217 in August 2020 (more than both deposits) and "one day in sixteen years" are concrete, checkable and turn a vague "rates are high now" into a measured rarity, and the $200 of commissions against a $37,625 swing makes the point that timing risk dwarfs cost.
7. Overstated or unclear: "whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them" is contradicted two lines later by "unless yields fall about 3.6 points", so "whatever" is too strong; say "unless". The 3.6-point figure covers falls "before January 2027" only, but any part of the ten not bought in January 2027 stays exposed to a further fall during 2027, until the 2028 deposit buys it, and the item does not say how that year is handled (which rungs are bought first, and how the 3.6 points shrinks if part of the fall comes in 2027). It also leans on the 2028 deposit arriving, the very event the plan names as a reason to change course. "the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002" is a WInS-trading fact; for Laura's real plan only the January 2027 price matters, so it is colour, not evidence.
8. Question: If yields fall during 2027, after the first deposit is spent but before the 2028 deposit arrives, how large a fall can the ten payments absorb, and which rungs does the first deposit buy if it cannot buy all ten?

## 05, item L

1. Assumption: that Laura's 2031 figure is one funder's contribution among peers, and that being conservative (stating less) is simply safer for everyone; I had not thought about who goes first in a lumpy capital campaign. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had the value of her locked figure backwards: its worth is not only that she cannot over-promise but that, because it is already funded, she can be the unconditional lead gift that breaks the "everyone pays last" deadlock among conditional funders, a role most givers can only take by over-promising.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the deadlock between two pay-last funders is a clean piece of logic and the List and Lucking-Reiley seed result is the right literature, so it lands as strategy; it lands less as evidence, because that experiment measured small mailed donations to a university project, not institutions with written pay-last rules.
7. Overstated or unclear: "The big capital funders ... write their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed" is a sweeping claim about every such funder; many use matching or challenge grants instead, and the item gives no source. The item says she goes first "without conditions" and then speaks of "Her condition": which is it? If her pledge carries the all-or-nothing condition, she is not going first unconditionally; if it does not, the item never says what happens to her $115,000 when the total is not pledged by December 2032. "nearly sixfold" is a household mail-solicitation effect and should not be read as a size for institutional funders. Also "her $115,000 is already held for her in Treasuries" overlooks that the top is $115,000 only on today's figures; the item treats a range's top as a fixed sum.
8. Question: If the building's total is not fully pledged by December 2032, what happens to Laura's $115,000: is it paid anyway, held back, or put to another use, and is that written into her pledge?

## 06, item M

1. Assumption: that the unpromised half is a general cushion, in effect a contingency on the building, and that sizing it at all sits awkwardly with the case's instruction not to estimate facility costs. How sure: 55.
2. What it means: It reframes the held-back half from a second contingency into the owner's own escalation and currency exposure, which cost engineers price outside contingency and which grant-makers explicitly leave with the grantee, so it duplicates nothing in anyone's budget.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the three sources (AACE, Taiwan's culture ministry's "may not ask for more", and two-thirds of English lottery overruns falling on the organisations) together make a real point that overruns land on the owner, but the headline move is definitional, and definitions alone do not tell me whether the reserve is the right size or held in the right asset.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the owner's share that the profession leaves outside contingency by definition": the quoted standard says contingency "usually excludes" escalation and currency, so "by definition" overstates it. Of the three uses named, only two (escalation, currency) are what AACE excludes; "a co-sponsor falling short" is a funding risk, not a cost-estimate item, so the AACE argument does not cover it. From an asset-liability view the item skips the real question: the money held back for building-cost escalation and a stronger Taiwan dollar stays in world shares, an asset that can fall hard in the same inflationary or dollar-weak spell that drives those costs up (2022 is the obvious case), so the reserve can shrink just when it is called.
8. Question: Why keep the money for cost escalation and a stronger Taiwan dollar in world shares rather than moving it into short Treasuries (or Taiwan dollars) as the building date approaches, and what is left of it if shares fall 40% in 2032 while costs overrun?

## 07, item G

1. Assumption: that the ladder was sized the standard way, cash-flow matched with coupons reinvested at a conservative positive rate (bills or a haircut forward rate), so the gap from assuming zero would be small. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: I had the size of the reinvestment problem backwards: with six years of coupons arriving before the first payment and zeros banned, the zero-reinvestment test costs about $54,600 of shortfall (filled by about $36,800 of extra Treasuries), so the coupon-waiting problem is a first-order sizing item, not a rounding note.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: it lands because it ties a correct piece of trade history (Leibowitz's dedicated portfolio, "no reinvestment") to two hard constraints (no STRIPS, no trading on market moves) and shows the cost in dollars; it is textbook to me, but the constraint logic is cleanly argued.
7. Overstated or unclear: "buying extra ordinary Treasuries is the only way left to pass the old test" is too strong: low-coupon Treasuries issued in 2020-21 (20-year bonds with coupons near 1-2% maturing in the 2040s, and older low-coupon notes) behave close to zeros and would cut the coupons that must wait, so the extra money needed falls; that is a way left open, not closed. Choosing which bonds to buy is not a "trade for a market move". "It is the oldest test in the trade" is a flourish; in the 1980s dedicated portfolios more often assumed a conservative positive reinvestment rate, and "sufficient without reinvestment" is really the defeasance-escrow standard. The item also leaves unexplained why $36,800 of extra Treasuries closes a $54,600 gap (their own principal and coupons, counted at zero reinvestment, I assume), which a lay reader will stumble on. Also unclear: are the $54,600 and $36,800 dated on the same curve?
8. Question: Did you price the ladder with the lowest-coupon eligible Treasuries for each rung, and how much of the $36,800 of extra Treasuries would that have saved?

## 08, item K

1. Assumption: that the plan's risks are best described in the usual funding triad (contributions, asset returns, liability growth) and that, like most funded promises, it stays exposed to all three until the payments begin in 2033. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: Set beside Illinois's prepaid tuition plan, which carried contributions, returns and liability growth open for as long as it sold contracts, Laura's plan closes two of the three for good in January 2028 and fences the third (building costs) off from anything she has promised, so the open list shrinks to default and fund costs five years before the first payment.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: prepaid tuition plans are a classic asset-liability failure, Illinois's disclosure names the three assumptions in its own words, and its later move into matched Treasuries is the institution conceding the plan's design, which makes the comparison both vivid and fair to teach.
7. Overstated or unclear: The comparison flatters the plan on the third leg: Illinois's liability was tuition, which grows with costs, while Laura's ten payments are fixed in dollars by the case, so most of that risk is absent by construction, not closed by the plan; the item should say so. "no fall in share prices can break a payment or a figure she has stated" holds only because she states nothing until after the January 2031 move; a fall between 2028 and 2031 still lowers what she can state, and the item could say so in the same breath. "What remains is what Illinois never listed" leaves out a few things a fixed income reader would list: a target-maturity fund closing early or merging, and the operational risk of one broker holding everything (delay, not loss, but real for a dated payment). The claim that Illinois "in 2024 ... moved most of its money into Treasuries matched to its payments" I cannot check from here; it needs a source.
8. Question: Since the case fixes the ten payments in nominal dollars, which of Illinois's three open risks did your plan actually close by its own choices, and which did the case close for you?

## 09, item H

1. Assumption: that the range's top would be a projection of the share half's value (an expected or percentile figure) carried into the 2031 meetings, with shares still at risk while the figure is out. How sure: 40 (earlier items had already hinted otherwise).
2. What it means: Nothing much new by this point: it restates that a share counts toward a promise only once it has been sold into something fixed, and adds the clean consequence that after the January 2031 move a 40% fall in shares moves neither end of the range, only the unpromised half.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: no
6. What made it land or not: the "40% fall changes neither end" line is the real content and is correct by construction, but the bought-deal parallel is strained, because a bought deal transfers price risk to an underwriter for a discount, whereas Laura simply sells her own risk to the market, which is the gift-office half of the analogy alone.
7. Overstated or unclear: "shares given to it count at what they fetch when sold" is, I believe, not the general rule: US accounting books a gift of securities at fair value on the day it is received, and the fundraising reporting standards I know value it on the day the donor gives up control, not at the later sale price; some offices credit donors that way, but it is a practice, not "the rule". "The two rules agree: a share counts only once it has been turned into something whose value is fixed" therefore overstates the agreement. "That half is the bought deal" is unclear: who is the bank and who is the issuer? Also unexplained: why exactly half, and why one dated move rather than a gradual one, given that a fall in 2030 hits the whole share holding before the move.
8. Question: Why move exactly half of the shares, all on one day in January 2031, rather than in steps through 2030, and what does a large fall in late 2030 do to the top you would then state?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- B: "one day in sixteen years" and $470,217 in 2020 turn "rates are high" into a measured rarity and show the 2028 deposit is the plan's rate insurance.
- K: a prepaid tuition plan that left all three funding assumptions open, set against a plan that closes them by January 2028, is the asset-liability lesson in one picture I would use in class.
- L: the only item that changes what the stated figure is for: a fully funded pledge lets her go first, unconditionally, where other funders can only wait or over-promise.

## Items that clash or repeat

- E and H repeat each other: both say a gain counts only once it is realised into something fixed (accounting's imparity rule in E, the bought deal and gift office in H); H adds little after E.
- A and L overlap and half clash: both are about co-sponsor coordination, but A has her quote a range while naming the top as her figure, and L has her go first "without conditions" yet attach a condition, so the two leave unclear whether the $115,000 is a commitment, the top of a range, or conditional.
- D and K repeat on one point: new money from her career closes in January 2028 and nothing after that depends on her.
- M and K agree, not clash: building costs reach only the unpromised half; but M keeps that half in shares, which sits awkwardly with H and E's rule that only fixed-value money counts, if the half is meant to cover dated cost overruns.
