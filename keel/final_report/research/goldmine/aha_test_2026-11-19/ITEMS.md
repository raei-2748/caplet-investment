**Item A.** When Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 that she will give $115,000 towards the residency's building, each of
them hears something else: what is left to raise, the building's total less her figure. That remainder is their target.
In a laboratory study of groups that had to reach a threshold together to avoid a loss (Barrett and Dannenberg, 2012),
groups that knew the threshold exactly avoided the loss in 18 of 20 cases; when the threshold was uncertain, the loss
came in 16 of 20, while uncertainty about the size of the loss did no such harm. Fundraising's own counting rules
already refuse to count a pledge whose conditions may fail. So a figure Laura stated while her shares could still fall
would be a target moving under every co-sponsor. She states her range only once both ends are Treasuries she owns, and
names the top as her figure, so every co-sponsor's target is exact: the residency's total less $115,000.

**Item B.** On the plan's own measure (the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance
ahead), $300,000 bought all ten on 10.9% of days since 2000, and on exactly one day from June 2007, the last run of such
days before 2026, to August 2026: Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have cost $470,217, more
than both of Laura's deposits together. Monday 5 October 2026, two trading days before the team's orders filled, was the
cheapest day for the ten since May 2002. Commissions came to $200, against a $37,625 swing in what the ten cost within
2026 ($325,563 on 27 February, $287,938 on 5 October). So the case is set in a rare year, and the plan is built to need
the year, not the day: whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them and buys
the extra Treasuries, and that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points
before January 2027.

**Item D.** Britain's pension regulator lets a pension scheme take more investment risk when its payments are far off,
but only as far as the employer behind it, its 'sponsor', can be relied on to pay in more if things go wrong. Its own
warning example is a house builder whose scheme holds property: the sponsor and the investments share 'common drivers',
so one slump hits both. Laura's plan has a sponsor too, her own writing income, which pays the $150,000 deposit of
January 2028. The team's policy says a writer's income comes 'later and smaller' in the same recession in which shares
fall: the regulator's house builder, in Laura's world. And she has only one call on that sponsor, in January 2028, and
none after it. By the regulator's logic she should stop leaning on her sponsor as soon as its money is in, and the plan
does: the first money of that deposit completes the ten Treasuries behind her payments, and from then on they need
nothing more from her. The UK code asks for less risk in such a case; the plan takes none, which is the team's own step,
not the code's.

**Item E.** Accountants book a likely loss as soon as it is likely, but a gain only once it is realised: 'Probability is
not a factor in the recognition of gain contingencies' (KPMG's handbook on the US standard), and lenders, whose claims
are capped, are the reason the rule exists (Watts, 2003). Old bookkeeping manuals kept a matching habit: entries in ink,
running totals in pencil until the account was closed, then 'write the footings in black ink'. Laura's plan keeps the
same books looking forward, in three places. A likely cost is taken out before any figure is stated: a 0.5% margin for
fund costs beyond their fee (the bond funds of this kind that have already ended cost about 0.27%). A likely gain is
counted as nothing: every payment is sized to be paid even if waiting cash earns no interest. And the likeliest gain of
all, her shares' growth, counts for nothing until January 2031, when half of them buy Treasuries before the meetings: a
top stated even one month before that move would be more than the move buys in about 3 futures in 100.

**Item G.** Laura's ten payments are paid by ten Treasury holdings, one ending before each January, and the interest
those holdings pay before 2033 has to wait, unspent, for years. The plan sizes the ten so that every payment is made
even if all that waiting money earns nothing, and in January 2028 it buys about $36,800 of extra Treasuries for that
alone; without them, the ten as bought in January 2027 would be about $54,600 short on that test. It looks like an
amateur's caution. It is the oldest test in the trade: Martin Leibowitz described a dedicated bond portfolio in 1986 as
'totally passive management' with 'no reinvestment'. The trade's usual ways of passing it are closed to Laura.
Zero-coupon Treasuries, which pay nothing until their end, are banned in this competition. Immunisation, the other
classic method, needs trading whenever rates move, and the plan's policy allows no trade for a market move. With zeros
banned and trading ruled out, buying extra ordinary Treasuries is the only way left to pass the old test.

**Item H.** An investment bank that offers a company a fixed sum for its new shares in a 'bought deal' takes the shares
onto its own books and carries their price risk until it resells them. A university's gift office does the same from the
other side: shares given to it count at what they fetch when sold, and many offices sell them the day they arrive. The
two rules agree: a share counts only once it has been turned into something whose value is fixed, and the one exception
is whoever chooses to keep the price risk. Laura's plan cuts her world shares along exactly that line. In January 2031,
before any co-sponsor hears a figure, half of them move into the Treasury fund that pays out in late 2032, the fund that
already holds the bottom of her range. That half is the bought deal, and the gift office's sale done before the gift.
The other half stays in shares, promised to no one: the exception both rules name, money whose owner carries the price
risk, which is why it never counts in her range. After the move a 40% fall in world shares changes neither end of the
range; only that kept half falls.

**Item K.** Illinois's prepaid tuition plan sold families a promise: pay now, and the state pays the child's university
tuition later. Its own disclosures named the three things the promise rested on: 'continued Contract sales within
projections', 'a projected annual net return on Fund investments', and 'tuition/fee increases being within projected
amounts'. All three stayed open for as long as it kept selling contracts; in 2024 it moved most of its money into
Treasuries matched to its payments. Laura's plan carries the same three: new money is her 2028 deposit, from her career;
returns are her shares; the bill is what the ten Treasuries cost before they are all bought, plus building costs. But
they land differently. Returns never reach a promise she has made: no fall in share prices can break a payment or a
figure she has stated. New money and the price of the ten both close for good in January 2028, when the last Treasuries
behind the ten are bought. Building costs reach only the money she keeps unpromised. What remains is what Illinois never
listed: a US default, and the funds' running costs beyond the small margin held for them.

**Item L.** The big capital funders a building campaign needs most, public arts programmes and large foundations, write
their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed. Two funders that each insist on paying last can
wait for each other indefinitely. Field experiments say a lumpy project, one that is worth nothing until fully paid for,
gets built far more often when someone goes first: seed money announced at two-thirds of the goal, against one-tenth,
raised what others gave nearly sixfold (List and Lucking-Reiley, 2002). So someone has to go first without conditions,
and for most givers that is the over-promise the case warns about, a pledge from money not yet in hand. Laura can go
first without that risk, because by the 2031 meetings her $115,000 is already held for her in Treasuries that pay out in
December 2032. Her condition then lets every other pledge stay conditional without anyone waiting on anyone: if pledges
to the building's total are not all made by December 2032, no co-sponsor has to pay.

**Item M.** Laura keeps half her shares promised to no one, for the three uses her investment policy names: building
costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, and a co-sponsor falling short. A reader may take this for a building's
contingency fund, which the case tells teams not to size. The cost engineers' own definition says it is something else.
AACE International's standard says a project's contingency 'usually excludes' escalation and currency effects, which are
priced apart. Taiwan's culture ministry tells a grantee whose costs rise to raise the shortfall itself, and that it 'may
not ask for more'; in England's arts lottery building programme about two-thirds of overruns fell on the organisations.
So what Laura holds back is money no building budget's contingency and no grant was going to carry: the owner's share
that the profession leaves outside contingency by definition, not a second contingency.
