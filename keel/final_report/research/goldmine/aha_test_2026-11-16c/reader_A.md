# Reader A, cold aha test 3 (16 Nov 2026, evening)

Persona: I build Treasury portfolios for pension funds and endowments and have twenty years in markets. I read only
`brief.md`, with no other repo files and no web, and took the items in order 1 to 8. The brief has no items 9 or 10.

Short version: the best items are about the right reasons the plan is safe (no forced seller, segregated custody).
The weakest ones flatter the plan with comparisons it wins trivially, or draw on evidence that points at a gap the
item doesn't mention (Item 8).

### Item 1

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the 2031 "confidence" would be the usual fan-chart statement:
   "we are 90% confident the contribution lands in $X-$Y". The item shows I had the question in the wrong place.
   Once the move is made the range is certain, and the probabilities only describe which range she will be able to
   state. That is a real reframing of the case's own "with how confident she is" demand, and most of 40 reports
   will get it wrong.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - The Neyman framing is a category stretch. A confidence interval estimates an unknown parameter. Laura's range
     is a pre-funded commitment whose outcome she controls. "The rare case where the answer is known" makes it sound
     like a statistical feat, when it is just defeasance: the liability is funded before you speak. The 59%-of-118
     study is a detour for a finance judge, and I can't check it, so it needs a citation.
   - The item asks the reader to hold too much at once: a 1937 date, a 2014 study, 59%, 118, 92.2%, 59.8%, $100,000,
     $115,000. Quoting 92.2% and 59.8% to one decimal from a simulation is false precision.
   - It opens a hole it doesn't close. The context says the range "at today's market rates" is about
     $95,000-$115,000, yet the model reaches a $115,000 top in only 59.8% of futures. A co-sponsor would hear the
     headline top as a roughly 60/40 call. The item needs to say plainly that $115,000 is today's figure and not a
     forecast.
   - "Both ends are Treasuries she owns, so the range holds" is true for the money. But "Treasuries" here partly means
     Treasury bond funds, not bonds, and the brief elsewhere (Item 6) cuts 0.5% off each end for fund costs. So
     "holds" is near-certain, not exact. That's fine if it is said once.
4. **My question.** If both ends are owned in Treasuries by the 2031 meetings, what decides where between them she
   actually pays in 2033? And why state a range at all rather than commit to the top?

### Item 2

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** For me, nothing new: segregation of customer assets is day-one custody
   knowledge. For a typical judge, the backwards assumption is that a broker failure is a credit loss to the client.
   The item turns it into an operational and timing risk. I didn't expect a student plan to trace the chain past
   the issuer, so it raised my view of the team.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "The only promise anywhere in the chain is the US Treasury's" is overstated. With ETFs there is a fund (a separate
     legal entity), an adviser, a custodian and very likely securities lending with cash collateral reinvested. There
     is also the intermediary-shortfall case: under UCC 8-503 entitlement holders share pro rata in what the
     intermediary actually holds, which is exactly why SIPC exists. These are small risks, but "only" is false. Say
     "the only promise to pay is the US Treasury's".
   - "The risk above it is delay, not loss" is a historical record, not a legal guarantee. Say "in the large failures
     so far, delay, not loss".
   - "Came back in full ... most of them within weeks." The account transfers were quick, but full satisfaction of
     the claims took years. As written, the two halves blur.
   - "89.55% ... 61.28% of it": two decimals is false precision, and "of it" is ambiguous (61.28% of the portfolio or
     of the 89.55%?). "Treasuries held for Laura" also slides: the funds are fund shares that hold Treasuries.
   - "'Owned' is a legal status with a price on it" is unclear. What price? I couldn't decode it.
4. **My question.** Why one broker for 89.55% of the money, when a second custodian costs almost nothing and removes
   even the delay risk around the payment dates?

### Item 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** Nothing new. I build these portfolios. For a lay judge it relabels
   "very conservative" as "endgame portfolio", and that is a useful relabel.
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - The comparison flatters the plan with a contest it wins trivially. A pension's liabilities are inflation-linked,
     tied to longevity and open-ended, and the scheme is usually underfunded with a sponsor in the loop. Laura's
     liability is ten fixed nominal payments, the easiest matching problem in finance, and she is overfunded on day
     one. She "starts where pensions end" because her problem starts at the pension's end state, not because the plan
     is advanced. A pensions person on the panel will say exactly that.
   - "Schemes take decades to reach it" is too general. After the 2022 yield rise many UK schemes reached
     low-dependency or buyout funding in a year or two.
   - Boots is accurate as I remember it, and a good story, but it was a decision by a fully funded sponsor. Say that,
     or the parallel looks borrowed.
4. **My question.** Given that the liability is fixed and nominal, what is the hardest part of Laura's matching
   problem that a pension doesn't face, if any? If there is none, why lead with the comparison?

### Item 4

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed a student team's named scenarios would be mild. The check
   shows they aren't, but "below the median" is not a stress. Nothing backwards; it is good methodological hygiene.
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "Picked to hurt" rests on being worse than the median replay. A stress test should sit in the tail. Give each
     named world's percentile (for example, "the 40% fall sits at about the 3rd percentile of history"). Otherwise
     "harsher than history's middle" is a weak claim dressed as a strong one.
   - "585 replays of history since 1962" are overlapping windows, so there are only a handful of independent
     six-year histories. World-share data also doesn't go back to 1962 (MSCI World starts in 1970, all-country later).
     What proxy fills the gap?
   - "The Bank of England checks ... in the same way" claims too much without a citation and a description of what
     it does.
   - The real surprise is buried: the worst named world costs her only about $3,000-$13,000 of flexibility against
     the median. That shows how small the share sleeve is, which matters more than "picked to hurt".
4. **My question.** At what percentile of the 585 replays does each named world sit, and what share index stands in
   for world shares before MSCI's data begins?

### Item 5

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I came in thinking "the plan is safe because Treasuries are safe". The
   item moves the reason to the liability side: safe assets fail their owners through funding, collateral calls and
   runs, and this plan has no one who can force a sale. That is the lesson of 2022 that I lived through, applied
   correctly. It is the item that most changed how I see the plan.
2. **Strength:** 4
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "Only two reasons to change ... the only two flows it does not own" is overstated. Laura herself can ask for the
     money early (illness, a creditor's judgment, divorce, her estate). So can a fund sponsor closing or changing an
     iBond fund early, and operational failure at the broker can delay a payment. "No one who can ask for the money
     early" is wrong about the one person who can. Say "no lender, no counterparty, no collateral call".
   - SVB fits less cleanly than the other two cases. Its safe assets' duration loss was the trigger, so "not through
     the safe assets" is half true there. "Through short funding against long safe assets" is accurate.
   - Risk of a straw man: few judges imagined Laura borrowing. The item works only if it is framed as why her safe
     assets stay safe, not as a danger avoided.
4. **My question.** What happens to the plan if Laura herself needs $100,000 in 2030? Is anything in the structure
   (a trust, a written undertaking to co-sponsors) stopping her from becoming the run?

### Item 6

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the plan's buffers were stacked prudence, the classic
   actuarial sin where every assumption carries its own margin and nobody knows the total. The item says each margin
   has one job and one spender, which is the right discipline. The airline image makes that vivid.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "If rates hold, about $45,500 of it comes back to her" confuses a cold reader. The context gives the whole
     2028 slice as $38,282, extra Treasuries included, so getting back more than was set aside needs one clause
     (interest over 2028-2042?).
   - The analogy is incomplete. ICAO fuel planning also has final reserve fuel, the fuel you never plan to touch. A
     reader who knows aviation will ask what Laura's final reserve is. If the answer is "the matched Treasuries
     themselves", say so; that completes the parallel.
   - The middle mapping is loose. Alternate fuel is for a named diversion. Zero reinvestment rates for years is a
     scenario, not a destination. That's acceptable, but "only in its named bad case" fits better than "alternate".
4. **My question.** Can any one bad event draw on two margins at once? For example, does a stronger Taiwan dollar ever
   reach the extra Treasuries, or is that firewall written down?

### Item 7

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the risk sat with Laura's own money. This shows the weaker
   promises in her world are everyone else's, with numbers I couldn't have guessed (141 pledged, 46 still paying in
   year five, none by year seven). It is strong client-world research that no other team will have.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - The item draws only the flattering half. If co-sponsors' pledges fade like this, the main risk to Laura's
     residency is the building and its other funders, not her Treasuries. Her money being owned doesn't help if the
     building is never finished. The item should say what her flexibility does about that, or it reads as
     self-congratulation.
   - "In Taiwan, as elsewhere": one foundation's donor drive, one founding law and one museum don't establish a
     general law. Pledge decay is well known in endowment work everywhere, so the line is fine as colour, not as
     proof.
   - "Her contribution figure is owned before she names it" carries the same fund-cost caveat as Item 1.
4. **My question.** If the co-sponsors pay at the rates this history suggests, does the building still get built, and
   which of Laura's margins absorbs the gap?

### Item 8

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed small venues fail on capital (the building). The item says
   they fail when operating money stops. That is plausible, but the evidence is weaker than the claim, and it points
   at the plan's own cliff.
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "All 22 changes of use came in the years after their subsidies ended" is close to automatic. If the subsidies
     were build-and-start grants, nearly any change of use falls after they end. The other 24 of the 46 (those that
     stopped) aren't explained.
   - 46 of 399 is about 12%, so about 88% kept going after their subsidies ended. That cuts against "close when the
     operating money stops" as a rule.
   - Self-own: Laura's operating money stops after January 2042. By the item's own logic, 2043 is when the residency
     is at risk, and the item is silent on it.
4. **My question.** What pays the residency's running costs from 2043, and doesn't your own evidence say that is the
   year it closes?

### Item 9

(This item was added to the brief after I had read items 1-8 and written the closing below. I read it last.)

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the team used bond funds for the middle payments for
   convenience, or to save trading costs, and I would have marked that as a weakness (a fund layer where a bond would
   do). It shows a market gap behind the choice: no original-issue bonds were sold that mature in 2031-2036. I knew
   about the 2001 suspension of the 30-year bond, but I had never tied it to anyone's liability schedule. A budget
   forecast that failed in Washington in 2001 decides how a novelist's 2033 payment is held. That chain is clever, and
   it quietly supports the plan's whole stance against forecasting. The item should say that link outright rather than
   leave it implied.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "No Treasury bond maturing between 15 February 2031 and 15 February 2036. None exists." This is true only in the
     Treasury's narrow naming (bonds as opposed to notes). Ten-year notes issued since 2021 mature right across that
     window, as the item admits two sentences later. A practitioner reads "None exists" as wrong until the
     qualifier arrives. Put the qualifier first: "no 20- or 30-year bond was ever sold that matures then".
   - "That gap was set in Washington in 2001" overstates the cause. The binding constraint for the plan was the
     platform's listing ("listed no Treasury bond"). The notes the funds hold exist in the market, and buying them
     directly would avoid the fund layer. 2001 explains why there are no long bonds in that window, not why Laura
     can't hold notes. Say which constraint actually bound the plan.
   - "The forecast failed within four years" blurs two dates. The surplus was gone almost at once (deficits returned
     in fiscal 2002). Four years is how long the Treasury took to bring the 30-year bond back. Say which.
   - The count doesn't add up. The item says "four of Laura's ten payments (2033-2036) rest on Treasury bond funds",
     then says the 2037 payment also rests on a fund. So five rest on funds: four by necessity and one by choice.
     Say so, or a careful judge will think the team miscounted its own holdings.
   - The last sentence is unclear. Why pass over the 4.5% February 2036 bond for a fund that holds that same bond?
     The likely reason is timing (a December 2036 end sits about two weeks before the payment, against about eleven
     months of waiting cash from a February maturity), but it isn't given. Against that, the plan already holds extra
     Treasuries so that cash waiting at zero does no harm. So a reader will ask why the team paid a decade of fund
     fees and took on fund-closure risk to avoid a wait it had already insured. As written, "a fund that itself held
     the same bond" reads as an irony with no point.
4. **My question.** Ten-year notes maturing in 2032-2035 exist, and the funds hold them. Could Laura's real plan buy
   them directly, or is the platform's list binding on her too? If it isn't binding, why keep four payments behind a
   fund layer?

## Closing

**Item 9's effect on the three I would remember.** It narrowly changes them. Item 9 replaces Item 6 as my third,
because it is the one fact from this set I would repeat to a colleague in my own field. It stays a 3 until the
platform-versus-market point and the four-or-five count are fixed.

**The three I would still remember after 40 reports** (as written before Item 9 was added).
1. Item 5, nobody can run on the plan. Orange County, the 2022 gilt crisis and SVB, set against "no forced seller",
   is the true reason the plan is safe, and the phrase sticks.
2. Item 2, owned, not owed. Lehman's customers were paid in full while its creditors got about 41 cents. I've never
   seen a student report trace custody, and it answers a question I would otherwise ask.
3. Item 6, the airline fuel. It is the most unexpected parallel and the one I could repeat. "Every margin has a job
   and a spender" is a discipline, not decoration.
(A close fourth: Item 1's core idea, that confidence is certainty after the move and the odds belong to the earlier
question. It is the most useful for the case's actual requirement, but its Neyman wrapping and its numbers make it hard
to carry away.)

**Items that clash or repeat.**
- **Items 3, 5 and 6 repeat.** Three cross-industry analogies (pensions, leverage blow-ups, airlines) all land on "the
  plan is matched and nobody depends on luck". Together they read as a parade. Items 3 and 5 overlap most (no sponsor
  relied on, no lender relied on). Keep 5, and fold 3's one good fact (Boots) into it or drop it.
- **Items 7 and 8 repeat.** Both say Taiwan's money stops, and 8 then points at Laura's own 2043 cliff. Use one; 7 is
  stronger.
- **Item 5 clashes with Item 6.** "Only two reasons to change" sits awkwardly beside three margins for fund costs,
  zero rates, overruns, the Taiwan dollar and co-sponsor shortfalls. The fix is one sentence: the margins absorb
  these without changing the plan. Without it, a judge sees a contradiction.
- **Item 1 clashes with Item 4.** If the range is certain, why stress-test it? Say once that the named worlds act
  before the 2031 move, and that the certainty starts only after it.
- **Items 1, 2 and 7 clash with Item 6 on "owned".** "The only promise is the Treasury's" and "both ends are owned"
  sit beside a 0.5% cut for fund costs beyond the fee. That cut shows the funds are not quite Treasuries. Say it once
  and every "owned" claim becomes defensible.
