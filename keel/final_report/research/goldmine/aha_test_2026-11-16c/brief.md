## Context every reader gets

Laura Gao, a graphic novelist, has $300,000 at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028. She must pay ten
fixed $50,000 payments, one each January 2033-2042, to run an artists' residency in Taiwan, "with a high degree of
certainty". In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders) a dollar range for her contribution to the building, with how
confident she is; over-promising would damage her credibility. A student team's plan: the first deposit buys ten US
Treasuries (bonds or Treasury bond funds that end on a set date), one maturing before each payment. The first $38,282 of
the second deposit completes them and buys extra Treasuries so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing.
Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund ending in late 2032, which fixes the bottom of the range; the remainder buys a
world share fund (VT). Half of the shares move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings, which fixes the top; the other
half is promised to no one (her flexibility). At today's market rates the range would be about $95,000-$115,000.

## The items

**Item 1.** Jerzy Neyman, who invented the confidence interval, wrote in 1937 that once a range has been worked out it
either contains its target or it does not: the probability is zero or one, and nobody knows which. That is why a "95%"
label on one range is so often misread (in a 2014 study, 59% of 118 researchers endorsed the false reading "there is a
95% probability that the true value lies in this range"). Laura's 2031 range is the rare case where the answer is
known: after half the shares move into Treasuries before the meetings, both ends are Treasuries she owns, so the range
holds. The percentages belong to an earlier question, asked before the move: which range will she be able to state? In
the team's model the stated top is at least $100,000 in 92.2% of simulated futures and at least $115,000 in 59.8%. So
the range records two facts, not a guess: what was owned first (the bottom, from January 2028) and what the move added
(the top).

**Item 2.** When Lehman Brothers' US broker failed in 2008, what its customers owned came back in full: $106bn,
"fully satisfying the 111,000 customer claims", most of them within weeks. Its unsecured general creditors received
about 41 cents in the dollar. Securities a broker holds for a customer are not the broker's property and are not
subject to its creditors' claims (Uniform Commercial Code 8-503). In the team's portfolio 89.55% is Treasuries held for
Laura at one broker, 61.28% of it through Treasury bond funds whose bonds sit with a custodian bank. "Owned" is a legal
status with a price on it: the only promise anywhere in the chain is the US Treasury's. SIPC's $500,000 limit caps only
its own top-up; the risk above it is delay, not loss.

**Item 3.** A UK defined-benefit pension scheme's last stage is "low dependency": its assets matched to the promised
payments in high-quality bonds, so the sponsor is no longer relied on. Schemes take decades to reach it, and UK law's
low-dependency objective "does not apply to any surplus". Boots, in 2000-01, sold every share for long AAA-rated bonds
while its scheme was still open, and was famous for it. Laura starts where pensions end: from January 2028, five years
before the first payment, all ten are matched in Treasuries and sized so they are paid even if cash waiting earns
nothing, and only money promised to no one takes market risk.

**Item 4.** The team's plan shows its 2031 range in five named markets (for example, world shares falling 40% before
the 2031 move). Checked against 585 replays of history since 1962, all five named markets leave Laura less flexibility
than the median replay ($19,500-$29,800 against $32,300), and none of them reaches history's 1-in-20 high top
($140,000). So the named markets are harsher than history's middle: they were picked to hurt, not to flatter. The Bank
of England checks its own named stress scenarios against a distribution of outcomes in the same way.

**Item 5.** The fixed promises that broke fastest broke through money borrowed on top of safe assets, not through the
safe assets. Orange County's investment pool borrowed $13.0bn to grow a $7.6bn base; five days after it announced a
$1.5bn loss in December 1994 it was bankrupt. In the UK's 2022 gilt crisis, pension schemes "generally benefit from rises
in bond yields", yet had to sell gilts to meet collateral calls on borrowing laid on top of them. Silicon Valley Bank
lost over $40bn of deposits in one day in March 2023. Laura borrows nothing: no loan, no margin, no derivatives, and
no one who can ask for the money early. So nobody can run on the plan, which is why it has only two reasons to change:
the 2028 deposit not arriving, and a default by the US Treasury, the only two flows it does not own.

**Item 6.** Airlines carry fuel in named parts, each with one job: contingency fuel is carried "to compensate for
unforeseen factors" and is expected to be partly burned; alternate fuel is used only in its named bad case, a
diversion; discretionary fuel is carried "at the discretion of the pilot-in-command". Laura's plan has three margins of
the same three kinds: a 0.5% cut on each end of the 2031 range, for fund costs beyond the funds' stated fee (partly
used: the five ended funds of this kind cost about 0.27%); the extra Treasuries bought in January 2028, used only if
cash waiting earns nothing (if rates hold, about $45,500 of it comes back to her); and her flexibility, half the
shares, promised to no one and spent only as she judges (building overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor
falling short). Every margin has a job and a spender.

**Item 7.** In 1996 Taiwan's national arts foundation asked 141 donors to pledge NT$200,000 a year for five years: 137
paid in year one, 46 in year five, none by year seven, and year five brought 32% of year one's money. The foundation's
own founding law promised a NT$10bn fund "within ten years"; fourteen years on it held about 60% of that. Taichung's
planned Guggenheim museum died when the central government withdrew its promised NT$5bn. In Taiwan, as elsewhere, a
promise to pay later fades. Laura's ten payments are owned in Treasuries before the first is due, and her contribution
figure is owned before she names it.

**Item 8.** From 2002 to 2015 Taiwan's culture ministry put NT$5.1bn into 399 local cultural halls. By the end of 2017,
46 had changed use or stopped, and all 22 changes of use came in the years after their subsidies ended (Control Yuan,
2018). Small cultural places close when the operating money stops, not when the building fails. Laura's ten $50,000
operating payments cover 2033-2042, the first ten years, and are owned before the doors open.

**Item 9.** Four of Laura's ten payments (2033-2036) rest on Treasury bond funds instead of single bonds, because the
trading platform listed no Treasury bond maturing between 15 February 2031 and 15 February 2036. None exists. That gap
was set in Washington in 2001: on 31 October 2001 the Treasury stopped selling 30-year bonds, saying it did not need
them and expecting budget surpluses to return. The forecast failed within four years, and the next 30-year bond was
sold on 9 February 2006: a 4.5% bond due 15 February 2036. (The 20-year bond, the other route to those years, had
stopped in 1986 and came back only in 2020.) Shorter Treasury notes do mature inside the gap, and the funds hold exactly
those. For the 2037 payment the team passed over that first 4.5% bond for a fund ending in December 2036, a fund that
itself held the same bond.
