# Cold aha test 3: reader B

Persona: a professor of fixed income at a business school who has judged student competitions. I read only `brief.md`,
in the order 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, with no other files and no web. I could not check any figure or quotation.
Where I say a fact may be wrong, it is a cold judge's doubt and not a verified correction.

### Item 9
**Strength:** 2

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I knew the 30-year bond was suspended in 2001 and came back in February
   2006, so I assumed the gap had a known cause. Nothing was backwards for me. A generalist judge would learn why the
   funds are there, but that tells them about the plan's plumbing, not about how good the plan is.
2. **Strength:** 2. It is good history and fine for a footnote in 2.2. It does not change how I see the plan.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "Four of Laura's ten payments (2033-2036) rest on Treasury bond funds" contradicts the item's own last sentence. If
     the 2037 payment rests on "a fund ending in December 2036", then five payments rest on funds, not four. A reader
     who notices this stops trusting the count.
   - "listed no Treasury bond maturing between 15 February 2031 and 15 February 2036. None exists." This is true only of
     bonds in the narrow sense (original term over ten years). The item then says "Shorter Treasury notes do mature
     inside the gap". So for a single security the binding limit is the platform's list, not Washington in 2001. Either
     the platform listed no notes either, and the 2001 story explains only part of it, or it did list them, and then the
     funds were a choice that needs its own reason. As written, the history explains less than it seems to.
   - "the funds hold exactly those" is too strong. A fund that ends in a given year holds whatever Treasuries mature in
     that year, and the 2036 fund presumably holds the February 2036 bond too, which the last sentence admits.
   - "passed over that first 4.5% bond" gives no reason. My guess is that a fund ending in December 2036 leaves cash
     waiting for weeks rather than eleven months. If so, say so; otherwise it reads as preferring a fee-charging wrapper
     to the bond it holds.
   - The item never says whether the platform's list binds Laura's real plan. If it does not, the gap is not hers.
4. **Question.** For the 2037 payment, what does the December 2036 fund give Laura that the February 2036 bond does not,
   once you count the fund's fee and the chance that it ends early, and did the platform list any 2032-2035 notes?

### Item 8
**Strength:** 2

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed small arts venues fail on demand or management, and I had heard
   of Taiwan's empty halls built without demand. The item claims the cause is operating money ending. It did not
   convince me I had that backwards, because the evidence cannot separate the two causes.
2. **Strength:** 2. It is useful Taiwan colour for client knowledge, but it does not change how I see the plan.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "all 22 changes of use came in the years after their subsidies ended" covers only 22 of the 46. What about the 24
     that "stopped"? Choosing the subset that fits weakens the claim.
   - The timing is close to automatic. If subsidies cover a hall's first years, almost any later change comes "after
     their subsidies ended". There is no comparison group of halls that kept their money.
   - "NT$5.1bn into 399 local cultural halls" sounds like money for building or refitting. If so, "close when the
     operating money stops" calls capital money operating money. The item does not say which kind it was.
   - "Small cultural places close when the operating money stops, not when the building fails" is a general law drawn
     from one Control Yuan count. 46 of 399 (about 12%) in up to fifteen years may not even be a high failure rate.
   - It undercuts itself. Laura's payments cover "the first ten years". By the item's own logic, the danger year is
     2043. A judge will ask that at once.
4. **Question.** If halls close when the money stops, what keeps the residency open in 2043, and does the plan or the
   co-sponsor text say anything about year eleven?

### Item 7
**Strength:** 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed multi-year pledges decay somewhat. I had not pictured how fast:
   from 137 payers to 46 in five years, with year five bringing about a third of year one's money. What I had
   backwards is where the risk sits. The fragile promises in this case belong to the co-sponsors, not to Laura.
2. **Strength:** 3. The decay curve is a usable number for the co-sponsor section. The closing contrast ("Laura's ten
   payments are owned") is the same point as item 2, said again.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "none by year seven" is unclear for a five-year pledge. Does it mean some kept paying in year six? As written it
     sounds like a failure, when the pledge had already ended.
   - Taichung's Guggenheim "died when the central government withdrew its promised NT$5bn". Was it a promise, or a
     proposed budget line the legislature cut? "Promised" carries the argument, so it should be exact.
   - "In Taiwan, as elsewhere, a promise to pay later fades" generalises from three cases of different kinds (donor
     pledges, a statutory endowment target, a budget item). That is fair for colour but not for a rule.
   - "her contribution figure is owned before she names it" is right only after the 2031 move, and only for the range's
     two ends. It is not right for the figure she finally gives.
   - The item draws the self-congratulating lesson and skips the useful one. If co-sponsors' pledges decay like this,
     the building's funding is the fragile part, and her flexibility is what covers it.
4. **Question.** If her co-sponsors' pledges decay the way the 1996 pledges did, how large a shortfall can her
   flexibility cover, and will Laura ask co-sponsors for money paid upfront rather than pledged?

### Item 6
**Strength:** 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the plan had "buffers", and I saw them as one pot of
   conservatism. The item shows they are three kinds with different rules: one expected to be partly used, one used
   only in a named bad case, and one spent at the owner's judgement. That separation is real and I had blurred it.
2. **Strength:** 3. This is the most striking parallel in the set and it tests something: each margin has a job and a
   spender. It is a governance point more than an investment one.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - The analogy leaves out the category that fits best, final reserve fuel, which is never planned to be burned. The
     plan's matched Treasuries are exactly that. With it added, the parallel has four parts and gets stronger, because
     the ladder itself becomes the fuel no one touches.
   - "the five ended funds of this kind cost about 0.27%": per year or over the whole life? Against a 0.5% cut, the
     answer decides whether the margin is ample or too thin. Five funds is also a small sample.
   - "about $45,500 of it comes back to her". From the context, the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit both completes the
     ten and buys the extras, so getting $45,500 back is more than was spent. Which date, and is interest included?
     Unexplained, it looks like an error.
   - "Every margin has a job and a spender": the 0.5% cut's "spender" is the funds' costs, not a person. That is fine,
     but it is not the same kind of spender as Laura's discretion.
   - The quoted aviation wording ("to compensate for unforeseen factors", "at the discretion of the pilot-in-command")
     should come from a named regulation (ICAO Annex 6 or the FAA), not be left floating.
4. **Question.** Is the 0.5% a cut for the whole life or a yearly cost allowance, and on what evidence is it sized
   larger than 0.27% rather than at some other multiple?

### Item 5
**Strength:** 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed a Treasury-matched plan cannot break, and that the risk worth
   checking is forced sale, not default. The item says this well. Nothing was backwards for me, but this is the lens a
   fixed-income judge uses, and "nobody can run on the plan" is the sharpest wording of it in the set.
2. **Strength:** 3. The cases are vivid and the conclusion is right. Two loose claims would cost it marks with me.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "broke through money borrowed on top of safe assets, not through the safe assets" fits Orange County and the 2022
     gilt crisis. It is wrong for SVB. SVB's unrealised losses on its Treasuries and agency mortgage bonds were why
     depositors ran: the safe assets' prices were the trigger, and the deposits were short-term funding that could
     leave. The real lesson is a maturity and liquidity mismatch, meaning funding that can leave before the assets
     mature. Say that and all three cases fit, and so does Laura's plan, because her liabilities are fixed dates she
     sets.
   - "it has only two reasons to change ... the only two flows it does not own" is a non sequitur. Having no leverage
     removes forced sales; it does not remove every other reason to change. Wind-down or merger risk in the four or five
     Treasury funds is a third route, and so is a change in the residency itself. A default is also not a "flow". It is
     a failure of a flow she does own a claim to.
   - "the 2028 deposit not arriving" is called a flow the plan "does not own", but it is Laura's own money. Better:
     "a flow not yet in the portfolio".
   - Orange County: I recall the pool was about $7.5bn levered to about $20bn and the loss as about $1.6-1.7bn. Check
     $13.0bn, $7.6bn and $1.5bn against the source. A fixed-income judge will know this case.
4. **Question.** Apart from those two reasons, what would force a sale before maturity, for instance a Treasury fund
   ending early or merging, and what does the plan do then?

### Item 4
**Strength:** 2

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed student stress scenarios are picked by hand and are often
   flattering, so I expected this item to show they sit in the tail. It shows only that they sit below the median. That
   is weaker than the headline, and it is the reverse of what I hoped to learn.
2. **Strength:** 2. It is a sound methods footnote, but "picked to hurt" overclaims.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "harsher than history's middle" is a low bar. The number that matters is missing: history's 1-in-20 low for
     flexibility. If $19,500 is near the 5th percentile, the claim stands. If it is near the 30th, the scenarios are
     mild.
   - "none of them reaches history's 1-in-20 high top ($140,000)" proves nothing. A downside scenario would not reach
     the 95th percentile of the upside in any case.
   - "585 replays of history since 1962" are overlapping windows, so the number of independent episodes is perhaps a
     dozen. A "1-in-20" read from overlapping windows is fragile. Say so, or do not lean on it.
   - The Bank of England comparison backfires. The Bank calibrates its scenarios to the tail of the distribution, not
     below its median. Inviting that comparison invites a judge to notice the gap.
   - "less flexibility than the median" and "1-in-20 high top" use two different measures (flexibility and the top) to
     make one claim.
4. **Question.** At what percentile of the 585 replays does each named market sit for her flexibility, and what is
   history's 1-in-20 low?

### Item 3
**Strength:** 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed this was textbook cash-flow dedication (Redington, Leibowitz),
   and so it is. What I had not connected is that the UK regime frees the surplus by law. A regulator's own design
   (match the promise, leave the surplus outside it) is the same split as Laura's plan, and that lends the plan an
   institutional precedent.
2. **Strength:** 3. For a generalist judge it is a strong credibility parallel, and Boots is a famous name. For me the
   only new part is the surplus point.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "Laura starts where pensions end" flatters the plan. A pension scheme takes years to reach low dependency because it
     is underfunded and its liabilities are long, often tied to inflation, and depend on how long members live. Laura's
     are ten fixed nominal payments, fully funded on day one. She starts there because her problem is easy, not because
     she is braver than a pension trustee. A judge will say "of course". Say why it is possible for her, and the point
     survives.
   - "Schemes take decades to reach it" is vague: decades from what point? It sounds like a statistic and is not one.
   - "UK law's low-dependency objective 'does not apply to any surplus'": name the instrument (the regulations or the
     Regulator's code). A quoted phrase of law without its source would worry me.
   - "sold every share for long AAA-rated bonds": check "every". I also recall the Boots move was partly reversed later.
     If so, a well-read judge may raise it.
   - "from January 2028 ... all ten are matched": the context says the ten were mostly bought in January 2027. "Fully"
     from 2028 would be more exact.
4. **Question.** If matching is where pension trustees aim to end, why does any part of Laura's money stay in shares
   once the payments and the bottom of the range are owned, and what does the surplus rule tell you about how much?

### Item 2
**Strength:** 3

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed customer assets at a US broker are kept separate from the
   broker's own (SIPA, the customer-protection rule) and that the risk is negligible. Nothing was backwards. What is new
   is the price: 100 cents on the dollar for customers against about 41 for unsecured creditors. That turns "owned
   versus owed" from a phrase into a number.
2. **Strength:** 3. It confirms what a specialist assumes, with a figure I would remember. It is the best answer in the
   set to the question "what if the broker fails?".
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "the only promise anywhere in the chain is the US Treasury's" is wrong as stated. What Laura holds at a broker is a
     security entitlement, which carries the intermediary's duties under UCC Article 8 (8-504 onward). Through the funds
     she owns fund shares, not Treasuries. The fund, its sponsor (who can end or merge it) and the custodian are all in
     the chain. Item 9's funds and item 6's 0.27% cost show there are more parties than the Treasury. Better: "the only
     promise to pay is the Treasury's; everyone else only holds or passes on".
   - "the risk above it is delay, not loss" overclaims. Above SIPC's top-up, loss is possible if the broker's customer
     property is short (fraud or bad records), and customers then share the shortfall pro rata. That is rare and did
     not happen at Lehman, but "not loss" is a guarantee the law does not give. "Delay, and loss only if customer
     property is short" is defensible.
   - UCC 8-503 applies "to the extent necessary" to satisfy entitlement holders. The paraphrase drops the limit.
   - "61.28% of it" is ambiguous: 61.28% of the 89.55%, or of the whole portfolio?
   - "'Owned' is a legal status with a price on it" is a good line, but it only lands if the reader links it back to
     100 against 41 cents. Put the two next to each other.
4. **Question.** If a Treasury fund's sponsor ended the fund early or merged it, what would Laura hold, when, and is
   that risk anywhere in your chain?

### Item 1
**Strength:** 4

1. **What I assumed / what I had backwards.** I assumed the team would do what most teams do: run a simulation and put
   "95% confidence" on its range. What I had backwards is where the probability belongs. It belongs to the question
   asked before 2031 ("which range will she be able to state?"), not to whether the stated range holds. After the move,
   the range holds because both ends are owned. That is the cleanest answer I have seen to Wharton's "range with
   confidence".
2. **Strength:** 4. It reframes the 2031 range for me. The framing needs one correction, below, or a statistician
   judge will mark it down.
3. **Overstated, unclear or likely wrong.**
   - "Laura's 2031 range is the rare case where the answer is known" is a category error if it implies her range is a
     confidence interval. A confidence interval estimates an unknown parameter from random data. Laura's range is a
     bound she has bought; nothing is being estimated. Neyman's point does not apply to it. It shows by contrast why
     her statement should not carry a confidence label at all. Say "this is not a confidence interval, and that is the
     point", not "the rare one that holds".
   - "so the range holds" needs a statement of what the target is. Her eventual contribution is her decision, so the
     range "holds" in the sense that she can pay any figure in it. Fund costs and how a bond fund ends make the ends
     close to known rather than exactly known, which is presumably what item 6's 0.5% cut is for. Say so once.
   - "92.2%" and "59.8%" have false precision. Give one decimal at most, and name the model and the share-return
     assumption behind them. A judge will ask, and item 4 uses a different base (585 historical replays) for the
     top's odds.
   - "59% of 118 researchers" (2014): I believe this is Hoekstra, Morey, Rouder and Wagenmakers (2014). Check the
     count and the exact statement endorsed, because a wrong figure in an item about misreading statistics would be
     fatal.
4. **Question.** Before the move, what is the probability that the top she can state is below $100,000, under which
   model and which share-return assumption, and does the historical replay give the same answer?

## Closing questions

**The three I would still remember after 40 reports**
1. Item 1, where the confidence belongs: the percentages answer "which range can she state?", and the stated range
   itself is certain. It is the only item that changes how the 2031 requirement is answered.
2. Item 2, 100 cents against 41 at Lehman: owned against owed, with a price on it.
3. Item 6, the airline fuel classes: every margin has a job and a spender. It is the most unexpected parallel, and it
   would be stronger with final reserve fuel added as the ladder itself.
   (A near fourth: item 7's 137 payers falling to 46. The number sticks, but its lesson belongs to the co-sponsor risk,
   not to Laura's strength.)

**Items that clash or repeat**
- **2, 7 and 8 repeat each other.** Each ends on "owned, not promised". Items 7 and 8 even close on the same sentence
  shape ("owned before the first is due", "owned before the doors open"). Use the idea once, in item 2, and give 7 and
  8 a different job (co-sponsor risk; year eleven).
- **2 and 9 (and 6) clash.** Item 2 says "the only promise anywhere in the chain is the US Treasury's". Item 9 rests four
  or five payments on funds, and item 6 prices those funds' extra costs at about 0.27%. The funds are a link in the
  chain with their own risks (early end, merger, costs).
- **5 and 9 clash.** Item 5's "only two reasons to change" leaves out a fund ending early or merging, the very
  instrument item 9 explains.
- **1 and 4 use different bases.** Item 1 gives the top's odds from "simulated futures" (92.2% and 59.8%). Item 4 gives
  "585 replays of history" with a 1-in-20 top of $140,000. A judge will ask which distribution the report stands on
  and whether they agree.
- **9 contradicts itself.** "Four of Laura's ten payments" rest on funds, yet the 2037 payment rests on a December 2036
  fund, which makes five.
- **3 and 6 overlap partly.** Both make the point that the surplus is free while the core is matched. Item 3 gives it
  legal precedent and item 6 gives it governance; one of them should carry it.
