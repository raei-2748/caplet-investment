# Cold aha test 3: reader C

Persona: finance director of a mid-sized arts nonprofit. I have run two capital campaigns and sit on a foundation's
grants committee, so I read a pledge the way a funder does. I read only `brief.md`, in the order 4, 7, 9, 1, 6, 2, 8,
3, 5. The items appear below in that order, so later notes can point back to earlier ones.

### Item 4

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a student team's named stress cases would be soft, chosen so the plan survives
   them. The item says the reverse: every named case is worse for Laura than an ordinary historical outcome. I had
   that half backwards, but only half, because "worse than the middle" is not the same as "harsh".
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "they were picked to hurt, not to flatter": being below the median replay is a low bar. A stress case that
     hurts should sit near history's bad tail, so give the 1-in-20 low flexibility next to the $19,500-$29,800. As
     written, the scenarios might sit at the 30th percentile and still pass this test.
   - "none of them reaches history's 1-in-20 high top ($140,000)": a downturn scenario should never reach the lucky
     tail, so this proves nothing about severity. It reads as padding.
   - "585 replays of history since 1962": I took these to be overlapping starting months. If so, they are far fewer
     than 585 independent histories, and the item should say what they are.
   - From a funder's chair, the reassuring fact is missing: does the bottom of the range, and every payment, hold
     in all five? The item talks only about flexibility and the top.
   - "The Bank of England checks ... in the same way" is borrowed authority. I can't tell how close "the same way" is.
4. **Question.** Where does each named market fall in the 585 replays (its percentile), and does any of them reach
   history's 1-in-20 low?

### Item 7

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed multi-year pledges fade. Every campaign I have run wrote some off. I also
   assumed government money is firmer than donors' money. I knew the first point already. The item shows I had the
   second backwards: the foundation's own founding law and the Guggenheim's NT$5bn were promises that faded too. I
   was also wrong about the size: 137 payers falling to 46 is far worse than the write-offs I am used to.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "none by year seven": the pledge ran five years, so nobody owed anything in year seven. Unless this means
     arrears were never caught up, it is no evidence of fading, and a careful reader will catch it and start
     doubting the other figures.
   - "In Taiwan, as elsewhere, a promise to pay later fades" generalises from three cases. The Guggenheim is a
     different animal: a capital project killed by politics, not a pledge left unpaid.
   - The last two sentences change the subject from co-sponsors' promises to Laura's. The item never draws the
     conclusion a funder draws at once: if co-sponsors' promises fade like this, Laura's owned figure protects her
     own share of the building, not the building itself.
4. **Question.** If co-sponsors' pledges fade the way the 1996 pledges did, how much of a shortfall can her
   flexibility cover? Does the co-sponsor language ask them to hold their share the way she holds hers?

### Item 9

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a Treasury existed for every year and that the funds were the team's
   convenience. The item says the funds were forced: there is a real hole in the bond calendar, made by a 2001
   policy call. That is new to me, but it is an origin story more than a change in how I see the plan.
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "None exists" followed by "Shorter Treasury notes do mature inside the gap" reads as a contradiction to anyone
     who does not know the difference between a bond and a note. Then why didn't the team buy those notes
     directly? Presumably the platform did not list them, but the item doesn't say.
   - "That gap was set in Washington in 2001": the next sentence gives the 20-year bond's 1986-2020 pause as a
     second cause, so 2001 alone did not set it.
   - The last sentence (passing over the February 2036 bond for a fund that held the same bond) is the most
     interesting line, and it is left unexplained. I guess the fund ends closer to the January 2037 payment, but I
     shouldn't have to guess.
   - For a funder it is trivia unless it ends on what the funds cost Laura compared with single bonds.
4. **Question.** For the four payments resting on funds, what does Laura give up compared with owning the notes
   herself: the fee, an end date set by someone else, the chance the fund closes or merges early?

### Item 1

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the 2031 range would be a forecast with a confidence label, the kind I discount
   whenever a campaign tells me "we expect $X-$Y with 80% confidence". The item shows that after the move the range
   is not a forecast at all: both ends are already owned, so the confidence question is settled before she speaks.
   For someone who reads pledges for a living, that is the right idea backwards. Most ranges a funder hears are
   hopes, and this one isn't.
2. **Strength:** 4
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - The Neyman analogy is slightly off. A confidence interval estimates an unknown quantity, and Laura's range is
     an amount she holds and controls. The point still lands, but a statistician on the panel may object that the
     two are different things.
   - "at least $115,000 in 59.8%": the context gives "about $95,000-$115,000" as the range at today's rates, so the
     headline top is close to a coin flip in 2027. That is fine once the item explains it ("an earlier question"),
     but the first time I read it I thought the range had been oversold.
   - "so the range holds": it holds net of fund costs. Item 6 says a 0.5% cut is taken on each end for exactly that
     reason, so "holds" leans on that estimate. Say "holds after a small cut for fund costs" or leave it, but the
     two items should agree.
   - Clear and well ordered otherwise. The two percentages are well placed.
4. **Question.** If the top is owned as well, what would make her give less than the top? What should co-sponsors
   budget on, the bottom or the top? (On a grants committee we would budget on the bottom and treat the gap as
   discretionary.)

### Item 6

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a plan like this keeps one buffer: a lump, a "contingency". In my sector a
   reserve policy with named purposes (operating reserve, capital replacement, board-designated opportunity fund)
   is ordinary good practice, so the idea itself is not new to me. The airline mapping is new, and it is vivid,
   especially alternate fuel (used only in one named bad case) set beside the extra Treasuries.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "each with one job": the third margin (her flexibility) has three listed jobs (overruns, a stronger Taiwan
     dollar, a co-sponsor short). That is what discretionary fuel is, so it isn't wrong, but "one job" and the list
     sit awkwardly together.
   - "about $45,500 of it comes back to her": the item never says how large the extra Treasuries were, so I can't
     tell whether that is most of it, all of it, or more than went in (with interest).
   - "a spender": for the 0.5% cut the "spender" is the fund's costs, not a person. The phrase works for the third
     margin only.
   - The funder's real worry is missing. If co-sponsors know Laura carries a pool that will cover "a co-sponsor
     falling short", some will give less. In a campaign you never tell the other donors there is a backstop.
4. **Question.** Will co-sponsors be told her flexibility exists? If so, how does she stop it becoming the money
   they count on?

### Item 2

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed that above SIPC's $500,000 a broker's failure could cost a customer real money.
   Our own investment committee has spent time on exactly that limit. The item shows the limit is the wrong thing to
   watch: securities held for a customer are not the broker's to lose, and Lehman's customers got everything back
   while its creditors got 41 cents.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "the risk above it is delay, not loss": too absolute. Segregation protects customers only if the broker obeys
     it. The well-known failures where customer property went missing (the Madoff case, or a broker that dips into
     customer accounts) are exactly where SIPC's top-up is needed. "Usually delay, not loss, unless the broker broke
     the rules" is the honest version.
   - "the only promise anywhere in the chain is the US Treasury's": the fund manager and the custodian bank still
     have to do their jobs (hold the bonds, pay out at the end). They make no promise to pay, but the chain does run
     through them, and a sceptic will say so.
   - "89.55% is Treasuries ..., 61.28% of it through Treasury bond funds": I can't tell whether 61.28% is a share of
     the whole portfolio or of the 89.55%.
   - "most of them within weeks": I would want the share and the date, since the whole item turns on delay.
4. **Question.** Why keep every holding at one broker? What would splitting across two cost, and would it shorten
   the delay the item admits?

### Item 8

1. **Before / backwards.** Nothing backwards. Every arts funder knows that buildings without operating money fail
   (the "edifice complex"), and it is the first thing a capital-campaign feasibility study tests. The Taiwan figures
   are good local evidence for something I already believed.
2. **Strength:** 2
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "all 22 changes of use came in the years after their subsidies ended": that covers 22 of the 46. The item is
     silent on whether the 24 that stopped also stopped after their money ended. If they did, say so. If not, the
     claim is selected.
   - 46 of 399 is under 12%, so most halls survived. "Small cultural places close when the operating money stops"
     sounds like the rule when it describes a minority.
   - The item backfires for a funder. Its lesson is that places die when the money stops, and Laura's money stops in
     January 2042. The next thing I read for is the plan for year eleven, and the item doesn't give one.
4. **Question.** What keeps the residency open from 2043, after the tenth payment? Is any of her flexibility, or the
   operating reserve, meant for that?

### Item 3

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed that matching a promise to bonds is what you do at the end, once a fund is
   mature, and that a plan five years out would still be growth-heavy. The item shows Laura matches at the start
   and takes risk only with money promised to no one. That reverses the pension order.
2. **Strength:** 3
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "Laura starts where pensions end" flatters the plan. A pension's promises run for decades, depend on how long
     people live and often rise with inflation, so matching them is hard. Ten fixed nominal payments are far easier
     to match. A pension expert on the panel will say the comparison is unequal.
   - "from January 2028 ... all ten are matched": the context says the first deposit buys them in January 2027 and
     2028 completes them. "Completed from January 2028" would be accurate.
   - The UK clause about surplus is the best detail, because it maps exactly onto her flexibility. The item could
     lean on it more.
   - "was famous for it": famous, but did it last? If Boots later reversed the move, a judge who knows will mark it
     down.
4. **Question.** What did matching so early cost: how much more would she expect to have in 2033 if the shares had
   stayed in until 2031, and why was that worth giving up?

### Item 5

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the risks in a Treasury plan were rates and inflation. I had never thought of a
   run as a risk to a plan like this. The item reverses that: safe assets break promises only when someone has
   borrowed on top of them and the lender can call. My own sector has seen the same thing, with campaigns that
   borrow a bridge loan against pledges that then fade (item 7). That connection makes it stick.
2. **Strength:** 4
3. **Overstated or unclear.**
   - "no one who can ask for the money early": Laura can. If her income fails before 2033 (she is a graphic novelist
     with an uneven income), she is the one person who can run on her own plan. The item should name her and say
     what stops her, or it will be the first objection a judge raises.
   - "it has only two reasons to change ... the only two flows it does not own": other things could change the plan
     too (a fund closing or merging early, which item 9 makes relevant), and calling a US default a "flow" is
     awkward.
   - The three examples are strong and well chosen. The Orange County line is the clearest.
4. **Question.** What stops Laura herself from selling the Treasuries in a personal emergency before 2033? Is there
   any written rule or structure, or only her intention?

## Closing

**The three I would remember after 40 reports.**
1. **Item 1** (the range that is known to hold). It changes what a funder does with the number: I would budget on
   an owned figure, and I would discount a forecast. No other item changes how I read her 2031 pledge.
2. **Item 5** (nobody can run on the plan). It names a risk I had not thought of and shows the plan doesn't carry
   it, as long as the team deals with Laura herself.
3. **Item 7** (Taiwan's pledges fade). 137 payers in year one and 46 in year five is the number I would quote to my
   own board. Drop "none by year seven", or explain it.

Runner-up: item 6, for the airline image, although its content is ordinary reserve policy to anyone in my job.

**Clashes and repeats.**
- **Items 7 and 8 repeat each other.** Both use Taiwan evidence to say that money promised later, or money that
  stops, sinks cultural projects, and both end on "owned before". Keep 7. Item 8 also invites the year-eleven
  question.
- **Items 2 and 7 share one idea** (owned, not owed: one legal, one cultural). They work together if they sit in
  different parts. Back to back, the second will feel like a rerun.
- **Items 1 and 6 pull against each other.** Item 1 says both ends are owned, "so the range holds". Item 6 says
  each end carries a 0.5% cut against fund costs that are only estimated (about 0.27% seen so far). The owned
  figure is an owned figure minus an estimate, so item 1 should say so.
- **Items 2, 5 and 9 against each other.** Item 2 says the only promise in the chain is the Treasury's, and item 5
  says the plan has only two reasons to change. Item 9 shows four payments rest on funds with end dates someone
  else sets, which is a third possible reason. Each item reads cleanly alone, but together they expose an
  overclaim.
- **Item 3 against the context.** "From January 2028, all ten are matched" sits oddly next to "the first deposit
  buys ten US Treasuries" in 2027.
- **Items 3 and 6 overlap.** Both make the same final point, that only money promised to no one takes risk. Use the
  point once, at full strength.
