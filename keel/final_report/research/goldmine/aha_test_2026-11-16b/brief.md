## Context every reader gets

Laura Gao, a graphic novelist, has $300,000 at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028. She must pay ten
fixed $50,000 payments, one each January 2033-2042, to run an artists' residency in Taiwan, "with a high degree of
certainty". In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders) a dollar range for her contribution to the building;
over-promising would damage her credibility. A student team's plan: the first deposit buys ten US Treasuries (bonds or
Treasury bond funds that end on a set date), one maturing before each payment. Most of the second deposit buys a
Treasury fund ending in late 2032, which fixes the bottom of the range; the rest buys a world share fund (VT). Half of
the shares move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings, which fixes the top; the other half is promised to no one.

## The eight items

**Item 1.** In January 2027 the plan buys ten Treasuries for about $302,000 that together pay $500,000: ten payments of
$50,000 from 2033 to 2042. No share price is needed to close that gap; the Treasuries' own interest does it. The interest
they pay before the first payment alone, reinvested until it is needed, is worth $93,941 by 1 January 2033, nearly two
of the ten payments. The shares in the plan are there for the building, not for the payments.

**Item 2.** A market fall can shrink the next figure Laura states, never one she has already stated. Most of each
payment is owned in Treasuries from January 2027; the rest, and the bottom of the 2031 range, from January 2028; the top
of the range before the 2031 meetings. If world shares fell 40% before the 2031 move, the range she states would be
about $95,000-$105,000 instead of about $95,000-$115,000, with the bottom and every payment unchanged. If they fell 40%
after the move, the range and the payments would not change at all; only the money promised to no one would fall.

**Item 3.** The ten Treasuries as first priced cost $302,287: "fully funded at market", as a pension would say. That
assumes every dollar of interest is reinvested at today's forward rates, which the ten need to earn about 5.12% a year.
Replayed over 585 starting months since 1962, the ten as priced would have come up short in 55.6% of them. Trusts sized
at the IRS's old 5% assumption failed the same way when rates fell. So the plan meets a stricter standard: the ten are
paid even if cash waiting for a payment earns nothing. That costs $336,656, about $34,000 more; it was short in none of
the 585 replays; and in the central case most of the extra (about $45,500 in 2033 money) comes back to Laura.

**Item 4.** Every documented failure of a high-grade fixed promise (pension funds, insurers, escrows, lotteries,
structured settlements) fits one of five patterns: leverage and collateral calls; relying on someone else's promise; a
payer who stops paying in; reinvestment at a set rate; and a payout that arrives late from a fund or a broker. Laura's
plan has none of the first two. It carries the other three, each small and sized: the 2028 deposit (if it never came,
the 2033 payment would be $3,016 short at forward rates); reinvestment (closed in January 2028 by buying extra
Treasuries); and a fund paying late (the late-2032 fund ends about 17 days before 1 January 2033).

**Item 5.** The top of the range Laura states in 2031 depends on how world shares do from 2028. Yet moving the assumed
share return anywhere from 4% to 10% a year moves the top by about two-thirds of one $5,000 step, and 14 of 15 major
investment houses' published forecasts give the same $115,000 top. What does matter is timing: a top stated 1, 3, 6 or
12 months before the shares are moved into Treasuries would not yet be owned in 2.9%, 6.2%, 8.7% or 10.7% of simulated
futures. So Laura names the top only after the move. US law holds the government to the same rule: an agency may not
commit money before it has been appropriated.

**Item 6.** Fundraising advisers tell charities to announce a building campaign once 60-80% of the money is secured. In
a study of America's new arts buildings from 1994 to 2008, theatres ended about 92% over their first budgets, while the
rises the public heard of were about 19%, because managers chose when to announce. Laura announces in 2031 with 100% of
the range she names already owned in Treasuries, bottom and top. A co-sponsor hearing her number is hearing money that
exists, not a target.

**Item 7.** Half of Laura's shares are promised to no one, as flexibility for a building that costs more than planned.
But building costs tend to rise in the same years shares fall: in 2008 Taiwan's construction costs rose 14.0% while its
stock market fell 46.0%. So the flexibility is a cushion, never a hedge: it is likely to be smallest in the very year it
is most needed. The plan says so, and its monitoring table watches for a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031.

**Item 8.** The team's first plan, in September 2026, put about three-quarters of Laura's money in shares to grow it.
Simulated, it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets, in 13.7% if the 2028 deposit was only $75,000, and in 40.8%
if it never came. The shares did well on average; the danger was that they could be down on one of ten fixed dates.
The plan's own test showed this on 27 September, before any trade, and the plan changed: buy the payments first, in
Treasuries, and put only unpromised money in shares.
