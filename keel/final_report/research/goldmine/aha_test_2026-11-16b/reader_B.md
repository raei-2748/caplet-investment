# Reader B (fixed-income professor), cold aha test 2

Read cold, in the order given (Item 8 first, Item 1 last), with no files and no web. My "before" for the later items
is coloured by the earlier ones, and I say where that happens. Scores: 1 = nothing new, 5 = changed how I see the plan.

## Item 8

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a share-heavy first plan fails because of market risk, and I'd have guessed a
   75% equity plan with six years to go misses a fixed payment more often than 3.2%. Backwards: in their own numbers
   the bigger danger was not the market but counting on a deposit that hadn't arrived (3.2% rising to 13.7% and then
   40.8%), and the item never says so.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "the danger was that they could be down on one of ten fixed dates": imprecise. In a plan that sells to pay, the
     danger is the order of returns. A fall early in the payout years forces sales at low prices and weakens every
     later payment, not one date. "One of ten dates" undersells how the risk works.
   - "Simulated", with no model given. A figure to a tenth of a percent (3.2%) depends on the assumed return,
     volatility and tail shape, and a normal-returns simulation understates tail misses. Give the inputs or round.
   - The item never gives the new plan's figures in the same three cases, so I can't see how much it improved.
     Item 4's "$3,016 short" is the comparable number and belongs next to 40.8%.
   - In its favour, the plan's own test changed the plan before any trade. That reads as learning, not hindsight,
     and it is good material for a reflection.
4. **Question.** "Put the current plan through the same three cases (base, a $75,000 deposit, no 2028 deposit): what
   are its numbers, side by side with 3.2%, 13.7% and 40.8%?"

## Item 7

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the unpromised half of the shares was a sensible buffer against cost overruns.
   Backwards, and partly new to me: the buffer may move the wrong way against the very cost it is meant to cover.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "building costs tend to rise in the same years shares fall": one year is not a tendency. 2008 is a special case,
     with a steel and commodity spike in the first half and the crash in the second. In 2021 both rose. Give a
     correlation over every year you have, or say "can".
   - "its stock market fell 46.0%": wrong index for this plan. Laura holds world shares (VT), not Taiwan's index.
     World shares fell by roughly 40-something percent in 2008, so use that series.
   - Currency: is the 14.0% in Taiwan dollars? Laura pays in US dollars, and the Taiwan dollar tends to weaken in
     global sell-offs, so the squeeze may be smaller in US-dollar terms. Also check that both figures use the same
     measure (annual average or December to December).
   - "it is likely to be smallest in the very year it is most needed": "likely" goes further than one year of evidence
     supports. "Can be" is defensible.
   - The jump to "a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031" is never explained. That is a currency risk, not the
     link between costs and shares that the rest of the item is about. And watching is not acting: what does Laura do
     when the watch trips?
   - In its favour, "a cushion, never a hedge" is the honest word. Most teams would have claimed diversification.
4. **Question.** "Across every year of data, what is the correlation between Taiwan's construction costs in US
   dollars and world shares, and what does Laura do when the Taiwan-dollar watch trips?"

## Item 6

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed Laura's 2031 range was a forecast with a confidence level, as the case asks,
   and I'd have guessed arts-building overruns at 20-40%. Backwards: her range is fully owned, so the uncertainty
   sits in the building's cost, not in her money, and 92% is far worse than I'd have guessed.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - Apples and oranges. The 60-80% rule is about a whole campaign's money, while Laura's 100% is one donor backing
     her own pledge. Any serious donor's pledge should be backed, so the contrast flatters her.
   - "theatres ended about 92% over" in a study of "arts buildings": a residency (studios and rooms) is not a theatre,
     and theatres are probably the worst subset. Give the figure for all arts buildings or for the closest type.
   - "because managers chose when to announce": this claims a cause. Does the study say it, or is it the team's
     reading?
   - The statistic cuts against the plan. If the building runs anything like 92% over, a $95,000-$115,000
     contribution plus an unpromised half of perhaps $20,000 is small beside the overrun. The item raises the risk and
     then leaves it.
   - "100% of the range she names already owned ... bottom and top": then why is it a range? If the top is owned she
     can pledge the top, and the "confidence" the case asks for becomes trivial.
4. **Question.** "If the top is already owned, what decides where in the range her contribution lands, and what is
   the confidence attached to?"

## Item 5

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the top of the range depends mostly on the assumed share return, and that the
   team's main modelling risk is the forecast. Backwards: the forecast barely matters, and the real risk is the gap
   between saying a number and owning it.
2. **Aha: 4.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "14 of 15 major investment houses' published forecasts give the same $115,000 top": this only restates the 4-10%
     test. If any return from 4% to 10% moves the top by less than one step, every forecast in that band rounds to
     the same top. It is not separate evidence, and the fifteenth house presumably sits just across a rounding line.
     It is also a projected top, not the top, which is set by prices on the day of the move.
   - Much of the insensitivity comes from the $5,000 rounding and a short horizon (2028-2031) on a small share
     holding. Say so, or a reader will think the team found a law.
   - The average return doesn't matter, but the path does: Item 2's 40% fall moves the top by two steps. "What does
     matter is timing" is right about when to state the number, while volatility still sets its level.
   - "would not yet be owned in 2.9% ... of simulated futures": awkward. "The shares, when sold, would fall short of
     the stated top in ..." is clearer.
   - The appropriations parallel (the Antideficiency Act) is about legal authority, not money in hand. Congress
     appropriates money it borrows, from the same issuer whose bonds Laura holds. "The same rule" overstates it,
     while "the same discipline" is fine. It is clever and judges will remember it, though a sceptical fixed-income
     reader will smile.
4. **Question.** "Is the date of the move fixed in advance whatever the market has done, and if world shares have just
   fallen 30%, does Laura still sell half?"

## Item 4

1. **Before / backwards.** I'd already list a Treasury ladder's failure modes as funding, reinvestment and timing.
   Nothing new.
2. **Aha: 2.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "Every documented failure of a high-grade fixed promise ... fits one of five patterns": overstated, and wrong as
     stated. It leaves out at least three causes:
     - Liabilities underestimated. Deficient reserves are the commonest cause of insurer insolvency, and pensions
       fail on longevity and salary assumptions.
     - Assets and liabilities of different lengths: the savings-and-loan crisis.
     - Fraud or misappropriation by a trustee or custodian.

     Laura's payments are fixed, so the first doesn't bite here, but "every" cannot be defended. "The failures we
     studied" can.
   - "Laura's plan has none of the first two": she does rely on someone else's promise, the US Treasury's, plus a
     fund sponsor's. "No promise but the US Treasury's" is accurate and still strong.
   - "$3,016 short at forward rates": this uses the forward-rate assumption that Item 3 rejects. On the plan's own
     zero-reinvestment standard, the gap without the 2028 deposit is far larger (on Item 3's figures, roughly
     $35,000 at 2027 prices). Use one standard throughout.
   - "a fund paying late (... ends about 17 days before 1 January 2033)": 1 January is a holiday in both countries
     and the money has to cross a border. Seventeen days covers the fund's final payout plus a wire only if someone
     has checked both. A buffer of days says nothing about the size of the risk.
4. **Question.** "Where in your five patterns does the commonest cause of insurer failure (reserves set too low) sit,
   and if it doesn't fit, why 'every'?"

## Item 3

1. **Before / backwards.** I expected the usual mistake: price the ladder at market and call it funded. Backwards:
   they caught it, and the 55.6% shows that forward rates overstated the rates actually earned more often than not.
   That is partly the term premium and partly the fall in rates after 1981, and many practitioners forget it.
2. **Aha: 4.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "it was short in none of the 585 replays": a tautology. With no reinvestment the cash flows are fixed by
     construction, so no history can make them short, unless the replay re-prices the ladder from each start month,
     which the item doesn't say. The replay is not evidence for the stricter standard. "By construction it cannot
     fall short" is the stronger and more honest claim.
   - "which the ten need to earn about 5.12% a year": unclear. Say it is the ladder's yield, and that reinvested
     interest must earn it too.
   - "55.6%" is a frequency with no size. Were the ladders short by $200 or by $20,000? Give the median and worst
     shortfall, because a judge cannot weigh a frequency alone.
   - "Trusts sized at the IRS's old 5% assumption failed the same way": which trusts, which rule, which years? It is
     unsourced and vague, and a fixed-income judge cannot check it. Cite it or cut it.
   - "costs $336,656, about $34,000 more ... about $45,500 in 2033 money comes back": this compares 2027 dollars with
     2033 dollars and reads like a gain. $34,000 at about 5% for six years is about $46,000, so the honest version is
     "nearly all of it comes back, with interest, in the central case".
   - $336,656 is more than the $300,000 first deposit. Say that the last part is bought in January 2028 at 2028
     prices, which leaves one year of rate risk on it.
   - In its favour, "sufficient without reinvestment" is the escrow standard used when bonds are defeased. It is real
     and well chosen.
4. **Question.** "In the roughly 325 replays that fell short, what were the median and worst shortfalls in dollars?"

## Item 2

1. **Before / backwards.** From the opening context I expected a crash to shrink the contribution, so the principle
   was nothing new. One thing I worked out from it was new: a 40% crash cuts the top by only $10,000, so the shares
   add only about $20,000 to the top at all.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "never one she has already stated": an absolute claim. Some of the ten are funds that end on a date, and such a
     fund has no contractual principal. Its end value is its net asset value: close to par, but net of fees and of
     cash sitting idle near the end, and the fund can close early. A judge accepts "never" for a bond more readily
     than for a fund, and one clause on the funds would cover it.
   - "Most of each payment is owned ... from January 2027" is correct, but it makes Item 1's "buys ten Treasuries for
     about $302,000" (from a $300,000 deposit) look wrong.
   - "only the money promised to no one would fall": "only" plays down what Item 7 says matters, since that money is
     the building's cushion.
4. **Question.** "If a 40% crash moves the top by just $10,000, the shares add about $20,000 to the top: why hold
   shares for the building at all, rather than pledge one owned figure?"

## Item 1

1. **Before / backwards.** Nothing new: $500,000 paid over 2033-2042 and discounted at about 5% is worth about
   $300,000, and interest closes the gap by definition. Having just read Item 3, I'd assumed the plan does not lean
   on reinvested interest, and this item says it does.
2. **Aha: 1.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / wrong.**
   - "ten Treasuries for about $302,000 that together pay $500,000": not without reinvestment. Coupon bonds costing
     $302,000 pay $500,000 only if their interest is reinvested at forward rates. That is the assumption Item 3 says
     failed in 55.6% of replays, and the plan replaced it by paying $336,656. On its face, this item describes the
     standard the team rejected.
   - "for about $302,000" from a $300,000 deposit doesn't add up unless the item says the rest comes in January 2028,
     as Item 2 does.
   - "$93,941", to the dollar, is a projection six years out at assumed reinvestment rates: false precision. "About
     $94,000" is the most it can claim.
   - "Treasuries ... pay": some of the ten are funds, and a fund pays what its holdings return, not a stated sum.
   - The line that interest alone is worth nearly two payments works for a lay co-sponsor, but not for a judge.
4. **Question.** "If the interest earns nothing, what do your ten pay in total, and why does this item quote $302,000
   rather than the $336,656 the plan actually buys?"

## The three I would remember after 40 reports

1. **Item 5.** The forecast doesn't matter, the timing does (2.9%, 6.2%, 8.7%, 10.7%), plus the rule against
   committing money before it is appropriated. It is the one idea here that is both original and decides something.
2. **Item 3.** The ladder priced at market would have fallen short in more than half of 585 replays, and the plan
   meets the escrow standard instead. It shows the team understands fixed income, not just a ladder.
3. **Item 6.** Theatres ran 92% over budget while the public heard of 19%. The number sticks, though it may stick
   against them unless they answer the overrun it implies.

A close fourth is Item 7's 2008 pair: building costs up 14.0% while shares fell 46.0%.

## Clashes and repeats

- **Clash: Items 1 and 3.** Item 1 sells a $302,000 ladder whose reinvested interest closes the gap. Item 3 says that
  ladder fails in 55.6% of replays, so the plan buys $336,656 instead. A judge reading both concludes the team
  contradicts itself. Item 4's "$3,016 short at forward rates" has the same problem, because it sizes a risk on the
  standard Item 3 discards.
- **Repeat: Items 2, 5 and 6.** All three make one point, that Laura states a figure only once it is owned. Item 5 is
  its strongest home and Item 2 its worked example, while the end of Item 6 says it a third time.
- **Smaller repeats.** Item 5's "14 of 15 houses" restates its own 4-10% test, and Item 4's reinvestment pattern
  restates Item 3.

## One general note

Most items carry three to five exact figures. After 40 reports the figures blur and the arguments stay with me. The
strongest items would each earn one memorable number and give the rest as support.
