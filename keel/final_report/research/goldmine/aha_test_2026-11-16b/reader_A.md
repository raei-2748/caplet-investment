# Reader A (practitioner), cold aha test 2

Read cold: no repository files, no web. I judged only the eight items and the context paragraph. My lens is a
fixed-income and asset-allocation practitioner with an MBA, reading report 23 of 40.

Scores: 1:2 | 2:3 | 3:4 | 4:3 | 5:4 | 6:3 | 7:2 | 8:2 (mean 2.9)

---

## Item 1. The interest closes the $302,000-to-$500,000 gap

1. **Before / backwards.** Nothing new. I assumed bond yields close the gap between about $300k today and $500k paid
   over 2033-2042, and they do: that is the present value at about 5%. The $93,941 sizes the interest earned before
   2033, which I had not sized, but it changes nothing about how I see the plan.
2. **Aha: 2.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "In January 2027 the plan buys ten Treasuries for about $302,000": the first deposit is $300,000. Read alone,
     this says the plan spends money Laura does not have in January 2027. Item 2 later says only "most of each
     payment" is bought then. Item 1 has to say where the extra $2,000 comes from, or not imply the whole $500,000
     is bought in 2027.
   - "ten Treasuries ... that together pay $500,000": coupon bonds pay more than $500,000 in total. The item's own
     next sentence counts $93,941 of interest on top. Say "pay the ten $50,000 payments".
   - "reinvested until it is needed, is worth $93,941": reinvested at what rate? Item 3 says the plan is built so it
     does not rely on reinvestment, so this item leads with the one assumption the team later throws out (see clashes).
   - The precision is uneven: "about $302,000" sits beside "$93,941". A modelled figure to the dollar reads as false
     precision.
   - The last sentence, "The shares ... are there for the building, not for the payments", is the strongest line, but
     the context paragraph already says it.
4. **Question.** If the plan is built to pay even when waiting cash earns nothing (Item 3), why is a reinvested figure,
   $93,941, the number you lead with?

## Item 2. A fall shrinks the next figure, never one already stated

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a range given in 2031 is a forecast that a bad market can break. The item shows I
   had the order backwards: each figure is bought before it is said, so market risk only reaches figures not yet
   stated. As finance this is dedication or immunisation, nothing new. As a way to explain it to a client, it is the
   clearest sentence in the set.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "never one she has already stated": this holds for share prices only. Between January 2027 and January 2028, "the
     rest" of each payment is not yet owned. A fall in Treasury yields during 2027 (a bond rally, which often comes
     with a share crash) makes that remainder dearer. The item reads "market fall" as a share fall only and is silent
     on rates.
   - "with the bottom and every payment unchanged": this depends on the 2028 deposit arriving. Item 4 lists that
     deposit as a live risk, so it is a condition, not a fact, until January 2028.
   - The item packs three dates and two scenarios into one paragraph. Each piece is clear, but the whole needs a small
     timeline table, and a judge skimming it will lose the 2027/2028 split.
4. **Question.** How much would a one-point fall in Treasury yields during 2027 add to the cost of the "rest" bought
   in January 2028, and which money pays for it?

## Item 3. "Fully funded at market" came up short in 55.6% of history

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a ladder of Treasuries matched to the payment dates, bought at market, is fully
   funded, with reinvestment risk as a rounding error. I had the size backwards: with coupon bonds, "at market"
   quietly assumes the coupons are reinvested at forward rates, and history fell short of that more often than not.
   Fixing it costs about 11% more. This is the item most likely to make a bond person stop and reread.
2. **Aha: 4.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "short in none of the 585 replays": if the standard is "paid even if cash waiting earns nothing", this is true
     by construction. Only a default could make it fail, so the replay proves nothing. Presenting it as evidence
     next to the 55.6% oversells it. Say instead that it cannot fall short, by design.
   - "585 starting months since 1962": these are overlapping windows dominated by one rate cycle (rising to 1981,
     then falling for forty years). That is a handful of independent observations, not 585. The 55.6% probably also
     reflects the term premium: forwards on average overstate the short rates that follow. Saying so would make the
     team look stronger, not weaker.
   - The frequency is given but not the size. Is the shortfall in the 55.6% $500 or $30,000? A practitioner cares
     about that more than the percentage.
   - "Trusts sized at the IRS's old 5% assumption failed the same way": I cannot place this. If it means the
     charitable-remainder-annuity-trust 5% test, that test is a 5% probability of the trust running out, not a 5%
     return assumption, and the parallel breaks. As written it is an unsupported appeal to authority. Name the trust
     type, the rule and the years, or cut it.
   - "which the ten need to earn about 5.12% a year": the grammar is tangled. Does the reinvested cash need to earn
     5.12%, or the whole ladder?
   - "That costs $336,656, about $34,000 more": this is more than the $300,000 first deposit. The item does not say
     the extra comes from the 2028 deposit (Item 4 hints at it), or that the plan meets this standard only from
     January 2028.
   - "most of the extra (about $45,500 in 2033 money) comes back to Laura": "most" is unclear. $34,000 grown to 2033
     at about 5% is roughly $44,000-$46,000, so this looks like all of it plus interest. Which is it?
4. **Question.** In the 55.6% of replays that fell short, how big was the typical shortfall, and the worst, in dollars?

## Item 4. Five ways high-grade fixed promises fail

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a Treasury-only plan has essentially no failure mode except a US default. The
   item shows the remaining risks are about flows and operations (a missed deposit, reinvestment, a fund paying out
   late), not credit. That is a useful re-framing. A failure taxonomy is the kind of thing a risk officer respects.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "Every documented failure ... fits one of five patterns": "every" cannot be shown and invites a judge to find a
     counter-example (fraud, operational error, legal or tax changes). Write "the failures we studied", and name one
     real case per pattern. With no examples, this reads as a framework the team made up and then passed.
   - "Laura's plan has none of the first two": the Treasury itself is someone else's promise, and so are the fund
     sponsors and the broker who hold the ten. "None" works only if pattern two is defined as below-Treasury credit.
     Define it.
   - "if it never came, the 2033 payment would be $3,016 short at forward rates": Item 3 just said forward rates fell
     short 55.6% of the time and the plan rejects that basis. Under the plan's own no-reinvestment standard, the
     shortfall without the 2028 deposit should be nearer the $34,000 of extra Treasuries. Quoting the lower number
     on the basis the team rejected looks like picking the flattering figure.
   - "each small and sized": only one of the three has a dollar size. Reinvestment is "closed" (no number), and the
     late fund has a margin in days, not a size.
   - "a fund paying late (the late-2032 fund ends about 17 days before 1 January 2033)": the context says some of the
     ten payment Treasuries are also funds with end dates. Why name only the late-2032 fund?
4. **Question.** Under your own no-reinvestment standard, not forward rates, how short would the 2033 payment be if
   the 2028 deposit never arrived?

## Item 5. The forecast barely matters; the timing does

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the top of the 2031 range turns on the share-return assumption, so the team's
   choice of forecast is the key debate. I had it backwards: over about three years, on a modest sleeve rounded to
   $5,000 steps, the forecast hardly moves the top. Volatility against the date it is stated is what matters. Most
   reports I read spend a page defending a return assumption. This one shows why that page is wasted.
2. **Aha: 4.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "would not yet be owned in 2.9%, 6.2%, 8.7% or 10.7% of simulated futures": before the move, the top is not owned
     in 100% of futures. What is meant is that the move would not then buy the stated top. Reword it. As written, a
     careful reader stumbles.
   - "14 of 15 major investment houses' published forecasts give the same $115,000 top": partly an effect of rounding
     down to $5,000 steps. That is fine, but say so, or the robustness looks like more than it is.
   - The appropriations-law parallel ("an agency may not commit money before it has been appropriated") is apt and
     memorable, but one sentence is the limit. Some judges will find it ironic to hold up the US government as the
     model of not over-promising.
4. **Question.** What does keeping the shares until just before 2031, rather than moving them earlier, add to the
   expected top in dollars?

## Item 6. Theatres ran 92% over; the public heard 19%

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed a donor's pledge range is a target like any campaign figure. The item shows that
   in arts building, announced numbers are managed by timing, and that Laura's is not: what she says is already owned.
   The 92%-versus-19% figure is new to me and sticks.
2. **Aha: 3.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - The comparison mixes two different things. The 60-80% rule is about a whole campaign's money from many donors.
     Laura's 100% is her own pledge, one donor's share. Owning all of her own share says nothing about whether the
     building is funded. The line "money that exists, not a target" is true of her slice only.
   - "because managers chose when to announce": that is the study's interpretation, stated here as settled cause.
     Soften it to "largely because".
   - The item misses its own biggest point. If arts buildings run about 92% over their first budgets, the real danger
     to Laura is the overrun, not her pledge. That makes the flexibility half the crux, and Item 7 says that half is
     weakest when needed. Neither item puts the two together.
   - "100% of the range she names already owned ... bottom and top": if both ends are owned before she speaks, what is
     the range, and what is the "confidence" the case asks for? A range whose top is fully owned is a guaranteed
     amount with a smaller guaranteed amount below it. A judge will ask why she does not simply pledge the top.
4. **Question.** If both ends of the range are already owned when Laura speaks, what confidence is she stating, and why
   give a range rather than the top?

## Item 7. The flexibility is a cushion, never a hedge

1. **Before / backwards.** I assumed the unpromised shares are a rough contingency for overruns, broadly unrelated to
   building costs. The item claims the opposite: costs rise when shares fall, so the cushion is thinnest when needed
   (wrong-way risk). The distinction between a cushion and a hedge is honest, and practitioners like a team that names
   its own weak spot. But I do not yet believe the claim.
2. **Aha: 2.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "building costs tend to rise in the same years shares fall: in 2008 ...": one year is an anecdote, not a
     tendency. 2008 had a steel and commodity spike early in the year and a crash late in it. Over most cycles I would
     expect construction costs and shares to rise together in booms. Give the correlation across all years, or write
     "can rise".
   - "its monitoring table watches for a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031": the link to building costs is not
     explained here, and neither is why the threshold is 10% or why 2031 is the base. As written, the last sentence
     reads as bolted on.
   - "likely to be smallest in the very year it is most needed": "likely" rests on that single year.
4. **Question.** Across every year you have, not just 2008, how do Taiwan's construction costs and world share returns
   actually move together?

## Item 8. The first plan failed its own test

1. **Before / backwards.** Nothing new as finance. I expect a first-instinct growth plan to fail fixed dated payments
   (sequence risk). What is new is the 40.8%: the growth plan leaned on the 2028 deposit far more than it looked. As a
   reflection it is the right kind of story, a decision changed by evidence before money moved.
2. **Aha: 2.**
3. **Overstated / unclear / likely wrong.**
   - "missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets": many advisers would call 96.8% a high degree of certainty. The
     item never says why 3.2% was too much. The strongest reason is in its own numbers: the dependence on the 2028
     deposit (13.7%, 40.8%). Lead with that.
   - "The plan's own test showed this on 27 September": the passive voice hides the team. For the reflection section,
     judges want who doubted what, what they argued and what changed their minds. "The plan's own test" reads like
     process, not experience.
   - "The shares did well on average; the danger was that they could be down on one of ten fixed dates": good, but
     slightly narrow. The risk is a run of bad years across the payment period, not one bad date.
4. **Question.** A 3.2% chance of a missed payment is a 96.8% success rate. Why wasn't that a "high degree of
   certainty", and what number would you have accepted?

## The three I would still remember after 40 reports

1. **Item 3.** "Fully funded at market" fell short in more than half of history, and the team paid about $34,000 to
   meet a standard that cannot fail. Almost no student team gets to reinvestment risk at all, let alone sizes and
   prices it. It needs the shortfall sizes and an honest note on overlapping windows.
2. **Item 5.** The return forecast doesn't matter and the timing does, with the probabilities by month. It ends the
   forecast debate most reports waste a page on.
3. **Item 6.** Theatres ran 92% over budget while the public heard 19%. It is a vivid number from outside finance, and
   it sticks even though the item draws the wrong lesson from it (see above).

Item 2's first sentence ("A market fall can shrink the next figure Laura states, never one she has already stated") is
the best single line in the set. I would remember the line, not the item.

## Clashes and repeats

- **Items 1 and 3 clash (the main one).** Item 1 sells the plan on reinvested interest closing the gap at about
  $302,000. Item 3 says that very basis fell short in 55.6% of history, and that the plan actually costs $336,656 so as
  not to depend on it. A judge reading both will think the team contradicts itself, or that Item 1 is the version from
  before Item 3's finding. Item 1's $302,000 also exceeds the $300,000 first deposit without explanation.
- **Items 3 and 4 also clash.** Item 4's "$3,016 short at forward rates" uses the basis Item 3 rejects.
- **Items 2, 5 and 6 repeat one idea.** Each says Laura states only what she already owns. Item 2 says it as sequence,
  Item 5 as timing, Item 6 against fundraising practice. In a 9-12 page report, give the idea one home (Item 2's line)
  and let 5 and 6 add only their new evidence.
- **Items 6 and 7 leave a gap between them.** Item 6's 92% overrun and Item 7's wrong-way cushion together imply the
  flexibility half may be far too small for the risk the building actually faces. Neither item says so, and a sharp
  judge will.
