# Reader C (nonprofit finance director), cold aha test 2

Read cold, once straight through, then a second time where I got stuck. Nothing else was opened. I am a numerate
board member, not a bond trader: I know what a capital campaign is, what "fully funded" means for a pension, and how
building budgets behave. I don't know Treasury mechanics beyond the basics.

---

## Item 1

(1) **Before:** I assumed that turning $300,000 into $500,000 of payments needed growth assets, so I expected shares
to be carrying part of the payments. **Backwards:** the bonds' own interest closes the gap, so the shares can be kept
entirely for the building.

(2) **Aha: 3.** It's a clean separation (bonds for the payments, shares for the building) and the "nearly two of the
ten payments" figure helps. To a finance person it is still present-value arithmetic, not a surprise.

(3) **Problems:**
- "reinvested until it is needed, is worth $93,941": reinvested at what rate? Item 3 says that counting on
  reinvestment at today's rates would have failed in more than half of history. So this figure seems to rest on the
  assumption the team later rejects. Read cold, I can't tell which basis it uses.
- "for about $302,000" against a $300,000 deposit: the item doesn't say where the extra $2,000 comes from. Item 2
  answers that, but on its own this reads as a small hole in a plan whose whole selling point is having no holes.
- "together pay $500,000": they pay more than that in total, since interest keeps coming after 2033. "Together fund
  the ten $50,000 payments" is what you mean. A minor point.

(4) **Question:** How much of the $93,941 is left if the cash waiting for a payment earns nothing, the standard you
use in Item 3?

## Item 2

(1) **Before:** I assumed a crash in 2029 or 2030 would threaten what she had promised, and that the plan's job was to
survive it. **Backwards:** she only states what she already owns, so a crash can only make her *next* figure smaller.
It can't touch a figure she has already given.

(2) **Aha: 4.** This changed the question I was asking from "how risky is the portfolio?" to "what has she said
yet?". The opening sentence is the best single line in the set. The 40%-fall case, with the bottom and the payments
unchanged, makes it concrete.

(3) **Problems:**
- "never one she has already stated": this holds against markets. Still, part of every payment is only bought in
  January 2028, with the second deposit. Between 2027 and 2028 the payments rest on that deposit arriving, and a
  novelist's income can fall in the same recession that drops shares. One clause naming the deposit as the exception
  would make "never" safe.
- "the 2031 move": I understood it only because the context defined it. In the report it needs its definition nearby.

(4) **Question:** How far would world shares have to fall before the move for the top to collapse onto the bottom,
and what would Laura say to co-sponsors in that year?

## Item 3

(1) **Before:** I assumed that bonds matched to the payment dates meant the job was done, and "fully funded at market"
is the standard I'd accept from a pension fund. **Backwards:** that standard assumes reinvestment at today's forward
rates, and when replayed through history it came up short more often than not.

(2) **Aha: 5.** This is the item that changed how I see the plan. The team didn't just use the professional standard;
they found where it breaks and paid about $34,000 to meet a stricter one that I could explain to my own board in one
sentence: "paid even if cash earns nothing."

(3) **Problems:**
- "would have come up short in 55.6% of them": without the size of the shortfall this is alarming. It reads as if a
  plain bond ladder fails more often than a coin toss. If the typical shortfall is a few hundred dollars, say so. If
  it is thousands, that is all the more reason to say so. Give the worst case in dollars.
- "Replayed over 585 starting months since 1962": I couldn't follow how a ladder bought in 2027 is "replayed" in 1962.
  I assume you ran historical changes in interest rates through the reinvestment, but one clause should say it.
- "Trusts sized at the IRS's old 5% assumption failed the same way": which trusts, and which 5% assumption? I sit on a
  foundation board and still couldn't place it. Name it in plain words or cut it.
- "about $34,000 more ... most of the extra (about $45,500 in 2033 money) comes back": $45,500 is bigger than
  $34,000, so "most" reads as a mistake, or as a profit. Put both figures in the same year's dollars.

(4) **Question:** In the replays that came up short, how many dollars short was the worst one, and for which payment?

## Item 4

(1) **Before:** I assumed high-grade promises fail through a default or a crash. **Partly backwards:** the failures
listed come from how the promise was built (borrowing, leaning on someone else's promise, assumed reinvestment), not
from the bonds themselves.

(2) **Aha: 2.** The idea of checking the plan against how other fixed promises actually broke is a good one, but the
delivery is a dense list. The item overreaches and I trusted it less by the end.

(3) **Problems:**
- "Every documented failure ... fits one of five patterns": "every" is an enormous claim. It reads like a taxonomy
  built to fit this plan. The fifth pattern, "a payout that arrives late from a fund or a broker", is suspiciously
  specific to a plan that owns a fund ending in 2032. And "lotteries" puzzled me: I don't know of a lottery failure of
  this kind.
- "Laura's plan has none of the first two": a Treasury *fund* is the fund company's promise to pay out. That is
  arguably relying on someone else's promise, which is presumably why pattern five exists.
- "each small and sized": only one of the three gets a dollar figure. Reinvestment gets a fix but no size, and "17
  days" is a margin, not a size.
- "the 2033 payment would be $3,016 short at forward rates": Item 3 calls forward rates the yardstick the team rejects.
  The reinvestment fix ("closed in January 2028 by buying extra Treasuries") is also bought with the 2028 deposit. So
  if the deposit never came, two of the three "small" risks would open at once. By the team's own stricter standard,
  the shortfall would then be far larger than $3,016, roughly the $34,000 of extra Treasuries as well.
- "a fund paying late (the late-2032 fund ends about 17 days before 1 January 2033)": I couldn't tell whether 17 days
  is the danger or the safety margin, or which promise it threatens. The context says the late-2032 fund holds the
  bottom of the building range, not a payment.

(4) **Question:** If the 2028 deposit never arrived, what would the shortfall be under your own Item 3 standard (cash
earns nothing), rather than at forward rates?

## Item 5

(1) **Before:** I assumed the share-return forecast would be the big swing factor in the top, as it is in every
endowment projection I've seen. **Backwards:** the forecast barely moves the top. When she names it is what matters.

(2) **Aha: 3.** The timing figures (2.9% to 10.7%) are a strong, quantified case for waiting, and that part works.
The rest of the item is hard going.

(3) **Problems:**
- "moves the top by about two-thirds of one $5,000 step": say "about $3,300". I had to stop and convert. I also
  couldn't see why the effect is so small until I guessed that the sum is modest and the years are few. One clause
  giving the amount would fix that.
- "14 of 15 major investment houses' published forecasts give the same $115,000 top": this repeats the first finding
  as if it were new evidence, when it follows from it. And what does the fifteenth give?
- "The top ... depends on how world shares do" and then "moving the assumed share return ... moves the top by" very
  little: these read as a contradiction until you separate the average return from a bad path. Item 2's 40% fall
  moves the top by two steps. Say the distinction in so many words.
- "would not yet be owned in 2.9% ...": I had to reread this. I think you mean that by the time of the move, the
  shares would no longer buy the top she had already named. Say that.
- "US law holds the government to the same rule": this is an analogy, not the same rule. An appropriation is legal
  permission to spend, not money owned. It's clever, but "the same" is overstated, and a jump to federal budget law in
  the last line may lose readers who were with you until then.

(4) **Question:** What would Laura give up by moving the shares earlier, say in 2030, so that she could name the top a
year before the meetings?

## Item 6

(1) **Before:** I assumed announcing at 60-80% secured is responsible practice. I've signed off on campaigns that way.
**Backwards:** Laura's announcement is a different category. Her number has no gap left to close.

(2) **Aha: 4.** This is my world, and the contrast landed. "Money that exists, not a target" is the line I'd repeat to
my board.

(3) **Problems:**
- "theatres ended about 92% over their first budgets": this is the strongest number in the set, but here it is used
  to make a point about *when* managers announce, and the item never comes back to it. To a board member, the 92% is
  the real threat to Laura (the building), and a fully owned pledge does nothing about that. Item 7 picks it up; this
  item leaves it hanging.
- "because managers chose when to announce": this is a causal claim on one study. "Largely because" or "in part
  because" would be safer unless the study says it outright.
- "100% of the range she names already owned in Treasuries, bottom and top": if the top is owned too, why is it a
  range at all? This was my biggest confusion in the whole set. A range normally signals uncertainty, and here
  nothing seems uncertain.
- The 60-80% figure is for a whole campaign's total, while Laura's 100% covers only her own pledge. The item states
  it correctly ("hearing her number"), but the comparison could let a skimming reader think the building is funded.

(4) **Question:** If both ends of the range are already owned, why state a range rather than one figure, and what
decides where in it her actual contribution lands in 2033?

## Item 7

(1) **Before:** I assumed the unpromised half of the shares was a sensible reserve for cost overruns.
**Backwards:** it is likely to be smallest in the very year overruns hit.

(2) **Aha: 4.** Admitting where your own cushion fails is rare in student work, and it made me trust the other items
more. The 2008 pair (+14.0% and -46.0%) sticks.

(3) **Problems:**
- "building costs tend to rise in the same years shares fall": one year is an example, not a tendency, and the claim
  is contestable. After 2008, many building bids came in *lower* as contractors chased work. If you have more years,
  give the count ("in X of the Y worst years for shares").
- "its stock market fell 46.0%": Laura owns world shares, not Taiwan's market. World shares also fell hard in 2008, so
  the point probably survives, but use the fund she actually owns.
- "The plan says so": this is weak, and it reminds me there is a plan document I haven't seen. Cut it, or say what the
  plan does.
- "its monitoring table watches for a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031": a non sequitur. The paragraph is about
  building costs and shares, then jumps to currency with no link. I worked out that a stronger Taiwan dollar means her
  US dollars build less, but the item should say it. And "watches" leaves me asking: then what?

(4) **Question:** If 2032 looks like 2008, what does Laura actually do: delay the building, shrink it, or ask the
co-sponsors for more?

## Item 8

(1) **Before:** I assumed a high school team would start with growth, and that a first plan which failed would be
quietly dropped rather than reported. **Nothing new on the finance:** the risk of being down on a fixed date is
familiar to me. What is new is that they show the numbers that killed their own plan.

(2) **Aha: 3.** It is credible and dated, and it belongs in the reflection. As an insight it is the most familiar of
the eight.

(3) **Problems:**
- "missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets": to many readers 3.2% sounds like success (about 97% safe). Say why
  1 in 31 fails "a high degree of certainty", or set it beside the new plan's figure on the same test.
- "The plan's own test showed this": a plan doesn't test itself. Who ran the test, and what did the team argue about?
  As written, the change has no people in it, and in a reflection the people are the point.
- The 13.7% and 40.8% figures need the new plan's numbers for the same deposits beside them (Item 4's $3,016). That
  contrast is the strongest thing Items 4 and 8 could say together, and neither one says it.

(4) **Question:** What failure rate would you have accepted for the payments, and who decided that 3.2% was too high?

## The three I would still remember after 40 reports

1. **Item 6.** "A co-sponsor hearing her number is hearing money that exists, not a target." It is in my world: the
   60-80% rule and the 92%-versus-19% study sit right next to a pledge with no gap.
2. **Item 3.** "Fully funded at market" came up short in more than half of history, so they bought the stricter
   standard. It's counterintuitive and I could retell it.
3. **Item 7.** Building costs +14%, shares -46%, in the same year. The cushion is smallest when it is needed, and the
   honesty sticks.

Runner-up: Item 2's first sentence. I might remember that sentence even without the item around it.

## Clashes and repeats

- **Items 1 and 3 clash.** Item 1 credits $93,941 of *reinvested* interest with closing the gap. Item 3 says that
  counting on reinvestment at today's rates failed in 55.6% of replays. Read back to back, I wondered whether Item 1's
  arithmetic is exactly what Item 3 rejects. The fix is to give Item 1's figure on the cash-earns-nothing basis, or
  to say which basis it uses.
- **Items 3 and 4 repeat, then disagree.** Both cover reinvestment risk being closed by extra Treasuries in January
  2028. Item 4 then sizes a missing deposit at forward rates, the yardstick Item 3 throws out, and ignores the fact
  that the reinvestment fix is paid for by that same deposit.
- **Items 2 and 5 overlap.** Both argue that the top is owned before it is stated. One item can carry both, with Item
  5's timing percentages as the evidence for Item 2's rule.
- **Items 6 and 7 don't clash, but they belong together.** Item 6 raises the 92% overrun and leaves it. Item 7 is the
  honest answer to it.
