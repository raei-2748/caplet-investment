# Reader B: cold read of three items (first-round judge)

Each "Assumed before" line was written before I opened or read the item.

## Item 1: chart, "What more shares would buy" (c2_more_shares.png)

- **Assumed before:** Yes. She has six years until the building money is needed, so more than a quarter in shares should
  leave her more to give, and a quarter looks timid.
- **What it shows:** Every step toward more shares lowers and widens the range she can state in 2031 (from a fixed $130k
  with nothing in shares down to $0-$70k with everything in shares), while what she has left in a typical future rises
  only about $10k (plan to all shares) and what she has left in a bad 1-in-20 future falls by about $25k.
- **What you had backwards:** I thought of shares as "more money for the building", but what she can promise in 2031 is
  what she owns, and more shares shrink that. The extra growth turns up in the leftover, and there's not much of it.
- **Aha strength:** 3. The left panel would score 4 on its own, because the staircase of falling ranges answers my
  question the other way round. But the right panel shows the plan barely beats "all to the floor" (about the same bad
  case, about $5k better median) while stating less than that row's $130k. So the chart raises doubts about the plan's
  quarter in shares about as much as it defends it.
- **Eye landed first:** The bold "The plan" row and its black bar at $95k-$115k. Then I went down the rows to "$0k-$70k".
- **Hard to read:**
  - I don't know what "All left to her beyond the ten" covers: the building contribution plus her flexibility? The
    operating reserve too?
  - The right axis starts at $125k, so the gaps between dots look larger than they are.
  - Nothing says why the TOP of the range falls as shares rise. I expected the top to rise, so this needs one line of rule.
  - The caption's "split five ways" reads like dividing the money among five buckets. "Five ways to split it" would be
    clearer.
- **Caption plus chart enough without prose?** No. The left panel's message lands, but without a sentence I can't tell why
  the top falls, what the right panel adds up, or why the plan is better than "all to the floor".
- **One question it leaves me with:** If putting everything into the floor lets Laura state $130,000 outright and leaves
  her about as well off in a bad future, what does the quarter in shares buy that is worth a lower, less exact number in
  front of co-sponsors?

## Item 2: words, why not spread the ten payments across several kinds of safe bond

- **Assumed before:** Yes, some spread across safe issuers (Treasuries plus high-grade munis, agencies or corporates) is
  ordinary prudence: don't put all the eggs in one basket.
- **What it shows:** When the goal is to make every one of ten payments, mixing independent issuers raises the chance
  that at least one fails (about 1 in 10 at 99% each). One issuer whose promises stand or fall together does not have
  that weakest-link problem. The diversification that matters is between this money and the rest of Laura's life, which
  all sinks in the same recession.
- **What you had backwards:** I took diversification to lower the risk of failing. It lowers how much the total swings,
  but with ten payments that each must be made, independent issuers multiply the chances of a miss.
- **Aha strength:** 4. The 9.6% sum is easy to check in your head, and it turns a textbook reflex upside down. The point
  about her book advances, share prices and co-sponsors falling together shows real client knowledge. It loses a point
  because the last sentence crams a second big idea into a clause, and "the diversification the plan does is of Laura
  herself" is clumsy.
- **One question it leaves me with:** Is 99 in 100 a fair figure for the real alternatives, given that AAA munis or
  agency bonds may be far safer than that? And does the "one issuer" logic still hold for the Treasury bond FUNDS, which
  add fund-level risks (early closure, one fund sponsor) that are not the US Treasury's promise?

## Item 3: chart, what the ten payments would have cost each day since 2000 (c1a_cost_history_strip.png)

- **Assumed before:** Roughly yes: $300,000 is about the going price for ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out,
  and the case writers chose the number to just about fit.
- **What it shows:** From mid-2007 to August 2026, $300,000 was enough on only one day (19 Oct 2023). For most of those
  19 years the same ten payments cost $350k-$470k, peaking at $470,217 in August 2020. Today's price is the cheapest
  since May 2002.
- **What you had backwards:** $300,000 is not a normal price at all. It is a rare window that has barely opened in two
  decades, so buying all ten payments now isn't just caution. It takes a chance that may not come back.
- **Aha strength:** 5. It changes the plan in my head from "a conservative team playing safe" to "a team that saw a
  rare price and took it". The single-day annotation stays with me.
- **Eye landed first:** The $470,217 peak label at the top. Then I went to the right edge, where the line drops onto the
  $300k rule.
- **Hard to read:**
  - The black ("enough") and dark grey ("not enough") line look almost the same, so the 2000-2002 stretch and the
    2004/2006/2007 dips are hard to pick out. A clearly different colour or weight would fix it.
  - "19 Oct 2023: the one day from June 2007 to August 2026" breaks off before saying "when $300,000 was enough". You
    need the caption to finish it.
  - The two right-edge labels ("$300,000: the first deposit", "5 Oct 2026: the cheapest since May 2002") are stacked.
    The dot sits below the line, so it takes a second to see which label belongs to which mark.
- **Caption plus chart enough without prose?** Yes. A line that sits above $300,000 for nineteen years and touches it
  once tells the story. A finance reader knows rates are the reason without being told.
- **One question it leaves me with:** Laura's money arrives in January 2027. If rates fall before then and the price
  climbs back over $300,000, what exactly does the plan do, and how much of the 2028 deposit could that use up?

## Which item I would remember after 40 reports

Item 3. A price line that sits at $350k-$470k for nineteen years and touches $300,000 on one day before now takes a
glance to read, and it changes the whole plan from caution into seizing a rare moment. Item 2 is the cleverest argument
of the three, but it is words in a paragraph, and the picture is what I would carry into the panel discussion.
