## Context both readers get (the same)
Laura Gao, a graphic novelist, has $300,000 at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028. She must pay ten
fixed $50,000 payments, one each January 2033-2042, to run an artists' residency in Taiwan, "with a high degree of
certainty". In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders) a dollar range for her contribution to the building; over-
promising would damage her credibility. A student team's plan: the first deposit buys ten US Treasuries (bonds or
Treasury bond funds that end on a set date), one maturing before each payment. Most of the second deposit buys a
Treasury fund ending in late 2032, which fixes the bottom of the range; the rest buys a world share fund (VT). Half of
the shares move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings, which fixes the top; the other half is promised to no one.

## The words
**W1.** Of the roughly $111,700 left in January 2028 after the payments are finished, the plan puts about three-quarters
in the late-2032 Treasury fund and about a quarter in shares. Put three-quarters in shares instead and, across 200,000
simulated futures, everything Laura is left with beyond the ten payments rises at the median by about $7,000 ($198,000
against $191,000) but falls by about $14,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future ($145,000 against $159,000); the range she can state
in 2031 drops from $95,000-$115,000 to $30,000-$85,000, and her contribution lands under $95,000 in 62 futures in 100
instead of 3. Put everything in the Treasury fund instead and she states one higher figure ($130,000) but is left with
less in total in about 63 futures in 100.

**W2.** Why not spread the ten payments across several kinds of safe bond, as diversification would suggest? If each of
ten promises were 99 in 100 safe on its own, independently, at least one would fail about 1 time in 10 (1 - 0.99^10 =
9.6%). Ten Treasuries are one issuer's promises, which stand or fall together, so there is nothing to spread; the
diversification the plan does is of Laura herself: her book advances, share prices and co-sponsors' budgets tend to fall
in the same recession, so the money already promised sits where a recession cannot reach it.

**W3.** On the plan's own measure (the ten payments, valued on each day's Treasury rates), $300,000 bought all ten on
exactly one day between June 2007 and August 2026: Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have
cost $470,217, more than both of Laura's deposits together. On 5 October 2026, when the team was buying, they cost about
$288,300, the cheapest since May 2002. Commissions came to $200, against a swing of $37,625 in the ten's cost within
2026. The plan never needed that luck: if the ten had cost more than $300,000, the first part of the 2028 deposit was
already set aside to finish them.
