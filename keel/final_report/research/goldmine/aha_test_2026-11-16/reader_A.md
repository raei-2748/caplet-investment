# Reader A: cold first-round read, three items (16 Nov test)

Read in order, one at a time. Only the chart image was opened.

## Item 1 (words): more shares, or fewer, in the 2028 money

- **Assumed before:** Yes. She has five years or more before the money is needed, so more shares should mean more money, and a young-ish plan can ride out a fall.
- **What it shows:** Moving the 2028 leftover from a quarter shares to three-quarters shares adds only about $7,000 at the median but costs about $14,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future. It also wrecks the 2031 promise: the range she can state falls to $30,000-$85,000, and she lands under $95,000 in 62 futures in 100 instead of 3. Going all-Treasury does the opposite: one bigger promise ($130,000) but less money in total in about 63 futures in 100. So the quarter-in-shares mix is a deliberate middle between what she can promise and what she ends up with.
- **What you had backwards:** I treated "years to go" as the horizon that matters, but the horizon that bites is the 2031 meeting, three years out, where a shrunken or missed range costs her credibility. The trade is also lopsided: the downside is twice the median gain.
- **Aha strength:** 4. It turned a default "more equity for a long horizon" reflex into a priced trade-off on three measures at once (median, bad case, the stated range). That is exactly what I want to see a team defend.
- **One question it leaves you with:** If the late-2032 Treasury fund "fixes the bottom", why does the plan's own mix still land under $95,000 in 3 futures in 100? Is $95,000 rounded above the true floor, or is the floor not really fixed? (Smaller stumble: "after the payments are finished" first read to me as "after 2042". It seems to mean "after the ten payments' bonds are fully bought". It also packs about ten numbers into one paragraph, so I had to read it twice.)

## Item 2 (chart): ten promises, icon array

- **Assumed before:** Yes. Spreading across several kinds of high-quality bond (Treasuries, agencies, top-grade corporates) is standard prudence. It cuts exposure to any one issuer and picks up a little yield.
- **What it shows:** If each of the ten payments has its own small, independent 1% chance of failing, the chance that at least one of the ten fails is about 1 in 10 (0.99 to the tenth power is about 0.90). So for ten separate must-pay promises, each one should sit on the safest asset there is, not on a mix of nearly-safe ones.
- **What you had backwards:** Diversifying protects a total. Here each payment is its own promise, and every extra independent risk adds another way to break one. Independence makes "at least one miss" likelier (about 10%) than if the risks moved together (1%). That inversion is the real insight, but I had to supply it myself; the chart does not say it.
- **Aha strength:** 3. The idea behind it is a 4 (diversification works against you when every link must hold), but the chart shows only the arithmetic. It never shows the alternative the plan chose, so the point half-lands.
- **Where my eye landed first:** The solid black block of ten squares at the top left, before the title.
- **What was hard to read:** The ten black squares look like "the ten payments", not "10 Lauras out of 100". With ten payments in the story, a row of ten filled squares is easy to misread. Nothing says one square = one Laura (no legend or label). There is no second panel or row for the plan's own Treasuries (presumably 0 in 100), so there is nothing to compare against. "99% safe" is not tied to any real bond type, so it reads as a made-up number. There is a lot of empty white space under the grid.
- **Caption plus chart enough without prose?** No. The caption asks about "several kinds of safe bond" and the chart talks about "each payment 99% safe". The step between them (other safe bonds carry a small default risk, Treasuries effectively none, and risks multiply across ten promises) is missing. So is the plan's own result to compare against.
- **One question it leaves you with:** Which "kinds of safe bond" were actually on the table under the competition's permitted list, and what does each one's real default rate over 6-15 years look like next to the 1% assumed here? (Also: are the risks really independent? If they move together, the miss chance falls toward 1%, though each miss would be worse.)

## Item 3 (words): what $300,000 bought, across 19 years

- **Assumed before:** Yes, roughly normal. $500,000 of payments starting six years out, discounted at 4-5%, comes to about $300,000, so I would have called $300,000 an ordinary fit.
- **What it shows:** The cost of the ten payments swings enormously with rates. $300,000 covered them on only one day from mid-2007 to August 2026, and in August 2020 they would have cost $470,217, more than both deposits together. The team bought at about $288,300, the cheapest since 2002. Trading costs ($200) were trivial next to a $37,625 swing within 2026 alone, and a slice of the 2028 deposit was held back to finish the purchase if the price had come in above $300,000.
- **What you had backwards:** I thought $300,000 was a normal price. Over the past two decades it was almost never enough. The plan's easy fit is a feature of this rate moment, and that changes how I read every "comfortable" number that follows.
- **Aha strength:** 4. The single date (Thursday 19 October 2023) and the $470,217 figure reset my sense of how lucky the starting point is. It nearly gets a 5, but the last line half-undercuts it (see question).
- **One question it leaves you with:** "The plan never needed that luck" holds only if the ten cost no more than about $450,000 (both deposits). By the item's own figure, August 2020 would have broken it, so how much bad luck could the backup actually absorb? Also, why does the "exactly one day" window run June 2007 to August 2026 when the data reaches back before May 2002 and forward to October 2026? It reads as a window chosen to make the line work.

## Which would I remember after 40 reports

Item 3. "Exactly one day in nineteen years", with a named Thursday, and "$470,217, more than both deposits" are concrete and unusual. No other team will have them, and they change how I read the whole plan's comfort. Item 1 is the one I would score highest for investment reasoning, because it prices the choice on three measures. But it is dense, and I would remember its conclusion rather than its numbers. Item 2's idea is the cleverest of the three, but as drawn it would not stick.
