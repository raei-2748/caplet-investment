# Reader N: the finance director of a mid-sized nonprofit arts organisation (outside fixed income)

## 01, item A

1. Assumption: that a range given to co-funders is a forecast with odds attached ("we are about 80% sure it lands between X and Y"), the way our board treats a pledge range from a major donor. Sureness: 70.
2. It shows I had it backwards: once both ends are already bought in Treasuries, the range stops being a forecast and becomes a statement of what she holds, so the "how confident" question is answered by ownership, and the model's odds only describe which range she will end up able to state.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (the idea "state the range only after you own both ends" is one I could use with our own pledges)
6. What made it land: the plain line "there is nothing left to estimate", set against the 91-in-100 and 57-in-100 figures that show why she waits to name it; the Neyman detour and the 59%-of-118 study were interesting but slowed me down and felt like a second, separate point.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the range she states, once both ends are owned, is certain" is followed straight away by a clause about fund costs eating "the room under the top", so it reads as certain-with-a-footnote; "at most about $120 for each further 0.1%" is unclear (0.1% of what, a year or in total?); "about 2% on today's figures" does not say 2% of what; "the range she owns" is odd wording for a non-specialist.
8. Question: If a co-sponsor needs a planning figure before the 2031 move, what does Laura say then, and is she allowed to quote anything above the bottom?

## 02, item D

1. Assumption: that the shares were what forced her to promise co-sponsors less than an all-safe plan could, i.e. that the lower figure is the price of taking market risk. Sureness: 60.
2. It shows I had the cause backwards: the lower counted figure comes from her choice to keep a contingency unpromised, and the shares, if anything, lift the top ($115,000 against $110,000 for an all-Treasury plan holding back the same amount).
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the side-by-side of $110,000, $115,000 and $130,000 is clear and useful, but the argument for not stating $130,000 rests on a median of about $4,000 that is small next to the $15,000 more the co-sponsors would see.
7. Overstated or unclear: "that would leave her less in all ... in about 63 futures in 100" is true as stated but sounds bigger than "a median difference of about $4,000" warrants, and it measures her total, not the building's; "the plan keeps its unpromised half and what its Treasuries for the building pay above its top" is hard to follow without a picture; "the bottom she could state would drop from $95,000 to $30,000" is striking but I could not tell what horizon or bad case it assumes.
8. Question: From the co-sponsors' side a firm $130,000 might draw in more of their money than $95,000-$115,000; why is keeping about $20,000 unpromised worth stating $15,000 less at the top?

## 03, item V

1. Assumption: that her 2031 figure would be heard by co-sponsors the way any campaign figure is heard in my world, as a goal partly in hand and partly hoped for, discounted accordingly. Sureness: 65.
2. It shows I had it backwards for her side of the table: unlike a campaign that announces at 60-80% raised, her figure is 100% in hand when she names it, so co-sponsors can treat it as cash, not a target.
3. Surprise: 3 (I know the 60-80% quiet-phase rule well; the 92% against 19% gap was new and the contrast with her figure is sharp)
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (the 92% against 19% figure, and "money that exists, not a target", to my board)
6. What made it land: it speaks my language (capital campaigns, MacDowell) and turns a finance point into the thing a co-sponsor actually cares about, whether the number is real.
7. Overstated or unclear: "theatres ended about 92% above their first budgets" is about theatres, and a residency is a smaller, simpler building, so the figure may overstate the overrun risk; "all of it, the bottom and the top, is already held for her" is true only after the 2031 move, which the sentence says, but a hurried reader may miss it; and the item's own evidence cuts the other way too: if buildings overrun that much, her solid share does not stop the co-sponsors' share from growing.
8. Question: If the building overruns the way those theatres did, how much of the overrun does her unpromised half cover, and who is expected to carry the rest?

## 04, item F

1. Assumption: that buying ten future $50,000 payments with $300,000 of safe bonds is an ordinary sum that would have worked in most years, so the date of purchase is a detail. Sureness: 50.
2. It shows I had it backwards: $300,000 was enough on only about one day in nine since 2000, and in 2020 the same payments would have cost more than both deposits together, so the case sits in a rare high-rate window and the plan's real safeguard is the 2028 deposit, not lucky timing.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes ("in August 2020 the same ten payments would have cost $470,217, more than both deposits")
6. What made it land: concrete dates and dollar figures ($470,217 against $450,000 of deposits; one single day in 2023) made "rare year" real in a way a percentage would not.
7. Overstated or unclear: "the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance ahead" is jargon to me and I had to read it twice; the last sentence, "keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027", stacks an "even if" and an "unless" and is hard to parse; "the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002", two days before the orders filled, risks reading as a boast of good timing when the point is that timing does not matter; the $200 of commissions seems beside the point.
8. Question: If the 2028 deposit has to cover a larger gap than expected, how much less is left for the building, and at what point does a gap start to eat into the bottom of the range?

## 05, item U

1. Assumption: that the team dropped the share-heavy first plan because it failed "a high degree of certainty", i.e. that anything with real share risk falls short of "high". Sureness: 60.
2. It shows I had it backwards: the first plan passed any usual reading of "high" (96.8 in 100, against the 90% courts and the SEC use), and the team gave it up for a reason about Laura's own income, then priced the extra safety honestly: about 1 chance in 31 of a final miss traded away for a smaller median outcome.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (the "passed the test and we dropped it anyway, and here is what that cost" story; it is exactly how I would want a board paper on our endowment written)
6. What made it land: the honesty of showing the price at both ends ($159,000 against about $20,000 at the bad end, about $19,000 to $65,000 less at the middle), though the sheer number of figures nearly buried it.
7. Overstated or unclear: "with half the second deposit, about 1 in 7 against none at today's rates" is unclear (does it mean only half the 2028 deposit arrives?); switching between the 27 September simulations (US shares at 6.7%) and the current ones (world shares at 7.5%) inside one paragraph makes the $19,000 and $65,000 hard to compare; the judges' "beyond a reasonable doubt" survey is a stretch as a yardstick for a payment plan, and the SEC reserves line does the job alone; "each miss would have been final" is the strongest point and could come earlier.
8. Question: Did Laura herself weigh about $65,000 less at the median against a 1-in-31 chance of missing a payment, or was "a step further than high" the team's call on her behalf?

## 06, item C

1. Assumption: that showing co-sponsors the money (a proof-of-funds statement) and pledging it were more or less the same act, as they often blur in the pledge letters I receive. Sureness: 50.
2. It shows I had it blurred: the statement of holdings is a fact and the signed letter is the promise, and a fact alone, like Malaysia Mining's "policy", commits no one, so she needs both, with the fact there to make the promise believable rather than to replace it.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes ("the fact does not bind her to the promise; it makes the promise believable" is a line I would use with our own funders)
6. What made it land: a real court case with a real sum (£12.26m won, then lost on appeal) made the gap between a statement and a promise vivid, and the closing line ties it straight back to Laura.
7. Overstated or unclear: "so no fall in markets can take it lower" is strong and seems to sit uneasily with the earlier item that allowed a small shortfall if fund costs ran above the margin; the tin-trading comfort letter is a long way from a novelist's pledge to an arts building, so the parallel needs the last two sentences to carry it; whether "a signed letter naming the facility" binds her in Taiwan is a legal question the item does not answer (and perhaps should not claim).
8. Question: What does her signed letter say happens to the pledge if the facility is delayed past January 2033 or never built?

## 07, item B

1. Assumption: that the $150,000 deposit from a writer whose income falls in slumps is the plan's weak point for the ten payments, so a smaller deposit would put payments at risk. Sureness: 65.
2. It shows I had it backwards: because the deposit is spent in a fixed order (payments first, building money last), a shortfall lands on the building money, and the ten payments survive a deposit about three-quarters smaller at today's rates, or about 60% smaller even if yields fell to zero.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 5
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes ("the order in which the money is spent decides where a shortfall lands": it is the restricted-funds waterfall I use in our own budgets, applied cleanly)
6. What made it land: it named the weak point I had already worried about, then answered it with a mechanism I know from my own work and a concrete trade ($112,500 deposit: bottom from $95,000 to $65,000, shares from $30,000 to $17,000).
7. Overstated or unclear: "leaves the ten $57,455 short of the $500,000 they owe ... if cash waiting earns nothing ($3,016, all of it in the 2033 payment, at today's market rates)" puts two very different shortfalls in one sentence and I could not tell at first which one to believe; "every payment would still meet that standard" relies on "that standard" from three lines earlier; "the fund always taking the larger part" is stated without saying why.
8. Question: If the deposit arrives short in January 2028, does Laura tell prospective co-sponsors then, three years early, or wait for the 2031 meetings?

## 08, item E

1. Assumption: that "buy the promises first, put only spare money in shares" was this team's own idea, untested outside a model, and that public bodies funding fixed promises usually manage the share risk well enough. Sureness: 45.
2. It shows I had it backwards: there is a real, recent test of exactly this choice, and the states that invested promised money in shares had to cut promises or find hundreds of millions, while the one that held shares only from money above its promises kept every promise through the crash.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 5
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes ("Alabama went from 97.6% funded to 62.3% in two years; Florida capped shares at the money above its promises and cut nothing")
6. What made it land: a side-by-side of real plans making the same kind of fixed promise as Laura, with the outcome known, is far more persuasive to me than any simulation.
7. Overstated or unclear: "no promise was cut" may owe something to things the item leaves out (Florida's size, new contracts coming in, and how far the state controlled its own tuition rises), so the share rule may not be the whole reason; Florida's reserve still fell from 12.9% to 4.9%, so its rule did not remove share losses, only kept them off the promises; "in nearly the same words" is fair in spirit, but Florida's rule was "the lesser of 15% ... or the actuarial reserve", which also caps shares at 15%, and Laura's has no such cap.
8. Question: Florida's payouts were matched to tuition; what plays the part of "tuition rising" for Laura, building costs, and what in the plan is matched to that?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- E: a real precedent with a known ending (Alabama cut, Florida kept every promise) is the kind of evidence a board or a co-sponsor trusts more than any model.
- B: "the order in which the money is spent decides where a shortfall lands" turns the obvious weak point into a strength, in terms any budget-holder understands.
- V: the 92% against 19% gap speaks directly to anyone who has run a capital campaign, and sets her figure apart as money that exists.

## Items that clash or repeat

- A and C clash slightly: A allows a small shortfall from the top if fund costs exceed the margin and cash earns nothing, while C says "no fall in markets can take it lower"; both may be true (costs are not markets), but side by side they read as a hedge in one place and a guarantee in the other.
- A, V and C say the same thing three ways: once both ends are held in Treasuries, her figure is money that exists, not an estimate or a target.
- U and E repeat each other: buy the promised payments first in Treasuries and put only unpromised money into shares, told once through the team's own first plan and once through Florida.
- B and F overlap: both rest on the first part of the 2028 deposit completing the ten payments, F from the side of rates and B from the side of a smaller deposit.
