# Reader X: an investment professional who builds Treasury and bond portfolios for institutions

## 01, item B

1. Assumption: the first $300,000 already does nearly all the work for the ten payments (their present value in early 2027 is roughly $300,000 at today's yields), so the 2028 deposit is mainly building money and the payments are only loosely exposed to it. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: nothing I had backwards, but it sharpens it: the payments are hostage to the 2028 deposit only for a small, zero-reinvestment safety slice (about $39,000), so a writer's slump hits the building range first and the payments only if the deposit shrinks by roughly three-quarters.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land: the funding waterfall is stated as a fixed order with a break-even deposit size (73.9% smaller) and a stress case (yields to zero in 2027, about $60,000, so 60% smaller), which is exactly how I would test a dedication with a contingent second contribution.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "leaves the ten $57,455 short of the $500,000 they owe, added across their ten dates, if cash waiting earns nothing ($3,016, all of it in the 2033 payment, at today's market rates)" is hard to parse: the parenthesis reads like a restatement of the $57,455 when it is a different case (reinvestment at today's forwards rather than at zero); it needs its own clause. "Yields often fall in a slump" is usually true for Treasuries but not always (an inflationary slowdown can push yields up), though here higher yields only help the top-up, so the claim errs on the safe side. "Even if yields fell to zero during 2027" is a bound, not a scenario; I would rather see a realistic 150-200 bp rally priced as well.
8. Question: What does the zero-reinvestment standard cost the building range compared with assuming reinvestment at, say, today's forwards less a haircut, and why is zero the right standard for "a high degree of certainty" rather than an overpayment for it?

## 02, item A

1. Assumption: the 2031 range would be a model output, something like a 5th-95th percentile band from a simulation, stated with a probability attached ("90% confident"). How sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had the confidence question pointed at the wrong object: once the top half of the shares is moved into Treasuries before the meetings, the range is a set of owned, dated cash flows, a defeasance rather than a forecast, so the honest confidence is "certain" and the probabilities only describe which range she will be able to state before the move.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: the Neyman quote and the 59% of researchers misreading a 95% interval give a familiar defeasance idea a sharp edge, and the costed tolerance (margin plus rounding, about 2%, then about $120 per further 0.1%) shows what "certain" is made of instead of just asserting it.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "is certain" applies to owned bonds held to maturity; with term funds the final payout also depends on the fund's last-year cash yield, its end date holding and its costs, which the margin and rounding are meant to cover, so "certain" is defensible only because those are spelled out; I would keep that sentence tied to them. "The top is her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count" sits oddly beside "a range": if the top is what she gives and it is owned, a co-sponsor may ask why a range is stated at all. "Which forecast for world shares is used barely moves today's estimate of the top" is plausible (about three years of expected-return differences on a small sleeve) but is asserted without a figure.
8. Question: If both ends are owned and co-sponsors count the top, what does the bottom tell a co-sponsor that the top alone does not, and how will you stop them reading a range as uncertainty?

## 03, item F

1. Assumption: $500,000 of nominal payments 6-15 years out costing about $300,000 needs yields near 5%, which I knew were uncommon after 2007, so I assumed the case had been set to fit today's curve; I had not put a frequency on it. How sure: 60.
2. What it means: nothing backwards, but it puts a number on how lucky the setting is: a fully Treasury-dedicated answer fits the first deposit on about one day in nine since 2000 and on a single day in the sixteen years before 2026, and in 2020 it would have cost more than both deposits, so the plan's feasibility is a feature of the rate year, not of the design.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: the 4 August 2020 figure ($470,217, more than both deposits) and the single day in October 2023 are concrete and checkable, and the closing clause turns the history into a robustness claim (needs the year, not the day) rather than a boast about timing.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027" is true but nearly empty, since January 2027 is weeks away; the useful threshold is how far yields can fall during 2027 before the 2028 top-up stops covering the gap, and that should be the figure here. "The plan's own measure (... always the same distance ahead)" values the payments on a constant-maturity curve, which is fine for history but mixes par and zero rates unless they bootstrapped; the exact dollar figures to the dollar ($470,217, $325,563) imply more precision than that method has. "$200 against a $37,625 swing" compares a cost with a price change; it makes the point but is rhetoric, not analysis.
8. Question: Given that 2026-27 is a rare year for this liability, what does the plan say Laura should do if yields fall 150 bp between now and the first purchase: buy anyway and take the larger 2028 top-up, or shade the building range at once?

3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe

7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "a broker statement shows as the funds held and their end dates" is true, but a statement shows market value, not what the funds will pay at their end; before late 2032 that value sits below the stated top (it accretes toward it and moves a little with rates), so a co-sponsor reading a 2031 statement sees a smaller number than $115,000 and needs the team's calculation to reconcile them. "No fall in markets can take it lower" holds for the value at the end date, not for the value on the statement in between; say which. The case is apt, but the passage spends most of its length on English contract law before reaching Laura.

## 05, item E

1. Assumption: the standard liability-driven lesson (defined-benefit pensions in 2008: fixed promises funded with equities blow up funded status at the worst time) applies here; I did not know the prepaid-tuition plans had run the same experiment side by side. How sure of the lesson: 85.
2. What it means: nothing backwards; it gives the lesson a near-controlled comparison: the same kind of promise, sold by states in the same years, broke where shares were the funding and held where shares were limited to the surplus.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: the paired outcome (Alabama 97.6% to 62.3% funded and benefits cut, Florida's reserve squeezed from 12.9% to 4.9% with no promise cut) and Florida's rule quoted word for word make the plan's rule look tested rather than invented.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "says the rule in nearly the same words" overstates it: Florida's rule has two limbs, a 15% cap and the reserve, and Laura's has only the second, so it is the same principle, not nearly the same words. The comparison also has a confound worth naming: tuition plans owed a cost that rises with tuition, and Florida's legislature had a hand in setting tuition, so part of Florida's survival may be liability control rather than asset policy; Laura's payments are fixed in dollars, which makes her case cleaner than either state's. Florida's squeeze to 4.9% is also a warning that surplus-only shares can still eat most of the cushion, which the item leaves unsaid.
8. Question: Florida also capped shares at 15% of the whole fund; what share of Laura's total money is in shares at the peak, and would you add a cap like that or rely on the surplus rule alone?

## 08, item U

1. Assumption: a growth-first plan that buys the payments only in 2033 would plainly fail "a high degree of certainty" for fixed nominal promises, so dedicating early was the obvious reading of the case rather than a step beyond it. How sure: 70.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that "high" alone rules the first plan out: at about 97 in 100 it passes the usual 90% readings, so the choice to buy first is a client judgement (her income falls with shares, and a miss in 2033 is final), and the item prices that judgement: 1 in 31 against none, 1 in 7 against none if her second deposit halves, a far better 1-in-20 low for a smaller median.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: the half-deposit case (about 1 in 7 against none) ties the probability to the writer's-income premise, and the tail-against-median trade ($159,000 against about $20,000 at the 1-in-20 low, for about $19,000 less at the median) is the honest way to show what certainty costs.

"almost nothing in the team's current ones (world shares at 7.5% a year)" looks inconsistent: a higher assumed share return should raise, not erase, the first plan's 1-in-20 low unless something else changed (volatility, starting yields, costs), and the item does not say what. "No payment short from January 2028" holds by construction under the zero-reinvestment sizing, which is fine, but "none" against "1 in 31" compares a model rate with a design property.

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- E: a near-controlled experiment from real public plans: same promise, shares as funding broke it, shares only in the surplus kept it.
- F: $470,217 in 2020 against $300,000 shows the case is solvable this way only because of the rate year, which most teams will never notice.
- A: answering the confidence question with "certain, because both ends are owned" instead of a percentage, sharpened by Neyman, is the one answer I have not seen before.
