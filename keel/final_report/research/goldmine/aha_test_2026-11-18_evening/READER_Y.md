# Reader Y: a finance professor who teaches fixed income

## 01, item D

1. Assumption: that holding shares is what forces the stated figure down, since you can only promise what is already locked in Treasuries. Sureness before reading: 65.
2. What it means: the lower count comes from the decision to keep a buffer unpromised, not from the shares; at the same buffer an all-Treasury plan states less ($110,000 against $115,000), and stating everything ($130,000) is bought by giving up the buffer and, in most futures, some total wealth.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the separation of two levers (how much to hold back, and what the building money sits in) is clean and correctly framed, and the point that the all-Treasury figure is itself unfixed until January 2028 because the purchase yield is unknown is a good fixed-income observation that most people miss; it lands less because the $115,000 against $110,000 comparison mixes a priced, locked figure with a projected one.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "that choice, not holding shares, is what lowers the figure co-sponsors count" — the plan's $115,000 top is "on today's figures", which must rest on a forecast of share returns to 2031, while the $110,000 is (nearly) a market price; set the share half to a risk-neutral (Treasury) return and the gap closes, so the five thousand dollars is the equity premium assumed, not a structural advantage. "less in all ... in about 63 futures in 100" is likewise just the assumed equity premium showing up over a few years; it is model-dependent and says nothing about risk-adjusted value. The sentence on the 1-in-20 low is unclear: is that the low of her total, her contribution, or the stated figure?
8. Question: What share return and volatility produce the $115,000 top and the 63 in 100, and what do both become if shares earn only a percentage point or two above Treasuries?

## 02, item F

1. Assumption: that the case's $300,000 was set to be roughly what ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out cost at ordinary yields, so buying them outright is the natural, always-available answer. Sureness before reading: 50 (a quick annuity sum says it needs yields near 5% across the curve, which I knew was uncommon after 2008, but I had not joined the two).
2. What it means: the "just buy the ten" answer is only open in a rare rate regime (about one day in ten since 2000 and essentially none between mid-2007 and 2026), so the plan's starting point is a gift of the year it is set in, and the team has built it so that it needs only the year, not the lucky day.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: the anchors are ones a rates person recognises at once (October 2023, when the ten-year touched 5%, and August 2020, the low), and $470,217 against $287,938 for the same ten payments is a vivid way to teach present value and rate risk in one line.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027" — unclear which yields (a parallel shift of the whole curve?) and it leaves out the second timing risk: the part of the ten that is completed in January 2028 is bought at January 2028 yields, so a fall during 2027 also matters (small if the unbought part is small, but it should be said). "more than both of Laura's deposits together" compares a January 2027 value with a deposit that arrives a year later; minor. The $200 of commissions sits oddly beside a plan that buys in January 2027 rather than on the simulator's October fill dates; it reads as the simulator's cost, not hers.
8. Question: At today's curve, how much of the ten does the January 2027 purchase leave unbought, and what does a two-point fall in yields during 2027 add to the cost of completing it in January 2028?

## 03, item A

1. Assumption: that the case's "how confident" wants a probability from a simulation (something like "a 90% chance the contribution lands in the range"), and that the range is a forecast interval around an uncertain outcome. Sureness before reading: 75.
2. What it means: once both ends sit in Treasuries she owns, the stated range is no longer a forecast but a fixed nominal claim, so the honest confidence is certainty, and the simulated percentages answer a different, earlier question (which range she will be able to state), not whether the contribution lands inside it.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: separating the ex-ante distribution of the stated figure from the ex-post coverage of the stated range is exactly the distinction students blur, and the conservative counting (cash at zero, fees, a margin, rounding down) shows the "certain" is earned rather than asserted; the Neyman opening is a clever hook but does more decorating than arguing, since a confidence interval estimates a fixed parameter and her range, after the move, estimates nothing.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "a 0.5% margin for other fund costs" and "for each further 0.1%" — per year or once? A fixed-income reader cannot tell, and it changes the figure several-fold over a few years. "is certain" — fair for a Treasury's nominal payment, but these are funds that hold Treasuries; the certainty also leans on the funds terminating as scheduled and on the custody chain, which the margin does not obviously cover (I would accept "certain" if those are named somewhere). "Neyman, who invented the confidence interval" — he formalised it in 1937; "invented" is a little strong but defensible. The 59% of 118 researchers I cannot check from here and would want the source in the reference list. The 7.5% for world shares sits at the upper end of the long-run forecasts I see; the item says the forecast barely moves the top, so I would like that shown beside the 91 and 57.
8. Question: If the contribution is the top and the top is certain once owned, what does stating the bottom tell a co-sponsor that the top alone does not?

## 04, item E

1. Assumption: that the plan is textbook liability-driven investing (immunise the promised payments, put only the surplus at risk, in the spirit of contingent immunisation), and that the prepaid tuition failures of 2008-09 were a known cautionary tale. Sureness before reading: 80.
2. What it means: nothing backwards; it gives a real, dated pair of outcomes for the same promise run two ways (shares against the promise, or shares only above it), which turns a principle I teach into evidence.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: the paired natural experiment (Alabama 97.6% to 62.3% funded and payouts cut, Florida's reserve falling from 12.9% to 4.9% with no promise cut) is concrete, dated and exactly the right comparison; it is the item I would put on a slide.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "says the rule in nearly the same words" — Florida's rule has a hard 15% cap and a reserve test; Laura's has only the second idea, so it is the same principle, not nearly the same words. The comparison also flatters the plan in one way and misses a lesson in another: tuition promises are real liabilities that no bond hedges, while Laura's ten payments are fixed in dollars and a Treasury matches them exactly, so her payment problem is much easier than Florida's; the part of her plan that does resemble a tuition promise is the building contribution, exposed to Taiwan building costs that rise when shares may fall, which is where the Alabama lesson really bites. "each needed state money too, from $280 million to about $814 million" — I would want each figure sourced and dated. One success against three failures is an illustration, not a test.
8. Question: How large is the unpromised half against what she promises the building, and what combination of a share fall and a Taiwan building-cost rise would use it all up, as Alabama's did?

## 05, item V

1. Assumption: that a founder's announced contribution to a building is, like most campaign figures, a pledge or a target backed by intent and future income rather than by money already set aside. Sureness before reading: 40 (outside my field).
2. What it means: campaign figures are conventionally announced when mostly raised yet still leave much unsaid, while Laura's figure, named in 2031, is fully held in Treasuries, so it is money that exists rather than a target, which sets it apart from what co-sponsors usually hear.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the contrast between "money that exists" and "a target" is clear and is the right thing to tell a co-sponsor, but the evidence chosen points more strongly somewhere the item does not go.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: the item uses "about 92% above their first budgets" to make a point about what announcements leave out, but its bigger implication is for Laura's plan itself: if arts buildings overrun on that scale, a fixed nominal contribution plus a half held back for overruns may be small against the risk, and the item is silent on that. "first budgets" is unclear: early feasibility estimates routinely grow with scope, so 92% mixes scope change with cost overrun. "a co-sponsor hearing her figure is hearing money that exists" — true of her share only; it says nothing about whether the building will be funded, which is what a co-sponsor most wants to know. The MacDowell quote ("has already been raised") reads oddly in tense; fine if verbatim.
8. Question: If arts buildings ran about 92% over first budgets, how large is Laura's held-back half against the building's likely cost, and is it sized for overruns of that order or only for ordinary cost inflation?

## 06, item U

1. Assumption: that "a high degree of certainty" itself forced the team into Treasuries, i.e. that a 75%-shares plan would plainly fail the client's wording. Sureness before reading: 60.
2. What it means: the share-heavy first plan actually met the ordinary 90% reading of "high", so the move to buying the payments first was the team's choice, made for the client's income and not forced by the words, and it is priced honestly: about 1 in 31 against none, bought with a lower median and paid back many times over at the bad tail.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: it shows both ends of the trade (about $159,000 left above the ten at the 1-in-20 low against about $20,000 or nothing, and $19,000 to $65,000 less at the median), which is the immunisation trade-off I teach, stated with a student team's own numbers and with the outside benchmarks (the SEC's 90% for proved reserves, judges' "reasonable doubt") used correctly.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "with half the second deposit, about 1 in 7 against none at today's rates" — unclear: I read it as "if only half the 2028 deposit arrives", but it should say so, since it is the figure that actually ties the choice to her income. The item mixes two model vintages (US shares at 6.7% on 27 September, world shares at 7.5% now) in the same comparison, so "$19,000 less" and "$65,000 less" are not like for like and the reader cannot tell how much is the plan and how much the model. The 96.8 and 3.2 come from simulated markets that, if they use normal or lognormal returns, understate fat-tail and sequence risk, so the first plan's true miss rate is probably higher; that strengthens the team's case but should be said. "none" is right by construction only given both deposits on time and no Treasury default.
8. Question: What chance do you put on a smaller or missed 2028 deposit if shares fall during 2027, given the writer's-income premise, and what is the first plan's miss rate once that link is in the simulation?

## 07, item C

1. Assumption: that showing co-sponsors the money (a statement of assets held) is itself the commitment, and that a comfort-letter style sentence ("it is our policy to ...") is weak; I knew comfort letters were soft, not this case. Sureness before reading: 55.
2. What it means: a credible commitment needs two distinct things, a checkable fact (the Treasury funds held, with end dates) and an explicit promise (a signed letter naming the facility), and Malaysia Mining's letter failed because it offered a fact that was only an intention and no promise at all.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: the case is a real, citable court ruling with a quotable line ("a statement of present fact and not a promise as to future conduct"), and the item turns it neatly into a design rule for her 2031 words; the last sentence, "The fact does not bind her to the promise; it makes the promise believable", is the clearest thing I have read in the pack.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "so no fall in markets can take it lower" — true of what the funds pay at their end if she holds them, not of their value before then, and only so long as the funds end as scheduled; worth one clause. The item admits the fact does not bind her, but a co-sponsor will notice the gap: the funds are hers to sell at any time, and the signed letter's enforceability depends on the law that governs it (an English case about a commercial lender says little about a charitable pledge to a Taiwan facility). Calling a broker statement "the fact" is fine for 2031 but says nothing about 2031-2033.
8. Question: Why a signed letter alone rather than also placing the Treasury funds in an escrow or pledged account until the late-2032 end date, so that the fact binds as well as persuades?

## 08, item B

1. Assumption: that the 2028 deposit is the plan's weak point, since the payments wait on money from a writer whose income falls in the same slumps. Sureness before reading: 60.
2. What it means: the fixed spending order makes the building money the shock absorber, so the payments depend only on the first quarter or so of the deposit (about $39,000; about $60,000 even if yields went to zero during 2027), and a shortfall lands on the range, which she learns three years before the meetings, not on the ten.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 5
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land or not: it takes the obvious objection head on, answers it with a waterfall and a hard bound (yields at zero) rather than a probability, and the extra Treasuries sized for cash earning nothing are the right, conservative answer to coupon-reinvestment risk in a ladder that cannot use zero-coupon bonds.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong: "leaves the ten $57,455 short of the $500,000 they owe, added across their ten dates, if cash waiting earns nothing ($3,016, all of it in the 2033 payment, at today's market rates)" — two figures for two assumptions in one sentence; I had to read it three times to see that $3,016 is the shortfall if waiting cash earns today's rates. Say so in words, and say that the gap between them is the value of reinvesting coupons. "at today's market rates the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller" — the relevant rates are January 2027's and January 2028's; the zero-yield bound covers this, so lead with the bound. The item says what the order does but not what she does if the deposit never comes, beyond "change course".
8. Question: If the 2028 deposit never comes, what exactly is the change of course: which payment falls short, and does anything get sold or re-sized in 2028 to spread or close the gap?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- E: a paired real-world test of the same promise run two ways, one cut and one kept, which no Monte Carlo can match for persuasion.
- C: a real court ruling turned into a design rule, ending in "the fact does not bind her to the promise; it makes the promise believable".
- A: the only item that answers the case's "how confident" by showing the probability belongs to an earlier question, and that the stated range is certain once owned.

## Items that clash or repeat

- A and C repeat each other: the same counting rule (what the funds pay with cash at zero, after fees and a 0.5% margin) and the same line that the top is her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count; A and V also both make the point that her stated figure is money held, not an estimate or a target.
- F and B may look like a clash to a quick reader: F says all ten stay paid unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027, while B says even yields at zero during 2027 leave every payment paid if the deposit is no more than about 60% smaller; both can be true (a fall before the first purchase shrinks what $300,000 buys, a fall during 2027 only makes the remainder dearer), but the reports should say why the two stresses differ.
- D and U both price shares against Treasuries with a median and a 1-in-20 comparison, on different model vintages and different "75% in shares" plans, and D's "less in all in 63 futures in 100" sits uneasily beside U's "$65,000 less at the median for the plan as built"; a reader will not easily reconcile the sizes.
