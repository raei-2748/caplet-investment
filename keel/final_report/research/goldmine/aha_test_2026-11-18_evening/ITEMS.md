**Item A.** Laura wants to give co-sponsors a credible range rather than one exact amount, the case says, and it asks
how confident the team is that her 2033 contribution will fall inside it. She will state the range she owns and give its
top: the top is her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count, and the bottom is the part owned since January 2028.
Jerzy Neyman, who invented the confidence interval, wrote in 1937 that a range once worked out holds its target with
probability zero or one, and nobody knows which, yet in a 2014 study 59% of 118 researchers still read one 95% interval
as holding its target with 95% probability. Laura's 2031 range is not an estimate at all: once half the shares have
moved into Treasuries before the meetings, both ends are Treasuries she owns, and there is nothing left to estimate. So
the range she states, once both ends are owned, is certain: each end is counted at what its funds pay if cash waiting
earns nothing, after their fees and a 0.5% margin for other fund costs, and stated in whole $5,000 steps, rounded down.
Only if such costs used up the room under the top, that margin plus the rounding (about 2% on today's figures), and cash
waiting really earned nothing, would the contribution fall short of the top, and then by at most about $120 for each
further 0.1%. The percentages in the team's model belong to an earlier question, asked before the move: which range she
will be able to state. Which forecast for world shares is used barely moves today's estimate of the top; what markets do
until the move changes it a great deal: with world shares assumed to earn 7.5% a year, the stated top is at least
$100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures and at least $115,000 in about 57, which is why she names it only after
the move.

**Item B.** By the plan's own premise, a writer's income can shrink in a slump, yet the plan waits on a second deposit
from that writer: $150,000 in January 2028. So the deposit looks like the plan's weak point. The order in which the
money is spent decides where a shortfall would land. The money Laura already has, the first $300,000, buys the ten
Treasuries in January 2027. The 2028 deposit is then spent in a fixed order: its first part, about $39,000 at today's
market rates, completes the ten and buys extra Treasuries sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing;
only what is left is split between the Treasury fund for the bottom of her range and the shares, the fund always taking
the larger part. So at today's market rates the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller (73.9% on the market
close of 6 November 2026) and every payment would still meet that standard; only a deposit more than about
three-quarters smaller would leave them short of it. Yields often fall in a slump, and lower yields make that first part
dearer, yet even if yields fell to zero during 2027, after the ten are bought, it would cost about $60,000 in January
2028, so a deposit about 60% smaller would still keep every payment at that standard. What shrinks is the building
money, both parts of it: a deposit of $112,500 instead of $150,000 lowers the bottom she could state from $95,000 to
$65,000, and the shares bought from about $30,000 to about $17,000. A deposit that never comes, one of the plan's two
named reasons to change course, leaves the ten $57,455 short of the $500,000 they owe, added across their ten dates, if
cash waiting earns nothing ($3,016, all of it in the 2033 payment, at today's market rates), and leaves nothing for the
building, which she would know three years before the meetings. The plan adds no rule for a smaller deposit; the same
order simply runs.

**Item C.** A lender reads a large company's written word of support as a promise. Kleinwort Benson lent to Malaysia
Mining's tin-trading company against a letter that said "It is our policy to ensure that the business of M.M.C. Metals
Ltd. is at all times in a position to meet its liabilities to you". The tin market collapsed in October 1985, and
Malaysia Mining then wrote that the policy had been "our policy at that time" and that "no assurance was given that such
policy would not be reviewed in the light of changing circumstances". A High Court judge awarded the bank £12.26m with
interest; in 1989 the Court of Appeal set the award aside, because the sentence was "a statement of present fact and not
a promise as to future conduct". Malaysia Mining gave only that fact, and the fact it gave was only an intention.
Laura's 2031 words have two parts. The fact is money held for her in Treasury funds ending in late 2032, which a broker
statement shows as the funds held and their end dates: all of the top, $115,000 on today's figures, the bottom held
since January 2028 and the rest from the time half the shares moved into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. Each end
is counted at what its funds will pay at their end if cash waiting earns nothing, after fees and a 0.5% margin for other
fund costs, so no fall in markets can take it lower. The promise, in a signed letter naming the facility, is that she
will give the top, her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count. The fact does not bind her to the promise; it
makes the promise believable.

**Item E.** Several US states sold families a fixed promise, a child's future college tuition for a price paid now, and
invested the money partly in shares. Alabama's plan aimed at 72% in shares; it was 97.6% funded in September 2007 and
62.3% two years later, as shares fell and tuition rose, and a 2011 settlement cut what it pays to Fall 2010 tuition,
leaving families to pay any difference. Alabama, Illinois and Texas each needed state money too, from $280 million to
about $814 million. Florida's board ran its plan on bonds matched to what the contracts would pay out, and from June
2006 limited shares to "the lesser of 15% of the total fund, or the actuarial reserve", the money above what the
promises needed. In the crash its reserve fell from 12.9% of what it owed to an estimated 4.9%, and no promise was cut.
Laura's plan buys its payments first, in Treasuries, and says the rule in nearly the same words: "only money you haven't
promised yet goes into shares".

**Item F.** On the plan's own measure (the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance

**Item U.** The case asks for "a high degree of certainty". The team's first plan, drawn up on 27 September 2026, held
75% of the money in shares to 2030, gliding to 40% in 2032, and on 1 January 2033 it would have bought all ten payments
in Treasuries with whatever the money had grown or shrunk to. In the team's 27 September simulations, run before any
trade with US shares growing 6.7% a year, it would have paid all ten in full in 96.8 simulated markets out of 100, which
passes the usual reading of "high": judges asked in surveys put "beyond a reasonable doubt" at about 90%, and the US
Securities and Exchange Commission, for oil and gas reserves, defines reasonable certainty as "a high degree of
confidence" and, where probabilities are used, asks for at least 90%. So "high" did not rule the first plan out. The
team gave it up once it thought about how a writer is paid (the premise in your summary), and went a step further than
"high": buy the payments first, in Treasuries, sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing. Those
simulations priced the change. The first plan fell short in 3.2% of simulated markets, and in 5.5% if shares grew 5.0% a
year; each miss would have been final, settled on 1 January 2033 before any payment, with no money coming after January
2028 to close it, and the median miss was $27,695 in 1 January 2033 money. Bought into Treasuries first, the same two
deposits leave no payment short from January 2028, once the first quarter or so of the second deposit is in. So the step
meant about 1 in 31 against none, and with half the second deposit, about 1 in 7 against none at today's rates. Its
price shows at both ends. At the 1-in-20 low, buying first leaves about $159,000 above the ten in 2033, where the first
plan leaves about $20,000 in the 27 September simulations and almost nothing in the team's current ones (world shares at

for the plan as built, which keeps more out of shares, in the current ones.
