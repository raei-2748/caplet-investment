# Reader B, cold aha test 4

Reader: finance professor, cold, pack B only, items read in the order given (2, 8, 6, 4, 7, 1, 5, 3).

ITEM 2

1. Before: I took "a high degree of certainty" the way most people would, as a probability bar of roughly 95% or better, so a plan that pays in full 96.8 times in 100 would pass. What it shows I had backwards: "high" is a relative word, and the bar is what the same money can do for sure. If two deposits can buy all ten payments outright, a 3.2% chance of a final, unrecoverable miss is a choice to take risk, not bad luck.
2. Aha strength: 4.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "'High' is measured against what the same two deposits can buy." This is stated as a fact. It is the team's reading of the case, and a reasonable one, but it needs an argument (for example, a fiduciary can't justify a 1-in-31 chance of a permanent shortfall when a riskless way to meet the duty costs the same money).
   - "they leave no payment short, by design." True in nominal dollars if the instruments behave as designed. But Treasury funds are not single bonds. Their final payouts are not fixed to the dollar, and fees, tracking and an early fund closure all leave some residual risk. "By design" is fine; it shouldn't be read as "under all conditions".
   - The cost side is missing. What did the riskless plan give up in expected money for the building and her flexibility? Without that, 3.2% against none sounds like a free win, which it is not.
   - The 3.2% depends on the model: return distribution, rates and the glide path. One sentence on what the simulation assumed would help.
4. Question: How much expected building contribution or flexibility did Laura give up by moving from the first plan to the Treasury-first plan, and would she take that trade if she saw it?
5. Assumption: "high degree of certainty" means about a 95% probability. How sure I was before: 70.

ITEM 8

1. Before: I already knew a deferred payment is only as good as whoever has to make it, and that Treasuries remove that risk. So: nothing new. The Kenya result is interesting in itself, but the item doesn't change how I see the plan.
2. Aha strength: 2.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "A payment made later is worth only its payer's credibility." Too strong. A later payment is still worth less because of time; credibility adds a credit discount on top, it doesn't replace the time value.
   - The study is used for a point it mostly isn't about. As I understand this literature, farmers chose monthly pay chiefly as a savings commitment (help in keeping money unspent), and buyer credibility explains why few buyers offer it. The item says "the scarce part is the payer", which turns a secondary finding into the main one.
   - The parallel is loose. The farmers' question is how often they're paid; Laura's is whether money set aside years ahead will be there. The item jumps from one to the other.
   - "the plan has one payer make them all, the US Treasury". Part of the ladder is in Treasury bond funds (and Item 3 mentions a bill fund fee), so there is a fund manager, a custodian and a broker between Treasury and Laura. "One payer" oversimplifies.
4. Question: For the payments held through funds rather than single bonds, who exactly pays Laura on each date, and what happens to that payment if a fund closes early or its custodian or broker fails?
5. Assumption: a deferred payment's risk is the payer's credit, and Treasuries remove it. How sure I was before: 90.

ITEM 6

1. Before: I assumed a founder's museum closes when the endowment the founder set aside is spent. What it shows I had backwards: these institutions had no endowment at all. They ran on the founder's income each year and stopped when that income stopped. The risk was the income stream, not a pot running dry.
2. Aha strength: 3. It is local to Taiwan, specific to the client, and the IT Park story (digital photography ending the trade that paid for an art space) is memorable.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "Thirteen Taiwanese art institutions were found". Found by whom, and how? The passive hides the method, and 13 is a small sample that could be skewed by which ones are easy to find.
   - "none had even one year of running costs set aside when it opened". How was this established for all thirteen? Private institutions rarely publish this, so I want the source.
   - Four of the thirteen stopped, which means nine without reserves are presumably still running. That partly undercuts the lesson: going without a reserve is common and often survivable. The item should say why four stopping still matters (the four failed on exactly this risk).
   - "the founder paid first in every one" is unclear. First before whom? Before co-sponsors, government or the public?
   - Ten years of funded payments still ends. After 2042 Laura is back to next year's income, like the founders here.
4. Question: What happens to the residency in 2043, after the tenth payment, and does the plan say anything about that cliff?
5. Assumption: founder-funded art institutions close when the founder's set-aside fortune is spent. How sure I was before: 40.

ITEM 4

1. Before: I read a 2031 range as a forecast whose ends a bad market could still move, and a comfort-letter-style statement of support as something lenders treat as soft. What it shows I had backwards: Laura's figure is not a forecast or a statement of intent but a description of assets already held. A crash can change a policy, but not a Treasury already owned.
2. Aha strength: 3. The Kleinwort Benson case is a strong, real parallel for a credibility question, and the "statement of present fact" line maps neatly onto owned assets. It loses a point for unclear mechanics.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "The figure co-sponsors count is the top". This seems to clash with the case's warning against over-promising. If the top is locked in Treasuries before the meeting, why state a range at all? And what does the "confidence" part of the statement say, if both ends are fixed? A reader needs one sentence on why there are two numbers.
   - "no fall in markets can change either end". This only holds after the move. Before half the shares move, the top depends on share prices, so the timing of the move needs to be clear. Treasury funds can also trade away from their value before they end, though held to the end this matters less.
   - Nothing can change the dollar figure, but a stronger Taiwan dollar or rising building costs can change what it buys. "Something a crash cannot change" is true in US dollars only.
   - "built on the ever-reliable Treasuries" is promotional (even as a quote from the plan) and weakens an otherwise lawyerly item.
   - Irony the team should face: Laura's 2031 statement is also not a legal promise. Co-sponsors may still want a signed pledge.
4. Question: If both ends of the range are already in Treasuries before the 2031 meeting, why give co-sponsors a range instead of one committed figure, and will Laura sign a pledge for the top?
5. Assumption: a 2031 range is a forecast that markets can still move. How sure I was before: 60.

ITEM 7

1. Before: I already knew the human-capital idea (an owner's career income can be stock-like, so their own portfolio should avoid adding the same risk). It is standard life-cycle finance, so: nothing new for me. The regulator's house-builder example is a nice concrete source, but the conclusion is familiar.
2. Aha strength: 2.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "her career 'is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell'". This is asserted, not shown, and the source of the quote is unclear. How strongly a graphic novelist's advances, fees and licences move with the stock market is an empirical question. I'd want evidence (publishing or licensing revenue in 2008-09 or 2020, for instance).
   - The argument doesn't quite bite. The case says the ten payments come from the portfolio with no outside money, so her career is not paying into this "scheme" after January 2028. The double-up matters for her personal flexibility, not for the payments. The item should say where it bites.
   - "only $29,829 of her $450,000 goes into them". That is about 6.6% in shares. The regulator's fix is to diversify away from the correlated market, not to hold almost no shares. The item makes the low share weight sound like a virtue without counting its cost in expected growth.
4. Question: What evidence shows Laura's income falls when shares fall, and if her career doesn't fund the payments, what exactly does this argument change in the plan?
5. Assumption: a writer's income is mostly idiosyncratic, not stock-like. How sure I was before: 50.

ITEM 1

1. Before: I assumed a share-heavy plan with a fixed target fails mainly in a crash, so a test of one 20% down year would cover it. What it shows I had backwards: over a third of the misses had no such year, only a run of poor ones. The risk is having too little on one conversion date, not one bad year.
2. Aha strength: 3. It is a real insight about point-in-time conversion risk (familiar to me from annuity-purchase and target-date research, but well shown here).
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "A crash was never the test." Overstated. By the item's own figure, 64.4% of misses did include a 20% fall. The fair claim is that a crash test alone would miss a third of the failures.
   - "97.5% of misses had shares lower after the four years at three-quarters". Lower than what: the starting level, or the median path? It reads as nearly true by definition.
   - "those four years carry about three-quarters of the shortfall". The attribution method isn't explained.
   - The cost of the ten Treasuries on 1 January 2033 also depends on interest rates that day. Did the simulation move rates and shares together? Some misses may come from low 2033 rates (expensive bonds), not shares.
   - Most of this item's text repeats Item 2 (same plan, same glide path, same 3.2% and $27,695).
4. Question: Of the 3.2% of misses, how much came from bond prices on 1 January 2033 (low rates making the ten Treasuries expensive) rather than from shares?
5. Assumption: a share-heavy plan fails mainly in a single crash year. How sure I was before: 55.

ITEM 5

1. Before: I pictured a residency as its place, the house and studios. What it shows I had backwards: the place is the fragile part, and what keeps the programme going is operating money kept apart from the building.
2. Aha strength: 2. The cases are vivid (Rauschenberg's storm-damaged Captiva site), but both foundations had large endowments, so they show something Laura's plan doesn't need: a fortune that outlives its site. For Laura, the link is thin. Her building contribution and operating payments are already separate in the case.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "The money outlasted the building." True of these two rich foundations, but it is the endowment's size that let them keep going, not separation as such. Laura has ten finite payments, not a perpetual fund.
   - The Aspen advice is to sustain a foundation "initially". Laura funds ten years, which is more than initially, so the parallel overreaches a little.
   - "none of them waits on selling anything". Held to the end, the bonds and funds pay out without Laura selling, but a fund ending does involve the fund selling or receiving its bonds' redemptions. The claim is fair but should say "nothing Laura must sell".
   - It raises a risk the item doesn't address: a Taiwan building faces typhoon, earthquake and upkeep risk, like Captiva. Is her flexibility half meant for that?
4. Question: If the Taiwan building is damaged or becomes too costly to keep, as Captiva was, can the ten operating payments move to artists' programmes elsewhere, and does the plan say so?
5. Assumption: a residency is mainly its building. How sure I was before: 40.

ITEM 3

1. Before: As a finance professor I already knew that yield to maturity on a coupon bond assumes coupons are reinvested at that yield, so: nothing new in concept. What the item adds is scale. The 1982 bond's $15,690 promised against $6,144 earned is a striking illustration, and it explains why the plan holds $35,894 of extra Treasuries.
2. Aha strength: 3 (2 for the concept, raised for the vivid numbers and for tying them to one figure in the plan). I checked the arithmetic: $1,000 at 14.25% compounded half-yearly for 20 years gives about $15,690, and $6,144 over 40 half-years is about 9.29% a year bond-equivalent. Both are consistent.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "the strictest rate practice used". Used by whom? Escrow agents, defeasance practice? The source isn't named. Also, by charging a 0.15% fee on a fund earning zero, the plan actually assumes slightly below zero.
   - "A Federal Reserve bank's 1981 table". Which bank? This is a vague source in an item that relies on it.
   - "the long bonds that pay nothing until the end". Stripped Treasuries come in all maturities, not only long ones. The point that the rules bar zero-coupon Treasuries is fine.
   - Missing cost: zero reinvestment for years is a very conservative stress. Bills were near zero in only some periods. What do the $35,894 of extra Treasuries cost her building contribution in a normal-rate world?
4. Question: How much of the $35,894 could cash-flow matching remove (using coupons from later bonds to fund earlier payments), and what does assuming zero cost Laura in expected building money compared with assuming, say, bills at half today's rate?
5. Assumption: reinvestment risk would be a small effect over a five-to-six-year wait. How sure I was before: 50.
