## Item 4 (Kleinwort Benson and the comfort letter)

1. Assumed: a big company's written word of support to a lender is a promise the lender can rely on. Had backwards: a sentence about "our policy" only describes the present, so a court will not hold the writer to it once things change. The team's twist is that Laura's figure describes money already held, not an intention.
2. Aha: 3
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "Laura's 2031 figure describes something a crash cannot change." The case's lesson is about whether a promise can be enforced, not about market risk. Malaysia Mining chose to drop its policy; the crash gave it the reason. Laura could also choose to spend her Treasuries some other way, so owning them is not the same as having committed them. The analogy blurs "cannot fall" with "cannot be withdrawn".
   - "no fall in markets can change either end". A crash cannot change the figure in US dollars, but the building is paid in Taiwan dollars. A Treasury fund that ends on a set date also has fees and a small reinvestment effect, so its payout is not to the dollar.
   - "The figure co-sponsors count is the top" reads oddly beside the context's warning against over-promising. If both ends are held for her before the meeting, why state a range at all instead of one figure? I could not work this out from the item.
   - The plan quote ("ever-reliable Treasuries") sounds like an advert.
4. Question: if both ends of the range are owned before the 2031 meetings, what does the bottom tell a co-sponsor that the top does not, and what stops Laura from moving the money later?
5. Assumption: a written word of support binds the writer. Confidence before reading: 60.

## Item 6 (Taiwan's founder-funded art institutions)

1. Assumed: a founder's museum closes when the founder's endowment runs out. Had backwards: there was no endowment to run out. These places ran on the next year's income (a company's profit, art sales, the founder's earnings), so they stopped when that income stopped.
2. Aha: 4. It is about the client's own country and kind of project, it is research nobody else will have, and it sets up the contrast with Laura's money, which is owned five years early.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "Thirteen Taiwanese art institutions were found": found by whom, and how? The sample could be biased (only the ones with records online).
   - "none had even one year of running costs set aside when it opened". That is a strong negative to prove from public sources. What is the evidence for each one?
   - "Four stopped". What happened to the other nine? If most of them survived for decades on next year's income, the lesson is weaker than it reads.
   - "the founder paid first in every one" is unclear. Paid first before whom: the government, other donors?
4. Question: how were the thirteen chosen, and did any of the nine still open last as long without a reserve as the ones that closed?
5. Assumption: founder museums fail when a set-aside fortune is spent. Confidence before reading: 55.

## Item 1 (the first plan: one date, not a crash)

1. Assumed: a share-heavy plan fails because of a crash. Had backwards: it fails on any run of poor years that leaves too little on the one date when it must buy, 1 Jan 2033, and nothing comes in after 2028 to close the gap.
2. Aha: 3. This is terminal-date or sequence risk, which a finance student half knows. The 35.6% figure makes it concrete.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "A crash was never the test." By the item's own figures, 64.4% of misses did include a year down 20% or more, so a crash is part of the story in most misses. "Not the only test" would be accurate.
   - "97.5% of misses had shares lower after the four years at three-quarters". Lower than what? The starting value, or the model's median?
   - "those four years carry about three-quarters of the shortfall". I cannot tell how a shortfall is split between years. This needs one plain sentence on how it was measured.
   - "In simulated markets": which return assumptions? The 3.2% will move with the expected return and volatility used.
   - It is long for one point, and it repeats almost all of Item 2's facts.
4. Question: what return model gave 3.2%, and how much does that miss rate change with a lower expected return or fatter tails?
5. Assumption: a share plan's risk is a single crash year. Confidence before reading: 70.

## Item 3 (holding a bond does not lock in its rate)

1. Assumed: a Treasury ladder bought to match the payments is fully paid for at its purchase cost. Had backwards: the interest paid along the way has to sit somewhere until the payment date, and the rate it earns there is not locked. That explains why the plan holds an extra $35,894.
2. Aha: 3. Reinvestment risk is textbook, but the 1982 example ($15,690 promised against $6,144 earned) is vivid, and the link to the stripped-Treasury ban is sharp.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "the strictest rate practice used". Strictest by whose practice? Zero is not a hard floor (rates have gone negative elsewhere), so this needs a source or should be dropped.
   - "counts the rate on waiting money at zero ... and then also charges a bill fund's 0.15% yearly fee". This seems to contradict itself. If the money sits in a bill fund, it earns the bill rate minus the fee; if it earns nothing, which fund is charging? As written, the sizing is either "below zero" or confused. Then the last sentence says "even if cash waiting earns nothing", which loses the fee.
   - "A Federal Reserve bank's 1981 table": name the bank.
   - The $15,690 checks out (14.25% compounded half-yearly for 20 years). The 9.29% only works as a half-yearly bond-equivalent rate (it is about 9.5% compounded yearly), so label it.
4. Question: is the $35,894 sized at zero, or at zero minus 0.15% a year? And what would it take to be short anyway?
5. Assumption: a matched ladder is fully funded at its cost. Confidence before reading: 65.

## Item 8 (Kenyan dairy farmers and a payer's credibility)

1. Assumed: money paid later is worth less only because of time and risk, which I knew. Nothing much new: payer credibility is just counterparty risk. The farmer study is a detour on the way to "use the US Treasury".
2. Aha: 2
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "86% of dairy farmers chose to be paid monthly rather than daily even when the daily price was 15% higher". As I remember it, the study's main explanation was that farmers wanted monthly pay as a way to make themselves save, and the payer's credibility was the supply side. The item presents the credibility point as if it explains the farmers' choice. Check the quote's context and both figures.
   - "A payment made later is worth only its payer's credibility." Overstated: later money is also worth less because of time and inflation.
   - "one payer make them all, the US Treasury". The context says some holdings are Treasury bond funds, so there is a fund company and a custodian between Laura and the Treasury. That is close to true, not exactly true.
   - "the time value of money ... says ... no one should take less in order to be paid less often". This mixes up how often you are paid with when.
4. Question: why did the farmers in the study say they preferred monthly pay, and does that reason apply to Laura at all?
5. Assumption: time value is the only reason later money is worth less. Confidence before reading: 80 (and I mostly still hold it).

## Item 5 (residencies outlive their buildings)

1. Assumed: an artists' residency is its place, the house and studios. Had backwards: in two famous cases (Tiffany, Rauschenberg on Captiva) the site was sold and the foundation's money kept paying artists, so the money is the residency.
2. Aha: 3. The Rauschenberg sale (decided 2025, sold March 2026) is fresh and striking. The Aspen Institute advice ties neatly to Laura owning the operating money first.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "The money outlasted the building." In both cases part of the money came from selling the building, so the building paid for what came after. That is a weaker lesson than it reads.
   - This item sits awkwardly beside Laura's goal: she is putting money into a building. If buildings are the part that fails, the item invites a judge to ask why she funds one.
   - "follow that advice in Treasuries". The Aspen notes are written for an artist's estate funding a foundation without selling art. Laura's case is close but not the same, so "follow" is a stretch; "in the spirit of" is fairer.
   - "$326 million" needs a source.
4. Question: if a building is the part most likely to be sold, what protects Laura's building contribution, and is it kept apart from the operating payments in law or only in the plan?
5. Assumption: a residency is its building. Confidence before reading: 40.

## Item 7 (her career is already a holding of shares)

1. Assumed: a portfolio's risk is judged by what it holds. As a finance student I knew about human capital, so this was not new in principle. What was new was the pension regulator's own house-builder example, and how small the share holding is ($29,829 of $450,000, about 7%). That figure explains why the plan holds so few shares.
2. Aha: 3
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - Her career "is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell". Whose quote is this? For a graphic novelist the link between income and share prices is plausible but unproven: book sales are not obviously cyclical. "can come later and smaller" is fair; "a large holding of shares" is a strong metaphor.
   - The analogy has a gap. The regulator worries that the employer cannot top up the scheme when needed. In the case, Laura adds nothing after January 2028 anyway, so which of her money does her career put at risk: the 2028 deposit, or only her flexibility? The item does not say.
   - "only $29,829 ... on today's figures". Make clear this is the January 2028 purchase, and say what share of the second deposit it is.
4. Question: after the 2028 deposit, which part of the plan still depends on Laura's career, and would a larger share holding really double her risk?
5. Assumption: about 30-50% in shares would suit a six-year horizon. Confidence before reading: 60.

## Item 2 ("a high degree of certainty" against what the deposits can buy)

1. Assumed: "a high degree of certainty" means very likely, say 95% or more, so 96.8 in 100 passes. Had backwards: "high" is measured against what the same money can buy. When the same two deposits can make every payment certain, 3.2% means about 1 in 31 against none, and every miss is final.
2. Aha: 4. It is the clearest reframe in the pack, and it answers the case's own phrase.
3. Overstated or unclear:
   - "they leave no payment short, by design". This rests on the zero-rate sizing (see Item 3's fee confusion) and on funds ending as scheduled. "By design" is fine if the design's assumptions are stated nearby.
   - It does not say what certainty cost: what the 96.8% plan would have given the building on average that this plan does not. A judge will want to know.
   - It repeats Item 1 nearly word for word (27 September, three-quarters to 2030, gliding to 40%, 1 January 2033, 3.2%, about 1 in 31, $27,695).
4. Question: how much expected building money, or how much of her flexibility, did moving from 1 in 31 to none cost Laura?
5. Assumption: "high degree of certainty" means a probability of about 95% or more. Confidence before reading: 75.
