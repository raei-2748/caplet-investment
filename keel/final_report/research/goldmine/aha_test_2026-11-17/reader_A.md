# Reader A, cold aha test 4

Reader: investment professional reading cold. I read the items in the pack's order: 1, 3, 5, 7, 2, 4, 6, 8.

## Item 1

1. Before reading, I assumed a share-heavy plan fails through a crash, so the right stress test is a year in which shares fall 20% or more. What I had backwards: the failure is a shortfall on one fixed purchase date, and a run of merely poor years can cause it as well as a crash can.
2. Aha: 3
3. Claims:
   - "A crash was never the test." The item's own figures say this goes too far. If 35.6% of misses had no 20% year, then 64.4% did, so crashes were still behind most misses. The real point is that a crash test alone would miss about a third of the failures. Say that.
   - "97.5% of misses had shares lower after the four years at three-quarters": lower than what? Than at the start, or than the median path? It is unclear.
   - "those four years carry about three-quarters of the shortfall": how is a shortfall split across years? The sentence needs one clause on method, or it should go.
   - 3.2% comes from a simulation whose return and volatility assumptions are not given, so I cannot tell whether it is generous or harsh. For a plan that was dropped, the figure's sensitivity matters more than its exact value.
4. Question: What return, volatility and tail assumptions produced 3.2%, and what does the miss rate become at a lower share return (say 5% a year) or on historical sequences?
5. Assumption: share-heavy plans fail through crashes. Confidence before reading: 60.

## Item 3

1. Before reading, I assumed that holding a bond to maturity locks in its yield only if every coupon is reinvested at that same yield (the textbook realised compound yield). On the concept: nothing new. What was new: how large the gap was in a real case, and that the plan pays $35,894 to remove it.
2. Aha: 2
3. Claims:
   - "the strictest rate practice used": used by whom? Zero is the floor of any assumption, so "the strictest possible" says it plainly. "Practice used" needs a source, or it should go.
   - "then also charges a bill fund's 0.15% yearly fee": if waiting cash earns nothing, why is there a bill fund at all? Is waiting money assumed to earn -0.15%? One clause would settle it.
   - The Federal Reserve's 1981 example (a 16% bond earning about 14.2%) makes the same point a second time. One of the two examples is enough, and the 1982 bond is the stronger one.
   - "the competition's rules bar stripped Treasuries" is stated as fact with no source. The whole reason this risk exists here rests on it.
   - I checked the figures: $1,000 at 14.25%, compounded semiannually for 20 years, gives $15,690, and $6,144 works out to 9.29% a year on a bond-equivalent basis. They are consistent.
4. Question: What does the zero-rate assumption cost Laura compared with a modest floor (say 1% on waiting money)? And did you consider choosing bonds whose coupons fall due close to the payment dates, to shrink the waiting money instead of buying $35,894 of extra Treasuries?
5. Assumption: a held-to-maturity yield is locked only if coupons are reinvested at that yield (I already had this right). Confidence before reading: 90.

## Item 5

1. Before reading, I assumed a residency is its building, and that the building is the asset that lasts. What I had backwards: in two well-known cases the building was the fragile part and the operating money outlived it. That is a good reason to keep the operating payments apart from the building and fund them first.
2. Aha: 3
3. Claims:
   - "The money outlasted the building." This is true of two large endowed foundations (Rauschenberg's received about $326 million), but Laura's whole plan is $450,000. The scale differs so much that a judge may ask whether the lesson carries over.
   - "Laura's ten operating payments follow that advice in Treasuries." The Aspen note says "sufficient liquid assets", not Treasuries. Choosing Treasuries is the team's step, not Aspen's, and the wording blurs the two.
   - Captiva was sold because of storm damage, climate risk and upkeep costs, which are building risks. That also argues for caution about the building contribution, and the item does not draw this out.
   - Tiffany: "kept paying artists after the sale". Was there a gap? The item gives the sale dates (1946, 1949) but not when the grants began.
4. Question: If the Taiwan building is delayed, over budget or lost, what happens to the ten operating payments? Can they be moved to another site or programme, as Rauschenberg's money was?
5. Assumption: a residency is its place. Confidence before reading: 50.

## Item 7

1. Before reading, I assumed that an earner's income is itself an asset that moves with markets, so a portfolio should offset it (human capital; Bodie, Merton and Samuelson; life-cycle investing). On the concept: nothing new. The new piece is a regulator's worked example.
2. Aha: 2
3. Claims:
   - The pension example works because the employer must pay in more when the scheme's funding falls. In this case no money comes after January 2028, and the payments must be funded from the portfolio with no outside money. Laura's income never tops up the plan, so the "double-up" channel is mostly absent and the analogy is weaker than it looks. The true point is narrower: she could not rescue a shortfall from her income.
   - "is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell": whose words are these? For a graphic novelist, the link between advances or licences and share markets is asserted, not shown. One figure (for example, publishing revenue in 2008-09) would carry it.
   - "only $29,829 of her $450,000 goes into them": that is 6.6% in shares, very low for someone with a long horizon. The sentence invites the opposite criticism, too little growth, and the item does not answer it.
4. Question: She cannot add money after 2028 anyway, so what does her career risk change in the plan that the no-outside-money rule had not already settled?
5. Assumption: an earner's income is a share-like asset that a portfolio should offset. Confidence before reading: 85.

## Item 2

1. Before reading, I assumed "a high degree of certainty" means a high probability, say 95% or more, so 96.8% would pass. What I had backwards: "high" should be judged against what is possible. When the same deposits can buy all ten payments outright, any chance of a miss is a choice, not a cost the goal forces on her.
2. Aha: 4
3. Claims:
   - "they leave no payment short, by design": this holds for the first deposit once it is invested in January 2027. But the January 2028 top-up ($38,282) is priced at today's rates and would cost more if rates fall before then. "By design" needs "once the 2028 top-up is bought", or the plan's answer to a fall in rates before 2028.
   - Treasury funds that end on a set date are not quite bonds: their final payouts vary a little with fees and with how they hold cash near the end. "No payment short" should rest on the sizing margin, and the item should say so.
   - The item repeats Item 1 almost word for word: the first plan, three-quarters in shares, 40% in 2032, 3.2%, 1 in 31, $27,695.
   - "3.2% meant about 1 in 31 against none" is crisp and works.
4. Question: In dollars, what did choosing "none" over "1 in 31" cost Laura in expected building money or flexibility, and how did you decide that price was worth paying?
5. Assumption: "a high degree of certainty" means very likely, about 95% or more. Confidence before reading: 70.

## Item 4

1. Before reading, I assumed a funder's range is a forecast, and that a written word of support is soft comfort. What I had backwards: the line that matters is between a statement of present fact and a promise of future conduct. Laura can make her 2031 figure a statement of present fact about money she already owns, so a crash cannot turn it into a broken promise.
2. Aha: 4
3. Claims:
   - "no fall in markets can change either end": both ends sit in Treasury funds that end on set dates. Their market value moves before maturity, and a fund's final payout is not fixed to the cent. Closer would be "no fall in markets can change what either end pays when its Treasuries mature".
   - If both ends are owned in Treasuries before she speaks, why is it a range at all? A cold reader expects the bottom to be certain and the top uncertain. "The figure co-sponsors count is the top" and "the bottom is the part held for her since January 2028" left me unsure what the width of the range means and what confidence goes with it. This is the most confusing passage in the pack.
   - "the ever-reliable Treasuries": a quotation from the plan, but it reads as puffery, and a judge may mark it down.
   - The irony of the case: the Court of Appeal found for the company, so the letter did not bind it. The item uses the case for its distinction, not its outcome, which is fine. One clause saying that Laura's credibility, not a court ruling, is at stake would stop a judge reading it backwards.
   - £12.26m, October 1985 and 1989 fit what I know of Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining.
4. Question: In 2031, what exact sentence does Laura say, with what stated confidence, and why give a bottom at all if the top is already owned?
5. Assumption: a funder's range is a forecast that a crash can break. Confidence before reading: 65.

## Item 6

1. Before reading, I assumed founder-funded museums close when the founder's endowment is spent. What I had backwards: in thirteen Taiwanese cases there was no endowment at all. They ran on the next year's income and stopped when that income stopped, which is exactly the risk Laura's prefunded payments remove.
2. Aha: 4
3. Claims:
   - "Thirteen Taiwanese art institutions were found": found by whom, and how? The selection method matters. If they were found through news of closures, the sample is biased.
   - "none had even one year of running costs set aside when it opened": a strong claim about all thirteen. What source shows it for each one?
   - "the founder paid first in every one": unclear. First before whom: the state, or other donors?
   - Four of the thirteen stopped and nine did not, so running on next year's income worked in most cases. The item should say why that still argues for prefunding (for example, that a writer's income is lumpier than a company's profit).
   - "digital photography ended the trade ... in 2025": digital overtook film well before 2010, so a trade ending in 2025 reads oddly. Say what the trade was (a photo lab? printing?) and why it ended then.
4. Question: How were the thirteen chosen, and of the nine still open, how many have since built a reserve?
5. Assumption: founder museums close when the founder's fortune runs out. Confidence before reading: 55.

## Item 8

1. Before reading, I assumed time value means sooner money is always worth more. What the item says I had backwards: people will give up price for a credible payer. As a finance reader I found nothing new. Credit risk has always been part of the value of a later payment, and the US Treasury's credibility was never in doubt.
2. Aha: 2
3. Claims:
   - "The authors found that the scarce part is the payer": as I understand this Kenyan dairy study, farmers chose monthly payment mainly because a lump sum works as a savings commitment (it is harder to spend). Buyers' credibility explains why traders could not offer that. The item treats the supply-side half as the main finding, and a judge who knows the paper will notice.
   - "86% ... even when the daily price was 15% higher": check this against the paper. It says farmers gave up 15%, and that is a striking number to get wrong.
   - "A payment made later is worth only its payer's credibility": this goes too far. A later payment is worth its present value, discounted for the payer's credit risk.
   - "one payer make them all, the US Treasury": the plan holds Treasury bond funds, so a fund manager, custodian and broker sit between Laura and the Treasury. "One payer" is true of the underlying bonds, not of what she holds.
4. Question: Which other payer did you weigh against the Treasury, and does putting all ten payments with one issuer, through funds, carry any risk that you checked?
5. Assumption: under time value, sooner is always better. Confidence before reading: 85.

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

1. Item 4: the tin-crash comfort letter ("a statement of present fact and not a promise as to future conduct"). It is distinctive and it changes how I read the co-sponsor figure. It is only let down by the unclear range passage.
2. Item 6: thirteen Taiwanese founder institutions with no year of running costs set aside. It is original local research, and it names the real failure mode.
3. Item 3: the 1982 bond that "locked in" 14.25% and delivered 9.29%. A concrete and memorable number pair, though the concept is textbook.

(Item 5's Rauschenberg Captiva sale was a close fourth. Item 2 is the strongest argument but the least memorable story.)

## Items that clash or repeat

- Items 1 and 2 repeat: the same first plan, the same 3.2%, 1 in 31 and $27,695, and the same "no money comes after January 2028". One of them should carry the first plan, and the other should only point to it.
