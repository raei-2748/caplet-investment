## 1. The one range known to hold (3c)

- **Claim.** The statistician who invented confidence intervals wrote that any one interval, once worked out, holds its
  target with probability zero or one and nobody knows which; after the 2031 move Laura's range is the rare case where
  it is known, and it is one.
- **Before and after.** Before: "confidence" must be a percentage below 100, so a plan that says "certain" is spinning,
  and two owned ends make a range pointless. After: for a single worked-out range the only honest answers are zero or
  one; hers is one because both ends are Treasuries she owns; the percentages belong to a different question, which
  range she will be able to state, asked before the move. The range then records two facts: what was owned first (the
  bottom, from January 2028) and what the move added (the top).
- **Rests on.**
  - Q74-002: Neyman (1937, p. 349): "no probability statement ... may be made, that is except for the hypothetical and
    trivial ones" (zero or unity). VERIFIED.
  - Q74-007: 59% of 118 researchers endorsed "There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies between ..." for a
    95% interval; 3% rejected all six false readings (Hoekstra et al. 2014). VERIFIED. The one case where that common
    misreading is true is a range known to hold.
  - Q74-010 (Morey et al. 2016, "the lost submarine": a figure below certainty for an interval "that surely contains the
    parameter would clearly be a mistake"); Q74-015 (NIST: an interval for a future value is a "prediction interval",
    so never "confidence interval" for hers); Q74-031 (ICD 203: name the outcome, its timing and its nature first);
    Q76-006 (FINRA and the SEC define a projection as "an estimate of the future performance": an owned end estimates
    none). All VERIFIED.
  - Q71-033 (Keel inference on IPS l.15 and NUMBERS 4): why two figures, the order of ownership.
  - NUMBERS 4: after the move both ends are owned "in every house path, every history window and every joint window",
    so the report says "certain"; before the move the stated top is at least $100,000 in 92.2% of simulated futures and
    at least $115,000 in 59.8%. NUMBERS 4 and 8l: fund costs beyond the two fees up to the 0.5% cut are covered
    everywhere; at 0.75% the top is missed in 6.01% of simulated futures by at most $402, the contribution still inside
    the range.
- **Part and job.** 3c, the confidence sentence: Wharton's "the level of confidence associated with that range and the
  factors that could affect it". It answers the question all three cold readers asked, in 3c's own paragraph, before a
  judge asks it. Conceit turn beside it: non-photo blue (the before-the-move percentages are the construction lines
  judges see and co-sponsors never will).
- **Strongest objection.** The professor: "a confidence of one by construction is a tautology; you defined the range
  as what she owns and dodged the case's question" (card Q4c row 26 named it). **Answer:** the construction is the
  recommendation, and Neyman's own word for this case is "trivial" because it is settled, not because it is empty. The
  case's real uncertainty is moved to where it lives, before the move, and stated there with its own percentages
  (92.2%, 59.8%; Table 2), and the conditions are named once (fund costs beyond the 0.5% cut, for the top). The same
  paragraph must carry both halves, or the tautology objection wins.
- **Strength 4.** It reverses an expert's prior with the inventor's own words, answers the set's one open question,
  and fits the client: Laura holds "a degree in Statistics & Information Decisions Management" (case l.16).
  Not 5: a lay reader needs one plain line ("a range you already own holds; percentages belong to ranges you might
  have owned"), and the tautology objection must be met in the same breath.
- **Guards.** Never "confidence interval", "confidence level" or "95% confident" for the owned range (Q74 claim 3);
  "certain" goes on the Treasuries and the event, never on "$115,000" (card Q71: heard as "at least"); no percentage
  label below one (Q74-010). Overlap: the reserve "the client is a statistics graduate" says the same conclusion from
  the client's side; if this row enters, that reserve becomes its clause (one row, not two). It does not restate row 4
  (certainty has dates) or row 7 (speaking early): its claim is what "confidence" means, not when she speaks.

## 2. Owned, not owed (2.1)

- **Claim.** When Lehman Brothers' broker failed in 2008, what its customers owned came back in full, most of it within
  weeks, while what it owed its lenders came back at about 41 cents in the dollar; Table 1's holdings are of the first
  kind, the funds' Treasuries included.
- **Before and after.** Before: holding Treasuries through a broker, and 61.28% of the book through BlackRock funds,
  stacks three credits (the broker, the sponsor, the US), so "owned in Treasuries" overstates it. After: "owned" is a
  legal status with a price tag. Customer assets "are not property of the securities intermediary, and are not subject
  to claims of creditors"; a fund's Treasuries sit at a custodian bank, ring-fenced by series, and the sponsor owns none
  of them; the only promise in any chain is the US Treasury's. The sponsor of these funds has changed hands once
  already (Barclays to BlackRock, 2009) with the funds running on.
- **Rests on.**

    $106bn, "fully satisfying the 111,000 customer claims"; "Most customer claims were satisfied within weeks" (SIPC
    2022). VERIFIED.
  - Q72-008 (UCC 8-503), Q72-023 (iShares Declaration s.2.07; Delaware 3804(a)), Q72-024 (1940 Act s.17(f): a fund's
    securities with a bank), Q72-030 (2009 sponsor sale), Q72-003 (31 U.S.C. 3123(a), the one promise). VERIFIED.
  - Q72-032 for one clause at most: an exchange-traded note on the same shelf "Do not represent ownership in the
    underlying assets" (Lehman's 2008 FWP), and "Unlike ETFs, ETNs do not own an underlying portfolio of assets"

    record (Q72 Gaps).
  - NUMBERS 8g: Treasuries 89.55% of the book at cost (iBond ETFs 61.28%, bonds 28.27%).
- **Part and job.** 2.1, where Table 1's "type" column says ETF or Treasury bond and where the conceit's definition lands

- **Strongest objection.** The practitioner: "SIPC covers $500,000 and her money reaches $589,655 near the end (NUMBERS
  8k); MF Global and Madoff show custody can fail." **Answer:** SIPC caps its own advance, not what comes back:
  customers first share the segregated pool ratably (15 U.S.C. 78fff-2(c)(1), 78fff-3(a); Q72-012, -013), and since

- **Guards.** Never "in her name" (street name: say "held for her", Q72-020); never call a fund's payout fixed (TNA
  note 2: "a net asset value, as opposed to a promise of par"); "owned" never means "fixed". Not a restatement of the

## 3. Short of money? Add money, never risk (3a, or 2.1 if register row 1's plain fact stays there)

## 4. Treasurers lock a currency at the contract, not the announcement (3e, area 4)

  meetings would swap a possible cost for a certain one about half its size.

  it open in 2031 is a gap in the plan. After: practice is the reverse. New Zealand's Treasury: "The latest point at

  known; firms take half at approval and all at the contract; in 2031 there is no NT$ bill, amount or date. Holding NT$

## 5. Layers for a covariance, not a bias (2.1, or 2.3)

- **Claim.** Behavioural finance predicts that an investor who splits money into layers holds "bonds and lottery
  tickets" and overlooks how the layers move together; Caplet's layers hold the textbook's two funds and exist because
  of how they move together.
- **Before and after.** Before: a finance professor sees buckets (the ten, the floor, the shares, the half kept) and
  reads mental accounting, a known bias (Shefrin and Statman 2000). After: the contents are the CAPM's two funds,
  Treasuries matched to dates and one world market fund; Waring and Whitney (2009) name exactly this pair "a new
  risk-free asset, the investor's liability-matching asset portfolio" plus "a slightly redefined world market
  portfolio", "a new two-fund theorem"; and the reason for the layers is a covariance the behavioural investor ignores:
  her career, her co-sponsors and share prices can fall in the same downturn (IPS l.9).
- **Rests on.** Q57-010 ("bonds and lottery tickets"), Q57-011 ("act as if they overlook covariances"), Q57-042
  (Waring and Whitney: abstract VERIFIED, body paywalled, so quote the abstract only), Q57-015 (Chhabra's aspirational
  bucket holds a business "that forms a significant part of an individual's wealth": her career, to which the
  portfolio adds nothing). NUMBERS 8g (89.55% Treasuries, VT 8.84%).
- **Part and job.** 2.1, each holding's role (why one world fund beside the Treasuries and nothing else), or 2.3 if the
  lead wants the covariance said where it already lives. Portfolio Analysis: the structure judged against the
  literature it resembles. Conceit turn beside it: the key plate (the ten printed first, everything else registered to
  them).
- **Strongest objection.** "Das, Markowitz, Scheid and Statman (2010) already showed mental accounts can be
  mean-variance efficient, so nothing is new." **Answer:** their accounts each carry a shortfall probability; Laura's
  first account carries none, which makes it the liability-matching asset, not a mean-variance account; and this row's
  claim is why the layers exist, not whether they are efficient. Second: the covariance is only partly true (CANON B4;
  card Q50: Taiwan's state and largest named sponsors did not cut in 2008-09). **Answer:** say "can fall in the same
  downturn", the career form (US arts sole proprietors' receipts -11.6% in 2009), never "always".
- **Strength 3.** A real "I had that backwards" for a finance professor; little for a nonprofit finance director.
- **Guards.** Never "hedge" in Keel's own words; the covariance said once in Section 2 (spine 2 leans on it too, so this
  row and spine 2 sit in different parts and do not both explain it).

## 6. The safest holdings swing the most (2.1 or 2.5)

- **Claim.** For ten payments fixed in dollars, the Treasury that pays on the date is the asset with no error even
  though its price swings, so the long bonds in Table 1 are the safest holdings for this promise, not the riskiest.
- **Before and after.** Before: Table 1's bonds maturing 2037-2041 are rate risk, and a mostly-Treasury book should
  hold its value. After: Roy (1952), the other founder of portfolio theory, measured in money: "the price of money in
  terms of itself is clearly unity and the standard error of this estimate is zero"; Merton (2014): matching income
  makes capital value "very volatile, because risk is being hedged to eliminate income volatility, not price
  volatility", and floor-on-value rules would bar "long-maturity U.S. Treasury bonds - the very assets that are the
  safest from a retirement income perspective". What counts as safe depends on what is promised: Peel's 1844 Act let
  government debt carry only the first £14m of notes and demanded bullion for every note beyond it, because the notes
  promised gold.
- **Rests on.** Q57-001, Q57-029, Q57-030, Q58-002. VERIFIED. Q57-008 (Markowitz: "Roy can claim an equal share of this
  honor") only as quoted by Rubinstein (2002), since Markowitz 1999 is paywalled. NUMBERS 1: the instruments' duration
  7.76 years ($234 a basis point) against the payments' 9.94 years ($300).
- **Part and job.** 2.1 or 2.5: what the book is judged by (what it pays, not what it is worth on a day). It also gives
  Wharton's rule a reason: WInS value changes say nothing about the ten payments.
- **Strongest objection.** The professor: "immunisation is textbook; Leibowitz (1986) is already in 2.3 as the
  'least-risk asset'." **Answer:** for that reader the surprise is only Peel and Merton's floor-rule warning; the row
  lands mainly on the nonprofit finance director. Second: the density cut took duration figures out of Section 2
  (SKELETON 2.5). **Answer:** no figure in prose; $234 a basis point stays in CANON C12 for questions.
- **Strength 2.5.** True and well sourced, but close to an existing SKELETON line (2.3's Leibowitz) and known to the
  most expert reader.
- **Guards.** The iBond funds pay their holdings' value less the fee (TNA note 2), so "no error" is said of the bonds,
  and of the funds only as "their Treasuries"; no WInS gain or loss is quoted.

## 7. In Taiwan the state is the co-sponsor whose date never holds (3e area 3, or 3d)

- **Claim.** Taiwan's legislature passed the national budget by its legal deadline in none of the eleven years
  2016-2026, and while a budget is late the law lets existing grants run but holds back new capital spending, which is
  exactly a new residency's building money.
- **Before and after.** Before: public money is the most dependable co-sponsor, and a late budget delays everyone
  equally. After: in Taiwan it is reliable in amount but not in date; the law (Budget Act Art. 54) keeps running money
  going at last year's level and makes "新興資本支出及新增計畫" (new capital spending and new programmes) wait for the
  vote. That is why 3d's pledges close on one date no later than December 2032, and why the figure she states was owned
  years before any vote matters.
- **Rests on.** Q49-010 (0 of 11 on time, FY2016-26; median 51 days late, about 20 January; 2026 passed on 14 Aug;
  Keel arithmetic on DGBAS releases; VERIFIED), Q49-012 (Art. 54; VERIFIED), Q49-027 (a private residency's public
  capital line sits inside the art-village grant's NT$3m cap). No plan figure.
- **Part and job.** 3e area (3), outside funding: public co-sponsors fall short through budget votes, not recessions
  (SKELETON already says so for amounts); this adds the date. Or one clause in 3d beside the close date.
- **Strongest objection.** "Median 51 days is small, and nobody schedules a building for January." **Answer:** yes,
  so the row is a date risk, not an amount risk, and 2026 (August) shows the tail. Second: Taiwan's legal rules are out

- **Strength 2.5.** Strong client knowledge for a nonprofit finance director, little for a fixed-income professor; it

## 8. A US downgrade is a label, a default is a reason (3e, Table 3 rows 2-3)

- **Claim.** Three downgrades of the United States moved Treasury-backed promises one notch each and changed no payment,
  because what such promises rest on is the Treasury's payments, not its rating.
- **Before and after.** Before: the US is no longer AAA at any agency, so a plan that calls Treasuries risk-free should
  watch ratings or carry a downgrade trigger. After: when S&P (2011), Fitch (2023) and Moody's (2025) cut the US,
  Treasury-backed escrows fell one notch with "no changes to the structural elements or cash flows" (Moody's), no sale
  was forced because covenants ask for "direct obligations", not a rating, and pre-refunded yields barely moved; the
  other way round, a company's bonds went from BBB- to AA+ the day Treasuries were owned against each payment (S&P,
  Iowa, 2024). A downgrade is a label; non-payment is the IPS's reason 2, and Table 3 row 2 defines it.
- **Rests on.** Q54-006, Q54-007, Q54-008, Q54-010, Q54-011, Q54-038. VERIFIED (some from excerpts, as each row says).
- **Part and job.** 3e, the IPS's condition named once (SKELETON allows it there), and Table 3 rows 2-3's "what it never
  changes". Conceit turn beside it: a US default is the paper; a downgrade is not.
- **Strongest objection.** "Rated escrows are irrevocable and verified, and none may hold a fund; Laura's account is
  neither, and her floor is a fund." **Answer:** the row is used only for what a downgrade does to Treasury cash flows
  (nothing), never to claim her account is an escrow; the plan "meets the escrow's sufficiency test" and no more.
- **Strength 2.5.** It meets a doubt a judge may well raise (the 2025 cut is recent) with a fact, but it defends rather
  than surprises.
- **Guards.** Never "escrow-grade" holdings; no "barring a US default" beside "risk-free" (Book 19); one clause.
