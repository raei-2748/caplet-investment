**Item F.** The big capital funders a building campaign needs most, public arts programmes and large foundations, write
their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed. A building is worth nothing until it is fully
paid for, and field experiments find that such projects are funded far more often when someone goes first: seed money
announced at two-thirds of a goal raised much more from others than seed money at one-tenth (List and Lucking-Reiley,
2002). For most givers, going first means pledging money not yet in hand, the over-promise the case warns about. Laura
can go first without it: by the 2031 meetings her $115,000 is already held for her in Treasuries that pay out in
December 2032, so she can be the first to commit, in writing, while every other funder keeps its rule of paying last.

**Item P.** A pledge to a building campaign carries two questions: will the giver pay, and can she? A gift office
answers the first with a signature: it counts a pledge once it holds a signed document giving at least the amount, the
purpose and the payment period. The second it can only guess, so it books an allowance for pledges that will not be
paid. The pledges that failed in 2008-10 failed on the second question: Lehman Brothers' $10 million pledge to Spelman
College, announced in 2007, turned into $3 million when the bank collapsed. Laura's figure answers the two questions in
the other order. By the 2031 meetings her $115,000 is already held for her in a Treasury fund that pays out in December
2032, so whether she can pay is a fact a co-sponsor can check; her signed pledge letter then answers whether she will.
Owned is why her figure will be paid; signed is why a funder can count it.

**Item V.** A building in Taiwan is paid for in Taiwan dollars, and Laura's $115,000 is in US dollars, so a co-sponsor
may ask what happens to her gift's worth if the Taiwan dollar strengthens before 2033. The question has an exact name
and a history. When the World Bank's fund for the poorest countries, the International Development Association, was set
up in 1960, its rules made a member whose currency fell pay "an additional amount of its own currency sufficient to
maintain the value" of its subscription: "maintenance of value". Its members were governments that could tax and print
their own money, and the rule ran both ways; the Association now writes into each funding round that maintenance of
value "will not be applicable" (2022) and fixes reference exchange rates instead. Laura can offer only the one-way form:
if the Taiwan dollar strengthens, an extra amount from the half of her shares promised to no one, capped by what that
half holds, and announced as a separate gift when it is paid, never as part of the figure she signs.
