# Reader Y: a finance professor who teaches fixed income

## 01, item F

1. Assumption: that the range and its Treasury backing were purely defensive, a way to avoid over-promising, with no bearing on where Laura sits in the order of funders. I had not thought about order at all. Sureness: 60.
2. What it means: I had the dedication as a cost (a capped, conservative promise), but it is also what lets her be the lead commitment that a capital campaign needs, because a pledge that is already defeased can go first without being an over-promise.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe.
6. What made it land or not: the logic connecting a defeased liability to the "who commits first" problem is clean and new to me, but it rests on a fundraising experiment I cannot judge and on a generalisation about funders' rules that I suspect is too tidy.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "her $115,000 is already held for her in Treasuries that pay out in December 2032": the item says Treasuries, but the context says the late-2032 piece is a Treasury fund that ends on a set date. A target-maturity fund is close to a bond but its payout is not a fixed contractual sum (fees, reinvestment of coupons at unknown rates, possible early termination), so "held" is a little strong. Also $115,000 is the top at November 2026 rates; the figure actually held in 2031 depends on share prices up to the move, so it may not be $115,000.
   - "A building is worth nothing until it is fully paid for": overstated. Phased or partially funded projects have some option value, and many large funders pay in milestones rather than last.
   - "write their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed": too sweeping. Challenge grants and matching commitments are often made early, conditional on others; "commit last" and "pay last" are different things and the item blurs them. Laura also commits early but pays in 2033.
   - The List and Lucking-Reiley result came, as far as I recall, from small direct-mail gifts to a university fund, not from institutional capital funders; carrying it over to foundations and public arts programmes is an extrapolation.
8. Question for the team: at the 2031 meetings, which figure does Laura commit to in writing as the "first" money, the bottom (already in the late-2032 fund since 2028) or the top (only fixed once half the shares have moved), and what does the letter say if the move has bought less than $115,000?

## 02, item V

1. Assumption: that a US-dollar pledge to a Taiwan-dollar building simply leaves the currency risk with the project and the co-sponsors, and that Laura's only response would be the general flexibility pot, unnamed and unsized. Sureness: 55.
2. What it means: nothing I had backwards on the economics, but it gives the co-sponsor's currency question a precise, recognised form: a one-way, capped maintenance-of-value top-up paid from the unpromised half and kept outside the signed figure, which is a cleaner answer than "we have flexibility".
3. Surprise: 2.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: maybe (the term "maintenance of value" and the IDA history, yes; the plan's use of it, less so).
6. What made it land or not: the separation between the signed US-dollar figure and a discretionary currency top-up is right and well put, but the IDA story is long for what it buys, and the item does not size the exposure, so I cannot tell whether it matters.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "Laura can offer only the one-way form": "only" needs its reason. A fixed-income reader's first thought is a forward or a Taiwan-dollar asset; if those are ruled out (derivatives banned, permitted list), say so, otherwise "only" reads as a choice dressed as a constraint.
   - "an extra amount from the half of her shares promised to no one": once offered to a co-sponsor, even informally, it is no longer quite promised to no one. And the same half is already named as the cover for building overruns and for a co-sponsor falling short. Three claims on one pot, all most likely in the same bad state, are a double count unless sized together.
   - "announced as a separate gift when it is paid, never as part of the figure she signs": unclear what a co-sponsor can rely on. If it is not signed, it is an intention, and a careful funder will give it close to zero weight; the item should say that plainly rather than call it an offer.
   - The IDA history is offered as precedent, but its rule ran between sovereign members and a lender, with symmetric adjustments; the analogy to a private donor topping up a building fund is loose.
8. Question for the team: how large is the currency exposure in dollars (say a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar on the signed figure), and how much of the unpromised half is left for it in the state where share prices have also fallen and building costs have overrun?

## 03, item B

1. Assumption: that the case was written so that $300,000 roughly defeases ten $50,000 payments 6 to 15 years out at ordinary rates (a back-of-envelope present value at 4.5-5% lands near $300,000), so the first deposit "naturally" buys the payments. Sureness: 70.
2. What it means: I had the fit between $300,000 and the ten payments as the normal state of things, when on the plan's measure it held on only about one day in nine since 2000 and on a single day between August 2007 and August 2026, so the 2028 deposit, not the first, is what makes the payments safe.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes.
6. What made it land or not: it is a plain present-value calculation I can check in my head (the $470,217 at 2020 rates is about right for yields near 1%), and the dated single day in 2023 plus "built to need the year, not the day" turn a known fact about the rate cycle into something about this plan's design.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027": two conditions stacked in one clause ("even if ... unless ..."); I had to read it twice. Also, the completion is bought in January 2028, at January 2028 prices, so the rate risk on the unbought part runs to January 2028, not January 2027. Either the gap is fixed in some other way or the date in the sentence is the wrong one.
   - "whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them": true only if the deposit arrives. The plan itself names a missed deposit as a reason to change course, so "whatever" should carry that condition.
   - "Commissions came to $200": commissions are not the whole trading cost; bid-ask spreads on individual Treasuries and on fund units can exceed $200 on $300,000 of retail trades. The point (costs are tiny next to the rate swing) survives, but the figure understates cost.
   - "valued on each day's Treasury rates": unclear whether the payments are discounted on the par curve or on zero-coupon (spot) rates bootstrapped from it; for single payments the spot curve is the right one, and the difference is not trivial at 15 years.
8. Question for the team: between January 2027 and January 2028, is the unbought part of the ten a fixed number of dollars or a fixed set of bonds still to buy, and what happens to the 3.6-point cushion if long yields fall during 2027 rather than before January 2027?

## 04, item P

1. Assumption: that a co-sponsor's worry about a founder's pledge is mainly whether she means it (intention, reputation), with ability to pay taken on trust for a wealthy individual. In credit terms I would separate willingness from ability as a matter of course, but I had not applied it to this pledge. Sureness: 50.
2. What it means: it shows the plan settles ability to pay first, in assets a funder can inspect, and leaves the signature only the narrower job of settling willingness, the reverse of how a gift office normally has to treat a pledge.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Would I repeat it tomorrow: yes (the Lehman and Spelman story, and the willingness-versus-ability split).
6. What made it land or not: the can/will split is the standard sovereign-credit distinction, so it landed at once, and the Spelman example is concrete; what held it back is that holding an asset is not the same as having it committed.
7. Overstated, unclear or wrong:
   - "whether she can pay is a fact a co-sponsor can check": it can check that she holds the fund on the day it looks, not that she will still hold it in December 2032. Unless the fund is escrowed, pledged or held in a restricted account, she can sell it the next morning, so "can pay" is only checkable at a point in time.
   - "Owned is why her figure will be paid; signed is why a funder can count it": this reverses the item's own mapping. Earlier the signature answers "will she pay" and ownership answers "can she"; the closing line gives "will be paid" to ownership. Either the line is loose or the mapping is.
   - "The pledges that failed in 2008-10 failed on the second question": too sweeping. Many individual pledges failed because donors chose not to pay after a wealth shock, which is willingness under strain, not inability; a bankrupt corporate donor like Lehman is the clean case of inability.
   - "her $115,000 is already held for her in a Treasury fund that pays out in December 2032": as in an earlier item, $115,000 is the top at November 2026 rates and depends on share prices until the move, and a fund ending in late 2032 pays out its net asset value, close to but not exactly a fixed sum.
8. Question for the team: will the Treasury fund behind her figure be held in a restricted or escrowed account, or pledged to the residency, so that a co-sponsor can check not only that she owns it in 2031 but that it cannot be redeployed before December 2032?

## Two items I would still remember after 40 reports

- B: it is a fact about rates that anyone in fixed income can check and almost no team will have dated: $300,000 bought the ten on a single day between 2007 and 2026, so the plan's safety rests on the second deposit.
- P: the willingness-versus-ability split applied to a donor's pledge, with Lehman and Spelman as the picture, is memorable and gives the co-sponsors a reason to count her figure.

## Items that clash or repeat

- F and P repeat: both rest on the same sentence (her $115,000 already held in Treasuries paying out in December 2032 by the 2031 meetings) and both argue that a co-sponsor can rely on her figure; F uses it for the order of commitment, P for can versus will.
- V and P clash: P says a signed figure is what a funder can count, while V's currency top-up is deliberately never part of the signed figure, so by P's own logic a co-sponsor should give V's offer little or no weight.

## One item for the co-sponsors

P: it speaks a gift office's own language (signed pledge, allowance for pledges not paid) and tells a co-sponsor in one move both why her figure can be paid and why it can be counted.
