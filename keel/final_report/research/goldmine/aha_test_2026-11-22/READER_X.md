# Reader X: a programme officer at a foundation that funds arts buildings, who reads founders' pledges before a capital campaign

## 01, item P

1. Assumption: a founder's pledge is a promise against her general wealth, mostly shares, so its real risk is that her capacity shrinks in the same downturn that hits the campaign, and my job is to discount it for that. Sure: 75.
2. What it means: I had the order backwards for this founder; I am used to getting the will first (a signature) and guessing the capacity, whereas here the capacity is settled first, in Treasuries I could ask to see, and the signature only adds the will.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: the two-question frame is exactly how my committee talks about pledges (will they, can they), and the Lehman-Spelman case is the kind of 2008 story every campaign officer has heard, so the reversal clicked at once.
7. Overstated or unclear: "whether she can pay is a fact a co-sponsor can check" - held for her is not the same as held for the building; unless the fund sits in escrow or under some instruction she cannot reverse, she could still sell it or spend it on something else, so what I can check is her capacity on the day I look, not that the money is reserved for us. Also "her $115,000 is already held" - the $115,000 is a figure at 2026 rates, so the 2031 figure may differ; and a fund that "pays out in December 2032" pays what its bonds are worth then, which I would want stated as a near-certain figure rather than an exact one. Lehman's pledge was a corporate pledge broken by bankruptcy, a somewhat different failure from an individual founder's.
8. Question: Will the Treasury fund behind her figure be held in a way she cannot redirect (escrow, a separate account with a standing instruction, or similar), and will co-sponsors get to see the statements in 2031 and again before December 2032?

## 02, item B

1. Assumption: that $300,000 in Treasuries covering $500,000 of payments six to fifteen years out was simply what safe bonds do in ordinary times, so the payments side was the easy part of her plan. Sure: 55.
2. What it means: I had it backwards that the payments were the easy part; on most days this century $300,000 would not have bought the ten, and in 2020 both deposits together would not have, so the plan only looks comfortable because 2026 happens to be a rare high-rate year, and it leans on the 2028 deposit to cover the gap if rates move first.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land: the 4 August 2020 figure, $470,217 against $450,000 of deposits, is concrete and startling, though the rest is a run of dates and figures I had to read twice.
7. Overstated or unclear: "that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027" - "even if ... unless" in one clause made me reread it, and I could not tell what happens to the building money if that cushion is used. "On exactly one day from August 2007 ... to August 2026" is very precise, and "the plan's own measure (... always the same distance ahead)" is not explained enough for me to judge it. "Commissions came to $200" and "the team's orders filled" refer to something I do not know about (her purchase is January 2027, so whose orders in October 2026?), and the commissions line adds nothing for a funder.
8. Question: If the ten cost more in January 2027 than today, how much of the 2028 deposit goes to them first, and how much lower would the building range be as a result?

## 03, item V

1. Assumption: a founder pledging in US dollars to a building priced in Taiwan dollars would simply leave the currency risk with the project, converted on the day paid, and at most mention it as a risk. Sure: 60.
2. What it means: not quite backwards, but new to me; she can go one step further than naming the risk, offering a capped, one-way top-up if the Taiwan dollar strengthens, paid from money she has not promised, kept outside the figure she signs, which is a small private version of a rule the World Bank once wrote for governments and later dropped.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe
6. What made it land or not: the practical offer at the end is the part I would use with a committee; the 1960 IDA history is elegant but took most of the paragraph to reach a point I could act on.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Laura can offer only the one-way form" - the reason (she cannot tax or print money, so she cannot ask for money back) is left for me to infer. "capped by what that half holds" - the same unpromised half is also her cover for building overruns and a co-sponsor falling short, so three calls sit on one pot and the cap could be much smaller than the half by the time a currency top-up is needed. And if the top-up is offered in 2031 but "never as part of the figure she signs", I am unsure whether it is a commitment or a hope; my committee would count it as zero. The IDA's own move away from maintenance of value in 2022 could be read as a reason not to offer it at all.
8. Question: If building costs overrun, a co-sponsor falls short and the Taiwan dollar strengthens at the same time, which claim on the unpromised half comes first, and roughly how much of a Taiwan-dollar rise could it cover after the other two?

## 04, item F

1. Assumption: in a capital campaign the founder is expected to give first, as the lead gift, and funders like mine wait for the gap to close before we commit; I assumed a founder's early pledge always carried some over-promise that we quietly discount. Sure: 80.
2. What it means: mostly confirms how my world works, but the new part is that her lead gift need not be discounted, because by 2031 it is already held in Treasuries, so she can be first without being the kind of early promise I normally haircut.
3. Surprise: 2
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes
6. What made it land: it describes my own foundation's rule accurately enough that I trust it, and it turns her caution into the thing a campaign most needs, a first commitment that is real.
7. Overstated or unclear: "write their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed" - too broad; many of us commit early with a challenge or matching condition and pay on milestones, so "commit conditionally" is closer than "pay last". List and Lucking-Reiley (2002), as I recall it, was a small mail-appeal experiment with modest goals, not a building campaign, so the leap to capital projects is a stretch. Most importantly, their finding is about seed money at two-thirds of the goal; nothing says what share of the building $115,000 is, and if it is a small share the same study suggests her going first helps much less. "A building is worth nothing until it is fully paid for" is strong; a part-funded building can be phased or scaled. The sentence about her $115,000 being "already held for her in Treasuries that pay out in December 2032" repeats item P almost word for word.
8. Question: What share of the building's expected cost is her $115,000, and who is meant to take the campaign from her first pledge to the point where the large funders' last-dollar rules let them commit?

## Figure recall (from memory)

- B: $470,217, what the ten would have cost on 4 August 2020, more than both deposits
- F: two-thirds (seed money announced at two-thirds of the goal raised much more than at one-tenth)
- P: Lehman's $10 million pledge to Spelman that became $3 million
- V: 1960, the year the IDA's maintenance-of-value rule was written

## Two items I would still remember after 40 reports

- P: "will she pay, and can she" is the question I ask of every pledge, and this is the only founder I can picture who answers the second before the first.
- F: it turns a cautious founder into the first mover a building campaign needs, while letting funders like mine keep our own rule.

## Items that clash or repeat

- P and F say the same thing at their core, with nearly the same sentence ("by the 2031 meetings her $115,000 is already held for her ... December 2032"); P is the reason her pledge can be counted, F is the use a campaign makes of that, so in one report they should be one argument.
- V sits a little uneasily with the plan's "promised to no one": offering a currency top-up from that half to co-sponsors starts to promise part of it, and the same half is also the cover for overruns and a co-sponsor falling short.

## One item for the co-sponsors

P: it answers, in our own language, the two questions every co-sponsor's committee will ask of her figure, and tells us what we can check.
