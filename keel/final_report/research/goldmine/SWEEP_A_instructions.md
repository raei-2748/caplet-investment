## 3. Aha candidates from the instructions

Each one turns something the judge expects into its opposite, and the plan's own numbers support it.

1. **Every fixed promise that broke broke one of four ways; this one has shut all four** (#20). The judge expects
   "safe" to mean a hope. Instead it gets a checklist with four histories, each door shut by a dated purchase, and one
   named exception, a US default.
2. **Diversifying the payments would make them less safe** (#23). Diversification is the first thing a judge looks
   for. Ten separate 99% promises miss one about one time in ten, while one government's debts stand or fall
   together. So the ten sit with one issuer on purpose, and the diversification moves to Laura's career and the shares.
3. **Since mid-2007, $300,000 could buy the ten on a single day** (#25, #26). The judge assumes buying the payments in
   2027 is caution. It is timing: 2026 is the rare year when the deposit can buy them outright, the reverse of 2015's
   savers, so the plan buys at once.
4. **Public pensions value promises at the return they hope for, and that made them take more risk. Laura values hers
   at what Treasuries charge** (#24). The plan's central rule is the reverse of a famous institutional failure.
5. **The range is a bought deal, and the newsvendor says the owned bottom is the right promise** (#43 with #42). Both
   ends are bought before she names them, as a bank buys shares before selling them. When breaking a promise costs
   far more than beating it (Gneezy and Epley), the arithmetic sets the promise at what she owns.
