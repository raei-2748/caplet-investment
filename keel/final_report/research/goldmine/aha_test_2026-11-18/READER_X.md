## 02, item T

1. Assumption: the 2028 deposit is the plan's single point of failure, because the premise itself says her income falls in slumps; I assumed a meaningful slice of the ten payments depended on it. Sureness: 65.
2. What it means: the payments lean on only about a quarter of the 2028 deposit (about $39,000 of $150,000), because the deposit is spent in a fixed priority order, so a short deposit lands on the building money first and the payments survive a deposit roughly three-quarters smaller (60% smaller even with yields at zero).
3. Surprise: 3. A priority-of-payments waterfall is standard liability-matching practice, so the design itself is not new to me; the size of the margin (73.9%, and 60% under a zero-yield stress) is what I did not know.
4. Conviction: 4. The arithmetic hangs together: $39,000 is 26% of $150,000; $57,455 of nominal shortfall bought at roughly 4-4.5% over an average nine years costs about $39,000, and at zero yield about the face amount plus costs, near $60,000. And it matters, because it answers the obvious objection to the premise.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes. "The deposit could arrive three-quarters smaller and every payment is still paid without reinvestment" is a line I would use.
6. What made it land or not: it raised the obvious objection first, then answered it with a mechanism (spending order) rather than a probability, and then stress-tested the answer at zero yields, which is exactly the test I would have asked for.
7. Overstated or unclear: "A deposit that never comes, one of the plan's two named reasons to change course" sits awkwardly beside "The plan adds no rule for a smaller deposit; the same order simply runs." If a 74%-smaller deposit needs no change but a 100%-smaller one does, the item should say what the change is and where the line sits. Also "$57,455 short of the $500,000 they owe, added across their ten dates" is hard to parse on first read (it means the nominal shortfall summed over ten dates under zero reinvestment, not a present value). And the item tests a smaller deposit but not a late one, which is the likelier way a writer's slump would show.
8. Question: If the 2028 deposit never comes, at what reinvestment rate on coupons and maturities do the ten payments still get paid in full from the January 2027 holdings alone, and what exactly does Laura do differently that year?

## 03, item K

1. Assumption: a ladder of coupon Treasuries priced off today's curve is not truly "locked in": it reaches the target only if coupons are reinvested at the forward rates, so a ladder sized at present value carries reinvestment risk unless it is overfunded or built from zeros. Sureness: 90. This is Homer and Leibowitz's realised-compound-yield point, and dedicated-portfolio practice handles it with a conservative reinvestment assumption.
2. What it means: nothing backwards for me in principle; what is new is that the team saw the reinvestment bet hidden in its own trade note, measured it historically (short in most start months since 1981, 1-in-20 shortfall about $34,600, worst about $45,300), and removed it by overfunding to the zero-reinvestment escrow standard at a cost of about $39,000 that mostly comes back (about $46,600 in 2033 money if rates hold).
3. Surprise: 2 on the idea, 4 on finding a student team that did it; the fact that the "insurance" is largely returned to Laura if rates hold is the part a lay reader would miss.
4. Conviction: 5. It is true, it is the standard a verification agent applies to a refunding escrow, and with STRIPS off the menu overfunding is the honest way to defease coupon bonds.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes. The 1982 bond that was "locked in" at 14.25% and returned 9.29% is a good teaching story, and "sized so all ten are paid even if cash earns nothing" is the right one-line standard.
6. What made it land or not: it opens with a concrete historical case, names the exact forecast in the team's own note, prices it, then removes it with a named professional standard, and it says what the fix costs and what comes back.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Laura's ten Treasuries carry the same kind of bet" is true in kind but not in size: a 14.25% coupon bought at par in 1982 is an extreme reinvestment exposure; 4-5% coupons are much milder, so the anecdote dramatises more than the numbers that follow. "a condition history has met" is a slightly loose way of saying zero-rate periods have occurred, which makes the standard realistic, not merely theoretical. "The ten as first priced, $302,287" against a $300,000 deposit needs the reader to infer that the first ~$2,400 of the 2028 deposit closes that gap; the item does say so later, but the order makes it hard to follow. And "worth about $46,600 in 1 January 2033 money if rates hold" needs "rates hold" defined (today's forwards, or today's flat curve?).
8. Question: Dedicated-portfolio managers usually assume a low positive reinvestment rate (say 1-2%) rather than zero; how much building money would that free, and why did you choose the zero standard over a conservative floor?

And "In the 27 September model's own terms" signals that these figures come from an older model with different assumptions (US shares at 6.7%, not world shares at 7.5%), so they are not directly comparable with the rest.
8. Question: Rerun the first plan and the Treasury-first plan with the 2028 deposit cut by a quarter, half and all of it: how often does each miss a payment, since that is the slump your own premise is built on?
