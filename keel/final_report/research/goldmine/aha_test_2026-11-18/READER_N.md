# Reader N: finance director of a mid-sized nonprofit arts organisation (outside fixed income)

## 01, item L

1. Assumption: the top of the range would swing a lot with the return forecast the team picked, and picking J.P. Morgan's above-median 7.5% would flatter it. Sureness before reading: 70.
2. What it means: the choice of forecast hardly changes the number she would say out loud, because the figure is rounded down to $5,000 steps and only part of the money is exposed for about three years; the real danger is naming the top before the money has been moved, so the rule is to name it only once it is bought.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes (the "name it only after you have it" part; less so the fifteen houses).
6. What made it land: the 2.9% / 6.2% / 8.6% / 10.6% ladder by month makes "speak early, get burned" concrete, and the rule matches what I already enforce at work (we never announce a match or commit a grant before the cash is in the restricted account), so the Antideficiency Act line felt like a familiar principle with a grander name.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Yet the forecast barely touches the figure she states." Partly an artefact of rounding down in $5,000 steps: if the unrounded top sits just above $115,000, almost any forecast stays in the same bucket, so this shows the rounding is coarse more than that the plan is insensitive. It is also unclear why a forecast matters at all if she only names the top after the move (then the realised share price sets it, not anyone's forecast); I could not tell whether the fifteen-house test is about today's estimate or about the 2031 figure. "GMO at about -2.5% a year still gives $110,000" surprised me and I could not check it.
8. Question: If the top is only named after the move, what exactly is today's $115,000 for, and what will she tell co-sponsors before 2031 if they ask for a number early?

## 02, item M

1. Assumption: a "range with confidence" would be a probability statement, something like "we are 90% sure we will give between X and Y", with the bottom as the bad case. Sureness: 60.
2. What it means: once the shares have moved, both ends are bonds she already owns, so the range is a record of when each part was locked in (bottom since 2028, top since the move), not a band of uncertainty, and the stated range is simply certain; the model's percentages only answer the earlier question of which range she will end up able to state.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe (the "two ends are two dates, not a margin for error" idea, yes; Neyman, probably not).
6. What made it land or not: the reframe of the bottom as "the part owned longest" was new to me and clean, but the Neyman and 2014-study opening is a statistics seminar I had to get through before reaching the point that matters to a funder.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Laura's 2031 range is the rare one known to hold." A confidence interval estimates something unknown; her range is a sum of bonds she owns, so the comparison is a bit of a category stretch (of course a figure you already hold "holds"). "So the range she states, once both ends are owned, is certain" comes straight after a condition (fees, the 0.5% margin, cash earning nothing), so it reads as certain-with-a-footnote; I believe the footnote is tiny (about $120 per extra 0.1%) but the word and the condition sit awkwardly together. Also unclear: if the top is certain and is what co-sponsors count, what job is the bottom doing for them? A co-sponsor of mine would hear a range and plan on the low end.
8. Question: If the top is owned and certain by the meetings, why state a range at all rather than one figure, and how will co-sponsors be stopped from planning on the bottom?

## 03, item J

1. Assumption: her 2031 statement to co-sponsors would be a pledge like the ones I get from donors, believed mainly on her reputation and the plan's forecasts. Sureness: 50.
2. What it means: what makes her word worth something is not a stronger promise but a checkable fact underneath it (money already held in Treasuries, on a broker statement), whereas the mining company's letter only stated an intention, which it could revise when the tin market fell.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes; I will use the fact-versus-promise split on our own pledge letters.
6. What made it land: the case is a real story with a twist (bank wins £12.26m, then loses on appeal), and it maps exactly onto a problem I see every year, donors whose "it is our policy to support you" letters evaporate when their own markets turn.
7. Overstated or unclear: "a broker statement shows" and "no fall in markets can take it lower." A broker statement in 2031 shows today's market value, which can sit below what the funds pay at the end of 2032 if interest rates have risen; the payout figure is a calculation, not the number printed on the statement, so a co-sponsor reading the statement could see less than $115,000. Also, "Treasury funds ending in late 2032" pay roughly, not exactly, a set amount (fund costs), which item M handled with a margin but this item drops. And "The fact does not bind her": then nothing stops the money being spent elsewhere between 2031 and 2033; the item is honest about that, but a co-sponsor will notice.
8. Question: Will she put the top in a separate, named account or sign a pledge agreement with the co-sponsors, or is the broker statement the only evidence they get?

## 04, item K

1. Assumption: buying one Treasury ending before each payment "locks in" that payment, so the first deposit alone settles the ten $50,000 payments. Sureness: 75.
2. What it means: I had it backwards; the ten bonds only reach $500,000 if their interest is reinvested at rates nobody can promise, and over most of the last forty years that bet would have lost (by around $16,000 typically, up to $45,000), so the plan spends about $39,000 of the 2028 deposit to make the payments safe even if waiting cash earns nothing.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes, the 1982 bond story: "locked in 14.25%", actually earned 9.29% a year.
6. What made it land: the 14.25% versus 9.29% story did the work in one line, and it undid the exact word ("locked") I would have used myself; the paragraph after it lost me in figures.
7. Overstated or unclear: far too many numbers for one paragraph ($302,287, 5.12%, 585 months, 1 in 6, $16,055, $34,555, $45,266, 90 of 222, 0.15% twice, 0.5%, $39,000, $2,400, $46,600); I could keep only one or two. "the first $2,400 or so of it completing the ten" is unclear: completing what, if the ten were already bought in 2027? "What comes back is worth about $46,600 ... if cash waiting earns nothing, none beyond that 0.5% margin" took me three reads; I think it means the insurance pays her back with a profit unless the bad case happens, but it does not say so plainly. "fallen short more often than not for start dates since 1981" leans on forty years of falling rates from a 1981 peak; today's rates are far lower, so the history may overstate how likely a shortfall is now (the item does not claim otherwise, but the reader is left to guess).
8. Question: Why not buy bonds that pay exactly $50,000 in each January with no interest along the way, so nothing ever has to be reinvested, instead of buying extra Treasuries as a cushion?

## 05, item T

1. Assumption: the 2028 deposit, coming from a writer whose income falls in slumps, is the plan's weak point, and a smaller deposit would put the payments at risk. Sureness: 65.
2. What it means: because the payments are first in line for the 2028 money, a smaller deposit shrinks the building money, not the payments; the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller (or about 60% smaller even if yields went to zero) and all ten payments would still be safe.
3. Surprise: 3
4. Conviction: 5
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes; "the order you spend in decides who takes the shortfall" is exactly how I explain our restricted funds and reserve policy to a board.
6. What made it land: it named the obvious worry first (the plan's own premise says her income can shrink) and then answered it with a plain spending order and one memorable number (about three-quarters smaller and still safe).
7. Overstated or unclear: "only what is left buys the Treasury fund for the bottom of her range, and then the shares" reads as a strict order (bottom first, shares last), yet "a deposit a quarter smaller ($112,500) lowers the bottom she could state from $95,000 to $65,000." If the bottom came before the shares, a $37,500 cut should come mostly out of the shares and barely touch the bottom; a $30,000 fall in the bottom suggests the leftover is split between the two in a fixed proportion. I could not reconcile these. Also "$57,455 short ... added across their ten dates" is a sum of shortfalls in different years' money, so it is a rough size, not a bill she would face at once.
8. Question: After the first part, is the rest of the 2028 deposit split between the bottom fund and the shares in a fixed proportion, or does the bottom fund really come first?

## 06, item P

1. Assumption: the shares are there to make the building contribution bigger, so more shares means a bigger figure for co-sponsors and fewer shares a smaller one. Sureness: 55.
2. What it means: I had it backwards; putting all the building money in the Treasury fund would let her promise more ($130,000, above the plan's top in about 95 futures in 100), and what holds the promised figure down is the decision to keep half the shares for her own flexibility, a trade of about $15,000 of median contribution for about $20,000 of median flexibility with the same bad-case total.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 3
5. Repeat tomorrow: maybe; the line "what lowers the figure co-sponsors count is keeping half the shares promised to no one, not holding shares" is worth repeating, the rest is too dense.
6. What made it land or not: the $130,000 versus $115,000 comparison was a real jolt, and the last two sentences were the clearest in the item, but the middle juggles contribution, flexibility, top, bottom and "what its funds pay above its top" so fast that I lost which pot each number belonged to.
7. Overstated or unclear: "Fewer shares would leave her less." Two sentences later, all-Treasury leaves her less in about 63 futures in 100, so in about 37 it leaves her more; "less" holds at the median and most of the time, not always. "with the same 1-in-20 low for all the money left to her beyond the ten payments, about $159,000" is the strongest point in the item (the shares do not worsen her bad case) and it is buried in a subordinate clause. As a would-be co-sponsor I also notice the item, read plainly, says the plan deliberately promises us less so that she keeps more; that is defensible (overruns, the Taiwan dollar) but it should be said as her choice, with the reason.
8. Question: Whose choice was it to trade $15,000 of the co-sponsors' figure for $20,000 of Laura's flexibility, and what will she say to a co-sponsor who learns that an all-Treasury plan could have promised $130,000?

## 07, item W

1. Assumption: $300,000 is roughly what ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out normally cost, so the case's numbers are ordinary and the plan works in most years. Sureness: 50.
2. What it means: I had it backwards; $300,000 would have bought the ten on only about one day in nine since 2000 (in August 2020 they would have cost $470,217, more than both deposits), so the plan stands on an unusually high-rate year, and it is built so that any January 2027 price is topped up from the 2028 deposit rather than depending on catching a cheap day.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes; "in 2020 the same ten payments would have cost $470,000, more than both her deposits" is the one figure of the day I would quote.
6. What made it land: one concrete, dated, outrageous number (2020's $470,217) made "rare year" real, and the closing line "built to need the year, not the day" says what it means for the plan in plain words.
7. Overstated or unclear: "two trading days before the team's orders filled" and "Commissions came to $200": the context says the ten are bought in January 2027, so as an outsider I do not know what orders filled in October 2026 (a practice or simulated portfolio, I assume), and the commissions line seems beside the point for Laura. "built to need the year, not the day" is followed by "unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027", so it is "the year, within limits"; 3.6 points in under two months sounds very unlikely, but saying how unlikely would help. "10.9% of days since 2000" and "exactly one day from June 2007 ... to August 2026" are two different windows back to back; I had to reread to see they do not conflict.
8. Question: If yields fell, say, 2 points before January 2027, how much of the 2028 deposit would completing the ten use up, and what would that do to the range she could state?

## 08, item R

1. Assumption: the team moved to Treasuries because a share-heavy plan could not meet "a high degree of certainty". Sureness: 60.
2. What it means: I had it backwards; the share-heavy first plan (96.8 in 100) would have passed the usual 90% reading of "high", and the team dropped it for a different reason, her income falling in the same slumps as shares, then priced that choice: about $19,000 less in the middle case in exchange for a bad case (1 in 20) of about $159,000 left instead of about $20,000, and a 1-in-31 chance of a final miss turned into none.
3. Surprise: 4
4. Conviction: 4
5. Repeat tomorrow: yes; "their first plan would have passed, and they changed it anyway: give up $19,000 in the middle to lift the bad case from $20,000 to $159,000" is the kind of trade I have to argue to my own board about reserves.
6. What made it land: the honesty of admitting the first plan was good enough on paper, and then a clean asymmetry ($19,000 given up against roughly $139,000 protected in the bad case) that a non-specialist can weigh in one breath.
7. Overstated or unclear: the borrowed yardsticks ("beyond a reasonable doubt" at about 90% in judges' surveys, the SEC's oil and gas reserves) are apt but there are two of them where one would do. "the same two deposits leave no payment short from January 2028, once the first quarter or so of the second deposit is in" is a model result under stated conditions (Treasuries pay, fund costs within the margin), so "none" is right inside that frame; outside readers may take it as a guarantee against everything. The model here grows US shares at 6.7% while item L used world shares at 7.5%; an outsider does not know why the two tests use different assumptions. "(5.5% at 5.0% a year)" is squeezed into brackets and easy to misread as a third rate.
8. Question: Did Laura herself choose to give up about $19,000 of the middle case for the safer bad case, or was going "a step further than high" the team's call on her behalf?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- W: one dated, outrageous number ($470,000 in 2020, more than both deposits) tells me the whole plan sits on a rare year and was built not to need a lucky day.
- K: "locked in 14.25%" that actually earned 9.29% killed a word I would have used myself, and showed why the extra Treasuries exist.
- R: a team admitting its first plan would have passed, then showing what it paid to go further ($19,000 in the middle for a far better bad case), is the judgment I would want from anyone managing money for me.

## Items that clash or repeat

- T and P: T describes the 2028 money as a strict order (the bottom fund, then the shares), but its own figure (a quarter-smaller deposit cuts the bottom from $95,000 to $65,000) only makes sense with P's fixed split of about three-quarters fund, one-quarter shares; T's wording clashes with P's.
- K, T and W repeat one point: the first part of the 2028 deposit (about $39,000) completes the ten and buys extra Treasuries so all ten are paid even if waiting cash earns nothing. Said three times, it starts to read as boilerplate.
- J and M repeat the "both ends are owned Treasuries, counted at what they pay with no further interest" point, with a mild clash: J says "no fall in markets can take it lower", while M carries a margin for fund costs and a possible small shortfall (about $120 per extra 0.1%).
- L and M sit oddly together: L says the forecast "barely touches" the $115,000 top (13 of 15 houses), while M says that even at 7.5% the stated top reaches $115,000 in only about 57 futures in 100; one is about the forecast, the other about luck, but read back to back the $115,000 sounds both firm and a coin toss.
- P and R both use "about $159,000" as the 1-in-20 low of the money left to her, compared with different alternatives (all-Treasury in P, the first share-heavy plan in R); consistent, but the same figure doing two jobs is easy to blur.
- L and R use different share assumptions (world shares at 7.5% versus US shares at 6.7% in the 27 September model) with no word on why.
