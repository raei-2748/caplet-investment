**Item R.** The case asks for "a high degree of certainty". The team's first plan (27 September 2026) held about
three-quarters of the money in shares to 2030, gliding to 40% in 2032, and on 1 January 2033 it would have bought all
ten payments in Treasuries with whatever the money had grown or shrunk to. In the team's test it would have paid all ten
in full in 96.8 simulated markets out of 100, which passes the usual reading of "high": judges asked in surveys put
"beyond a reasonable doubt" at about 90%, and the US Securities and Exchange Commission, for oil and gas reserves,
writes "a high degree of confidence" as at least a 90% probability. So "high" did not rule the first plan out. The team
gave it up once it thought about how a writer is paid (the premise in your summary), and went a step further than
"high": buy the payments first, in Treasuries, sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing. A test run
on 27 September, before any trade, priced the change. With US shares growing 6.7% a year, the first plan fell short in
3.2% of simulated markets (5.5% at 5.0% a year), and each miss would have been final: settled on 1 January 2033, before
any payment, with no money coming after January 2028 to close it; the median miss was $27,695 in 1 January 2033 money.
Bought into Treasuries first, the same two deposits leave no payment short from January 2028, once the first quarter or
so of the second deposit is in. So the step meant about 1 in 31 against none. In the 27 September model's own terms,
buying the Treasuries first gave up about $19,000 of the median money left above the ten in 2033, and at the 1-in-20 low
left about $159,000 where the first plan left about $20,000.
