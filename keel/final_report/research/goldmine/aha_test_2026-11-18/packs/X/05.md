**Item M.** Jerzy Neyman, who invented the confidence interval, wrote in 1937 that once a range has been worked out it
either holds its target or it does not: the probability is zero or one, and nobody knows which. So a "95%" label does
not give that one range a 95% chance, yet in a 2014 study 59% of 118 researchers endorsed the false reading "There is a
95% probability that the true mean lies" inside the range. Laura's 2031 range is the rare one known to hold. After half
the shares move into Treasuries before the meetings, both ends are Treasuries she owns: the top is her contribution and
the figure co-sponsors count, and the bottom is the part owned since January 2028. The two figures record how long each
part has been held, not a margin for error. One condition goes with them: each end is counted at what its funds pay if
cash waiting earns nothing, after their fees, with at least 0.5% of the funds' value to spare for other fund costs
(about 2% on today's figures); only if such costs used up that margin, and cash waiting really earned nothing, would the
contribution fall short of the top, and then by at most about $120 for each further 0.1%. So the range she states, once
both ends are owned, is certain. The percentages in the team's model belong to an earlier question, asked before the
move: which range will she be able to state. With world shares assumed to earn 7.5% a year, the stated top is at least
$100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures and at least $115,000 in about 57.
