**Item J.** A lender reads a large company's written word of support as a promise. Kleinwort Benson lent to Malaysia
Mining's tin-trading company against a letter that said "It is our policy to ensure that the business of M.M.C. Metals
Ltd. is at all times in a position to meet its liabilities to you", and a High Court judge awarded the bank £12.26m with
interest. The tin market collapsed in October 1985. Malaysia Mining then wrote that the policy had been "our policy at
that time" and that "no assurance was given that such policy would not be reviewed in the light of changing
circumstances". In 1989 the Court of Appeal set the award aside: the sentence was "a statement of present fact and not a
promise as to future conduct". Malaysia Mining gave only that fact, and its fact was an intention. Laura's 2031 words
have two parts. The fact is money held for her in Treasury funds ending in late 2032, which a broker statement shows:
all of the top, $115,000 on today's figures, since half the shares moved before the 2031 meetings, and the bottom since
January 2028. Each end is counted at what its Treasuries pay if they earn no more interest, after fees, so no fall in
markets can take it lower. The promise is that she will give the top, her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count.
The fact does not bind her to the promise; it makes the promise believable.
