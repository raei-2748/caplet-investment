# Reader Y: a finance professor who teaches fixed income

## 01, item T

1. Assumption: that the 2028 deposit is a real hole under the payments, but probably a small one, since a quick
   discounting of ten $50,000 flows 6-15 years out at roughly 4.5-5% comes to about $300,000, so the first deposit
   should carry nearly all of it. Sure of that: about 65.
2. What it means: I had the deposit filed as a risk to the payments; the spending order turns it into a risk almost
   entirely to the building money, since the payments can survive a deposit roughly three-quarters smaller (60% smaller
   even with yields at zero).
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes (the "order of spending decides where a shortfall lands" point, with the 60%-at-zero-yields
   stress).
6. What made it land: it names the correlated case a fixed-income person would raise at once (the slump that cuts the
   deposit is the one that cuts yields and makes the top-up dearer) and prices it, rather than only quoting the base case.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "about three-quarters smaller (73.9%)": a decimal place on a figure that depends on where
   rates sit in January 2028 is false precision; "about three-quarters at today's rates" is enough. (b) "only what is
   left buys the Treasury fund for the bottom of her range, and then the shares" reads as strict priority, yet a quarter
   smaller deposit ($37,500 less) lowers the bottom by $30,000, which only fits if the remainder is split roughly in
   proportion between fund and shares; if the fund came first, the shares should absorb most of the cut. Which is it?
   (c) "$57,455 short ... if cash waiting earns nothing" mixes a no-deposit case with a zero-reinvestment case; the
   shortfall at realistic reinvestment rates would be smaller and should be shown beside it.
8. Question: How is the remainder after the first part split between the late-2032 fund and VT when the deposit comes
   in smaller, and why does the bottom fall by $30,000 rather than the shares taking the cut first?

## 02, item K

1. Assumption: that a zero-reinvestment sufficiency standard (which item T had already mentioned) is costly
   over-insurance, money taken from the building for a near-impossible case. Sure of that: about 50. (That coupon bonds
   do not "lock in" their yield I took as given: I teach it, 95.)
2. What it means: the buffer is not deadweight: if rates hold, the roughly $39,000 comes back as about $46,600 in 2033
   money, so its real cost is only the share return forgone on it, while without it the ten would have missed $500,000
   in most post-1981 starts.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes; the 1982 bond (14.25% "locked in", 9.29% realised with coupons rolled in bills) is the
   realised-compound-yield lesson in one line, and the 585-month replay on today's ladder makes it this plan's problem.
6. What made it land: it takes the team's own sentence ("reinvested coupons fund the rest") as a forecast, prices what
   that forecast has cost in history, and then shows the cure is mostly refunded, which answers the objection I had.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "reach $500,000 only if the interest ... earns today's forward rates ... or a flat 5.12%":
   "only if" is too strong; those are sufficient paths, and any reinvestment path compounding to the same value works.
   (b) "a condition history has met": bills under 0.15% in 90 of 222 months is not zero for the whole six-to-fifteen
   year wait the test assumes; the test is stricter than history, which is fine but should be said that way.
   (c) the statistics switch denominators ("when short, by a median $16,055" versus "at 1 in 20 of all starting months,
   by $34,555"), which makes them hard to compare. (d) The replay applies past bill levels to today's yields, so "more
   often than not since 1981" describes a falling-rate era, not a probability; it should not be read as one.
   (e) A newspaper column is a thin source for a point Homer and Leibowitz made canonical.
8. Question: Did you look at low-coupon, deep-discount off-the-run Treasuries, the closest permitted substitute for
   STRIPS, to shrink the coupon stream and so the size of the buffer?

## 03, item R

1. Assumption: that a growth-first plan could not meet "a high degree of certainty" for fixed nominal payments, so the
   case's wording itself forced cash-flow dedication. Sure of that: about 70.
2. What it means: I had it backwards: the share-heavy plan passed the usual 90% reading of "high" (96.8 in 100), so
   dedication was a choice made for the writer's-income reason, and its price is small at the median (about $19,000)
   against a far better bad case (about $159,000 left at the 1-in-20 low against about $20,000).
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes; "1 in 31 against none" and the tail comparison are the surplus-risk argument for
   immunisation in two numbers, and the point that the growth plan's misses are final (settled in one January, nothing
   coming after 2028 to repair them) is the part students usually miss.
6. What made it land: it concedes the opponent's best fact first (the old plan was "high" by the courts' and the SEC's
   bar) and then prices the step beyond it, which is more convincing than claiming the old plan failed.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) the SEC point: as I recall the reserves definition, "a high degree of confidence" is the
   gloss for deterministic estimates and "at least a 90% probability" the test for probabilistic ones, both under
   "reasonable certainty"; saying the SEC "writes" one as the other is a slight squeeze. (b) "1 in 31" and "96.8" are
   one model's numbers (6.7% equity growth, presumably thin-tailed returns); the 5.0% case already moves it to 1 in 18,
   so the model risk deserves a word. (c) The comparison seems to assume both deposits arrive in full; the plan's own
   premise is that the 2028 deposit may shrink in a slump, which is exactly when the share-heavy plan is hurt.
   (d) "US shares growing 6.7%" while the plan holds a world fund (VT).
8. Question: Under your own premise, with the 2028 deposit smaller in the same slump that cuts shares, how often would
   the first plan have missed, compared with 1 in 31?

## 04, item P

1. Assumption: that the shares in the building money are what make the range wide and pull its bottom down, and that
   an all-Treasury building sleeve would give a lower but certain figure. Sure of that: about 60.
2. What it means: I had the shares' job backwards: all Treasuries would let her state a higher single figure ($130,000,
   above the plan's top in about 95 futures in 100), so the shares are there for her own unpromised cushion, and what
   lowers the number co-sponsors count is the hold-back, not the equity.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: maybe; the last sentence (an all-Treasury plan holding back the same flexibility would state
   $110,000) is the separation of the two decisions, how much to promise and what to hold, and I would use it.
6. What made it land, and what nearly stopped it: the closing reframe is clean, but it comes after about ten figures
   across four alternatives, and I had to rebuild the comparison on paper (plan median contribution about $115,000 and
   flexibility $29,000, against $130,000 and $9,000) before it clicked.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "with the same 1-in-20 low for all the money left to her beyond the ten payments, about
   $159,000": with a quarter of the building money in shares, I would expect the plan's low to sit below the
   all-Treasury plan's, more so if a slump lowers yields and shares together as the premise says; identical lows need
   explaining (separate tail paths, or the model's correlation). (b) "gives up $15,000 of the median contribution for
   $20,000 more median flexibility": medians do not add; the trade should be stated on the total's median, or as the
   median of the difference. (c) "more shares would be the risky choice" is obvious and could go; the 64-in-100 figure
   already says it. (d) "Fewer shares would leave her less" is unclear: less of what, the total or the flexibility?
8. Question: Why is the plan's 1-in-20 low for everything left to her the same as the all-Treasury plan's, when a
   quarter of the building money is in shares that fall in the slumps that also cut yields?

## 05, item W

1. Assumption: that $300,000 covering ten $50,000 payments 6-15 years out is simply what the case was calibrated to at
   today's rates (my own rough discounting in item T said so), so I took the fit as normal and treated a rate rally
   before January 2027 as the plan's main timing risk. Sure of the "normal fit": about 60; of the timing risk: 55.
2. What it means: the fit is historically rare (about 1 day in 9 since 2000, a single day between mid-2007 and August
   2026), and the plan does not depend on catching it, because the 2028 deposit's first call absorbs any price for the
   ten short of a fall of about 3.6 points in yields.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes; "$300,000 bought the ten on one day in nineteen years" and "$470,217 on 4 August 2020" are
   the clearest way I have seen to show students what a rate level does to a liability's price.
6. What made it land: the 2020 figure (more than both deposits together) makes the rate level tangible, and "built to
   need the year, not the day" turns a lucky fact into a design point.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002" and "Commissions came to $200" are about
   the team's practice trades in October 2026, not Laura's January 2027 purchase; mixed in here they read as timing luck
   and blur which book is meant. (b) "unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027": with about six weeks to
   go this is near-certain safety, but the exposure that matters runs on through 2027 to the 2028 deposit (item T's
   zero-yield case), and the deposit can be short at the same time; the threshold alone flatters. (c) "valued on each
   day's Treasury rates" should say which curve (par or spot) and how the coupon ladder was turned into a price.
   (d) "a rare year": 10.9% of days since 2000 is mostly 2000-2002 and 2023-2026, two regimes, not a random draw.
8. Question: What is the joint threshold: how far can yields fall and the 2028 deposit shrink together before a payment
   is short of the zero-reinvestment standard?

## 06, item L

1. Assumption: that the 2031 top is the soft spot, sensitive to the equity forecast, and that leaning on J.P. Morgan's
   7.5% flatters it. Sure of that: about 65.
2. What it means: I had the risk in the wrong place: with a small share slice, a short horizon and $5,000 steps rounded
   down, the forecast hardly moves the stated top (13 of 15 houses give $115,000), and the real danger is naming the
   top before the move has bought it.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes; the Antideficiency Act line ("the very act of obligating ... when the necessary funds are
   not already in the account is also a violation") is a clean statement of the rule "promise only what you hold", and
   the 1/3/6/12-month overshoot rates show the cost of breaking it.
6. What made it land: it answers the obvious objection to the 7.5% (a reader like me raises it at once) with the
   fifteen-house sweep, then moves the reader to the variable that does matter, timing, and gives it numbers.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "the forecast barely touches the figure she states" is partly an artefact of the coarse
   rounding: any modest input looks inert under $5,000 steps rounded down; the real reason is that the share slice at
   stake is small and its horizon short, and that should be the stated reason. (b) Fifteen houses' forecasts are not
   like for like: GMO's are, as I recall, real and over seven years, while J.P. Morgan's are nominal over 10-15 years;
   a "-2.5% to 9.0%" spread may mix real and nominal figures. (c) Since she names the top only after the move, today's
   forecast does not set the figure at all; it only sets today's estimate of it, and the item could say so plainly.
   (d) The legal parallel is apt but its subject is budget authority, not cash; "funds ... in the account" is the GAO's
   wording, so fine, but it is ornament more than evidence.
8. Question: What does Laura say if a co-sponsor needs a figure before the move, for example to close its own budget a
   year earlier; does she give the bottom alone?

## 07, item M

1. Assumption: that "a range with how confident she is" means a probabilistic band, something like "90% confident
   between X and Y", with the confidence taken from the model. Sure of that: about 70.
2. What it means: I had the range's nature backwards: once half the shares have moved, both ends are Treasuries she
   owns, so the stated range is certain (on a stated zero-reinvestment-after-fees condition), its two ends mark how long
   each part has been held rather than an error margin, and the model's percentages answer only the earlier question of
   which range she will be able to state.
3. Surprise: 4.
4. Conviction: 3.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes, in class: Neyman's "zero or one" and the 2014 finding (59% of 118 researchers endorsing
   the false reading) is a good warning against putting "95%" on a single range for co-sponsors.
6. What made it land, and what held it back: separating the question asked before the move (which range can she state:
   top at least $115,000 in about 57 futures in 100) from the question after it (does the stated range hold: yes) is
   clear and correct; the Neyman detour takes three sentences to reach a point the plan does not need, since her range
   is not an estimate of an unknown parameter at all.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "the two figures record how long each part has been held, not a margin for error": if
   the top is certain and is what co-sponsors count, the bottom tells them nothing about her contribution, and a reader
   will still take "$95,000-$115,000" as "she may give as little as $95,000"; the item does not say why a range is
   stated at all rather than one figure. (b) "Laura's 2031 range is the rare one known to hold" sets it beside
   confidence intervals as if it were the same kind of object; it is a commitment backed by holdings, not an inference.
   (c) "certain": the ends are funds, not bonds, so wrapper risks (early closure, operational error) remain; the 0.5%
   margin covers costs, and the item should say whether it is meant to cover those too. (d) "at least $115,000 in about
   57" sits uneasily beside the earlier item's "thirteen give the same $115,000 top": the familiar $115,000 is close to
   a coin flip even on the 7.5% forecast.
8. Question: If her contribution is the top and it is certain, what does the bottom of the stated range tell a
   co-sponsor, and why state a range rather than one figure with the bottom noted as already held?

## 08, item J

1. Assumption: that what makes Laura's 2031 words credible to co-sponsors is the strength of the wording (a firm
   pledge reads better than a hedged one), with the holdings as background. Sure of that: about 50.
2. What it means: I had the source of credibility backwards: a stated policy or pledge is worth little on its own (the
   comfort letter was only "a statement of present fact", and that fact was an intention), whereas a verifiable present
   fact of money held makes a plain promise believable without needing the words to bind.
3. Surprise: 3.
4. Conviction: 4.
5. Repeat it tomorrow: yes; Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining is the comfort-letter case I would use in a credit
   class, and splitting her words into "the fact" and "the promise" is a precise way to write co-sponsor language.
6. What made it land: the case is concrete (a named letter, a collapse in October 1985, an award won and then set aside)
   and its lesson maps one-to-one onto the case's warning about over-promising.
7. Overstated or unclear: (a) "so no fall in markets can take it lower": true of prices for bonds held to their end, but
   the holdings are funds, and the earlier item's own condition (a 0.5% margin for other fund costs, a shortfall of about
   $120 per further 0.1%) means "no fall" should carry that condition. (b) "which a broker statement shows": a broker
   statement is itself a statement of present fact; it shows what she holds today, not that it cannot be sold or
   redirected tomorrow, so the parallel cuts both ways and the item should face that. (c) "its fact was an intention" is
   compressed to the point of being hard to parse on first reading.
8. Question: Would Laura put the held Treasuries beyond her own reach once co-sponsors rely on them (an escrow, a signed
   pledge, an account in the project's name), and if not, what stops the fact changing after the promise is made?

## Three items I would still remember after 40 reports

- R: it concedes that the share-heavy plan already met the usual 90% bar for "high" and then prices the step beyond it
  (1 in 31 against none, about $19,000 of median for a 1-in-20 low of $159,000 instead of $20,000): the surplus-risk
  case for dedication, argued honestly.
- W: "$300,000 bought the ten on one day in nineteen years" and the $470,217 of August 2020 make a liability's rate
  sensitivity unforgettable, and "built to need the year, not the day" turns it into design.
- J: no other student report will answer the co-sponsor question with a comfort-letter case, and the fact/promise split
  is exactly right for that paragraph.

## Items that clash or repeat

- T, K and W repeat one mechanism: the first part of the 2028 deposit (about $39,000) completes the ten and buys the
  zero-reinvestment buffer; T stresses the deposit's size, K the reinvestment rate, W the price level before 2027. One
  report could carry this once and let the other two lean on it.
- M and J repeat nearly the same sentences: both ends owned Treasuries, counted at zero reinvestment after fees, the top
  her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count.
- L and M sit uneasily together: L's "thirteen give the same $115,000 top" reads as robust, while M shows that even on
  the 7.5% forecast the top reaches $115,000 in only about 57 futures in 100 (forecast spread against path spread, not a
  contradiction, but a reader of L alone would over-trust $115,000).
- J and M clash slightly: J says "no fall in markets can take it lower", while M keeps a condition under which the
  contribution can fall short by about $120 per further 0.1% of fund costs.
- P and R both lean on the 1-in-20 low of about $159,000; R uses it against the first plan, P says the all-Treasury plan
  has the same low, which I could not reconcile with a quarter of the building money in shares.
