**Item J.** A lender reads a large company's written word of support as a promise. Kleinwort Benson lent to Malaysia
Mining's tin-trading company against a letter that said "It is our policy to ensure that the business of M.M.C. Metals
Ltd. is at all times in a position to meet its liabilities to you", and a High Court judge awarded the bank £12.26m with
interest. The tin market collapsed in October 1985. Malaysia Mining then wrote that the policy had been "our policy at
that time" and that "no assurance was given that such policy would not be reviewed in the light of changing
circumstances". In 1989 the Court of Appeal set the award aside: the sentence was "a statement of present fact and not a
promise as to future conduct". Malaysia Mining gave only that fact, and its fact was an intention. Laura's 2031 words
have two parts. The fact is money held for her in Treasury funds ending in late 2032, which a broker statement shows:
all of the top, $115,000 on today's figures, since half the shares moved before the 2031 meetings, and the bottom since
January 2028. Each end is counted at what its Treasuries pay if they earn no more interest, after fees, so no fall in
markets can take it lower. The promise is that she will give the top, her contribution and the figure co-sponsors count.
The fact does not bind her to the promise; it makes the promise believable.

**Item K.** A 1992 Baltimore Sun column said that buyers of the new 20-year Treasury of January 1982 had "locked in" its

Each payment rests on its own Treasury, and the team's own trade notes left one forecast in the plan: for the later
payments, "reinvested coupons fund the rest". The ten as first priced, $302,287, reach $500,000 only if the interest
they pay before the payments earns today's forward rates (the rates for future years built into today's Treasury
prices), or a flat 5.12% a year on every waiting dollar. Replayed over 585 starting months since 1962, the ten as priced
would have fallen short more often than not for start dates since 1981, the years rates fell, and for about 1 start in 6
from 1962 to 1980: when short, by a median $16,055; at 1 in 20 of all starting months, by $34,555; at worst, by $45,266
(a July 2007 start). So the plan takes the forecast out. Extra Treasuries, bought in January 2028, are sized so all ten
are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing, the arithmetic an escrow verifier applies to a bond escrow and a condition

2026). The test still charges the 0.15% yearly fee of the Treasury-bill fund where waiting cash would sit, so waiting

**Item M.** Jerzy Neyman, who invented the confidence interval, wrote in 1937 that once a range has been worked out it
either holds its target or it does not: the probability is zero or one, and nobody knows which. So a "95%" label does
not give that one range a 95% chance, yet in a 2014 study 59% of 118 researchers endorsed the false reading "There is a
95% probability that the true mean lies" inside the range. Laura's 2031 range is the rare one known to hold. After half
the shares move into Treasuries before the meetings, both ends are Treasuries she owns: the top is her contribution and
the figure co-sponsors count, and the bottom is the part owned since January 2028. The two figures record how long each
part has been held, not a margin for error. One condition goes with them: each end is counted at what its funds pay if
cash waiting earns nothing, after their fees, with at least 0.5% of the funds' value to spare for other fund costs
(about 2% on today's figures); only if such costs used up that margin, and cash waiting really earned nothing, would the
contribution fall short of the top, and then by at most about $120 for each further 0.1%. So the range she states, once
both ends are owned, is certain. The percentages in the team's model belong to an earlier question, asked before the
move: which range will she be able to state. With world shares assumed to earn 7.5% a year, the stated top is at least
$100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures and at least $115,000 in about 57.

**Item R.** The case asks for "a high degree of certainty". The team's first plan (27 September 2026) held about
three-quarters of the money in shares to 2030, gliding to 40% in 2032, and on 1 January 2033 it would have bought all

gave it up once it thought about how a writer is paid (the premise in your summary), and went a step further than

any payment, with no money coming after January 2028 to close it; the median miss was $27,695 in 1 January 2033 money.
Bought into Treasuries first, the same two deposits leave no payment short from January 2028, once the first quarter or

**Item T.** By the plan's own premise, a writer's income can shrink in a slump, yet the plan waits on a second deposit
from that writer: $150,000 in January 2028. So the deposit looks like the plan's weak point. The order in which the
money is spent decides where a shortfall would land. The money Laura already has, the first $300,000, buys the ten
Treasuries in January 2027. The 2028 deposit is then spent in a fixed order: its first part, about $39,000 at today's
market rates, completes the ten and buys extra Treasuries sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing;
only what is left buys the Treasury fund for the bottom of her range, and then the shares. So at today's market rates
the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller (73.9%) and every payment would still meet that standard; only a
deposit more than about three-quarters smaller would leave them short of it. Yields often fall in a slump, and lower
yields make that first part dearer, yet even if yields fell to zero during 2027, after the ten are bought, it would cost
about $60,000 in January 2028, so a deposit about 60% smaller would still keep every payment at that standard. A deposit
that never comes, one of the plan's two named reasons to change course, leaves the ten $57,455 short of the $500,000
they owe, added across their ten dates, if cash waiting earns nothing. What shrinks is the building money: a deposit a
quarter smaller ($112,500) lowers the bottom she could state from $95,000 to $65,000. The plan adds no rule for a
smaller deposit; the same order simply runs.
