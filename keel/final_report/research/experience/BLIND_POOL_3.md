### E1: "A copout": the world fund against its strongest objection
- **Date (Sydney):** the bar written Wed 30 Sep; the objection Mon 5 Oct; both before any order.
- **What happened:** The plan's growth money sat in one fund of the whole world's shares (VT). On 30 Sep, before any
  number was run, the team wrote a bar for replacing it: switch only if an alternative raised the bad-case 2033 gift by
  $2,000, or narrowed its spread by a tenth, on every run. Of four alternatives tested, none moved the bad case by
  more than about $1,500. VT with 10% gold and 10% property trusts came closest: a spread ratio of 0.8991 against the
  0.90 bar, passing about four runs in five, half or more of it by cutting the good outcomes (30 Sep model, on an
  older design).
- **The other side:** on 5 Oct a teammate argued that one world fund was "a copout" and proposed picking stocks and
  ETFs as a normal team would; a Taiwan fund and gold were raised.
- **Decision:** the record: "We decided, in the end, to keep the one world fund (VT)."
- **What changed:** before, VT was a sensible default; after, the team had to say why it suited Laura. What it
  understands now: the 2031 top can be checked against public forecasts for world shares (fourteen of fifteen houses'
  give the same $115,000 top); a holding that needs no opinions keeps the IPS's two reasons to change; a Taiwan fund
  would concentrate what the building already carries (TSMC was 21.89% of it on 30 Sep 2026).
- **Explains:** the share fund (the top of the range).
- **Luck and the unknown:** which fund does better is unknown; the choice is worth about $800-$1,600 of Laura's
  flexibility, market luck $9,881-$41,258 either way (Sun 15 Nov model).
- **Detail:** that day's journal entry left "Outcome and reasons: [fill in]".

### E2: Buying it: from a plan to a portfolio
- **Date (Sydney):** Tue 29 Sep to Thu 8 Oct.
- **What happened:** Before any order, every bond price on the trading platform was checked against the official
  Treasury curve. Two sat about a full point of yield away (111 and 92 basis points; screen readings, 29-30 Sep). From
  then on every bond had to be within 0.25 points of the curve before an order, and the planned Feb-2038 bond was
  swapped for the May-2038 one. Wide after-hours spreads and an errored ticket made every order a limit order in
  market hours. Orders went in from 10:55 on Wed 7 Oct to fill at New York's open, 00:30 in
  Sydney; a teammate fell asleep waiting. During the session a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled, and
  the platform cancelled the May-2038 order on the spot, with no confirmation step. It was re-placed at 15:22.
- **The other side:** until then, any member could place or cancel orders.
- **Decision:** the same day: only the team leader places or cancels orders. The rule held to the freeze.
- **What changed:** before, the strategy was a list of assets; after, a procedure: prices are evidence to check, and
  an action that cannot be undone has one owner.
- **Explains:** how the plan is bought: the holdings and the implementation section.
- **Luck and the unknown:** every order filled by 8 Oct (US time); the funds filled about $800-900 below the previous
  closes, which the team's own review called luck.
- **Detail:** the record: "our time is freaking 12:30 AM that the market would open"; and on a price far off the
  curve, "An artificially low yield would mean immediately overpaying ... an artificially high yield would probably
  signal extreme underlying illiquidity".

### E3: A knife edge: what $300,000 could buy
- **Date (Sydney):** Mon 28 Sep to Tue 29 Sep, 13:03. Before any order.
- **What happened:** The plan's opening claim was that Laura's first $300,000 buys all ten $50,000 payments in
  Treasuries. Pricing the ten on every Treasury close of 2026 showed they cost more than $300,000 on 173 of the 185
  closes to 25 Sep, fitting only from 10 Sep, after long yields rose; on 25 Sep the room was $7,736 (register). Two
  independent pricings of the 28 Sep curve differed by $204; changing two conventions (the day count and the 1-3 month
  rates) reproduced the gap to the cent (that day's check).
- **The other side:** the plan's own headline, "fits inside $300,000".
- **Decision:** the headline was dropped. Certainty was defined by what is bought, and the 2028 deposit was given a
  first job: complete the ladder.
- **What changed:** the submitted IPS carries the rule: "Even if yields fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up
  being above $300,000, we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit." Through 6 Nov the ten cost under $300,000 on
  only 37 of 214 closes, all between 10 Sep and 3 Nov. On 6 Nov, when trading froze, they cost $301,544, so the rule
  is live: $2,388 of the 2028 deposit completes the ladder (register).
- **Explains:** the completion rule and the first use of the 2028 deposit.
- **Luck and the unknown:** the orders filled on 7-8 Oct, within two trading days of the year's cheapest close
  ($287,938 on 5 Oct, register). That was timing, not skill.
- **Detail:** two pricings $204 apart, and the reason found to the cent.

### E4: "Too timid": the plan priced from the other side
- **Date (Sydney):** Mon 28 Sep to Tue 29 Sep; priced again Thu 12 Nov.
- **What happened:** In a blind test, three readers each asked to rank six anonymous plans as Laura would all put this
  plan first, and all three called it too timid, "with only about 9% in stocks" (the record). The team tested smaller
  floors for the 2028 deposit, $150,000 down to $50,000, the rest in the world share fund. Each $25,000 less floor
  added about $2,000-3,000 to the middle outcome but cost about $9,000-10,000 in a bad market and about $15,000 in the
  worst historical case (29 Sep model, on a design since revised).
- **The other side:** the readers' verdict that the plan left Laura's money on the table.
- **Decision:** the floor stayed; "timid" was answered with the price of certainty, not more shares. The record, 1 Oct:
  "model check backed the $150k floor and 50/50 split".
- **What changed:** "timid" became a trade-off with a number on each side. What the team understands now (Thu 12 Nov
  model, re-run Sun 15 Nov): putting half, three quarters or all of what is left of the 2028 deposit into the world
  fund leaves Laura more in about 63 of 100 simulated futures, by a median $4,000, $8,000 or $12,000, but cuts the
  bottom she could name from $95,000 to $65,000, $30,000 or nothing, and leaves her contribution under $95,000 in 27,
  62 or 79 futures in 100, against 3.
- **Explains:** the floor and the bottom of the range. Today the floor pays $103,178, stated as a $95,000 bottom
  (register).
- **Luck and the unknown:** US shares since 1871 grew faster than the 7.5% assumed, flattering the share-heavy
  options on history.
- **Detail:** with a $150,000 floor Laura already owned 86% of her likely gift; with $100,000, 68% (29 Sep model).

### E5: The promise called certain was the weakest one in the plan
- **Date (Sydney):** Tue 27 Oct, 13:40 (model) and 19:21 (a check of the draft). Before the IPS.
- **What happened:** The belief that morning: read as a lender reads a company's debts (who is paid first), the ten
  payments stay whole in at least 99.5% of paths. The test said otherwise. The bonds pay interest years before the
  payments, and it must be reinvested; after the gift left in 2033, only Laura's half of the share fund stood behind a
  shortfall. A payment came up short after 2033 in 6.8% of paths, 8.4% when yields and shares fell together (27 Oct
  model, 200,000 paths). One Trading Note had named this risk for one bond; the test found it across the plan.
- **Stakes:** the case asks for the payments "with a high degree of certainty"; the draft now contradicted a Trading
  Note reflection already with the judges.
- **Decision:** borrow the coverage test loan funds use to protect their safest notes: before anything goes to the
  facility, money for any visible shortfall stays behind. Payments short fell to 0.01%; the price was the gift falling
  below the range's bottom in 2.9% of paths instead of 0.75% (27 Oct model).
- **What changed:** certainty was checked at the payments, not the range. The submitted IPS says none of the ten "will
  depend on a share price". On today's register the interest the ten must earn by 1 Jan 2033 is worth $93,941, nearly
  two payments, and $35,894 of extra Treasuries bought in January 2028 pay the ten even if cash waiting earns nothing.
- **Explains:** the order of claims and the first use of the 2028 deposit.
- **Luck and the unknown:** the 27 Oct figures are a model on a plan since revised.
- **Detail:** 6.8% short, against a belief of under 0.5%.

### E6: The first plan failed its own test
- **Date (Sydney):** Sun 27 Sep. Before any trade.
- **What happened:** The team's first plan put about three-quarters of the money in shares and trusted ten years of
  growth to cover the ten payments. Tested before any trade, it missed a payment in 3.2% of simulated markets, in 13.7%
  if the 2028 deposit came in at $75,000, and in 40.8% if it never came. A plan that bought the ten payments in
  Treasuries first never missed one. The share weight the team had argued over (50, 60 or 70% of the growth money)
  moved the median by about $2,000 ($205,000, $207,000, $209,000). (27 Sep model: J.P. Morgan's 6.70% compound for US
  large caps, the 25 Sep curve; it reproduces exactly on a re-run.)
- **The other side:** the first instinct, that ten years is long enough for shares to carry the payments.
- **Decision:** the plan was dropped before a single trade and rebuilt with the payments first. The record: "first
  model of our original plan ... We changed strategy." The submitted IPS gives a second reason for the same switch,
  from the same weekend: what a writer's income does in a recession.
- **What changed:** the order of claims became fixed: the payments, then the bottom of the range, then the top, then
  what Laura keeps. The team also learned to weigh a decision by what it changes: the weight argued over was worth
  about $2,000; payments first was worth the whole case.
- **Explains:** the order of claims in the recommendation.
- **Luck and the unknown:** the model's inputs and design differ from today's register; its figures are that day's.
- **Detail:** one simulated market in thirty; two in five if one deposit slipped.
