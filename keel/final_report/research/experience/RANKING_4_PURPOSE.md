## 1. The rule

**Who may appear.** The only test is whether the moment could authentically be a human experience, told so that the
point lands without saying whose work it rested on. It does not matter whether the work behind a moment was a
teammate's, Keel's or Claude's.

**What stays true.** Every moment happened. It is told without saying who did the work, and never with a false
attribution. For example, write "a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled", never "one of us clicked the
wrong row". Keel still invents no event, person or detail (I113, 5.1).

**Why each moment is there.** Every moment, and every detail inside it, has a stated purpose. Either it carries a
lesson that shaped the team's investing thinking or its recommendation (Wharton's Articulation criterion: "how
research, collaboration, disagreement, decision-making, uncertainty, and learning shaped its investment thinking and
final recommendation"), or it is a detail that earns its place. The 00:30 open is an example: it shows an evaluator
the real, unfiltered conditions in one clause. A moment that has no reason to be there is cut, however true it is.

**Few, and impressive.** The Articulation section serves the report and the win, not the record. Choose few moments,
and choose the ones a Wharton judge would find impressive. Because this is an investing competition, the best moment
is one where the team's investing erudition shows.

**What is out.** Out are:
- a strategy premised on what last year's team did;
- an idea passed off as an experience (her career as "a stock she can't sell" is an idea);
- trivia (that WInS never showed the second deposit);
- anything only an AI would plausibly do (catching the facility floor counted inside the operating reserve);
- a retelling of the Trading Notes Analysis (Wharton: "do not repeat").

**Four checks for each moment:**
1. Would it read as a human experience?
2. What is its purpose: which of Wharton's words does it serve, and which part of the recommendation does it explain?
3. Would a Wharton judge find it impressive?
4. Is it new beyond the TNA?

### Moment 2: our first plan failed its own test

- **Facts, dated.** The team's first plan, Sun 27 Sep (its notes, `keel/library/council_2026-09-27/team_notes_2026-09-27.md`),
  put about three-quarters in shares and trusted growth to cover the ten payments. It was tested before any trade:
  - it missed a payment in 3.2% of simulated markets;
  - it missed one in 13.7% if the 2028 deposit came in at $75,000;
  - it missed one in 40.8% if the deposit never came;
  - a plan that bought the ten in Treasuries first never missed one.

  These are figures from 27 Sep's model (`keel/library/verified_2026-09-27/strategy_mc.py`), always dated. Say "in our
  simulations", without counting paths. The plan was dropped before a single trade and rebuilt with payments first. The
  share weight the team had argued over (50, 60 or 70% of the growth money) moved the median by only about $2,000.
  The team's timeline: "first model of our original plan ... We changed strategy."
- **What changed.** Before, the team believed ten years was long enough for shares to carry the payments. The test
  showed the plan failing about once in thirty, and two times in five if one deposit slipped. After it, the order of
  claims became fixed: payments first, then the bottom of the range, then the top, then what she keeps. The team also
  learned to weigh a decision by what it changes. The weight argued over was worth about $2,000. Payments first was
  worth the whole case.
- **The erudition it carries.** Use one or two items, in the team's own voice.
  - The first instinct, "over ten years shares will win", is the time-diversification fallacy. Samuelson (1963): a man
    who refuses one favourable bet cannot rationally accept a long run of them (card Q39-016).
  - Bodie (1995): the cost of insuring shares against trailing the risk-free bond rises with the horizon, to about 25%
    of the stake at ten years at 20% volatility (Q39-017).
  - A fixed dollar promise belongs in what Leibowitz called a dedicated portfolio: "a least-risk asset, minimizing the
    risks involved in fulfilling a large class of nominal-dollar liabilities" (Q1c-029).
  - Roy's (1952) safety first: make the disaster unlikely first, then seek return (H3-022).
- **Guard.** The career idea stays out of this moment. It is an idea, and it belongs in 2.3.

### Moment 3: buying it

- **Facts, dated.**
  - *Prices.* Before any order (Tue 29 to Wed 30 Sep), every WInS bond price was checked against the official Treasury
    curve. Two prices sat about a full point of yield away (the Feb-2036 bond 111 basis points off, the Feb-2038 bond

  - *Order tickets.* Orders are entered in $1,000-face units. After-hours spreads on the iBond funds were wide, and one
    ticket errored, so the team used limit orders in market hours (W-38).
  - *The night of the orders.* Orders went in from 10:55 on Wed 7 Oct to fill at New York's open, which fell at 00:30 in
    Sydney. One of us fell asleep waiting. The team's journal: "our time is freaking 12:30 AM that the market would
    open".
  - *The mis-click.* During the session a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled. WInS cancelled the
    May-2038 order on the spot, with no confirmation step. It was re-placed at 15:22. The same day the team made a
    rule: only the team leader places or cancels orders (W-02; `competition/COMPETITION.md`).
  - *The fills.* Every order filled by 8 Oct (US time). The funds filled about $800-900 below the previous closes, which
    the team's own review called luck. If told at all, it is told as luck.
- **The team's own words.** The Experience tab's "Execution Alpha" paragraph: "An artificially low yield would mean
  immediately overpaying ... an artificially high yield would probably signal extreme underlying illiquidity". Avoid
  that paragraph's word "optimal".
- **What changed.** Before, the strategy was a list of assets. The stale prices, the wide spreads and a cancel with no
  confirmation step turned it into a procedure. Prices became evidence to check, orders were set ahead of an open the
  team would sleep through, and any action that cannot be undone got one owner.
- **The erudition it carries.** Use one item.
  - Perold (1988): implementation shortfall, the gap between the paper portfolio at decision prices and the one
    actually bought (Q17-001).
  - If room, Citibank and Revlon (11 Aug 2020). Citibank meant to send a $7.8 million interest payment and wired nearly

    approval, three people, missed it (US Court of Appeals, 2nd Cir., 8 Sep 2022). The same lesson at the top of
    finance: an irreversible action needs the interface's default checked and an owner named.
- **Guards.**
  - Never say that a teammate made the wrong click; the record says the click was automated. Say what happened, not who
    did it.
  - One WInS note already says a price "was consistent with the Treasury yield curve" (Nov-2040). Tell the rule and the
    lesson, not the price analysis. The IBTR choice and its cost stay in 2.2.
  - The 00:30 detail is one clause at most.

## 3. The world fund: the strongest case

The question the moment answers: why would a team that could pick anything keep one fund of the whole world's shares?
The reasons are ranked by how much they belong to Laura's case. Sources are verified at source on Sun 15 Nov unless
marked; full APA elements are in `cards/Q46_world_fund_case_and_moments.md`.

1. **A range co-sponsors can check needs a forecast that exists.** Because the shares are the world market, the 2031
   top can be tested against fifteen houses' published forecasts for world shares, and fourteen of them give the same
   $115,000 top (NUMBERS 8p). No house publishes a forecast for a basket a team picked, so its expected return would
   be the team's belief. Wharton asks for "a credible dollar range". This is Keel's reasoning on NUMBERS 8p, and it is
   the most case-specific reason.
2. **The one holding allowed to be wrong should be wrong only with the market.** Risk that pays a premium is the
   market's. Firm-specific risk pays none and can be diversified away (Markowitz, 1952; Sharpe, 1964). Concentration
   costs more than it looks, because single-stock returns are skewed:
   - 57.4% of US stocks lost to one-month Treasury bills over their lives, and 4% of companies (1,092) created all the
     market's net wealth, 1926-2016 (Bessembinder, 2018);
   - worldwide, 55.2% of US and 57.4% of non-US stocks lost to bills, 1990-2020, and 2.4% of 63,785 firms created all
     US$75.7 trillion of net wealth (Bessembinder, Chen, Choi & Wei, 2023).

   A few picks will most likely miss the few winners. VT's 10,185 stocks cannot miss them (fact sheet, 30 Sep 2026).
3. **The arithmetic, and the record.** Sharpe (1991): before costs, the average actively managed dollar earns the
   market's return, and after costs it earns less, "for any time period". To 30 Jun 2026, 94.71% of global equity funds
   trailed the S&P World over 15 years (SPIVA U.S. Mid-Year 2026, Report 6a). Fama and French (2010): few funds earn
   enough to cover their costs. Say this in one sentence, as evidence; the TNA already made the claim.
4. **A holding that needs no opinions keeps the IPS's promise.** IPS l.17 gives Laura only two reasons to change the
   plan. A fund held at market weights never drifts from its own mix and needs no decision until the 2031 move (card
   Q6d row 29). A basket of picks needs a decision each time one doubles or halves. Barber and Odean (2000) studied
   66,465 households, 1991-1996: those that traded most earned 11.4% a year against the market's 17.9%, and they put
   it down to overconfidence. Ellis (1975): when the game is a loser's game, you win by avoiding errors.
5. **Gold answers a risk the payments do not have.** The payments are fixed in dollars, and Treasuries match them (TNA
   l.7).
   - Gold's real return since 1900 is about 1.3% a year in US dollars, and it fell in 13 of the 28 years when
     inflation exceeded 3% (Dimson, Marsh & Staunton, UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2026, summary edition).
   - Erb and Harvey (2013): gold is an unreliable inflation hedge over horizons up to 20 years.
   - The shares' job is the top of the range (IPS l.15), and in the test gold with property narrowed the range largely
     by lowering its top.
6. **A Taiwan fund concentrates what the building already carries.**
   - EWT on 30 Sep 2026 (iShares fact sheet): TSMC 21.89%, information technology 75.26%, top ten 49.31%; three-year
     standard deviation 24.88%; beta 1.36; fee 0.59%, against VT's 0.06%. VT already holds Taiwan at 3.5% (Q6d-021).
   - Any Taiwan-dollar hedge it gives is small next to its share risk (H9-104, H9-105). The home-currency hedge also
     fails in the data (Cooper and Kaplanis, via Q39-022).
   - The event most likely to stop a building in Taiwan would hit a Taiwan fund at the same moment. This is Keel's
     inference, on the IPS's own logic: do not hold what moves with what Laura already has at stake.
   - EWT had risen 86.65% in the year to 30 Sep 2026 (same fact sheet). That is a reason the idea was tempting, not a
     forecast. Use it only as context, if at all.
7. **The decision was smaller than the argument.** Which shares to hold is worth about $800-$1,600 of her
   flexibility over five years. Market luck is worth $9,881-$41,258 either way, 1 in 20 each side (Q6d row 30).
   - Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986): investment policy explained 93.6% of the variation in 91 large pension plans'
     returns over time.
   - Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) make it precise: about 90% across time, about 40% across funds, and about 100% of the
     level.
   - The decision that mattered was how much went into shares at all, and the IPS made it.
8. **The bar came before the numbers.** "Close" is not passing. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that with hundreds of
   factors tried, a new one needs a t-statistic above 3.0, and that "most claimed research findings in financial
   economics are likely false". The every-run clause is the team's small version of the same discipline: a result that
   passes four times in five is noise until it passes every time.

**What to say in 3f, about 220 words.** The objection, the bar set in advance, and how close gold came. Then reasons 1,
4 and 6, and either 2 or 7 (one number). End on what the team understands now: VT is held for Laura's reasons, not as
a default. The rest go to 2.3 (reason 2's one number fits its "Conventional diversification lives in VT" line), to
Table 3's basis column (reason 4), and to the References.
