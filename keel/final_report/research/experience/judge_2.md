# Judge 2: the admissions editor's read

Lens: a reader of personal statements who has seen a thousand "we learned so much" essays. What I believe is a
specific moment where something went wrong, someone said a blunt thing, and the plan changed because of it. What I
distrust is a story that is all process, all numbers, or too neat. Scores are in `judge_2.csv` (W Wharton fit, V vivid,
C credible and plain, S strategy link; 1 to 10 each). Only 65 of 412 single scores are above 7.

Most of the pool fails the same way: a real finding buried in percentages, internal labels (K-02, MODEL, mock trial,
rooms) and a "we" that keeps changing who it means. The events that survive are the ones a person could tell across a
dinner table.

## Top 12, in order

1. **X076** The team ran its own 75%-shares plan through the model, saw it miss a payment two times in five if the 2028 deposit failed, and dropped it before a single trade. Real change, owned as a mistake, and it is the root of every holding.
2. **X084** Four returning members, a 54% loss on FMC and a year of silence from the judges. An honest "before" that makes every later rule (permitted list, one world fund, client first) read as learned rather than recited.
3. **X082** An experienced outside reader says the IPS cannot make it and gives no reasons; the team treats it as a diagnosis, not an insult. "Laura is barely in it" is the line a reader keeps.
4. **X063** "She doesn't need any rehab." A blunt teammate kills the draft's central image and forces the real question, what is new in the plan itself. Funny, human, and it changed the work.
5. **X026** The team's own October trade note predicted the ladder would cross $300,000; it did, and that note was not one of the three the judges read. Rueful, specific, and it explains the 2028 completion rule.
6. **X103** On the last day the team set the drafts aside and wrote to Laura in its own words. The strongest ending in the pool, and the letter's closing lines ("You take risks on the page...") are genuinely good.
7. **X100** A teammate calls the world fund a "copout", gold gets a fair hearing against a bar written before any number was run, and fails by 0.001. Disagreement settled by a rule, not by rank.
8. **X006** $470,249 in August 2020 against $289,119 on 28 Sep 2026. One comparison that turns "buy the payments first" from timidity into timing.
9. **X003** Wharton's email takes away the floor on the day of the first orders; the team rebuilds on listed instruments and is untouched when TIPS and STRIPS are banned two weeks later. A clean response to a challenge.
10. **X018** The career-as-a-stock idea was in the first client notes and took six weeks to become the opening line. Believable: good ideas usually sit unused before someone finds the words.
11. **X099** The draft's best line was false by the team's own model; the replacement, "The shares can help a cheque, never hurt one", is shorter and true. A tidy honest-mistake story with a payoff.
12. **X001** After submitting, the team tested its own central claim against 2008-09 and 2020 and found it only partly holds (graphic novels up 29% the year Laura's comic went viral). The kind of honesty an essay reader trusts.

Near misses: **X092** is the most human moment in the pool (orders placed from Sydney, a teammate asleep before the
00:30 open, "our time is freaking 12:30 AM"), but the phrase "automated browser click" is where a reader will stop
believing it. **X044** ("IBTN" turned out to be INSCORP Inc.), **X013** (a bond fund never matures) and **X098** (a
"rule" traced back to a 2015-16 guidebook) are all credible and plain, just smaller.

## Three to avoid

1. **X081** (a thousand pieces read in one night). Batches saved every seventy minutes until 05:43, and a teammate's essays credited to the wrong teammate for days. No reader believes six students did this, and once one story reads as impossible the reader starts doubting the true ones next to it.
2. **X069** (a vote five minutes after a message nobody read). The team's messages "blatantly ignored", 13 of 33 instructions not applied, a 5-1 vote on a plan already cancelled. It shows a team not listening to itself, and a reader cannot tell who "we" and "the team" are.
3. **X021** (13 edits rejected on deadline night). One of the rejected edits flagged calling inflation risk "nonexistent" against the case's own words. Telling it points judges straight at an uncorrected error in a piece they already hold, and makes the leader look as if he chose tone over truth. X017, its twin, carries the same risk more mildly.
