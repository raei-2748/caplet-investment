# Blind re-rank, pool 3: reader 3

Lens: a portfolio manager who also edits nonfiction. I asked of each event two things: would a practitioner nod, and
can it be told in plain sentences without a narrator dressing it up. Scored only from the sheets in BLIND_POOL_3.md,
against RERANK_RUBRIC.md, with the submitted IPS and Trading Notes Analysis beside me for criterion E. Each criterion
was scored on its own. No event is dated after the IPS, so the cap on A did not bite (E4's 12 Nov re-pricing is a later
check on a 29 Sep decision, not a redesign).

## E1: "A copout": the world fund against its strongest objection

Gate
- Human: pass. A teammate calling the plan a copout, and a bar written before any number was run, are things a team does.
- Purpose: pass. It explains the share fund and so the top of the range.
- Impressive: pass. A switching bar set before the test, and a near miss read correctly (half the gain came from
  cutting the good outcomes), is how a practitioner reviews an asset.
- New: pass. Trading Note 3 defends VT; it says nothing of the bar, the four alternatives or the objection.

Scores
- A. Influence: 4. Names the part it tested and kept (the share fund, the top), with the $115,000 top at the end; the
  before and after are states of reasoning more than of the plan.
- B. Reasoning: 5. Options rejected (stock picks, a Taiwan fund, gold and property), the evidence that decided (no
  alternative moved the bad case more than about $1,500; 0.8991 against a 0.90 bar), and what was given up.
- C. Disagreement: 4. The only in-team dissent in the pool, put fairly enough, and partly settled by a test written in
  advance; but the 5 Oct objection itself ends in "we decided, in the end" and a journal left "[fill in]".
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 5. Says what is unknown (which fund wins) and sizes decision against luck: the choice is
  worth $800-$1,600, the market $9,881-$41,258 either way (Sun 15 Nov model).
- E. New and checkable: 4. Dated and sourced, and the bar and objection are new; its conclusion is Trading Note 3's,
  so a careless telling would re-argue that note.

The one detail that carries it: the bar was written on 30 Sep before any run, and the closest challenger scored 0.8991
against it, a miss by a thousandth.

What would make it corny: turning "copout" into a closing reversal ("our copout was our conviction"), or repeating
Trading Note 3's "vain illusion" line as the moral.

## E2: Buying it: from a plan to a portfolio

Gate
- Human: pass. A 00:30 open, a teammate asleep, a click on the wrong row as the page scrolled: plainly human.
- Purpose: pass, narrowly. It explains how the plan was bought and the implementation section, not what Laura was told.
- Impressive: pass. Checking every bond quote against the official curve, finding two about a point off (111 and 92
  basis points), and setting a 0.25-point tolerance is pre-trade price checking a desk would respect.
- New: pass. The Trading Notes say nothing about the curve check, the order rules or the mis-click.

Scores
- A. Influence: 3. One holding changed (Feb-2038 swapped for May-2038) and a procedure was set, but none of the
  recommendation's figures moved; the main change is process.
- B. Reasoning: 4. The rejected options (the Feb-2038 bond, after-hours market orders, anyone placing orders) and the
  evidence are there, with the team's own two-sided reason on off-curve prices; the cost of one owner is not stated.
- C. Disagreement: 2. "Any member could place or cancel orders" is a prior arrangement, not a second view held by anyone.
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 4. A success told with luck: the funds filled $800-900 below the previous closes, which
  the team's own review called luck; nothing is left open.
- E. New and checkable: 5. Dated to the minute, quoted from the record, clear to an outsider, and wholly absent from the
  Trading Notes and IPS.

The one detail that carries it: the click that landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled, cancelling the May-2038
order with no confirmation step; it was re-placed at 15:22.

What would make it corny: playing the sleeping teammate and "12:30 AM" for laughs, a "trial by fire" arc, or a
slogan for the rule ("one click, one owner").

## E3: A knife edge: what $300,000 could buy

Gate
- Human: pass. Pricing the ten payments on every close of the year and reconciling two pricings is analyst work a
  student can do.
- Purpose: pass. It explains the completion rule and the first job of the 2028 deposit.
- Impressive: pass. Dropping your own headline because it was true on only 12 of 185 closes is the discipline a
  practitioner wants.
- New: pass. The IPS states the rule; the sheet supplies why, which the IPS does not.

Scores
- A. Influence: 5. Names the part changed (the headline dropped, the completion rule added), with register figures at
  both ends: $7,736 of room on 25 Sep, $301,544 at the freeze, $2,388 of the 2028 deposit.
- B. Reasoning: 4. The rejected option is a claim rather than an alternative plan; the evidence (173 of 185 closes) is
  decisive; the cost ($2,388 of the 2028 deposit) is there but implied.
- C. Disagreement: 2. The other side is the plan's own headline; two pricings $204 apart is a reconciliation, not two
  people's positions.
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 5. Believed it fitted, tested it on every close, and says plainly that filling within two
  trading days of the year's cheapest close ($287,938 on 5 Oct) was timing, not skill.
- E. New and checkable: 4. Dated and on the register; it quotes the IPS's rule, and the $204 reconciliation needs inside
  knowledge (day counts, 1-3 month rates) to follow.

The one detail that carries it: the ten payments cost more than $300,000 on 173 of the 185 closes to 25 Sep.

What would make it corny: leaning on "knife edge" as an image, crediting the near-cheapest fill as good judgment, or
ending on "never trust a headline".

## E4: "Too timid": the plan priced from the other side

Gate
- Human: pass. Asking three readers to rank six anonymous plans as Laura would is something a team can do; the point
  lands without saying who they were.
- Purpose: pass. It explains the floor and the bottom of the range.
- Impressive: pass. "Too timid" is the objection every liability-matched portfolio meets; answering it with the price on
  both sides is what a practitioner would do.
- New: pass. Neither the IPS nor the Trading Notes mention the verdict or price the trade-off.

Scores
- A. Influence: 5. Names the part tested and kept (the floor, the bottom), before and after: 29 Sep model to the
  register's $103,178 floor stated as a $95,000 bottom.
- B. Reasoning: 5. Smaller floors rejected; each $25,000 less floor adds about $2,000-3,000 in the middle and costs about
  $9,000-10,000 in a bad market and about $15,000 in the worst historical case; what Laura gives up is stated.
- C. Disagreement: 4. An outside verdict, but not thin: the losing side is put at its strongest (more money in about 63
  of 100 futures, by a median $4,000-$12,000) and settled by a test; it is outside the team rather than within it.
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 5. Admits the share-heavy options win more often and that US shares since 1871 grew
  faster than the 7.5% assumed, so history flatters the side the team rejected.
- E. New and checkable: 4. Dated, sourced and new; but the floor moves from $150,000 (29 Sep) to $103,178 (register)
  without the sheet saying why, which an outsider will trip on.

The one detail that carries it: all three readers put the plan first, and all three called it too timid, "with only
about 9% in stocks".

What would make it corny: a reversal line ("timid was the bravest choice"), or spelling out "certainty has a price"
after the numbers have already said it.

## E5: The promise called certain was the weakest one in the plan

Gate
- Human: pass. A model run at 13:40 and a check of the draft at 19:21 are a team's working day; the point lands without
  saying whose.
- Purpose: pass. It explains the order of claims and the first use of the 2028 deposit.
- Impressive: pass. Finding reinvestment risk inside a "matched" ladder, and fixing it with the coverage test loan funds
  use, would impress any fixed-income practitioner.
- New: pass. Trading Note 1 named the risk for one bond; this finds it across the plan.

Scores
- A. Influence: 5. Names what changed (the order of claims, money held back before the facility), with figures at both
  ends: 6.8% short to 0.01% (27 Oct model), and $93,941 of interest covered by $35,894 of extra Treasuries (register).
- B. Reasoning: 5. The lender's reading rejected on evidence (6.8%, 8.4% when yields and shares fell together), and the
  price stated: the gift below the bottom in 2.9% of paths instead of 0.75%.
- C. Disagreement: 2. A belief overturned by a test, with no second position held by anyone.
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 5. The cleanest belief-against-test in the pool (under 0.5% believed, 6.8% found), and
  the fix holds even if cash waiting earns nothing.
- E. New and checkable: 4. Dated to the hour and extends Trading Note 1; "read as a lender reads" and the coverage test
  need a sentence of explanation for an outsider.

The one detail that carries it: 6.8% of 200,000 paths came up short, the same day the team believed under 0.5%.

What would make it corny: using the title's irony as the closing twist, a moral such as "certainty must be earned", or
burying it in loan-fund jargon.

## E6: The first plan failed its own test

Gate
- Human: pass. A first plan heavy in shares, tested and dropped before a trade, is a plainly human weekend.
- Purpose: pass. It explains the order of claims.
- Impressive: pass, narrowly. A 3.2% miss is not dramatic, but weighing the argued-over weight ($2,000) against the
  switch (the whole case) is a sharp lesson.
- New: pass. The Trading Notes do not tell it; the IPS's opening does tell the same switch, with another reason.

Scores
- A. Influence: 5. It changed the whole recommendation, from shares carrying the payments to payments first, with that
  day's model figures (no register figure exists for the old plan).
- B. Reasoning: 4. The rejected plan and the evidence (3.2%, 13.7%, 40.8% missed) are clear; what the switch cost Laura
  in growth is not stated.
- C. Disagreement: 3. The team argued over 50, 60 or 70% and a test showed it barely mattered; but no positions or
  roles are shown, and the other side is the team's own first instinct.
- D. Uncertainty and learning: 4. Specific learning (weigh a decision by what it changes), little on what remains
  unknown.
- E. New and checkable: 3. The arc repeats the IPS's first paragraph ("naturally, that was our plan, until ..."); the
  numbers are new.

The one detail that carries it: the share weight the team argued over moved the median by about $2,000 ($205,000,
$207,000, $209,000).

What would make it corny: a "journey" from bold to careful, "we learned humility", or retelling the IPS's opening.

## My top four, in order

1. E4: the most practitioner-grade event in the pool, a fair outside critique answered with a price on both sides and
   an honest admission that history flatters the side rejected.
2. E1: the only dissent inside the team, a bar written before the numbers, and the clearest sizing of decision against
   luck.
3. E5: a belief tested and found wrong by a factor of about thirteen, fixed with a borrowed professional tool, with its
   price stated.
4. E3: the plan's own headline dropped on evidence from 173 of 185 closes, with the lucky timing of the fills called
   timing.

## The set I would pick

E4, E5 and E1, with E2 as the fourth if there is room.

The three cover the recommendation from top to bottom without overlap: E5 the payments, E4 the bottom of the range, E1
the top. C is covered twice at 4 (E4 outside the team, E1 inside it). The luck rule is met by E1 (a decision kept after
the dated 30 Sep test, with market luck sized at $9,881-$41,258 against a choice worth $800-$1,600). None retells the
Trading Notes, though E1 must be told through the bar and the objection, not through Trading Note 3's argument for VT.

I would take E2 over E3 as the fourth even though E3 scores higher. E3 and E5 both land on the first $38,282 of the
2028 deposit (E3 the $2,388 completion, E5 the $35,894 extra Treasuries), so together they spend half the reflection on
one tranche, and E3's rule is already in the IPS. E2 is the only event about doing rather than modelling, it covers the
implementation section, and it carries its own luck line. If the lead prefers three events in 600 words, drop E2 first.

## What separates the top from the bottom

The top events each set the plan against its strongest opposite and put a figure on both sides: readers who ranked it
first and still called it timid, a teammate who called the core holding a copout, the team's own belief that the
payments were safe. Each then says what was luck or what is still unknown, so the learning is in the numbers and needs
no narrator. The bottom events are good stories missing one of those parts. E2 is the most vivid and the most checkable,
but it changed how the plan was bought, not what Laura was told, and no one disagreed. E6 changed everything, but the
IPS already opens with that change, and no second view is shown. E3 is rigorous but its opponent is a headline, not a
person. As an editor I also noticed that the top three carry their own turn (first and timid; 0.8991 against 0.90;
under 0.5% against 6.8%), so a writer can state the facts and stop; the bottom three tempt a writer to add the turn
with a kicker.
