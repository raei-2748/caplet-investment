### S-06: Not a protected note: the floor named for what it is
- Date (Sydney): Fri 13 Nov, morning
- Origin: KEEL
- What happened: Since late October the notes had described the plan's 2028 money as a capital-protected note built without a bank: a Treasury floor plus shares. Two research cards read the floors that held and the floors that broke: 1987's portfolio insurance, capital-protected notes, target-date funds in 2008, Orange County, the 2022 gilt crisis, Silicon Valley Bank. Each one that broke needed a sale at a moment its owner did not choose. In Perold and Sharpe's 1988 classification the plan is not portfolio insurance or CPPI but the plainest member, buy-and-hold: a floor bought once in Treasuries, shares that are only the remainder, and one dated move written down in advance. And it is not capital-protected: the share fund ($29,829) costs more than the floor's expected interest ($21,289), so if shares went to zero Laura would get back $103,178 on $111,718.
- Stakes: A bond reader hears "capital-protected" and "portfolio insurance" as specific products with known failures; a wrong name costs more than a missing fact.
- Decision or response: The report calls the plan buy-and-hold in Perold and Sharpe's terms and never CPPI, portfolio insurance, "protected" or "guaranteed"; the 2031 move is named as a reset fixed by date in the IPS.
- What changed afterwards: The word list and the plan changed the same morning, and the floors lesson entered the argument: a floor holds when its owner holds what pays it to the date it is owed, with no outside flow able to force a sale.
- Vivid detail: At a multiplier of one, shares must fall 100% before the floor is touched: with the share fund at zero, the floor's $103,178 is untouched.
- Strand: research and decision process
