## Best ten in my files for Caplet's FINAL plan

1. **Bought deal vs best efforts (row 1).** FINAL's sentence "whatever figure you choose is money you already own" is the line underwriters draw between a bought deal, where the bank owns the shares before it names the price, and best efforts, where it promises nothing. The aha: Caplet's 2031 range is an underwriting, not a forecast, and the kept half of the shares is the best-efforts part. Few teams will turn an investment-banking term into a rule for a personal promise.
2. **Riskless needs a date, and also nothing to reinvest (rows 2 and 4).** Shiller says a bond is riskless only when its maturity matches the date. Damodaran (P1 S108) adds a second test: no reinvestment. The aha: Caplet never calls the ladder "risk-free". It shows which test the ladder passes (no default, barring the US) and which it had to work for (reinvestment, the 10.5-month gap that TNA note 1 already names). The weakness becomes the textbook definition, applied.
3. **Two budgets (row 22).** Every US state must balance its operating budget from money it has, while capital projects may wait on growth. The case already splits Laura's money into ten operating payments and a facility contribution. The aha: Caplet's design is ordinary public-finance practice, in the language of co-sponsors and governments. (Pair it honestly with the COMPLETE2028 caveat.)
4. **Debt design run backwards (row 7).** CFOs match debt to assets on four lines: duration, currency, inflation and cyclicality. Caplet does the same with Laura's fixed liabilities. The cyclicality line is FINAL's opening idea about her career, and the one line that isn't matched (the Taiwan dollar) is the reason half the shares stay unpromised. One four-row table shows the whole plan.
5. **Norway's oil and the Ford employee (rows 89 and 90).** FINAL's "your career is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell" has a sovereign precedent: Norway held about 70% in oil and resisted hedging it. Mullainathan's experiment showed only 40% of advisers told a client to diversify out of his employer's stock. The aha: Caplet said what most advisers don't.
6. **Financial flexibility, Proposition 5 (row 91, with Disney in row 92).** A figure said to co-sponsors behaves like debt, and firms facing an uncertain future take on less debt. The aha: "half of the value of the shares will be promised to no one" gets a capital-structure reason, not just caution. Almost nothing else in these files supports HALFKEPT.
7. **Swensen in Shiller's classroom (rows 13, 65, 14, 15).** Yale's own endowment chief says that in a panic "the only diversification that would matter" is Treasuries. Barron's in 2008 found colleges "scaling back building projects" first, which is exactly the failure Caplet's floor prevents. His "huge opportunity cost" objection can be voiced and answered: an endowment lasts "in perpetuity", while Laura's payments end in 2042.
8. **Intuitive 90% ranges are too narrow (row 118).** The case asks for a range with a confidence level. Shiller's Yale class landed only 10-80% of answers inside ranges they were "90% sure" of. The aha: Caplet's bottom is owned, not estimated. The team could run Shiller's three questions on itself and report the result, which also serves the Articulation criterion.
9. **"Portfolio insurance wasn't really insurance" (rows 96-98).** Rules triggered by prices can be fired by a crash, a volatility spike that "came right back down", or a fake press release. The aha: this is the reason behind FINAL's "a fall in the market is not one of them". A missed deposit or a Treasury default can't be faked.
10. **Zaibatsu bonds against nominal-for-nominal (rows 155 and 154).** The strongest case against government bonds: Japan's Zaibatsu were forced into yen bonds, and hyperinflation wiped them out. Caplet can face it and answer it with the case's own facts. Laura's payments are fixed nominal dollars, so nominal Treasuries match them; the facility's real costs (Taiwan dollars, materials) get a real asset, shares. That matches TNA note 1 ("we don't need TIPS").

Close runners: ten-in-a-row arithmetic (row 10: 0.95^10 is about 0.60), dividends are sticky (row 141), defined benefit vs defined contribution (row 19), the forward rate and break-even for the 2028 Treasury fund (row 51), the one-page register (row 159), Basel's countercyclical buffer for MOVE2031 (row 108), and Speenhamland (row 67).
