## Best ten in my files for Caplet's FINAL plan

1. **Taiwan's grants are annual and cuttable (row 116).** The Ministry of Culture's artist-village subsidy "depends on the Legislative Yuan budget review", may be reduced or stopped, and won't pay twice for the same thing. The aha: the case's rule that the ten payments may not lean on grants is also how Taiwan's own system works. Laura's prepaid decade is the stable base that grants sit on top of. Few teams will read Taiwan's subsidy rules.
2. **The reserve is its own LDROM (rows 34-35).** US public pensions must now report what their promises would cost at low-default-risk bond yields. Laura's reserve actually holds those bonds, so her liability and that measure are the same figure, and the funding ratio is one she can check herself. The aha: a regulator's "what if" is her plan's daily reality.
3. **The 2-point fall is the median, so price for it (rows 65 and 36).** Since 1953, when the 10-year started where it is now, reinvestment rates over Laura's window fell a median 2 points. Sizing the ladder the way bond escrows are sized, "without reinvestment", with the 2028 deposit covering the gap, makes "none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price" literally true. The aha: the TNA's named weak point gets an honest normal-case price. (Check the $306,663 caveat in row 36 first.)
4. **The 2031 move is a "planned adjustment", and the two reasons are the no-redesign rule (rows 93-94).** Guide p.5 allows "planned adjustments as funding dates approach" and bans redesign after results. Last year Westminster was praised for pivoting. The aha: FINAL's discipline is exactly what this year's Guide asks for.
5. **Certainty as how often and how much (row 33).** Cambridge Associates' "shortfall risk" and "shortfall severity" give a definition of "high degree of certainty" that a statistics graduate respects: the chance of any shortfall, its average size and the worst 1 in 100, in each model world. That answers Case p.3 directly.
6. **Arts giving fell 10.1% in 2008 (row 13).** All giving fell 2.4% that year, inflation-adjusted. FINAL's line "co-sponsors cut their budgets" becomes a number, specific to an arts residency, in the same years shares fall and advances shrink.
7. **Owned money passes funders' tests (rows 131, 129, 134).** Kresge counted written pledges toward a goal. Capital campaigns secure 65-80% before going public. The Royal Ontario Museum's $23M of unpaid pledges shows why money already paid in matters. The aha: Laura's owned floor is what a foundation's readiness test looks for. Present it as an analogy, not a rule fundraisers follow.
8. **Set in Stone (rows 95-96).** Arts buildings ran 46-92% over their first budgets. Producing theatres looked better (about 19%) only because they announced late. That supports both halves of FINAL: keep half the shares unpromised, and name a figure only once it is owned.
9. **The CFA Asset Manager Code disclosure table (row 149).** Wharton's rules tell teams to follow this code, and few teams will cite it. One table covering fees, risk factors (a US default, NAV payouts, reinvestment), valuation and risk processes meets its "truthful, accurate, complete, and understandable" standard.
10. **Bamboo Curtain (row 117).** A Taiwan residency that ran mostly on its founder's own resources stopped operating at the end of the year she died. State no cause. The aha: paying the ten payments in advance means the first decade doesn't depend on Laura's later income or her yearly decisions. That is the career-as-stock idea seen from the residency's side.
