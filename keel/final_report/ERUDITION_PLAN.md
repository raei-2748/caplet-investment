### 3c. Six fusions that are ours

These are the places where two or three borrowed ideas combine into something no single source has. Each one is a
paragraph in §2 of the report, with its test.
1. **The note without the option** (rows 3, 4, 12). Floor plus surplus, with the option swapped for shares, funded
   only by the floor's own interest, and timed around Laura's income risk.
2. **Hold it, then say it** (rows 15, 17). The promised half moves into Treasuries before Laura names the range, so a
   number she says is already owned. Fundraisers' "quiet phase" (40-60% raised before a goal is public) is the same
   rule from another field (`IDEA_BANK.md` #113).
3. **Sized as if nothing is reinvested, handed back when not needed** (rows 2, 18, 19). The escrow's sizing rule plus
   the clearing house's mark-to-market, aimed at the ladder's one weak point.
4. **The owner's money goes first** (rows 13, 18). Laura's spare shares absorb a shortfall before the gift does, and
   the gift before a cheque: a capital structure with the ten cheques as the senior tranche.
5. **Zero-order release** (rows 6, 7). Extended-release tablets deliver a constant dose; fixed-dollar cheques are a
   constant draw, which is exactly what sequence risk punishes. The motif does investing work here, and the maths
   matches: a tablet's mean dissolution time is the same first moment as a bond's Macaulay duration (`IDEA_BANK.md`
   #17; Costa et al. 2003).
6. **One storm, three holes** (rows 12, 2, 22). A deflationary slump makes the ladder dearer, widens the reinvestment
   gap and shrinks Laura's 2028 income together, so the defences aren't independent. The plan is built so that only
   the gift feels it (`R07_synergies/FUSIONS.md` A).

### A. ★ The price of certainty (why buy the cheques now)
- **What it shows.** On the 22 Oct curve the ten cheques cost $298,861 on 1 Jan 2027, so $300,000 just covers them.
  That is rare. $300,000 could have bought all ten in only one month in four since 1871, and in no month from mid-2007
  until this autumn; at the 2020 low they cost $469,000, more than both deposits (M1). Across 2026 the ladder cost
  more than $300,000 on 173 of 185 trading days and fitted under it only from 10 Sep (28 Sep check, on the old STRIPS ladder: re-run on the WInS ladder before quoting). A one-line
  rule (about $290 per 0.01% move in yields) predicted the cost on every close within $652 (L-01). Caplet's own
  3 Nov idea adds the three prices of the same cheques: $500,000 added up, about $298,000 as Treasuries, about
  $250,000 if priced at 7.0% a year from shares ($239,000 at the IPS's 7.5%); the gap is a return the plan refuses to spend before it's earned.
- **Method.** Treasury par curve bootstrapped to zero rates; history uses each month's 10-year yield since 1871.
- **Where.** §3 (why the reserve is bought in 2027), §2 row 11, §10 (the October fills happened in that window).
- **Why it reads as erudite.** It turns "we chose safety" into timing: certainty was on sale, and we bought it.
- **Read.** Macaulay 1938; US Treasury daily par curve; Shiller online data; Fama & Bliss 1987 (UNVERIFIED) on
  forward rates.
- **Files.** `../models/` (M1, chart `charts/c1_price_of_certainty.png`), `../scripts/ladder_history.py`,

### B. Building the ladder in January 2027 (what if yields fall first)
- **What it shows.** Each 0.1-point fall in yields before 1 Jan 2027 moves about $3,000 of the 2028 deposit from the

### C. ★ The ladder's one weak point: reinvesting the interest
- **What it shows.** The ladder costs less than $500,000 because its bonds pay interest along the way, which is
  reinvested. If yields fall for good by one, two or three points, the ladder delivers about $11k, $21k or $31k less
  (15 Oct number sheet). The rooms then tried every cure:
  - *A dedicated ladder* (later bonds' interest pays earlier cheques) cut the bad tail by about 40% for $1,486, but
    needed about ten more trades on a frozen book (L-09). Kept for §10 as "priced and declined".
  - *Thin-coupon twins* of the same maturities (1.375% Nov-2040, 2% Nov-2041) carry less reinvestment risk (W-01).
    Kept for §10 as "what we would do differently", labelled as such.
  - *Redington's hedge*, a long bond whose price rises as the gap widens: $64-74k, about half the gift (L-11). Refused.
  - *Price it once at that day's yields*: a cheque short in about one future in four without a margin, one in 550
    with one (L-16).
  - *Price it as if nothing more were reinvested* (the final IPS rule): every cheque paid in full whenever the money
    exists in the base and rates-fall-for-good worlds, and in 98.7% of futures in the recession world (yields a
    point lower before the build and the 2028 deposit halved); costs about $670 of the average
    gift; Laura's kept shares run dry by 2033 in 28 futures in 100, against 19 (L-17). Modelling the five iBond funds
    as funds rather than as bonds, the set-aside as the model first sized it paid every cheque in 97% of base futures
    and 82% when rates fall for good; sized as the IPS words read, 100% in both, for about $3,559 more set aside
    (Courtroom exhibit N). The report must use the words' sizing. Public escrows are sized the same way and hand back the excess (LR-11).
- **Method.** 200,000 rate paths; every coupon reinvested at its day's yield; the gap priced on naming day.
- **Where.** §3 (the reserve's one risk and its rule), §2 rows 1, 2, 19, §10.
- **Why it reads as erudite.** We found the weakness ourselves, tried four textbook cures, priced each, and chose the

- **Read.** Fisher & Weil 1971; Redington 1952; Fong & Vasicek 1984 (why a duration match isn't a cash-flow match);

### D. ★ Certainty, given a number
- **What it shows.** The case asks how we evaluated "a high degree of certainty" (:97-98). On the 1 Nov design (V47-V49) all ten
  cheques were paid in full in about 998 futures in 1,000, whether or not the 2031 step was taken; the rare gap was a
  few hundred dollars across $500,000. The thin case is the recession the IPS itself names (yields lower before the
  build and the 2028 deposit halved): about 98 in 100 (LR-09). Nine "as needs change" stresses (smaller or no deposit,
  yields falling before or after, shares halving before or after naming) left a cheque more than $1,000 short in
  0.01-0.05% of futures in seven of them; only a deposit under about $50,000 breaks the cheques (L-12). The IRS
  disqualifies a fixed-payment charitable trust if its chance of running out is above 5% at one assumed rate; ours
  passes with rates allowed to move (IDEA_BANK #37, re-run needed).
- **A result that changed the plan.** L-10 read the plan as a capital structure and expected the cheques to be the
  safest layer. They were the weakest: short in 6.8-8.4% of futures, because the gift left in 2033 while the interest
  kept needing reinvestment until 2042. That finding is why the reinvestment rule exists at all.
- **Method.** 200,000 futures per world; six worlds; "short" = more than $1,000 short.
- **Where.** §3 (the certainty definition and its test), §10 (the hypothesis that was wrong).
- **Read.** Roy 1952; Leibowitz & Henriksson 1989; IRS Rev. Proc. 2016-42; Parks, Finestone & Leahy 2015 (*Tax Notes*).
- **Files.** `../rooms/lauras_room/LR-09_the_promise_has_no_number.md`, `../rooms/lab/L-10_last_in_line.md`,
  `L-12_four_doors.md`, `../ips_rewrite/IDEA_BANK.md` #37.

### E. ★ The deletion tests: wipe out every share and see which cheque notices
- **What it shows.** The submitted TNA says VT is "the only investment not standing between Laura and a payment
  deadline". The Archive tested it by deleting the shares. Wiping out every share Laura owns on naming day left **no
  cheque worse off in any of 600,000 futures** (base, recession-linked, rates fall for good); the range pays instead.
  Only with the 2028 deposit also halved do the shares matter at all (0.94% of futures, median $758), and then they
  help (A33, final rule). An earlier version found the same on the 28 Oct design (A19: 0 paths worse, 354-412 better).
- **Method.** Ablation: run the plan, run it again with the shares set to zero, compare every future cheque by cheque.
- **Where.** §3 and §6, and the link back to the TNA (judges read the three together).
- **Why it reads as erudite.** It's a clean counterfactual, the way an experimental scientist proves a part does
  nothing: delete it.
- **Files.** `../rooms/archive_checks/A33_lock_deletion_test.py`, `A19_delete_vt_test.py`, `../rooms/archive.md`.

### F. Risks that arrive together
- **What it shows.** If the 2028 deposit falls with shares and yields (a 2008-style year), the earlier champion design
  left a cheque short in 0.35-0.82% of futures, against 0.20% with a fixed deposit (K07 T1). A full 2008 replay (yields
  1.9 points lower by Jan 2027, deposit $75k, shares down 40%) made the ladder cost $361,003 and left a cheque short in
  14-18% of futures on that design, by about $2,600 over the decade, which is what the later rules were built to close.
  History is kinder than the fear: from 1954 to 2011 a lasting rate fall and a burst of inflation arrived together

### G. ★ The 2031 range and how sure we are
- **What it shows.**
  - *How high to set the top.* "Floor plus half the shares" is reached about 3 times in 4 in the house world, 3.6 to 1
    in the storm world and 5 to 1 in history; quoting two-thirds collapses it to about 1 in 3, a cliff (M6).
  - *The lock.* Once the range is capped, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura: it is short a put
    nobody paid for. Moving the promised half into Treasuries fixes that (R08_2), and moving it *before* Laura names
    the range removes the temptation to skip it after a boom year, when skipping would cut the top's odds to about 86
    in 100 (L-15).
  - *Why the top used to miss.* 88-97% of misses were money the plan already knew the cheques would need. Taking it off
    both ends lifted the top's odds to 96-98 in 100 in all four worlds, the bottom never broken, the typical gift
    unchanged (L-13).
  - *The range's own confidence.* On the V47 design the gift landed inside the quoted range in 200,000 of 200,000
    futures in two worlds and 199,999 in the third; when it missed the top, the median miss was $865 (L-15 C).
  - *Marking the bottom.* Marked to the bond market in 2031, the named bottom broke in 0 of 200,000 futures (7 in the

### J. Every month since 1871, and market storms
- **What it shows.** Replayed from every start month since 1871, neither version of the plan bounced a cheque or
  landed the gift below its bottom (M1b; the Window asked for this figure to be re-derived before use). In a storm
  world (crashes that cluster, with fat tails) the gift's 1-in-20 value moved by under $1,000 (M2): storms hit the
  share fund, not the cheques. US shares had no two-year loss in 80% of windows since 1871 (IDEA_BANK #24, HISTORY).
- **Method.** Four worlds: a lognormal house world; a two-state regime model fitted to 152 years of shares (storms
  start about 1 month in 35, last about 6 months, with Student-t tails); a stationary block bootstrap of real months

## 6. How to present the models so judges trust them

A judge who sees forty numbers and no method will trust none of them. One short method note fixes that, and is itself
a mark of expertise.

1. **Validate them the way banks must.** The Federal Reserve's model-risk guidance (SR 11-7, 4 Apr 2011, read 4 Nov)
   names three parts of validation: "Evaluation of Conceptual Soundness", "Ongoing Monitoring" and "Outcomes
   Analysis", plus "effective challenge", defined as "critical analysis by objective, informed parties that can
   identify model limitations and produce appropriate changes". Our work maps onto it cleanly: the family tree is
   conceptual soundness; re-running on every new curve is ongoing monitoring; the history replay and the cross-engine
   reconciliation are outcomes analysis; the devil's-advocate test written before each run is effective challenge.
   It is how a practitioner on the panel would expect a model to be defended.
2. **Say each world in one line** (section 4J), and why four: a bell curve, fat tails, real history resampled, and
   history straight.
3. **Show two times we were wrong.** L-10 expected the cheques to be the safest layer and found them the weakest; L-13
   expected the range's misses to be market noise and found they were money the plan already knew about. Both changed
   the plan. That is "learning" and "response to challenges" in Wharton's own criteria.
4. **Stamp every number** with its curve, its return assumption and "we modelled" (Commandment V).
5. **Name what we never modelled.** A US government default; the facility's cost (the case puts it out of scope);
   yield curves that twist rather than shift (A02). Section 7 proposes a twist test.

## 9. Guardrails

- **No modelling in the IPS.** Book 11 (Ahaan, 1 Nov): no "our model", no model outputs, no citations. Every number in
  this plan is Final Report material.
- **Every number explains itself** (Commandment V): "we modelled", with the curve and the return assumption.
- **Fiction stays fiction.** Keel's rooms, people and their debates are a simulation. Their findings may be used once
  re-checked; their events never appear as Caplet's teamwork. §10 uses only real team events: the first plan, the
  6 Oct rule change, the 7-8 Oct trades, the TNA, the IPS rewrites and the feedback that drove them.
- **WInS gains are never added** to Laura's projections (Wharton's advice; the case, p.4).
- **Only allowed instruments.** No STRIPS-era figure is quoted without a re-run on the WInS basis.
- **Follow the frozen IPS.** The report evaluates V56 as submitted; anything else is labelled "what we would do
  differently" (W-01's thin-coupon twins, L-09's dedicated ladder).
- **Curate, don't decorate.** A concept earns its place by changing or defending a decision. Name-dropping is
  "futile" (Book 4).
- **No invented Laura quotes**; simulated readers are tests, never quotes.
