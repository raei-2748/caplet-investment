## The governing thought (one idea the whole report serves)

Money Laura has promised is owned in Treasuries before she promises it; only money promised to no one takes market
risk. The IPS set this rule (the payments in Treasuries; the bottom of the gift a Treasury fund; half the shares moved
into Treasuries before she names a figure; half promised to no one). The report shows that the WInS book put the rule
into practice, and applies the same rule to each thing Wharton asks Caplet to recommend: the operating reserve, the
facility contribution, the 2031 range and its confidence, the words for co-sponsors and what to watch.

It opens Section 2, not Section 3 (panel 1, all three judges): judges read Section 2 first, and the idea is what makes
the holdings table make sense.

## What it means to a co-sponsor (panel 1, the funder judge)

Ten years of the residency's operations are owned in Treasuries before the doors open, so the facility is the only
thing Laura asks anyone to share. No other founder in a co-sponsor's pile can say that. It leads 3.0 and the passage
in 3d.

## The argument in three sentences (EXPECTATIONS 2.9: the strategy, its biggest strength, its main trade-off)

1. The strategy: ten Treasuries pay the ten operating payments; the 2028 deposit completes them, then buys a Treasury
   fund maturing late 2032 for the bottom of the gift and a world share fund for the top and for flexibility.
2. Its biggest strength: every promise Laura makes, to the residency in 2033 and to co-sponsors in 2031, is backed by
   money she already owns, so the confidence she states comes from structure rather than from a forecast.
3. Its main trade-off: a lower certain gift than an all-Treasury plan at the bottom, and a smaller cushion in the years
   shares fall, in exchange for a share of world growth, half of which is never promised to anyone.

## Six things a judge should be able to say after the first page (version 2)

1. The ten payments rest on ten Treasuries, each paying out just before its payment; from 2033 they are the operating
   reserve, about $444,500 on 1 Jan 2033 at today's forward rates against the $399,089 the payments are worth at the
   same rates, about 111% funded (NUMBERS 8h, Tue 10 Nov, MODEL). The standard behind that is the escrow verifier's: every payment is paid
   even if no coupon earns a cent (decided Tue 10 Nov, card Q20), and the report says what that standard costs the gift.
2. The WInS book is the whole plan built at $300,000, and it cost more than the deposit: the ten cost $302,287 at the
   6 Nov close, and the IPS's own clause completes them from the 2028 deposit. A team that shows its overrun and the
   rule that covers it is believed on the rest.
3. In 2031 Laura states a range by a written rule (each end at what it pays if no more interest is earned, worked out
   when it is bought; a cap with any excess joining the half kept; no figure before the move; card Q19), about $95,000 to $115,000 at today's central figures (NUMBERS 4, Tue 10 Nov; the escrow standard for the payments
   costs the range about $15,000, and about $45,000 of reserve overflow returns to Laura in 2033 if forward rates hold); once half the shares have moved, both ends are money
   she owns in Treasury funds, so the gift lands inside it unless the US Treasury defaults or a fund's own costs or losses take more than the
   0.5% each end is cut by (IBTM's prospectus lets a fund pay below its holdings and sets no cap; card Q26). The real uncertainty is earlier and is shown: which range she will be able to announce
   (card Q19).
4. Half the shares are promised to no one, as a designed contingency with a purpose, an order of use and a release
   rule; that cushion is smallest in the years it is most likely to be needed, and the report says so, with a
   recession replayed from history (card Q24).
5. Laura watches many things, but only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan; everything else changes
   a number, never a payment.
6. The team changed its own first plan on its own evidence, and its recommendation came from disagreements it settled
   on reasons; each event in the reflection ends on something that changed, or was tested and kept, in a
   recommendation earlier in the report (card Q27).

## Ways the report could fail (each has a scorecard row)

1. **It reads as the IPS again.** Wharton: "without repeating the content of your IPS". Guard: every paragraph holds a
   measure, a test, a decision or a number that is in neither the IPS nor the Trading Notes (SKELETON's rule).
2. **A bond reader catches a loose claim** ("immune to markets", "without doubt", "pay what they say" for an iBond
   fund, "diversified by date", "duration-matching"). Guard: the canon's exact meanings (`CANON.md`); net asset value,
   not par; maturity-matched, not duration-matched; reinvestment priced; the margin explained.
3. **The confidence statement overpromises or can be made to fail.** Guard: one definition of the contribution (floor
   plus moved half, capped at the stated top, CANON D12); the range as a rule; the confidence said in numbers and
   words; what could lower it named.
4. **The reflection is generic, retells the Trading Notes, or is about analysis rather than people.** Guard: three or
   four real events from the blind ranking, each naming who did what and when, each ending on what changed or what was tested and kept,
   with a number where the register has one; one of them a success.
5. **It is too dense to follow at speed.** Guard: the stack test on the first page; word budgets per part
   (`SKELETON.md`: about 2,700 words of prose in all); cut whole ideas, never squeeze sentences (EXPECTATIONS 3.2).
6. **It ignores what the case names.** Guard: liquidity, inflation, the Taiwan dollar and a recession that hits shares
   and rates together each appear once, with a number (panel 1: all three were thin in version 1).
