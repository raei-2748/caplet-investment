=== P1 ===
In January 2027 Laura's first deposit buys ten Treasuries, one for each $50,000 payment, each maturing before the 1 January it pays. The US government guarantees every Treasury, and a Treasury bond held to the end pays exactly its stated interest and face value. Its price in between does not matter: we never sell. Treasury bonds pay 2038 to 2042; for 2033 to 2037 we chose iShares iBonds funds. Each holds Treasuries maturing in one year, then closes that December and pays out what its holdings are worth. That sum is nearly fixed but not quite, because in its last months the fund holds cash, and what cash earns is not fixed.

At Treasury rates on 6 November 2026 the ten cost about $301,500, just over $300,000, so for a year there are two gaps. One is the part her deposit cannot buy, which, with fees, will cost $2,388 in January 2028. The other is interest: the ten reach $50,000 each only if the interest they pay along the way is reinvested at those rates. The first $39,162 of the 2028 deposit closes both. It buys the missing $2,388, then $36,774 more of the bonds and funds for later payments: enough to meet all ten even if every dollar paid out early earns nothing while it waits, and even if every fund pays out 0.5% less than its holdings are worth. Until that deposit arrives, the ten lean partly on her career. Once it is in, no payment depends on a share price, an interest rate or anyone else's money.
=== P2 ===
Laura's $150,000 arrives in January 2028, the last money in before the residency opens. We spend it in order: the payments, then the least she will give to the building, then shares.

The payments come first because they are already fixed. In January 2027 her first deposit bought ten Treasuries, one for each $50,000 payment, but at Treasury rates on 6 November 2026 it could not pay for all ten in full; the missing part costs $2,388. Then $36,774 buys more of the same Treasuries and Treasury funds, enough to meet every payment even if the money they pay out early earns nothing while it waits. From then on, no payment rests on a future interest rate.

Most of what remains buys more of IBTM, the Treasury fund that already holds the money for the 2033 payment and pays out in mid-December 2032: about $102,000 from this purchase. Counted as if the interest it pays out from then on earns nothing, cut 0.5% for costs and rounded down, that is $95,000, the lowest figure Laura can give co-sponsors in 2031. Until the day it is bought, that figure moves with Treasury rates; from that day it is fixed, three years before she states it.

The rest buys VT, a fund of shares in thousands of companies worldwide. Shares get only money no promise depends on, and get it now because they need time to grow. In January 2031 half their value moves into IBTM to set the top of her 2031 range; the other half stays hers, promised to no one.
=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors two figures, a bottom and a top, in $5,000 steps. Both will be paid by IBTM, a Treasury fund that pays out in mid-December 2032.

The bottom is bought in January 2028, with most of what her $150,000 deposit has left once the ten payments take their share: enough of IBTM to pay about $102,000. Counted cautiously (as if the interest it pays out from then on earns nothing), less 0.5% for costs and rounded down, that is $95,000 on Treasury rates of 6 November 2026. It does not change after that.

The top waits for her shares. The rest of that deposit buys VT, a world share fund. In January 2031, before the meetings, half the value of her shares is sold and put into IBTM; the other half stays in shares, promised to no one. The top is the same cautious count on both purchases together: $115,000 if world shares grow 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's long-term forecast. Laura names nothing before she owns it: a top named a year earlier would be more than the sale could buy in about 11 of 100 futures our model simulates.

When she speaks, Treasuries she owns will pay both figures: the range is certain, and her contribution is the top. Why not just say $115,000? Because the two ends say different things. The bottom has been held in Treasuries since January 2028, whatever shares did; the gap above it is what shares added by 2031. Had shares fallen to nothing, the range would be one figure, $95,000.
=== P4 ===
Laura's plan makes two promises: ten payments of $50,000, and the building contribution she names in 2031. Her flexibility is what it promises to no one. Part of her 2028 deposit buys VT, a world share fund; in January 2031 half of it is sold for Treasuries that will pay the contribution, and the other half stays in shares. If world shares grow 7.5% a year, that half is worth about $21,200 on 1 January 2033, and with what her Treasuries pay above the contribution she keeps about $28,700. In 1 of 20 futures our model simulates, her flexibility is about $9,600 or less.

It is there for three things. The builder's price can rise: in Taiwan and the other places we checked, building costs rise in about four years out of five, whether shares rise or fall. The Taiwan dollar can strengthen: the building is paid for in Taiwan dollars, so a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger when she pays than at the 2031 meetings makes the same building about $11,500 dearer. Her flexibility alone covers that in about 93 of 100 model futures. And a co-sponsor can fall short, its budget cut in a recession that also cuts share prices.

The hard case is two at once. World shares bought at the start of 2000, as hers are in 2028, had lost about 41% by January 2003, the point that matches her 2031 meetings. Taiwan's building costs then rose about 18% by the point that matches her contribution. A slump then a cost burst like that could leave her flexibility short.
=== NOTES ===
P3 was the hardest. A reader who knows only the case has to hold four steps at once (the fund bought in 2028, the cautious count, the 2031 sale of half the shares, the rounding down) before either figure makes sense, and every step wants its own explaining. Then the question the brief asks, why give a range at all, has an awkward answer once both ends are certain, so it had to come last, after the mechanics, as what each end tells a co-sponsor. P1 came second: the iBonds funds' payout is nearly but not quite fixed, and that one caveat is what the $36,774 and the 0.5% exist for, so it could not be cut.
