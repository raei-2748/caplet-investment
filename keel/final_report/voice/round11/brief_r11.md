# Voice round 11: four parts of the plan, explained so they are understood first time (Fri 20 Nov 2026, morning)

You are writing four short passages for Team Caplet's Final Report to the Wharton Global High School Investment
Competition. Caplet is six school students in Sydney. Their client is Laura Gao (a case, not a real client of theirs).
The passages are practice, not the report itself.

## 1. Read first, in this order
1. `/home/user/caplet-investment/keel/final_report/voice/CORPUS.md`, whole. It is everything the team has written
   itself: its IPS (a letter to Laura), its Trading Note reflections, its own lines. Read the IPS twice, the second time
   slowly, as if aloud. Do not learn from section 4 ("Not their voice").
2. `/home/user/caplet-investment/keel/final_report/voice/FACTS_PLAIN.md`: the facts of the case and the plan. Every
   figure you use comes from there.
Do not open any other file in the repository.

## 2. Who reads these passages
Each passage will be read **on its own**, by an intelligent reader who has not read the IPS or any other part of the
report, and who knows only this about the case:

> Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan,
> opening in 2033. She puts $300,000 into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of
> 2028; that second sum comes from her book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before
> 2033. From the portfolio she must pay $50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments) to run the
> residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money aside for
> those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards the
> residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she
> tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she
> is. Overpromising would hurt her credibility. A team of school students advises her; it may use US Treasury bonds,
> Treasury funds, shares and share funds.

Nothing else. If a passage uses something the reader has not been told, the reader has nothing to go on.

## 3. The four passages
Write in the team's voice, as the team ("we"), to Wharton's judges as readers of a report (never address "the judges"
or "you, the reader"; never copy the IPS's sentences). About 200-260 words each. No images or metaphors set up for
their own sake; this round is about whether every sentence is understood the first time and makes sense once it is.
Figures are "on today's figures" (Treasury rates at the close on 6 November 2026, the day trading froze); use only the
figures each passage needs.

- **P1. The ten payments.** What Laura's first deposit buys in January 2027, and why each $50,000 will be there on its
  1 January from 2033 to 2042.
- **P2. The 2028 deposit.** What Laura's $150,000 buys in January 2028, part by part, and why each part is bought then.
- **P3. The 2031 range.** How the two figures Laura gives co-sponsors in 2031 are set, what happens to her shares
  before she meets them, and why she gives a range at all.
- **P4. Her flexibility.** What she keeps promised to no one, about how much, and what it might have to pay for, shown
  as things that could actually happen to a building project in Taiwan.

## 4. Words the team does not use
Treasuries may be called guaranteed, locked or risk-free, with nothing attached (never "barring a US default"). Never
"secured", "prudent", "full funding", "fail-safe", "optimal", "natural hedge", "endowment", "building blocks", "glide
path", "in her name" (say "held for her"), "the consensus", a median of forecasts, or a probability rarer than 1 in 100.
Never mention the team's process, Wharton, models' names, the report's section numbers, or anyone's use of AI.
