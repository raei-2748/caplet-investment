=== P1 ===
In January 2027 Laura's first deposit of $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one for each payment, each maturing just before the 1 January it pays. A Treasury is a loan to the US government, which guarantees it: held to the end, it pays exactly its stated interest and its face value on fixed dates. The last five payments rest on Treasury bonds, the first five on iShares iBonds funds, because the plan could buy no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036. Each fund holds Treasuries that all mature in the same year, closes in mid-December of that year and pays out what its holdings are then worth: close to a fixed sum, though not exactly one.

At Treasury rates on 6 November 2026, the ten cost slightly more than $300,000, and they reach $50,000 each only if the interest they pay along the way is reinvested at those rates. So in January 2027 two parts of the ten are not yet owned: the slice the deposit could not buy, and the interest that reinvested cash has still to earn. The 2028 deposit takes care of both first: $2,388 buys the missing slice, and $36,774 buys more of the same Treasuries, enough for all ten payments to be made even if every dollar waiting for its 1 January earns nothing (one bond pays out in February 2037 for the payment of January 2038). From then on each $50,000 is already owned, and no share price stands between Laura and any of the ten. Until then, they depend on the 2028 deposit arriving.
=== P2 ===
In January 2027 Laura's first $300,000 buys ten Treasuries (guaranteed US government bonds), one maturing just before each $50,000 payment, and falls just short of buying all ten. Her second deposit, $150,000 from her books, talks and licences, arrives in January 2028 and is spent that month, because nothing comes in after it. It goes, in order, to the things that most need to be sure. (Figures are at Treasury rates on 6 November 2026.)

The first $2,388 completes the ten. The next $36,774 buys more of the same Treasuries. With them, all ten payments are made even if every dollar paid out early earns nothing while it waits for its 1 January, and even if each of the Treasury funds behind five of them pays out 0.5% less than its holdings are worth. Anything the payments still lacked after January 2028 could only come from selling her shares.

Most of what is left buys IBTM, a Treasury fund that closes in mid-December 2032, just before the building money is needed. This is the floor. It will pay about $102,000, and that payout, counted with room to spare and rounded down, is the lowest figure Laura will give co-sponsors in 2031: $95,000. Bought in January 2028, that figure is fixed three years before she says it.

The rest buys VT, one fund of shares in thousands of companies worldwide. Shares come last because no promise depends on them yet, and they have three years to grow before 2031. How they do decides how far above that lowest figure her top reaches.
=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors two figures, a bottom and a top. Both are paid by one fund, IBTM, a fund of US Treasuries that pays out when it closes in mid-December 2032.

The bottom is fixed in January 2028, when most of her second deposit buys this fund. On Treasury rates of 6 November 2026, that purchase will pay about $102,000. We count it as if the interest the fund pays out along the way earns nothing once paid, take off 0.5% for its costs beyond its fee, and round down: $95,000.

The rest of that deposit buys world shares, whose price decides for three years how much more she can give. In January 2031, before the meetings, half the shares are sold to buy more of the same fund, and the other half stays in shares, promised to no one. The top is what the fund will pay on both purchases, counted the same way and rounded down once: $115,000 if world shares grow 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's long-term forecast.

She names neither figure before that sale. If she named a top a year early, the sale would buy less than it in about 11 of 100 futures we simulated. Named after it, both figures are paid by Treasuries she already owns, so she can call them certain.

Why a range, then? Because its ends say different things. The top is what she will give; co-sponsors plan around it. The bottom is the part held since 2028, which would have stood even if her shares had fallen to nothing.
=== P4 ===
In January 2031, before the co-sponsor meetings, Laura sells half of the world shares she bought in 2028 and buys Treasuries to pay her contribution. The other half stays in shares, promised to no one: her flexibility. At Treasury rates of 6 November 2026, and with world shares growing 7.5% a year, it is worth about $21,200 at the start of 2033. In 1 simulated future in 20, her flexibility is $9,600 or less, and after 2031 a fall in shares shrinks this money and nothing else.

It is there for three things that could happen to a building in Taiwan.

The builder's price could rise between the 2031 meetings and the day she pays. Building costs, in Taiwan and elsewhere, rise in about four years out of five whether world shares rise or fall, so her shares will not reliably be up when the price is. World shares bought at the start of 2000, as hers are bought at the start of 2028, were about 41% down by January 2003, the point that matches her meetings; Taiwan's building costs then rose about 18% in Taiwan dollars before the point that matches her payment. A run like that could leave her short.

The Taiwan dollar could strengthen, so the same building costs more US dollars. A 10% rise would need about $11,500 more, which her flexibility alone covers in about 93 of 100 futures.

And a co-sponsor could fall short, most likely in a recession: the same downturn that tends to delay her own book income and cut share prices.
=== NOTES ===
P3 was the hardest. The reader is told only that Laura states "a range" and "how confident she is", so a range whose two ends are both certain looks odd until the passage says what each end is for, and the cautious count behind the bottom (interest assumed to earn nothing, a 0.5% cut, rounding down) has to fit in one sentence without losing the reader before the $95,000 arrives. P1 was close behind, because the reinvested interest is the less obvious of the two gaps the 2028 deposit closes, and it only became clear once a concrete dollar waiting (the February 2037 bond) sat beside it.
