=== P1 ===
In January 2027 Laura's first deposit of $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one for each payment, each maturing shortly before the 1 January it pays. A Treasury held until it matures pays exactly the interest and the sum it promised, on the dates it promised, because the US government stands behind it; what its price does in between doesn't touch us, because we never sell one early. For the payments of 2033 to 2037 we use iShares iBonds funds. Each holds Treasuries that mature in one year of its own, closes in mid-December of that year and pays its holders what it then holds. We used them because the competition's simulator listed no Treasury bond maturing in those years. The payments of 2038 to 2042 come from Treasury bonds themselves.

There is one catch. Treasuries pay interest along the way, often years before the payment it belongs to, and that interest has to earn something while it waits. At the rates of 6 November 2026, the day trading froze, the ten cost about $301,500 on the assumption that it does. That is slightly more than Laura has, so the last $2,388 is bought a year later from her second deposit, which also buys more of the same Treasuries: enough that every payment is met even if the interest waiting for it earns nothing at all. From January 2028, then, no payment depends on a share price or on interest rates. Only a US default, or no second deposit, could stop one.
=== P2 ===
Laura's second deposit, $150,000, arrives in January 2028 and is spent in a fixed order. First, $2,388 finishes buying the ten payments, the small part her first deposit could not afford. Second, $36,774 buys more of the same Treasuries. The ten are only enough if the interest they pay along the way is reinvested at today's rates; this extra makes every payment safe even if that interest earns nothing at all while it waits. Together these Treasuries are the operating reserve the case asks us to size.

Third, most of what is left, about $81,000, buys more of the iBonds Treasury fund that closes in December 2032, the same fund that already holds the money for the 2033 payment. What this purchase pays out in December 2032, about $102,000 on today's figures, is the least Laura will be able to promise towards the building, so we call it the floor. We buy it in January 2028 rather than later because the only other place for that money in the meantime is shares, and then the least she could promise would depend on what shares did. Bought as Treasuries, it is fixed on the day it is bought.

Last, the remaining $29,600 or so buys VT, one fund holding shares in thousands of companies around the world. It is the only money in the plan that can grow beyond what Treasuries pay, and the only money that can fall. Nothing anyone is promised depends on it.
=== P3 ===
In January 2028 most of what is left of Laura's second deposit buys an iBonds Treasury fund that closes and pays out in December 2032, just before her building contribution falls due. In 2031 she tells co-sponsors two figures for that contribution, and by the time she says them, both are owned in that fund.

The lower figure, the bottom, is what the 2028 purchase will pay out. We count it cautiously, as if the interest the fund collects from then on earned nothing more, take off 0.5% for the fund's costs beyond its fee, and round down to a multiple of $5,000. On today's figures that turns an expected $102,000 into $95,000, and once the fund is bought the bottom cannot move.

The upper figure, the top, takes one more step. In January 2031, before the meetings, half the value of her shares is sold and the money buys more of the same fund. Both purchases counted the same cautious way, and rounded down once, give the top: $115,000 on today's figures. How far the top sits above the bottom depends on what shares did between 2028 and 2031. Had they fallen to nothing, the range would be a single figure.

Why give a range at all, when both ends are owned? The case asks Laura for one, and the two ends tell co-sponsors different things. The top is what she will give, and the figure they should plan around. The bottom is how much of it has been held in Treasuries since January 2028.
=== P4 ===
Half of Laura's shares are never promised to anyone; the IPS calls this her flexibility. On today's figures it is worth about $21,200 at the start of 2033, and about $28,700 once we add whatever the December 2032 Treasury fund pays beyond the top figure she has stated. It is there for the three things the IPS names, and each can be pictured.

A builder's tender could come in above the estimate. Taiwan's construction costs rose about 7% in the year to October 2026, and across the places we checked they rise in about four years out of five, whether shares are rising or falling. The Taiwan dollar could strengthen between the 2031 meetings and the day she pays, so the same building would cost more in US dollars: a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger would need about $11,500 more, which her flexibility covers in about 93 of 100 of our model futures. Or a co-sponsor could give less than it said, perhaps in the same recession that cut the value of Laura's shares.

The honest limit is the order in which those things can come. World shares bought at the start of 2000, as hers are bought at the start of 2028, were about 41% down three years later, the point that matches her meetings; Taiwan's building costs then rose about 18% before the point that matches her payment. A slump followed by a building boom is the case her flexibility handles worst. Even then, it runs out without breaking anything: the payments and the figure she stated are already owned in Treasuries.
