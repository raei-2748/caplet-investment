# Reading 3 (blind reader, 13 passages)

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Surely not
- **Why:** It packs dates, fund names, dollar figures and legal thresholds into long, tightly jointed sentences, then lays a printing image over the top that it has to keep explaining. It sounds like an expert editor compressing a policy manual into prose, not the chatty, slightly loose letter-writers of the IPS.
- **Meaning:** Only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan; everything else (fund notices, the debt limit, a second custodian, flexibility risks) is watched quietly and never shown to co-sponsors.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. The bottom of the range has been fixed since January 2028 and the top has not been said aloud yet, so a 2030 fall only lowers the top she will name at the 2031 move; only a missed deposit or a default changes the plan.
- **Image:** It got in the way: "both are about the ink itself – whether the last of it ever goes down, and whether it holds" needed several reads, and the registration marks arrive mid-list and add little that the plain facts did not already say.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens on a crafted literary line and then runs at high density, with figures stacked in every sentence and a neat closing aphorism ("a deposit, not a fee"). It reads like a skilled writer building a conceit, more compressed and more ornamental than anything in the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The January 2027 Treasuries secure most of each payment, but what waiting cash earns is still uncertain, so in January 2028 extra Treasuries are bought so the payments are met even if that cash earns nothing, and most of the extra comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The extra Treasuries bought in January 2028 make the ten "sufficient without reinvestment", so every coupon and dollar of waiting cash could earn nothing and all ten would still be paid.
- **Image:** Pencil and ink helped me see which single part was not yet secure; the Authors Guild reserve was clever but "except Laura holds it for herself" needed a second read to land.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clear and well defined but reads like a policy document, much of it in the passive ("the notices are read", "is logged") and packed with specifics; "a loose trigger is just permission to fiddle" and "Market prices? Carry on." are the only moments of a person speaking. The IPS writers talk to Laura; this one administers her plan.
- **Meaning:** Only a missed deposit or a Treasury default, each tightly defined, can change the plan; everything else is a watch that at most swaps one Treasury holding for another.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A fall in shares in 2030 lowers only the top, which has not yet been stated; nothing reaching the payments has changed, and only the two named events change the plan.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves like someone explaining across a table, with short asides ("Almost all.", "It waits more than you'd think.", "And it isn't a small line, either") and a "we" that acts ("So in January 2028 we ink it"). It is more polished and image-led than the IPS, but the conversational push and the turn at the end feel within reach of the same writers.
- **Meaning:** The one uncertain part of the payments is what idle cash earns while it waits, so the team buys extra Treasuries in January 2028 to make the payments safe even if that cash earns nothing, and most of the extra money comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The extra Treasuries mean the payments are met even if every dollar of waiting cash earns nothing at all, so zero rates from 2029 do not touch them.
- **Image:** It helped: "one line is still in pencil" made the reinvestment risk concrete, and "the same idea pointed the other way" made the royalty-reserve comparison easy to follow.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It opens with the same move as the IPS letter, setting up what you would expect and then reversing it, and keeps a plain "we" throughout, ending flatly on "We'd carry on." The middle paragraph turns into a dense list of fragments, more organised than the IPS writers usually are, but the overall voice is close.
- **Meaning:** From January 2028 the market cannot change what Laura owes, so only a missed deposit or a default changes the plan, and everything else is a short list of things to watch.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A crash in 2030 lowers a top she has not yet stated; the payments and the bottom are already in Treasuries, so the team carries on.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** One long, slightly over-stuffed sentence that rushes from diversification to fees to a forecast "(as per JPMorgan)", with a punchy opening claim and a question at the end. The eagerness, the "just 0.06%" and the faintly grand "vain illusion" sound like the same students who wrote "So we hedge it!" and "ever-reliable Treasuries".
- **Meaning:** VT is the only holding allowed to go wrong, because it sits behind no payment; it buys the whole world market cheaply and decides how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (More shares):** The passage does not settle it; it implies shares only shape how generous the contribution can be, never the payments, so more shares would raise the possible top, not what she can promise for sure.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It sounds like a trade note: practical, a little breathless, with a run of adjectives ("defined-maturity, investment-grade, liquid, cheap and transparent about yields") and the IPS's own "simply". "The catch is, ..." is the kind of honest-but-upbeat concession the IPS writers make.
- **Meaning:** Where no Treasury bond of the right year was available, iBonds funds were bought instead; they behave like bonds when held to the end, paying out net asset value rather than par, in exchange for better liquidity.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is flat, accurate and textbook-like, opening with how pension funds work and stepping through the plan without any personality or turn. Nothing in it is wrong for the team, but it has none of the IPS's warmth, push or second-person address.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan uses one bond per payment held to maturity, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** By this passage, yes, because each payment has its own Treasury held to maturity that pays what it promised whatever markets do; it does not mention what coupons and waiting cash earn.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It argues in questions and answers ("Why 111%, and not simply 100%?", "Is that wasteful? Less than it looks!"), with an exclamation mark and a plain "we" that tested things, just as the IPS letter answers its own doubts. It carries more figures than the IPS, but it explains rather than packs them, and sounds like keen students showing their working.
- **Meaning:** The operating reserve is complete in January 2028, sized so the payments need nothing from reinvested interest, and the extra cost mostly comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The reserve is sized to the escrow test "sufficient without reinvestment", so the ten are paid in full even if every coupon and dollar of waiting cash earns nothing.
- **Image:** No extended image; the escrow and court-settlement comparisons are brief and helped show the standard is a real one.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It sets up a printing image in a full paragraph and then names everything after it ("The page itself", "The marks are everything else", "The page carries on"), which is more deliberate craft than the IPS ever uses. The voice in between is friendly ("That's it.", "What's interesting is how the list shrinks"), but the frame feels laid on by a writer, not grown from the team's letter.
- **Meaning:** Only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan; everything else is watched behind the scenes, and the list of things that matter shrinks as the dates pass.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A crash in 2030 moves a top she has not stated; the plan carries on.
- **Image:** It mostly helped: marks that are checked every pass and then trimmed away is a clear picture of watching without telling co-sponsors, though "The page carries on" stretches it past sense.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is well organised and confident, raising the obvious objection ("looks like paying for the same payments twice. It isn't.") and answering it, with a "we" that chose things "on purpose". It is tidier than the IPS, and the final line is a little too finished, but the register and the use of "10" and the case's own words match.
- **Meaning:** The reserve is all Treasuries by January 2028, including extra Treasuries so the payments need nothing from future interest rates, and most of that extra comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. With the extra Treasuries the ten are paid in full even if every coupon and dollar of waiting cash earns nothing, so zero rates do not matter.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Short, clipped and very clear, with one good human line ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year"). It is more economical and less chatty than the IPS writers, and reads like a careful summary of the mechanism by someone outside the letter.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 Laura names a range from the floor up to the floor plus half her shares, and before she names it any reinvestment shortfall in the ladder is priced and covered from her kept shares, shrinking her spare money or, if needed, the range.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** By this passage, yes, as long as her kept shares can cover the shortfall priced before 2031; if they cannot, the range comes down, so the payments are protected first.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the IPS's energy and its slight awkwardness: "Notably –" as a stand-alone opener, "near-ironclad guarantee", "restless markets", a strong claim made and then backed. It sounds like a student proud of a technical point about TIPS and keen to show it.
- **Meaning:** Treasuries do not create wealth but remove uncertainty; because the payments are fixed in nominal dollars, a nominal Treasury matches them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** By this passage, yes, because the bonds are held untouched and pay fixed nominal amounts; it does not address what coupons earn while they wait.
- **Image:** None.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like the IPS letter: a little loose and eager, with student touches ("restless markets", "vain illusion", "as per JPMorgan", "Less than it looks!"), the odd clumsy join, only a few numbers, and one idea pushed with conviction by a "we" that acts. The middle group keeps that "we" and the IPS's habit of setting up an expectation and reversing it, but is noticeably tidier and carries far more figures. The lowest-ranked passages pack exact dates, thresholds and dollar amounts into long, tightly jointed sentences, lay an extended borrowed image (pencil and ink, printers' marks) over material the IPS explained plainly, and close on a polished aphorism, so they read like a skilled professional writer rather than six students talking to their client.
