# Blind reading, rounds 3 and 4: reader 2 (13 passages)

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. Its writers sound like students talking to Laura: warm, a little excitable
(two exclamation marks, "worry not", "Fair enough"), fond of a confident adverb ("naturally", "definitely", "simply",
"ever-reliable"), one idea per sentence, reasoning said out loud as "we", and one or two images that are allowed to be a
bit loose (her career as a stock she cannot sell; risks on the page).

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not.
- **Why:** It moves like a compressed checklist, mostly in the passive ("are read", "is logged", "is swapped"), and the last paragraph is one long inverted sentence stacking four items, which the IPS writers never do. "A loose trigger is just permission to fiddle" and "Market prices? Carry on." sound like a person, but they are islands in a spec.
- **Meaning:** Only a missed deposit (nothing by 31 March 2028) or a Treasury default (a payment more than five business days late) changes the plan; everything else, from iBonds fund notices and the debt limit to the Taiwan dollar, building costs and custody, is watched and logged, with at most one Treasury swapped for another.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A share fall is not one of the two triggers; in 2030 it comes before the 2031 move, so it can only lower the top of a range she has not said aloud yet, while the bottom and the payments sit in Treasuries she already owns.
- **Image:** None extended.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably.
- **Why:** It thinks out loud the way the letter does, asking itself questions ("Why 111%, and not simply 100%?", "Is that wasteful?") and answering with a cheerful "Less than it looks!" that sounds like "worry not". It is more numerical than the IPS, but the person behind it is the same kind: explaining, a bit pleased with the answer, still plain.
- **Meaning:** The operating reserve is all in Treasuries by January 2028 and is sized, with $35,894 of extra Treasuries, so the ten payments are met even if coupons and waiting cash earn nothing (without it they would have fallen short in about 56 of 100 starting months since 1962), and most of the extra, about $45,500 in 2033 money, likely comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The reserve was sized "sufficient without reinvestment": the Treasuries pay fixed amounts on their dates and the extra Treasuries cover the waiting, so zero rates from 2029 leave every payment whole; only less money comes back to Laura afterwards.
- **Image:** "A deposit, not a fee" is a small comparison, and it helped: I understood at once why the extra money is not wasted.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably.
- **Why:** It reads like a student explaining a trade: a run of adjectives ("defined-maturity, investment-grade, liquid, cheap and transparent"), "so we'll simply ignore price noise", "The catch is," and an honest trade-off at the end. The tense slips (purchased, then we'll) and the slightly showy jargon ("terminal payout", "promise of par") feel human, not polished.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS listed no Treasury maturing in a needed year, the team bought iShares iBonds Treasury funds instead, which behave like bonds held to their end; the cost is a payout at net asset value rather than par, in return for more liquidity than an odd-lot bond.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably.
- **Why:** Plain, short and frank ("The ladder's one weakness is ..."), with one good human line ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year"). It is tighter and calmer than the IPS, with no exclamations, but it does not sound engineered.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 the range runs from the floor (rounded down) to the floor plus half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it; and the ladder's reinvestment shortfall is priced then and covered from her kept shares, so her spare money shrinks before her promise does. (This fixes the reinvestment gap in 2031 from her shares, where Passages 2, 6, 7 and 9 fix it in 2028 with extra Treasuries.)
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes, as this passage tells it, but the range pays for it: before naming the range in 2031 the team prices the shortfall at that day's near-zero rates, adds a margin and moves that much of her kept shares into Treasuries; if her shares cannot cover it, the whole range comes down rather than the payments.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely not.
- **Why:** It is Passage 1's dense checklist with a literary image dropped in, and it opens on "the ink itself – whether the last of it ever goes down, and whether it holds", which assumes a metaphor the passage never sets up. The IPS writers explain first and decorate rarely; this decorates a list that is already hard to follow.
- **Meaning:** Only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan; everything else is a watch, like printers' registration marks that are checked on each pass and trimmed away, so none of it reaches what Laura tells co-sponsors.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A 2030 fall is not a trigger and comes before the 2031 move, so it only lowers a top she has not stated; the payments and the bottom are untouched.
- **Image:** It got in the way: I only understood why the registration marks were there at the last sentence, and the unexplained "ink" at the start confused me.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably.
- **Why:** It walks Laura's reader through an objection the way the IPS does ("At first glance that last figure looks like paying for the same payments twice. It isn't."), with numerals ("the 10") and dashes like the letter. It is steadier and less playful than the IPS, and the last line is a little neat, but it sounds like the same people being careful.
- **Meaning:** The operating reserve is all Treasuries by January 2028, including $35,894 of extra Treasuries so the ten payments pass the escrow test "sufficient without reinvestment", because what waiting money earns is the one part no Treasury locks in; most of the extra comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The payments were sized to be met even if every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash earns nothing, so zero rates from 2029 do not touch them.
- **Image:** None extended.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably not.
- **Why:** Some of it is conversational in the IPS's way ("It waits more than you'd think", "And it isn't a small line, either"), but the whole is built like an essay: a fragment for effect ("Almost all."), an extended pencil-and-ink image, then a researched parallel turned "the other way" and a summing-up line. It sounds like a skilled writer shaping the reader, not students explaining.
- **Meaning:** By January 2027 the payments are mostly locked in Treasuries ("ink"), but what cash earns while it waits is still a forecast ("pencil"); in January 2028 extra Treasuries remove that, and, like a publisher's royalty reserve, most of it (about $45,500 in 2033 money) comes back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. From January 2028 the payments are met even if waiting cash earns nothing, so zero rates in 2029 change nothing for them.
- **Image:** Pencil and ink helped me see which piece was still uncertain; the Authors Guild comparison is apt for a writer but "the same idea pointed the other way" needed a second read, so it half helped.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not.
- **Why:** It is clear and correct but voiceless: a textbook framing ("Pension funds cover fixed payments either by ... or by ...") followed by a flat description, with none of the letter's warmth, enthusiasm or loose edges. The IPS writers could have produced these facts, but I do not hear them in it.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, matching average life and rebalancing, or one bond per payment held to maturity, the team uses the second, so the first $300,000 buys a Treasury ladder maturing just before each January payment and no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** From this passage alone, yes, because each payment rests on a Treasury held to maturity; but it never mentions coupons or waiting cash, so it does not show the reinvestment question the other passages raise.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Surely not.
- **Why:** Every paragraph is shaped for effect: a writerly opening ("Laura's pages begin in pencil and end in ink, and so can her money"), clipped kickers ("Most, not all.", "Publishers know this shape.", "The certainty costs her a deposit, not a fee."), images layered over a dense run of figures. The IPS has one epigram, at its close; this one is epigram throughout, and it reads as composed rather than spoken.
- **Meaning:** A figure is ink once a Treasury she owns will pay it; most of each payment is ink by January 2027, the waiting-cash interest is the last pencil line, and in January 2028 extra Treasuries ink it so the escrow test is met, with most of the extra (like a publisher's royalty reserve) coming back to Laura.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. After January 2028 every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash could earn nothing and all ten payments are still paid.
- **Image:** The definition up front made the idea clear, but stacking pencil, ink and the Authors Guild over so many numbers crowded it; on balance it got in the way.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Surely.
- **Why:** It has exactly the letter's mix of confidence and slight awkwardness: a bold opening ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong"), a phrase that reaches a bit too far ("avoid paying the price for the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), "just 0.06%", "as per JPMorgan", and a clean turn to the question VT answers. It sounds like the same students, pleased with an idea and saying it straight.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong because no payment depends on it; it buys the whole world market cheaply, for a forecast of about 7.5% a year, and decides how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (More shares):** Not reliably, and the passage does not quite say: more VT could raise the hoped-for top, but shares can fall, so it would shrink the floor she can promise without doubt and make the top less certain; VT decides generosity, not the promise.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably not.
- **Why:** The talk around the image is close to the IPS ("That's how we think about monitoring", "That's it.", "What's interesting is how the list shrinks"), but the printers' marks are a crafted, researched metaphor carried through to a kicker ("The page carries on."). It reads like the team's ideas arranged by a more literary hand.
- **Meaning:** Monitoring works like printers' registration marks: only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan (with a written deposit confirmation asked for by 1 December 2027), everything else is checked and kept out of what Laura tells co-sponsors, and the list shrinks as the market's reach shrinks.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A 2030 crash is not a trigger and only lowers a top she has not stated yet; the plan carries on.
- **Image:** Here the image is set up and explained before the list, so it helped me hold the difference between triggers and watches, though the last line stretches it.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Surely.
- **Why:** It sounds like the IPS writers in a trade note: a balanced claim ("won't create wealth, but they will remove uncertainty"), an over-sure flourish ("near-ironclad guarantee", "restless markets", "nonexistent inflation risk"), "Notably –" as an opener like "Importantly for you", and the same reading of "high degree of certainty" taken one step further. It is confident, slightly overstated and plainly human.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury will not grow wealth but removes uncertainty by matching a payment; the team reads "high degree of certainty" as a near-guarantee, and since the payments are fixed in nominal dollars, nominal Treasuries match them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably.
- **Why:** It opens with the same move as the IPS ("Most monitoring advice says to watch the market. Ours says nearly the opposite", like "You'd think ... until we stopped"), stays in "we" and "we'd", and has a relaxed aside ("that's rather the point"). The middle is a list, but it is told rather than filed, and it ends simply.
- **Meaning:** From January 2028 nothing the market does changes what Laura owes, so only a missed deposit or a Treasury default changes the plan; everything else is a watch (fund notices, debt limit, trade days, a second custodian), and the market's reach shrinks to her flexibility after 2031.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. A 2030 crash lowers a top she has not stated yet and touches nothing she owes; it is not one of the two triggers, so she carries on.
- **Image:** None.

### What separates them

The passages I ranked highest sound like students talking: one idea per sentence, reasoning said out loud in "we", a
confident adverb or an exclamation, and the occasional phrase that reaches a bit too far ("the vain illusion",
"near-ironclad", "Less than it looks!"), which is exactly the texture of the IPS letter. The middle group keeps that
conversational "we" and the habit of answering an objection, but is calmer and more controlled than the IPS. The lowest
are compressed and composed: every clause carries a fact, lists run in the passive or in inverted sentences, and
researched images (registration marks, pencil and ink, the Authors Guild) are layered on with clipped kicker lines, so
they read as a skilled editor shaping the reader rather than the team explaining its own plan. The short top three are
also trade-note length, so part of their advantage may be genre: they never have to carry a dense run of figures.
