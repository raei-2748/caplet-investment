# Writer F, voice round 3

## Warm-up

The submitted IPS has no paragraph that starts "We'll start with the 10 payments", so I took the paragraph that does
that job, the one that starts "In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit to buy 10 Treasuries".

Notes, one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027, first deposit, 10 Treasuries; T-Bond or iBonds fund, whichever fits each year; each matures just before needed.
2. Held to end = pay what they say; dash; prices nothing to fear.
3. Today's yields: ~$300k, dash, i.e. the whole first deposit.
4. "But" + don't worry, dash, plan doesn't need yields to stay; exclamation.
5. If yields fall and ladder > $300k, finish it with the 2028 deposit ("simply").

Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027, your first deposit goes on 10 Treasuries – a T-Bond where there's one for that year, an iShares
iBonds fund where there isn't – each one maturing just before its payment is due. Held to the end, they pay what they
say, so whatever their prices do in between is nothing for us to worry about. At today's yields that costs about
$300,000 – in other words, all of your first deposit. But don't worry – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they
are! If they fall and the ladder ends up costing more than $300,000, we'll simply finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Comparison: mine drifted towards explaining why some years need a fund (the IPS just says "whichever works best" and
trusts Laura), and it flattened the original's two small bits of relish, "no price fluctuations for us to fear" and
"worry not", into plainer, safer words. The shape held – the dashes, the exclamation, the "simply" – which tells me the
rhythm is easier to keep than the word choice.

=== P1 ===
The operating reserve is the one part of Laura's plan where we left no room for opinion. The case wants 10 payments of $50,000 from 2033 to 2042 met with 'a high degree of certainty' from the portfolio alone, so the reserve is all Treasuries, all bought by January 2028: the 10 in January 2027, for about $302,000, then the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit – $2,388 to finish the 10 (the top-up), and $35,894 on extra Treasuries.

At first glance that last figure looks like paying for the same payments twice. It isn't. A Treasury pays what it says, but on its own date, and Laura pays on 1 January – the February 2037 bond ends ten and a half months before the 2038 payment it funds, and coupons arrive all along the way. Whatever that waiting money earns is the one part of the payments no Treasury locks in. Without the extra Treasuries, the 10 need it to earn about 5.1% a year; tested on every starting month since 1962, all 585 of them, the 10 alone would have fallen short in about 56 of every 100, and by about $34,500 in a bad 1-in-20 case. Roughly a coin toss – hardly 'a high degree of certainty'.

So we used the escrow agent's test, 'sufficient without reinvestment': the 10 are paid in full even if every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash earns nothing at all. It asks more than the case does, on purpose – it means the payments need nothing from future interest rates.

And most of that price comes back. By 1 January 2033 the reserve is worth about $444,500, about 111% of what the 10 are worth that day; if rates simply do what the market expects, about $45,500 (in 2033 money) is left over and comes back to Laura, about $74,100 by 2042 if it sits in a Treasury-bill fund. The payments rest on Treasuries alone, and on nothing that has to earn a cent.

=== P2 ===
Laura's pages begin in pencil and end in ink, and so can her money: a figure is ink once she owns Treasuries that will pay it on its date, and pencil while no Treasury she owns will pay it yet. The operating reserve's job is to leave no pencil at all in the 10 payments by January 2028.

Most of each payment is inked in January 2027, when the first deposit buys the 10 for about $302,000. Most, not all. A Treasury pays on its date and Laura pays on 1 January (the February 2037 bond ends ten and a half months before the 2038 payment it funds), and coupons land all along the way. What that waiting cash earns is the one line left in pencil, waiting a year for the second deposit, and a heavier line than it looks: on their own, the 10 need it to earn about 5.1% a year, and across all 585 starting months since 1962 they'd have come up short in about 56 of every 100 – by about $34,500 in a bad 1-in-20 case.

In January 2028 the first $38,282 of the second deposit goes back to the payments: $2,388 completes the 10 (the top-up), and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries that ink the last line. They pass the escrow agent's test, 'sufficient without reinvestment' – every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash could earn nothing, and all 10 are still paid.

Publishers know this shape. The Authors Guild's model contract lets a publisher hold back up to 35% of an author's earned royalties against returns of unsold copies, then release it once the risk passes. The extra Treasuries are that reserve, except Laura holds it for herself. By 1 January 2033 the reserve is about $444,500, about 111% of what the 10 are worth that day, and if rates do what the market expects, about $45,500 (in 2033 money) comes back to her – about $74,100 by 2042 in a Treasury-bill fund. The certainty costs her a deposit, not a fee.

=== P3 ===
The IPS gives only 2 reasons to change the plan – a missed deposit or a Treasury default – and both are defined tightly, because a loose trigger is just permission to fiddle. A missed deposit means nothing received by 31 March 2028; anything that does arrive is simply a smaller deposit, run in the same order as a full one. If nothing came, no range is stated in 2031 and the 2033 payment is about $3,000 short at today's rates. A Treasury default means a Treasury payment more than five business days late. Those are the only 2 events that reach what the payments rest on – the money to buy the Treasuries, and the Treasuries paying.

Everything else is a watch: checked and logged, and at most a swap of one Treasury holding for another. Each September and October the iBonds funds' notices are read, since a fund can end early (IBTA did, in 2021, 15 days before its stated date), merge, or have its end moved. If an end moves past the payment it funds, it's swapped for a Treasury maturing first, any cost paid from the extra Treasuries, never the shares. The debt limit is logged whenever the Treasury's estimate of when it runs out of room lands within a month of a date the plan needs cash. Nothing is sold.

Market prices? Carry on. A fall in yields matters to the bottom of the range only until January 2028, and to the top only until the 2031 move, so the top isn't said aloud until then. After it, a fall in yields or shares reaches only her flexibility, kept for a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 (about $11,500 more), Taiwan's building costs (up about 3.3% a year over five years, in bursts) or a co-sponsor falling short. Nearer 2033 come the floor fund's December 2032 payout, the debt limit each mid-December and, once the holdings (about $589,700 by late 2032) pass the $500,000 a broker's SIPC cover protects, a second custodian.

=== P4 ===
Laura's plan names only 2 reasons to change, and both are about the ink itself – whether the last of it ever goes down, and whether it holds. A missed deposit means nothing received by 31 March 2028; anything that does arrive is a smaller deposit, run in the usual order. If nothing came, no range is stated in 2031 and the 2033 payment is about $3,000 short at today's rates. A Treasury default means a Treasury payment more than five business days late. Nothing else changes the plan.

Nobody stops looking, though. Printers put small registration marks outside the trim of every plate, check them on each pass so the plates line up, then cut them away with the trim. Laura's watches are the same: outside the page, checked each pass. Each September and October the funds' notices get read: a fund can end early (IBTA did, in 2021, 15 days ahead of its stated date), merge, or have its end moved; if an end moves past the payment it funds, it's swapped for a Treasury maturing first, paid from the extra Treasuries, never the shares. === NOTES ===
Next to the IPS these read a notch too orderly: the team would let a sentence run on with one more aside after a dash, and would probably have let an exclamation mark slip somewhere, which I never quite earned; the 'simply', the 'Carry on' and the dashes feel like theirs, but the density of figures is more mine than theirs. The pencil turn in P2 did real work – 'the one line left in pencil, waiting a year for the second deposit' explains why the $35,894 is not paying twice faster than P1's plain version does – though the Authors Guild reserve sitting beside it starts to feel like a second reference rather than part of the same turn. The registration marks in P4 helped less: they say why the watches never reach the co-sponsors, but plain P3 had already made 'a watch is not a reason' clearer, so the image mostly decorates a point already landed. Topic two holds more facts than 300 words want, and in both versions the middle paragraph slides towards a list; I'd cut a watch before I'd add an image.
