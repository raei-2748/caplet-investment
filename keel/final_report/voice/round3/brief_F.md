## Topic one (P1 plain, P2 with one turn): the operating reserve
What the operating reserve is, why it is sized the way it is, what that costs and what comes back. Evaluate, do not
repeat the IPS. Checked facts (model figures on the 6 Nov 2026 Treasury curve, not forecasts):
- The case asks Laura to set aside an operating reserve at the start of 2033 that meets ten fixed $50,000 payments
  (2033-2042, $500,000 in all) with "a high degree of certainty", from the portfolio alone.
- In the plan the reserve is all Treasuries, bought by January 2028: the ten bought in January 2027 (about $302,300 on
  the bonds and funds the team holds), then the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit: $2,388 finishes the ten (the
  top-up), and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries.
- By 1 January 2033 the reserve is worth about $444,500, about 111% of what the ten payments are worth that day.
- The bonds pay interest along the way, and some money waits as cash between a bond's end and the 1 January it pays
  for (the February 2037 bond ends ten and a half months before the 1 January 2038 payment). Interest earned on that
  waiting money is the one part of the payments a Treasury does not lock in.
- The standard the team chose is the escrow agent's test, "sufficient without reinvestment": the ten are paid in full
  even if every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash earns nothing at all. The extra Treasuries are what makes the
  plan pass it.
- Without them, the ten alone need waiting cash to earn about 5.1% a year. Tested on every starting month since 1962
  (585 of them), the ten alone would have fallen short in about 56 of every 100; in a bad 1-in-20 case, by about
  $34,500.
- Most of the extra money is not lost. If rates simply do what the market expects, about $45,500 (in 2033 money) is
  left over and comes back to Laura, about $74,100 by 2042 if it all stays invested in a Treasury-bill fund.
- Precedent: US law lets a company that takes on the fixed payments of a court settlement fund them with "any
  obligation of the United States" or an annuity (26 U.S.C. section 130(d), "qualified funding asset").
- Never call the reserve an "endowment". Never say the payments "can't miss": say what they rest on.
- **The turn for P2: "the one line that waited, and the reserve that inks it."** From January 2027 to January 2028
  the interest that waiting cash earns was the one pencil line left in the payments; the extra Treasuries ink it. A
  publisher does something with the same shape: the Authors Guild's model contract lets a publisher hold back part of
  an author's earned royalties (up to 35%, for the first four accounting periods) as a reserve against returns of
  unsold copies, and release it once the risk passes. The price of certainty is a deposit, not a fee: most of it comes
  back.
