# Keel's main session, voice round 3 (Mon 16 Nov 2026)

Written straight after re-reading the IPS and the three Trading Note reflections whole (CORPUS section 5), from the
facts in `brief_F.md` only. No warm-up; the control writer.

=== P1 ===
Laura's case asks for an operating reserve set aside at the start of 2033. Ours is set aside five years early. By January 2028 every dollar of it sits in Treasuries – the ten we buy in January 2027, plus the first $38,282 of the second deposit – and by 1 January 2033 it should be worth about $444,500, roughly 111% of what the ten payments are worth on that day.

Why 111%, and not simply 100%? Because a Treasury locks in what it pays, but not what happens to that money afterwards. Our bonds pay coupons along the way, and some of them finish months before the 1 January they're meant for – the February 2037 bond waits ten and a half months for its payment. Whatever that waiting cash earns is a forecast, and a forecast is exactly what we told Laura her payments wouldn't rest on. On their own, the ten need it to earn about 5.1% a year. We checked that against every starting month since 1962, all 585 of them, and the ten alone would have come up short in about 56 of every 100 – by around $34,500 in a bad 1-in-20 case. Close to a coin toss, which is hardly a 'high degree of certainty'.

So we sized the reserve the way an escrow agent would: 'sufficient without reinvestment'. The $2,388 top-up finishes the ten, and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, so the payments are met in full even if every coupon and every dollar of waiting cash earns precisely nothing. US law takes the same view when a company takes over someone's court settlement – those fixed payments can be funded with 'any obligation of the United States'.

Is that wasteful? Less than it looks! If rates simply do what the market expects, about $45,500 (in 2033 money) is never needed and comes back to Laura – roughly $74,100 by 2042. So the extra Treasuries work more like a deposit than a fee: she pays for certainty up front, and gets most of it back once the risk has passed.

=== P2 ===
By January 2027 almost all of Laura's ten payments are ink – figures she owns in Treasuries, each paying on its date. Almost all. One line is still in pencil, and it's easy to miss: the interest her money earns while it waits.

It waits more than you'd think. The bonds pay coupons every six months, and a few of them finish well before the payment they fund – the February 2037 bond sits for ten and a half months before 1 January 2038. What that cash earns in the meantime depends on rates nobody can know, so it's a forecast, written in pencil, inside a plan that promised Laura no forecasts. And it isn't a small line, either: the ten on their own need waiting cash to earn about 5.1% a year, and against every starting month since 1962 they'd have fallen short in about 56 of every 100.

So in January 2028 we ink it. The first $38,282 of the second deposit finishes the ten ($2,388) and buys extra Treasuries ($35,894) – enough that the payments are met even if every dollar of waiting cash earns nothing at all, which is the escrow agent's test of 'sufficient without reinvestment'. From that day, by 1 January 2033 the operating reserve is about $444,500, roughly 111% of what the payments are worth.

Laura's own industry already does something with this shape. Under the Authors Guild's model contract, a publisher can hold back up to 35% of an author's earned royalties for the first four accounting periods, as a reserve against unsold copies coming back – and release it once that risk has passed. Our extra Treasuries are the same idea pointed the other way: Laura holds the reserve, against a risk to herself, and if rates simply do what the market expects, about $45,500 (in 2033 money) is never needed and comes back to her. The price of certainty, then, turns out to be a deposit rather than a fee.

=== P3 ===
Most monitoring advice says to watch the market. Ours says nearly the opposite: from January 2028 nothing the market does can change what Laura owes, so the list of things worth watching is short – and that's rather the point.

Only two things can change the plan, and we've defined both so nobody has to argue about them later. A missed deposit means nothing has arrived by 31 March 2028; anything that does arrive, however small, just runs the same order – top-up, extra Treasuries, floor, shares. We'd ask Laura to confirm it in writing by 1 December 2027, so the one input she doesn't yet own gets checked before it's needed. A Treasury default means a Treasury payment more than five business days late.

Everything else is a watch, not a trigger. Each September and October, the iBonds funds' notices: a fund can close early (IBTA did, in 2021, 15 days before its stated date), merge, or push its end back – and if an end ever moves past the payment it funds, we swap it for a Treasury maturing before that payment, paid for from the extra Treasuries, never the shares. The debt limit, if the Treasury's estimate of its X-date lands within a month of a day we need cash: logged, nothing sold. Our own trade days – Monday 4 January 2027, Monday 3 January 2028, and the 2031 move – with limit orders, and each fund's price checked against what its bonds are worth. And by late 2032, a second custodian, since the holdings pass the $500,000 that SIPC covers.

The market matters less every year. Falling yields touch the bottom of the range only until January 2028, the top only until the 2031 move, and after that nothing but her flexibility – which is where we'd look as 2033 gets close: a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 (about $11,500 more), the building-cost trend, and co-sponsors' budgets. A crash in 2030 lowers a top she hasn't stated yet. We'd carry on.

=== P4 ===
Printers put small marks outside the edge of the page – crosshairs on every plate – and check them on every pass, so the colours land exactly where they should. Then the sheet is trimmed and the marks are cut away; nobody reading the book ever sees them. That's how we think about monitoring Laura's plan. The things worth watching sit outside the page: checked every pass, and never in what she tells co-sponsors.

The page itself has only two reasons to change. A missed deposit means nothing arriving by 31 March 2028 (anything that arrives, however small, runs the usual order: top-up, extra Treasuries, floor, shares), and we'd ask Laura to confirm the deposit in writing by 1 December 2027. A Treasury default means a Treasury payment more than five business days late. That's it.

The marks are everything else. Every September and October, the iBonds funds' notices – IBTA closed 15 days before its stated date in 2021, so funds can end early, merge, or move their end – and if one ever moves past the payment it funds, it's swapped for a Treasury maturing in time, paid from the extra Treasuries and never from the shares. The debt limit, whenever the Treasury's estimated X-date falls within a month of a day we need cash: logged, never sold. The three trade days (4 January 2027, 3 January 2028 and the 2031 move), with limit orders and each fund's price checked against its holdings. A second custodian by late 2032, once the holdings pass SIPC's $500,000.

What's interesting is how the list shrinks. Falling yields matter to the bottom of the range only until January 2028, and to the top only until the 2031 move; after that the market reaches nothing but her flexibility. So by 2033 the marks worth checking are the ones that eat into it – a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 (about $11,500), the building-cost trend, a co-sponsor falling short. A crash in 2030 moves a top she hasn't stated. The page carries on.
