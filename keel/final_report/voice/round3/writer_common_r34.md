# Voice rounds 3 and 4: what every writer does (Mon 16 Nov 2026)

You are practising the voice of six Sydney high-school students (team "Caplet") who are writing a Final Report for the
Wharton Global High School Investment Competition. Their client is Laura Gao, a graphic novelist planning an artists'
residency in Taiwan. The team asked (Book 17): "you must START with OUR voice and style and tone, and move forward from
there. Literally write like we do." They want much more voice practice before the report is written (Book 19).

## 3. What the last two rounds found (descriptions, not rules)
- Round 1: the team's real writing was heard as people "sure of their plan, warm towards Laura, and plainly enjoying
  the idea: explaining in quick bursts, with asides and turns on a dash, reaching a little further than they need to,
  and saving one flourish for the end". Writing that lost was "too finished": "staged set pieces, neat reversals,
  rounded endings", "a teacher's guided-tour structure".
- Round 2: the two writers heard as Caplet's sounded like "the IPS's writers on an ordinary day: earnest, a little
  eager to show what they know, comfortable sounding slightly naive or overstated, with asides tacked on after dashes";
  they "explain an idea rather than polish it". The tidy question, answer and honest-caveat pattern repeated paragraph
  after paragraph pulled passages down. Writers who STACKED several images in one passage (trim, bleed, non-photo blue,
  royalties, Image Comics) were heard as "a skilled adult writer working a conceit", every time: "by the third image I
  was reading the references, not the plan". Yet the core image helped every reader: "one eraser across three pencil
  lines" was called the clearest statement in the pack of why her advances, shares and co-sponsors fail together.
- The team does not hedge: Treasuries may be called guaranteed, locked, set in stone or risk-free, and nothing needs
  "barring a US default" attached (Book 19). Accuracy still matters: never state a false fact.
- The report is written about Laura and to the judges' questions, but never addresses "the judges" or "you, the reader".

## 4. This round's question
Each topic is written TWICE, so the readers can hear the difference:
- **plain**: no conceit at all;
- **with one turn**: exactly ONE turn of the pencil-and-ink conceit, the one your brief names, set up inside the
  passage where it is used (assume the reader has met only the bare definition: ink = a figure Laura owns in
  Treasuries that will pay it on its date; pencil = a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet). Introduce it the way
  the team brings in an idea, "with a bit of relish", once, and let it do one investing job. No second image, no craft
  aside, no pun. The two versions should carry the same facts and argument, so that the turn is the only difference.

## 5. Warm-up (write it in your output file, short)
Take the IPS paragraph that starts "We'll start with the 10 payments". Write a few words of notes per sentence. Put the
IPS away. Rebuild the paragraph from your notes alone. Compare in two sentences: where did yours drift?

## 6. The four passages (about 280-340 words each)
Each is a part of a longer Final Report, which is not a letter: it is an in-depth analysis of the plan, written for
Wharton judges, in the team's voice. It evaluates and explains why; it never just repeats the IPS. Your brief names the
topics. Order: P1 topic one plain; P2 topic one with its turn; P3 topic two plain; P4 topic two with its turn. Do not
copy sentences from P1 into P2: write P2 fresh, so it is a second attempt, not an edit.

## 7. Output
Write everything to the output file named in your brief: the warm-up, then the four passages, each starting on its own
line with `=== P1 ===`, `=== P2 ===`, `=== P3 ===`, `=== P4 ===` and nothing else on that line, then `=== NOTES ===`
with three or four honest sentences on how the passages felt next to the team's writing, and whether the turn helped
or got in the way.
