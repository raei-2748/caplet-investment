### Portfolio Implementation

*“How should we invest when the consequences of our risks extend beyond ourselves?”*

Taking risks has served Laura rather well. They brought her from a career in technology to the pages of bestselling books. Yet while the rewards of her artistic risks are hers to share, the consequences of her investment risks may be others’ to bear.

Perhaps this distinction is best understood through Laura’s own craft. In pencil, a line remains a possibility; in ink, it becomes a commitment. Her portfolio needs both: the freedom to pursue ambitions not yet realised and the means to honour commitments already made.

In practice, this meant putting nearly 90% of our portfolio in WInS, the competition’s trading simulator, into US Treasuries. Rather unenterprising for an entrepreneur, perhaps. But consider what Laura already has: a career whose rewards are uncertain, and a decade of payments whose dates and amounts are not. Why should the former decide whether she can meet the latter?

Our starting point, then, was not a return we hoped to achieve, but ten payments we could not afford to miss. Each has its own Treasury holding, which pays out shortly before that payment is due. Our equity investment serves a different purpose: expanding what Laura might achieve without making her operating commitments depend on that growth.

Wharton gave every team $300,000 to trade, the size of Laura’s first deposit. Her plan, though, invests $450,000: $300,000 in January 2027 and $150,000 more in January 2028. So we built her portfolio as it would stand once both deposits are in, scaled down to $300,000. That is why IBTM, a fund holding Treasuries that mature during 2032, takes about a third of the account. Laura buys it twice: from the first deposit for the 2033 payment, and from the second as the floor, whose payout sets the lowest figure she can promise her co-sponsors, the other funders she approaches in 2031. VT, one fund of shares in thousands of companies worldwide, gets what is left once those promises are paid for. (We built the account in October, before sizing one more set of Treasuries the plan now buys first, so in the plan the floor and VT are smaller.)

Two things the account could not hold shaped the rest. The first was a bond for every year: WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036, so the payments of 2033 to 2036 rest on iShares iBonds funds. Each holds Treasuries maturing in the year in its name, closes in mid-December and pays out what its holdings are then worth – not a sum fixed to the dollar. For 2037 the fund was our choice: a bond maturing in February 2036 would have paid out ten and a half months early, while IBTR pays about two weeks before and cost a little less. The second was a Treasury that pays nothing until it matures. These, called STRIPS, are banned this year, so every Treasury we hold pays interest along the way.

The buying itself tested us once. On 29 and 30 September we found two WInS bond prices about a full percentage point of yield away from the real Treasury market. A yield too low would have had us overpay; one too high could have meant a bond nobody was trading. So before any order we set a rule: every bond’s yield had to sit within 25 basis points, a quarter of a percentage point, of the official Treasury yields. Every bond passed on 5 October, from 10 basis points under to 6 over, and again when the orders filled. Passing ruled out only a gross error, but gaps that small told us we were paying close to the market’s price. The market opened at 00:30 Sydney time on 7 October, and Eric fell asleep before it; by 8 October all eleven orders had filled.

What Table 1 cannot show is the question Section 3 must answer. Each Treasury pays interest years before the payment it is for, and a payment reaches $50,000 only if that interest is reinvested and earns interest of its own until the date. Counted without that reinvestment, the ten holdings as bought pay about $445,400 of the $500,000. And nobody knows what the interest will earn in the meantime.
