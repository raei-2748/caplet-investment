# Voice round 7: blind reading, reader 3

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly, then the thirteen passages in my pack. The IPS writers sound like
students talking straight to their client: warm, quick, fond of the dash, a little grand in places ("immune to
markets", "ever-reliable Treasuries"), willing to joke ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not"), with plain glosses
("In other words", "Importantly for you") and figures kept round. That is the voice I listened for.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the team's dry humour ("which was generous", Eric falling asleep before the 00:30 open) and keeps
  Laura at the centre of each reason, but it is far denser with dates and exact figures than anything in the IPS, and
  it reads like a careful log turned into prose. The closing line names "the knife edge" as if the reader had already
  met it, which the passage never does.
- **Meaning:** The first deposit alone only barely bought the ten payments, so the 2028 deposit was given the job of
  finishing them; the first plan was dropped because a writer's income falls with shares, and the model only priced
  that afterwards; VT was tested against a bar set in advance and kept, now for reasons about Laura.
- **Check question (reflection):** The team dropped the three-quarters-shares plan on 27 September, before any trade
  and "not because of a number", because a writer's income shrinks and comes late in the same downturn that sinks
  shares; the first model came after and only priced it (about 1 run in 31 missing a payment, 40.8% with no 2028
  deposit), showing the danger sat on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (world fund):** It kept the one world fund, VT. Before, VT was just the sensible cheap, diversified
  default; after the tests and Darren's "copout" argument, the team could say why it suits Laura: it is the one
  holding allowed to be wrong, only with the whole market, it needs no opinions (so no third reason to change the
  plan), and a Taiwan fund would stack more Taiwan risk on her building.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves plainly from the one rule, to the IPS's own line, to Laura's words, then walks through the table;
  the tone is calm and reasonable, closer to a tidy report than to the IPS's spark, though "on purpose, even for a
  client who is plainly comfortable with risk" has some of the IPS's directness.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 shows the plan's one rule in practice: ten Treasury positions each owned to pay a payment on
  its date, plus VT as the one row no Treasury pays, so nearly 90% of the book sits in Treasuries by design.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury positions (iBonds funds for 2033-2037, Treasury bonds for
  2038-2042, with IBTM also holding the floor) already pay on their dates; VT does not. The book is built so every
  dollar Laura promises is owned in Treasuries first and only unpromised money is in shares, because, as she says,
  she can't know how a book will do until it hits the shelves.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's voice almost exactly: a confident opening turn, a long run-on sentence carried by its
  own enthusiasm, a slightly grand phrase ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), a student's touch ("as per
  JPMorgan", "just 0.06%") and a dash that hands over to a plain question.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong because no payment rests on it; it buys the whole market
  cheaply instead of trying to pick winners, and its job is to answer how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clean and correct but impersonal, like a textbook paragraph; it opens on pension funds rather than on
  Laura, and has none of the warmth, jokes or direct address that run through the IPS. The IPS writers say "these pay
  what they say", while this says "pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do": same idea, flatter person.
- **Meaning:** There are two ways to cover fixed payments, and the plan uses the simpler one: buy one Treasury per
  payment in January 2027 and hold it, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question (opening page, in part):** The Treasury ladder pays each payment on its date; shares are only
  mentioned as what no payment depends on; it is built this way because holding one bond per payment needs no
  forecast or trading.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** The rhythm is the IPS's: a small practical admission, a pile of adjectives, "so we'll simply ignore",
  then "The catch is" and a trade-off stated with confidence. It sounds like the same students explaining a choice
  they made at the trading screen, a bit breathless and a bit proud.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS had no Treasury bond for a year, the team bought iShares iBonds funds instead; held to
  their end they behave like bonds, though they pay out their net asset value rather than par, in exchange for more
  liquidity.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The content and the quotes sit well with the team, but the shape is more staged than the IPS: short
  paragraphs, a reveal ("The surprise was where the danger had been sitting"), and a fragment built for effect ("Not
  in a crash – on one date."). It sounds like someone telling the team's story a little more dramatically than they
  would.
- **Meaning:** The team's first, share-heavy plan listened only to Laura's taste for risk, so it was dropped the same
  day because a writer is paid less and later in the recessions that sink shares; the model then priced it and showed
  the danger sat on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (reflection):** It was dropped because a writer's income falls and arrives late in exactly the
  recessions that drag shares down; the first model that day came afterwards ("Then we priced what we'd done") and
  showed the old plan missing a payment in about 1 run in 31, or 40.8% with no 2028 deposit, while the Treasury plan
  misses none.
- **Image:** It mostly helped: "our first pencil, rubbed out ... before a single trade had put anything in ink" is a
  quick, vivid way to say the plan was dropped before anything was bought, though calling a whole plan a "pencil"
  stretches the definition, which is about dollar figures.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It keeps the team's humour and its habit of grounding every reason in Laura ("more shares would only add to
  a risk she already carries" is the IPS's own counterbalance idea, and "a third reason to change the plan" ties back
  to its two reasons), and it flows more easily than passage 1. It is still longer and more precise than the IPS
  writers usually are, and the last line again uses "the knife edge" without having named it.
- **Meaning:** Three moments shaped the plan: pricing showed the first deposit could barely buy the ten payments, so
  the 2028 deposit finishes them first; how a writer is paid took the payments out of shares; and a test plus a
  "copout" argument kept VT, for reasons about Laura.
- **Check question (reflection):** The first plan was dropped before any trade because Laura's income is lumpy and
  comes later and smaller in the same downturn that knocks shares down; the first model "only priced the change",
  showing about 1 in 31 runs missing a payment (40.8% without the 2028 deposit) and that the danger was one date,
  1 January 2033.
- **Check question (world fund):** The team kept VT. Before, it was a sensible cheap, diversified default; after
  writing a bar in advance, testing four alternatives and hearing Darren's "copout", the reason became Laura's: it is
  the one holding allowed to be wrong, only with the whole market, it needs no opinions, and a Taiwan fund would add
  to the Taiwan risk her building already carries.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Conversational and well ordered ("and that's on purpose", "it's worth saying what it leaves out"), with
  the team's direct manner; the defining paragraph is careful almost to the point of a lawyer's caution ("owned, not
  merely signed for"), which is a little more guarded than the IPS ever is.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 shows the plan's one idea once bought: ten Treasury rows, each owned to pay a payment, VT as
  the only row not yet paid by a Treasury, and two things the October snapshot leaves out.
- **Check question (opening page):** Every Treasury row (five iBonds funds for 2033-2037, five Treasury bonds for
  2038-2042, IBTM also holding the floor) pays its figure on its date; VT does not, because nothing pays the shares'
  value on a date. The book is built so every promised dollar is owned in Treasuries before it is promised, and no
  payment rests on the shares.
- **Image:** It helped: the definition is given plainly and then applied straight to the table's rows, so "VT is the
  one row in pencil" made the point at a glance.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The first paragraph is close to the team's plain telling, but the second stops mid-sentence for a long
  printer's definition ("a knockout – a print where the colour leaves a hole for the black to fall into ...") and then
  answers it with "an overprint". That is a specialist reaching for a clever comparison; the IPS's own images are
  simple ("a giant stock she can't sell") and never need explaining.
- **Meaning:** The first, share-heavy plan was dropped the same day because a writer's income falls with shares; the
  model then priced it and showed the plan relied on shares being worth enough on one date, 1 January 2033, whereas
  buying the payments first removes that gap.
- **Check question (reflection):** It was dropped because a writer is paid in lumps that come later and smaller in a
  recession, just as shares fall; the first model "came after the decision and only priced it" (about 1 in 31 runs
  missing a payment, 40.8% with no 2028 deposit) and showed the danger was one date, not a crash.
- **Image:** It does not use pencil and ink but a different printing image (knockout and overprint), and it got in
  the way: I had to decode a technical definition inside the sentence before I could follow the point.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is tight, clipped and aphoristic ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year", "Her
  spare money shrinks before her promise does"), the work of a controlled, economical writer. The IPS writers are
  looser and chattier and talk to Laura; this talks about her in short, finished sentences.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 Laura's range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half her shares, moved into Treasuries
  before she names it; any shortfall from reinvesting coupons at lower rates is priced and covered from her kept
  shares first, and only if that fails does the range come down.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** Every move here is the IPS's: an honest concession, then "In other words" and a bold claim
  ("near-ironclad guarantee ... we provide exactly that"), a dash into a flourish ("the restless markets"), and
  "Notably –" in the place where the IPS says "Importantly for you". It sounds like the same confident students
  justifying a trade.
- **Meaning:** Treasury bonds won't grow Laura's money but remove uncertainty by matching the payment dates, and
  because her payments are fixed in nominal dollars, nominal Treasuries match them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The opening deliberately echoes the IPS's first line ("a taste for thoughtful risk ... and that's on
  purpose"), and the middle paragraph ties the image to Laura's own words in a way that sounds like people who know
  her; the long third paragraph, packed with parentheses and qualifications, slows it into report prose.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 is almost 90% Treasuries because every promised dollar must be owned in Treasuries first; the
  ten Treasury rows are ink, VT is the one row in pencil, and the October snapshot leaves out the 2028 purchases and
  the 2031 move.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury positions (five iBonds funds for 2033-2037, five Treasury bonds
  for 2038-2042, IBTM also holding the floor) are ink and pay on their dates; VT is pencil because no Treasury will
  pay its value yet. The book is built that way because Laura states a figure only once it is owned, just as she can't
  know how a book will do until it hits the shelves.
- **Image:** It helped most of the four uses: the examples of pencil (the 2028 deposit before it arrives, her shares,
  her next advance) and the link to her own quote made the idea feel like hers, not a device.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Plain and easy, with the IPS's conversational tics ("and that's on purpose", "it's worth saying what it
  leaves out") and a simple contrast ("VT is different."); it is more measured than the IPS but sounds like the same
  people taking a little more care in a longer document.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 shows the plan's one idea once bought: ten Treasury rows that pay on their dates, VT as the
  only holding whose worth is still open, and what the October snapshot leaves out.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury rows (iBonds funds for 2033-2037, Treasury bonds for 2038-2042,
  IBTM also holding the floor) are money Laura already owns that pays out when needed; VT does not, since its 2031
  value is still open. The book is built so only unpromised money takes market risk, so no payment rests on the shares.
- **Image:** Not used.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like students talking to a client they like: quick, a little grand ("restless
markets", "vain illusion", "near-ironclad"), carried by dashes and plain glosses ("In other words", "The catch is",
"simply"), with a small clumsiness here and there that a careful editor would have smoothed. The long reflection and
table passages in the middle keep some of that warmth through jokes and Laura-centred reasons, but they are more
measured and far denser with dates and exact figures than the IPS, so they read like the same team on its best
behaviour. The lowest either lose the person altogether (the textbook paragraph on pension funds), tighten into an
analyst's aphorisms that talk about Laura rather than to her, or reach for a staged reveal or a specialist printing
image that stops the sentence to explain itself, which the IPS writers, whose images never need a definition, do not do.
