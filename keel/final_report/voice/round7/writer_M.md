# Writer M, voice round 7

## Warm-up (IPS line 13, "In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit")

Notes, one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027 – first deposit buys 10 Treasuries; T-Bonds or iBonds, whichever suits each year; each matures just before it's needed.
2. Held to the end – pay what they say; no price wobble to fear.
3. Today's yields – about $300,000 – the whole first deposit.
4. "Worry not" – plan doesn't need yields to stay put (exclamation).
5. If yields fall and the ladder costs more than $300,000 – the 2028 deposit completes it.

Rebuilt from the notes:
In January 2027 your first deposit buys ten Treasuries – T-Bonds or iShares iBonds funds, whichever suits each year – and each one matures just before the payment it's for. Held to the end, they pay exactly what they say, so there's no price swing for us to fear. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is the whole of your first deposit. But worry not – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ladder ends up costing more than $300,000, we'll simply finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: the notes were tight, so the content held, but my sentences came out tidier and more explanatory ("the payment it's for", "exactly what they say") and lost the IPS's looser asides, like "in other words, your entire first deposit". I also wrote "finish" where the IPS says "complete", which is a small but real change, because the report keeps "complete" for the top-up alone.

=== P1 ===
Our first "Big Idea" was that the first $300,000 alone could buy Treasuries for all ten payments. Our notes called it "very tight", which turned out to be generous. We priced the ten at every close of 2026, and they cost less than $300,000 on only 37 of 214 – all between 10 September and 3 November. On 16 October the room left was about $1,900. Laura can't lean on that, so we stopped asking the first deposit to do it alone: finishing the ten became the 2028 deposit's first job, ahead of the floor and VT. Our orders filled on 7-8 October, within two trading days of the year's cheapest close. That was timing and luck, not skill – the market opened at 00:30 Sydney time and Eric fell asleep before it.

The case pulls Laura two ways: she is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and her payments must be met "with a high degree of certainty". Our first plan, in our notes of 27 September, answered the first half – about three-quarters shares, with six years to wait. We dropped it before a single trade, because of how a writer is paid. Her income is lumpy (our notes said so that day), and in a recession it comes later and smaller, in the same downturn that knocks shares down, so more shares would only add to a risk she already carries. Our first model only priced the change. The old plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs, and in 40.8% if the 2028 deposit never came. The danger it showed wasn't a crash: the plan needed the shares to be in the right place on one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten payments had to be bought with whatever the shares had become. On 28 September we wrote the new plan down with its reason, "better outcomes in bad years", and priced it against the same words of the case before locking it on 1 October.

On Wednesday 30 September, before anyone had objected or we'd run a single number, we wrote down what it would take to leave VT: an alternative had to raise the facility contribution in a bad 2033 by $2,000, or narrow its spread by a tenth, on every run. We tried four – US and international funds held apart, US shares alone, a small-and-value tilt, and VT with a tenth each in gold and property trusts. None moved the bad case by more than about $1,500; gold and property came closest, still fell short in about one run in five, and got half or more of their narrowing by cutting the good outcomes. On Monday 5 October Darren argued that one world fund was a "copout", and that we should pick stocks and ETFs like a normal team; a Taiwan fund and gold both came up. We decided, in the end, to keep the one world fund. Before, VT was a sensible default, cheap and diversified; after, we could say why it suits Laura. It's the one holding allowed to be wrong, and it can only be wrong together with the whole market. It needs no opinions, so it never hands us a third reason to change the plan. And a Taiwan fund would add to the Taiwan risk her building already carries – more than a fifth of it was one company, TSMC.

How Laura earns took her payments out of shares; the knife edge is why the 2028 deposit finishes them before anything else; and the copout argument is why VT stayed, for reasons about her.

=== P2 ===
Our first plan, in our notes of 27 September, was about three-quarters shares. On paper it suited Laura – she is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and she had six years to wait. But the case also asks for her payments to be met "with a high degree of certainty", and her own work is what pulled us the other way. A writer is paid in lumps (our notes that day already called her income "lumpy"), and a recession makes the lumps later and smaller just as shares fall. So we dropped the plan that same day, before a single trade.

Our first model came after the decision and only priced it. The old plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs, and in 40.8% if the 2028 deposit never came. What it showed was the shape of the danger, and it wasn't a crash. The plan was a knockout – a print where the colour leaves a hole for the black to fall into, so if the sheet slips a white gap shows – because it needed the shares to be in the right place on one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten payments had to be bought with whatever the shares had become. Buying the payments first lays them over everything, as an overprint does, so no gap can open. === P3 ===
At 89.55% Treasuries, Table 1 hardly looks like the portfolio of someone with a taste for thoughtful risk – and that's on purpose. Every dollar Laura promises is owned in Treasuries before she promises it, and only money not yet promised takes market risk. The IPS put it in one line: "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares".

Comic pages are pencilled first and inked after, and we borrow the two words. A figure is in ink when it is already owned – held in a Treasury that will pay it on its date. It is in pencil when no Treasury she owns will pay it yet: the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the value of her shares, her next advance. She has said as much about her own work: "As an author and illustrator, I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves." Laura states a figure only once it's in ink.

Table 1 is our competition portfolio: Laura's plan as it will stand after January 2028, with both deposits scaled to $300,000 of the plan's $450,000. We built it in October, before the operating reserve was sized and before half the shares move into Treasuries ahead of the 2031 meetings, so it holds neither the top-up nor the extra Treasuries (those are bought in 2028, at that day's rates). It holds ten Treasury positions, one for each payment: five iShares iBonds Treasury funds for the payments of 2033 to 2037, because WInS lists no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036, and five Treasury bonds for 2038 to 2042. The late-2032 fund, IBTM, also holds the floor, the bottom of her range. Every one of those rows is ink. VT, about 8.8% of the table, is the one row in pencil – she owns the fund, but no Treasury will pay its value yet. The rest is a little cash.

=== P4 ===
The whole plan comes down to one rule, and Table 1 is the first place to test it: every dollar Laura promises is owned in Treasuries before she promises it, and only money not yet promised is exposed to markets. The IPS said it plainly – "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares" – and it fits how Laura herself describes her work: "As an author and illustrator, I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves." Nothing in her plan asks her to promise a figure on those terms.

The table is our competition portfolio, which is Laura's plan as it will stand after January 2028, with both deposits scaled to $300,000 of the plan's $450,000 – "scaled to the model portfolio", as our trade notes put it. We built it in October, before the operating reserve was sized, so the top-up and the extra Treasuries aren't in it; they'll be bought in 2028 at that day's rates. Nor has half of the shares yet moved into Treasuries, as it will before the 2031 meetings.

What it does hold is ten Treasury positions, one for each payment. For 2033 to 2037 they are iShares iBonds Treasury funds, because WInS lists no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036; for 2038 to 2042 they are Treasury bonds. Each is already owned and will pay out on its date. The late-2032 fund, IBTM, also holds the floor, the bottom of her range. Then comes VT, about 8.8% of the table and the one row whose value no Treasury will pay yet, and a little cash. That leaves 89.55% of the book in Treasuries – on purpose, even for a client who is plainly comfortable with risk.

=== NOTES ===
P1 felt closest to the team where it stayed with what we did and let one aside in (Eric asleep, "which turned out to be generous"); it drifted towards a register report where the third moment had to carry the $2,000 bar, the four alternatives and the one-in-five miss, and the explaining paused while the numbers went past. The knockout turn in P2 did a real job – it is the one sentence that says why the first plan's danger was a date and not a crash – but it is long, with the printer's gloss sitting between dashes in the middle, and a listener may hear the printing lesson before the reason. In P3 the definition sat easily because the 89.55% opening gives ink and pencil something to point at; the half-sentence borrowing "the two words" from comic pages is the nearest thing to a set-up and could go if the definition can stand alone. P4 is plainer and a little flatter, but nothing in it needed the conceit to be understood.
