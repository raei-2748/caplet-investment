## Warm-up: IPS line 13

Notes, one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027 - first deposit - 10 Treasuries - bonds or iBonds, whichever fits the year - each matures just before it's needed.
2. Held to the end = pay what they say. Price wobbles don't scare us.
3. Today's yields: about $300k = the whole first deposit.
4. Don't worry - plan doesn't need yields to stay put (exclamation).
5. If yields fall and it costs more than $300k, the 2028 deposit finishes it.

Rebuilt from the notes:
In January 2027 your first deposit buys 10 Treasuries – T-Bonds, or iShares iBonds funds in the years that need them – each one maturing just before its payment is due. Held to the end, they pay what they say, so their prices along the way are nothing to fear. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is the whole first deposit. Don't worry, though – nothing here needs yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ladder costs more than $300,000, the 2028 deposit simply finishes it.

Where mine drifted: I wrote "in the years that need them", which is the Trading Note's reason (no bond that year) leaking in, where the IPS just says "whichever works best for each year" and leaves the reader to trust it. And "nothing here needs yields to stay where they are" is flatter than "our plan isn't dependent on yields staying put!", which has the cheek of "put" and the exclamation doing the reassuring.

=== P1 ===
Our first Big Idea was that the first $300,000 alone buys Treasuries for all ten payments. Our notes called it "very tight", which was generous. Priced on each day's curve, the ten cost less than $300,000 on only 37 of 214 closes in 2026 (every one between 10 September and 3 November), and on 16 October the room was down to about $1,900. Our orders filled on 7-8 October, within two trading days of the year's cheapest close. That was timing and luck, not skill: the market opened at 00:30 Sydney time, and Eric fell asleep before it. Laura's real purchase comes in January 2027, and certain payments can't lean on luck twice. So the 2028 deposit got its first job – if the ten cost more than $300,000, it finishes them before anything else.

The case pulls Laura two ways: she is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and her payments must be met "with a high degree of certainty". Our first plan, on 27 September, heard mostly the first half – about three-quarters shares. We dropped it that day, before any trade, and not because of a number. In a downturn a writer's income shrinks and arrives late, just as shares fall, so a portfolio mostly in shares would sink alongside her (our notes already called her income "lumpy"). Only then did we price it. The first model had the old plan missing at least one payment in about 1 run in 31. If the 2028 deposit never came – and it hangs on her career – that rose to 40.8%. The Treasury plan misses none once that deposit's first dollars arrive. And the danger wasn't a crash. It was one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten payments had to be bought with whatever the shares had become. On 28 September we wrote down a more careful plan – "Reason: better outcomes in bad years" – and priced it against both halves of the case before locking it on 1 October.

VT sets the top of her range, and it had to survive an argument. On 30 September, before anyone objected, we wrote down what it would take to leave it: an alternative had to raise the bad-case 2033 contribution by $2,000, or narrow its spread by a tenth, on every run. We tried four, from US shares alone to VT with 10% each in gold and property trusts. None moved the bad case by more than about $1,500. Gold and property came closest, but passed on only about four runs in five, and half or more of that came from cutting the good outcomes. On 5 October Darren called the one world fund a "copout" and wanted us to pick stocks and ETFs like a normal team; a Taiwan fund (EWT) and gold (GLD) came up. In our timeline's words, "We decided, in the end, to keep the one world fund (VT)." What changed was the reason. Before, VT was the sensible default – cheap, diversified. After, we could say why it suits Laura: it's the one holding we let be wrong, and it's only ever wrong with the whole market; it needs no opinions, so it keeps the IPS's promise of only two reasons to change the plan; and a Taiwan fund would pile more Taiwan onto a plan whose building is already there – TSMC alone was 21.89% of EWT on 30 September.

Two moments changed what we recommend – the knife edge gave the 2028 deposit its first job, and how a writer is paid took shares off the payments – and the third tested VT and kept it.

=== P2 ===
The case pulls Laura two ways at once. She is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and yet her ten payments must be met "with a high degree of certainty". Our first plan, about three-quarters shares with six years to grow, listened mostly to the first half – and our notes from 27 September were already calling her income "lumpy". That plan was our first pencil, rubbed out the same day before a single trade had put anything in ink, because a writer is paid later and less in exactly the recessions that drag shares down.

Then we priced what we'd done. The first model that day had the old plan missing at least one payment in about 1 run in 31. If the 2028 deposit never came at all, it was 40.8%. The Treasury plan misses none from January 2028, once that deposit's first dollars arrive.

The surprise was where the danger had been sitting. Not in a crash – on one date. On 1 January 2033 all ten payments would have had to be bought at once, with whatever the shares happened to be worth that morning.

So on 28 September we wrote down a more careful plan, with the note "Reason: better outcomes in bad years", and priced it against both halves of the case – the thoughtful risk and the certainty – before we locked it on 1 October.

Laura's pages start in pencil and finish in ink, and we borrow both words. Ink, in this report, is a dollar figure she already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on its date – owned, not merely signed for. Pencil is a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet. Every Treasury row in Table 1 is ink. VT is the one row in pencil: she owns the shares, but nothing pays their value on a date, which is exactly why no payment rests on them.

=== NOTES ===
P1 felt closest to the team when it was quick and a little cheeky (the "which was generous", Eric asleep before the open), and furthest when the copout moment had to carry a bar, four alternatives, a near miss and three reasons in 240 words – that paragraph reads more like our working notes than the IPS, and I had to drop figures to stop the explaining from stalling. The turn in P2 sat comfortably because it does a real job there: "rubbed out the same day before a single trade had put anything in ink" says when the first plan died and that nothing had been bought on it, which the plain version needs a clause or two to say. P3's definition costs a short paragraph that P4 doesn't spend, and P4 says the same thing ("no Treasury pays their value yet") almost as fast, so on the opening page the conceit is earning its keep more by what it sets up for later than by anything it adds here. The hardest part of matching the IPS was resisting a neat last line on each passage; I cut one from P2 for that reason.
