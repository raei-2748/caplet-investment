# Voice round 7: blind reading, reader 1

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly, then the definition of pencil and ink in the brief, then the thirteen passages.
In the IPS I hear six people talking straight to Laura. They are enthusiastic, slightly over-sure and fond of a dash
aside ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not", "no price fluctuations for us to fear", "the ever-reliable Treasuries"). Their
sentences are mostly plain and run on a little, with one figurative turn now and then ("a giant stock she can't sell",
"You take risks on the page"). They state choices as choices, not as findings.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Clean and correct, but it reads like a textbook paragraph on liability matching. It opens on pension funds,
  not Laura, and has none of the IPS's asides, warmth or small boasts. Nothing in it is unlike the team, but nothing is
  like them either. Any careful writer could have produced it.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways institutions cover fixed payments, the plan uses one bond per payment held to maturity, so
  no payment depends on shares and nothing has to be forecast or traded.
- **Check question (opening page, partly):** The Treasury bonds and fixed-end Treasury funds each pay a set amount just
  before a January payment. The passage never names the holding that does not; shares are only implied. The book is
  built this way so that no payment needs a forecast, a trade or a share price.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The first line is very close to the IPS. It turns "taste for thoughtful risk" back on the table with the same
  "and that's on purpose" shrug, and it quotes the IPS's own line. After that it moves into a defined image and then a
  long third paragraph full of parentheses and qualifications, which sounds more like a careful editor than the people
  who wrote the letter.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 is almost all Treasuries on purpose: every dollar Laura promises is owned in a Treasury first, and
  only VT, the one unpromised row, carries market risk.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury positions already pay a set figure on a date: five iBonds Treasury
  funds for 2033-37, five Treasury bonds for 2038-42, with IBTM also holding the floor. VT does not, because Laura owns
  the fund but no Treasury pays its value yet. The book is built so that a figure is owned in Treasuries before Laura
  promises it.
- **Image:** It helps. Calling every Treasury row ink and VT the one row in pencil made the split easy to see at a
  glance. The definition paragraph does break up the description of the table, though.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds just like the IPS's writers on a short leash. It has the same breezy confidence ("we'll simply
  ignore price noise"), a pile of adjectives tumbling into a dash, "The catch is," said out loud, and a trade-off owned
  frankly. It sounds like a student explaining a purchase they are pleased with.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS lists no Treasury maturing in a year they bought iShares iBonds funds instead. Held to their end,
  these behave like bonds and give each payment its own instrument. In exchange for paying out at net asset value
  rather than par, they are easier to trade than an odd lot of bonds.
- **Check question (opening page, partly):** Each payment rests on a T-Bond or an iBonds fund. Of these, the iBonds pay a
  net asset value rather than a fixed par amount. The ladder is built this way because no Treasury bond matures in
  some years.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the team's candour and its people ("which turned out to be generous", "timing and luck, not skill",
  Eric falling asleep, Darren's "copout"). But the sentences stack figures and clauses more densely than the IPS ever
  does. The closing summary line also names "the knife edge" without having set it up, which feels like an editor's
  tidy-up.
- **Meaning:** Three moments shaped the plan. The first deposit barely covered the ten payments, so the 2028 deposit
  finishes them first. A writer's income falls with shares, so the payments came out of shares. And a test plus an
  argument kept VT, now for reasons particular to Laura.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** The team dropped a mostly-shares plan because a writer's income arrives
  later and smaller in the same recessions that knock shares down, so more shares would add to a risk Laura already
  carries. The first model only priced that decision afterwards. The old plan missed a payment in about 1 run in 31,
  or 40.8% without the 2028 deposit, and the danger was one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten payments had to be
  bought with whatever the shares were worth.
- **Check question (reflection, world fund):** The team kept the one world fund. Before, it was simply a sensible, cheap,
  diversified default. Afterwards the team had reasons tied to Laura: it is the one holding allowed to be wrong, it can
  only be wrong with the whole market, it needs no opinions so it never forces a third reason to change the plan, and
  a Taiwan fund would add to the Taiwan risk the building already carries.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This has the IPS's voice almost word for word in manner. It uses the same single-quoted 'high degree of
  certainty', the same habit of taking Laura's request "one step further" ("a near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide
  exactly that"), the same personified markets ("restless markets") and the same "Notably –" dash. It sounds like an
  eager student who is sure of the answer and a little proud of it.
- **Meaning:** This Treasury bond (maturing 2037) will not build wealth but removes uncertainty for a fixed nominal
  payment, so the team needs no inflation-protected bonds.
- **Check question:** None fits closely.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The first paragraph is plain and close to the team: the first plan, the two halves of the case, the "lumpy"
  income. The second paragraph then stops mid-sentence to explain a printing term at length (knockout, overprint) and
  builds the argument on it. It reads like a writer setting a piece, not like the people who wrote the letter.
- **Meaning:** The team dropped its three-quarters-shares plan on the first day because of how a writer is paid. The
  model it ran afterwards showed the danger was a single date, 2033, when all ten payments had to be bought from
  whatever the shares were worth.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** Dropped because a writer is paid in lumps that arrive later and smaller
  in a recession, just as shares fall. The first model came after the decision and only priced it: about 1 run in 31
  missed a payment, 40.8% without the 2028 deposit, with all the risk sitting on 1 January 2033.
- **Image:** It does not use pencil and ink. Its own knockout-and-overprint image got in the way: I had to stop and
  picture a printing press to follow a point the plain sentence had already made.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain, orderly and clear. It quotes the IPS and Laura and adds a good closing turn of its own ("Nothing
  in her plan asks her to promise a figure on those terms"). It is more measured and formal than the letter, like the
  same people writing for a judge instead of a client, with the excitement taken out.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 shows the plan's one rule: ten Treasury positions, one per payment, already owned, and VT the
  only row not yet paid by a Treasury. Some 2028 purchases are not in it yet.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury positions (iBonds funds for 2033-37, Treasury bonds for 2038-42,
  IBTM also holding the floor) are owned and will pay on their dates. VT will not, because no Treasury pays its value
  yet. The book is built so that every promised dollar is owned in Treasuries first and only unpromised money faces
  markets.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves in short, paced paragraphs, with a deliberate reveal ("The surprise was where the danger had been
  sitting. Not in a crash – on one date."). That is livelier than a plain report and has some of the IPS's punch, but
  it feels more staged than the letter's easy run-on enthusiasm.
- **Meaning:** The team's first, mostly-shares plan was dropped the same day because a writer earns less in the
  recessions that sink shares. Pricing it afterwards showed its danger sat on one date, 1 January 2033, so the team
  wrote a more careful plan and locked it on 1 October.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** Dropped because a writer is paid later and less in the recessions that
  drag shares down (the notes already called her income "lumpy"). The first model priced it afterwards: about 1 run in
  31 missed a payment, 40.8% if the 2028 deposit never came, and the Treasury plan missed none once that deposit
  arrived. The danger was one date, not a crash.
- **Image:** Mostly it helps ("our first pencil, rubbed out the same day" is a nice fit for an erased plan). But it bends
  the definition: pencil here is a whole plan, not a figure no Treasury pays yet, so the two meanings blur.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the IPS's voice and confidence: "where we allow ourselves to be wrong", the slightly grand "vain
  illusion that we can pick winners", "(as per JPMorgan)", "T-Bonds and iBonds", and a closing question set up by a
  dash. It sounds like the same students explaining a purchase with conviction.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding that can be wrong because no payment depends on it. It buys the whole world market
  cheaply rather than picking winners, and decides how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (reflection, world fund, partly):** It says why VT is held (cheap, whole market, not standing between
  Laura and a payment) but not that it was challenged or that the reason for keeping it changed.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is the fullest version of the reflection. It keeps the team's people and admissions ("timing and luck,
  not skill", Eric asleep, Darren's "copout") and has one line I could hear the IPS writers saying ("certain payments
  can't lean on luck twice"). It is very dense with dated figures and tickers, though, and the last sentence is
  awkward. Overall it sounds like their material put through a careful edit.
- **Meaning:** Three moments shaped the plan: the ladder barely fit the first deposit, so the 2028 deposit finishes it
  first; a writer's income falls with shares, so the payments left shares; and a pre-set test plus a teammate's
  challenge kept VT for new reasons.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** Dropped on 27 September, before any trade and not because of a number: a
  writer's income shrinks and arrives late in a downturn just as shares fall. The first model priced it only afterwards:
  about 1 run in 31 missed a payment, 40.8% without the 2028 deposit, none for the Treasury plan once that deposit
  arrives, with the danger on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (reflection, world fund):** The team kept VT. Its reason changed from "cheap and diversified default"
  to reasons about Laura: the one holding allowed to be wrong, wrong only with the whole market, needing no opinions so
  the IPS's two reasons to change the plan stay two, and avoiding more Taiwan risk (TSMC was 21.89% of the Taiwan fund).
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain and conversational in the IPS's way ("does double duty", "and that's on purpose", "VT is
  different."). It explains one idea and then admits what the table leaves out. It sounds like the letter's writers
  speaking to a judge, calm but still personal.
- **Meaning:** Table 1 shows the report's one idea in practice: ten owned Treasury rows, one per payment, plus VT, whose
  value nothing yet pays. Some purchases due in 2028 are not in it yet.
- **Check question (opening page):** The ten Treasury rows (iBonds funds for 2033-37, Treasury bonds for 2038-42, IBTM
  also holding the floor) will pay out on the dates needed. VT will not, because what it is worth in 2031 is still open.
  That is why the book is built so that no payment rests on it.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Apart from its third paragraph it is the same as Passage 11, and it reads the same. The inserted definition
  ("we borrow both words", "owned, not merely signed for") adds a formal, slightly defensive note the rest of the
  passage does not have.
- **Meaning:** The same as Passage 11, with the Treasury rows called ink and VT called pencil.
- **Check question (opening page):** As for Passage 11: the ten Treasury rows are ink and will pay on their dates; VT is
  the one row in pencil, owned but paid by nothing on a date. The book is built so that no payment rests on it.
- **Image:** It helped a little, because ink and pencil label the split neatly. But the plain version (Passage 11) said
  the same thing just as clearly, and the "not merely signed for" aside got slightly in the way.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is terse and tightly controlled, close to aphorism in places ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise
  does"), with semicolons and colons doing compressed work. It has one very human line ("a number said aloud can't
  quietly be halved after a bad year"), but overall it is more clipped and less chatty than the IPS's writers.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half the shares, moved into Treasuries before
  Laura names it. Any shortfall from reinvesting the ladder's interest at low rates is covered from her kept shares
  first, and the range comes down only if those cannot cover it.
- **Check question:** None fits closely.
- **Image:** Not used.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest (5, 9 and 3) sound like students talking about one purchase they are sure of. They are
short and a little over-confident, and they are personal: dash asides, "simply", personified markets, a slightly grand
phrase, a trade-off admitted in the same breath. That is the IPS's voice. The middle passages (11, 4, 10, 7, 2) carry
the team's facts, people and candour, but they stack figures and qualifications in a more careful, edited register than
the letter. The lowest are either neutral and textbook-like (1), clipped into aphorism (13), or built on a crafted image
that stops the sentence to define itself (6, and to a lesser degree 8 and 12), which made them sound like a different,
more literary hand.
