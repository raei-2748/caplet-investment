First rebuild, from the notes only:

> The building works differently. Most of the 2028 deposit goes into a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032, and

### Reading theirs again

Reading theirs straight after mine, the first thing I notice is that theirs is a promise and mine is a description:
they write "we'll" and "will", with the team standing inside each sentence doing something for Laura, while in mine
the deposit "buys" and the fund "sets" and nobody is there at all. Theirs has a mood that rises and falls – it admits
the ladder eats the whole first deposit, then breaks off with "But worry not" and an exclamation mark, then settles
her down – and mine holds one even level from start to finish, so the reassurance arrives as a "though" that's easy to
miss. Theirs enjoys its own words ("pay what they say", "bare minimum", "the ever-reliable Treasuries", VT's full name
set between dashes), and mine has had every bit of that pleasure cleaned off, which leaves it correct and a bit cold.
In the building paragraph they keep turning back to her ("Importantly for you", "your planned band", "whatever figure
you choose"), so the half promised to no one lands as something done for Laura rather than one more fact in a row.
And their sentences are allowed to run a little long and loose, the way people talk when they're explaining something
they care about, where mine have been trimmed into a summary of it.

### Second rebuild, from the notes

> In January 2027 we'll put your first deposit into 10 Treasuries (T-Bonds or iShares iBonds Term Treasury ETFs,
> depending on the year), each one maturing just before the payment it's there for. Held to maturity, each pays exactly
> what it says it will – so whatever their prices do in the meantime, we've nothing to fear from it. At current yields,
> all of this would cost roughly $300,000 – in other words, the whole of your first deposit. But don't worry – the plan
> doesn't need yields to sit still! If they fall and the ladder ends up costing more than $300,000, we'll simply finish
> it off with the 2028 deposit.

> The building is another story. Most of the 2028 deposit will go into a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, and its
> payout is the bare minimum you'll be able to promise co-sponsors without any doubt at all. The rest will go into a
> world share fund – VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF, thousands of companies in one – and how it grows will help
> decide the most you can contribute. So as 2031 draws closer, the markets can push the top of your range up or down,
> but the bottom won't budge, because it's resting on Treasuries. Importantly for you, half of what the shares are worth
> will be promised to nobody. That half is your flexibility – there for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan
> dollar or a co-sponsor who falls short. The other half will move into Treasuries before your 2031 meetings, so
> whatever number you give co-sponsors will be money you already own.

## Exercise 2: writing as them on new ground

### Passage 1: How certain is certain?

**First attempt (thrown away).** Read straight after the IPS, it sounds like a careful outsider explaining the team's
plan back to it, not the team talking.

> In our IPS we told Laura that, barring a US default, none of her ten payments would depend on a share price when it
> fell due. We still believe that. But a plan can be free of shares and still lean on something else, and it's worth
> being honest about what that something was.
>
> The Treasuries pay interest along the way, and the money has to wait somewhere between each bond maturing and each
> 1 January. One bond, February 2037, matures ten and a half months before the payment it funds, on 1 January 2038. If
> we'd counted on that interest and that waiting cash earning something, the payments would have quietly depended on
> future interest rates, which is exactly the kind of forecast the plan was meant to avoid.
>
> So the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes to the payments. $2,388 is the top-up the IPS promised, finishing the
> ten. The other $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, so the ten are paid even if the waiting cash earns nothing at all. In
> our model, by 1 January 2033 the operating reserve is about $444,500, about 111% of what the payments need.
>
> What could still go wrong? A US default, which nothing in the plan protects against. The iBonds funds pay out what
> their bonds are worth, which is close to a promised amount but not exactly one. And the biggest: the 2028 deposit
> comes from her advances, speaking and licensing. If yields fell a point before January 2027, the ten would cost about
> $334,000, and only the 2028 deposit covers the gap. That is why a missed deposit is one of the only two reasons we
> would change the plan.
>
> Was $35,894 worth it? That's money that won't go towards the building. But the building is the part of Laura's plan
> that was always meant to flex; the payments aren't. We would make the same call again.

### Passage 2: Why half?

> Half of Laura's shares are promised to no one. It's the one number in our plan that no model picked, so it's fair to
> ask why it isn't 30%, or 70%.
>
> Notably, the split hardly changes how much Laura ends up with – at the median, her total moves by about $1,100 either
> way. What it changes is where the money sits: in what she promises co-sponsors in 2031, or in what stays hers. Every
> extra $5,000 she promises at the top of her range costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps. In other words, it's a
> trade – and very nearly one-for-one.
>
> Keep only 30% back, and she could tell co-sponsors about $10,000 more. That's tempting. But her flexibility would
> then cover a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 in only about half of our model futures, which isn't much of a
> cushion for someone whose credibility rests on keeping her word. Keep 70% back, and the cushion grows, but she
> promises about $10,000 less and her range narrows, selling short what her money can actually do.
>
> At half, her flexibility is about $21,300 in January 2033 in our central case. It's there for building costs that
> overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short, and the Taiwan dollar is the one we can put numbers
> on. A 10% stronger one would need about $11,500 more; half covers that in about 93 of 100 model futures, and in about
> 98 of 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983.
>
> It would be lovely if her flexibility grew just when she needed it – but building costs more often rise in the years
> shares fall, so the cushion can be at its smallest exactly when costs press hardest. In a bad 1-in-20 future, it's
> about $9,900. That's why we didn't shave it any thinner.
>
> So half is our choice, not a model's: the middle of the trade, picked by people who'd rather Laura promise a little
> less than ever have to take a promise back.

### Passage 3: Team Reflection, 5 October

> On 5 October Darren called our world fund a 'copout'. He wanted us to pick stocks and ETFs ourselves, and two more
> ideas were raised alongside his – a Taiwan fund (EWT) and gold (GLD). We kept the one world fund, VT.
>
> It would be easy to tell this as the sensible side winning. Honestly, though, Darren had half a point. Buying
> thousands of companies at once is the choice that takes the least cleverness, and in an investing competition, 'we
> bought the whole world' can look an awful lot like not trying.
>
> What decides it for us, looking back, is what the shares are actually for. Laura's 10 payments are already bought with
> Treasuries, so her shares stand between her and no payment at all – they only decide how high her facility
> contribution can reach. Picking stocks would have tied that to whether 6 students in Sydney can pick winners, and we
> had no evidence that we can. The other two ideas felt closer to Laura, which made them harder to wave away. Her
> residency will be in Taiwan, so a Taiwan fund seemed to fit her story – but fitting a client's story isn't the same
> as doing a job in her plan, and one country is a far narrower bet than the world. Gold felt safe, but the safe job in
> her plan already belonged to the Treasuries.
>
> So 'copout' did us a favour, because answering it is still the clearest way we know to explain VT: it's the one place
> in Laura's plan where being wrong would cost her some generosity, never a payment.
>
> The instinct behind the word – that we should be working harder – was right, too. The work just belonged somewhere
> duller. The week before, two WInS bond prices had been stale, about a point of yield off the real Treasury curve, and
> from then on we checked every bond's yield against the curve, to within a quarter of a point, before ordering – the
> kind of unglamorous effort that actually earned its keep.

## What was hard

The hardest part was that my first try always came out correct and quiet. Their writing has a pulse: it gets excited,
it reassures, it shows off a word it likes, it throws in an exclamation mark, and I kept smoothing that away without
noticing, because smooth is what I do when I'm not paying attention. The opposite trap was nearly as bad. Once I could
hear the pulse, I wanted to hand their favourite phrases back to them, and that turns into a costume rather than a
voice. Writing to the judges about Laura, instead of to Laura, made it harder again, because so much of the warmth in
the IPS comes from talking to her directly. And the numbers kept dragging me into a flat, stacked way of writing where
every sentence carries a figure and nobody sounds as if they care what it means for her.
