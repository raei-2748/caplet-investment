### First attempt, from the notes alone

In January 2027, your first deposit will buy 10 Treasuries – T-Bonds in the years WInS has them, and iShares iBonds
Term Treasury ETFs in the years it doesn't – each one maturing just before the payment it's for. Held to the end, they
pay exactly what they promise, so whatever their prices do in between simply doesn't matter to us. At today's yields
the whole set costs about $300,000, which is to say, all of your first deposit. Now, that might sound a little tight –
and it is! But the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are. If they fall and the ladder ends up costing more
than $300,000, we'll just finish it off with the 2028 deposit.

The building works differently. Most of the 2028 deposit goes into a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032, and what
it pays out is the least you can promise your co-sponsors – and promise without any doubt. The rest goes into VT,
Vanguard's Total World Stock ETF, which owns thousands of companies, and how it does will decide how much more than
that you can offer. So as 2031 gets closer, the markets can push the top of your range up or down, but the bottom
stays exactly where it is, because it's sitting on Treasuries. And here's the important part for you: half of what the
shares are worth is promised to no one. That's your flexibility, for a building that costs more than planned, a Taiwan
dollar that gets stronger, or a co-sponsor who doesn't come through. The other half moves into Treasuries before you
sit down with co-sponsors in 2031, so whatever number you give them is money you already have.

### How mine and theirs feel different, read as whole pieces

Theirs reads like people calmly doing a job for Laura and telling her about it, while mine reads like someone
presenting a mechanism to her and working hard to seem friendly while doing it. Their warmth turns up once or twice,
where it has been earned, and the rest is steady and a little formal, the way a careful student writes to an adult
they respect; mine scatters small conversational turns all the way through, and the evenness of that makes the whole
thing feel performed. Theirs stays entirely inside Laura's world and tells her only what she needs to know, whereas
mine let our own machinery leak in (the simulator, which years happen to have bonds) and even invented a worry for her
so that it could then reassure her about it. Read straight through, their two paragraphs walk at one pace, each
sentence handing on to the next; mine has more peaks and dips, as though every sentence wanted to be noticed.

## Exercise 2: three passages for the Final Report

### Passage 1: How certain is certain?

**First attempt (thrown away)**

Laura asked for her 10 payments to be met with a 'high degree of certainty', and in the IPS we promised her something
stronger: that, barring a US default, none of them would depend on a share price or on anybody else's money. That is a
big promise, and it's worth asking how much of it actually holds up.

Most of it does. At the close on 6 November, the ten payments cost about $301,500 – roughly the whole first deposit –
and the 2028 deposit is there to cover the difference if yields fall before January (a full point lower, and the same
ten would cost about $334,000). But buying the bonds was never the hard part. The hard part is what happens between a
bond paying out and Laura's payment falling due. Our bonds pay interest along the way, and one of them, maturing in
February 2037, arrives ten and a half months before the 1 January 2038 payment it's meant for. Somewhere in that
interest and in that gap, a plan that calls itself certain starts relying, without saying so, on what interest rates
will be years from now. And we can't know that. Nobody can.

So the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes to the payments. $2,388 of it is the top-up the IPS promised, finishing
the ten. The other $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, enough that every payment is met even if the cash waiting for each 1
January earns nothing at all. By 2033 the operating reserve should be about $444,500, about 111% of what the payments
need.

Was it worth it? That's nearly $36,000 that could have gone towards the building, and the scenario it protects against
– cash earning literally nothing for years – is an extreme one. But we think it was. A guarantee that depends on a
forecast isn't a guarantee; it's a forecast with better manners. And what's left after that is honest: a US default, a
missed 2028 deposit, and iBonds that pay out close to, but not exactly, a promised amount. Those are the risks we can
name. We'd rather name them than pretend they're not there.

**Second attempt (kept)**

Laura asked for her 10 payments to be met with a 'high degree of certainty', and in our IPS we went one step further:
barring a US default, none of them would depend on a share price, or on anybody else's money. That's an easy promise
to make and a harder one to check, so here we look for every place where the payments could still depend on something
Laura can't control.

The first is the price of the ladder itself. On 6 November the ten payments cost about $301,500, and if yields fell a
full point before January, the same ten would cost about $334,000. The 2028 deposit covers that difference.

The second is less obvious. Our bonds pay interest along the way, and that money has to wait somewhere until each 1
January; one bond, maturing in February 2037, arrives ten and a half months before the payment it funds. If we'd
counted on that waiting money earning a decent rate, the line in our IPS that the plan 'isn't dependent on yields
staying put' would only have been half true. So the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes to the payments: $2,388 is
the top-up that finishes the ten, and the other $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, enough to pay all ten even if the
waiting cash earns nothing at all.

Is that worth nearly $36,000 that could have gone towards the building? We believe it is – not because we expect cash
to earn nothing for a decade, but because the payments are the part of Laura's plan that can't bend, and the building
is the part that can. And with the operating reserve at about $444,500 by 2033, around 111% of what the payments need,
it's a margin rather than money thrown away.

That leaves a US default, the iBonds (which pay out what their bonds are worth – close to, but not exactly, a promised
amount) and the 2028 deposit itself. That last one depends on Laura's career going well, which is the very thing the
whole plan was built to counterbalance.

### Passage 2: Why half?

Half of the shares are promised to no one, and half is a number we chose ourselves. No model chose it for us. What the
modelling can do is show what the choice actually decides – and, notably, it isn't how much money Laura ends up with.

Whether we keep back 30%, 50% or 70% of the shares, the total she ends up with barely changes; at the median it stays
within about $1,100 either way. What the split decides is who that money is for – how much of it she promises
co-sponsors in 2031, and how much stays hers. In effect it's a swap, and a slightly expensive one: each extra $5,000
she adds to the top of her range costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps.

So the question is which risk Laura would rather carry. Keep only 30% back, and she could promise about $10,000 more –
but a Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031, which would add about $11,500 to the building, would then be covered in
only about half of our model futures. At half, it's covered in about 93 of 100, and in about 98 of 100 two-year
stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983. Keep 70% back, and she'd promise about $10,000 less, with a bigger
cushion and a narrower range – safer, but further from giving as much as she responsibly can. To us, 30% asks her to
bet her credibility on a currency, and 70% asks her to give less than she reasonably could. Half sits in the middle of
that trade, and we chose it knowing that.

The catch is that her flexibility isn't at its biggest when she needs it most. Building costs more often rise in the
years shares fall, so the cushion can be at its smallest just when costs press hardest – about $9,900 in a bad 1-in-20
future, which is less than that stronger Taiwan dollar would need. Half makes a shortfall unlikely, not impossible,
and we'd rather Laura knew that now than found it out in 2033.

### Passage 3: Team Reflection – the world fund argument

On 5 October, Darren called our world fund a 'copout'. He wanted us to pick stocks and ETFs ourselves, and in the same
argument a Taiwan fund (EWT) and gold (GLD) were both raised. We kept the one world fund, VT, but the argument taught
us more than the result did.

First, Darren had a point. One fund that owns thousands of companies really does look like refusing to make a
decision, and in an investment competition that's a strange thing to be proud of. If our only defence had been that
picking winners is hard, 'copout' would have been a fair word for it.

But that isn't the defence that holds up. The shares are the one part of Laura's money not standing between her and a
payment, which is why we've called VT the place where we allow ourselves to be wrong. They still have two jobs,
though. They set the top of the range she gives co-sponsors in 2031, and the half promised to no one is her cushion if
the building costs more than planned. A handful of our own picks would have tied both of those to how good our
judgement turned out to be. The Taiwan fund was the most tempting idea, since it seemed to belong in a plan for a
Taiwan residency. Yet the danger Taiwan poses to her plan is a stronger Taiwan dollar making the building dearer,
which is what her flexibility is for. As for gold, it's hard to name a job in Laura's plan that it would have done.

Read again, the line in our VT trading note about 'the vain illusion that we can pick winners' is a little unfair to
Darren. The real reason was never that picking is impossible. It was that this money has a job, and a job like that
needs a fund dull enough to plan around. The lesson we take from it is that the reason which holds up is often not the
first one we reach for, and an argument like 5 October's is the cheapest way to find it.

## What was hard

The IPS sounds simple, and simple turned out to be the easiest thing to fake badly. Every time I tried to sound
friendly I added small turns and asides they never needed, because their warmth comes from being sure of the plan, not
from trying to be liked. The Final Report's job made it harder again. The IPS tells Laura what will happen, but these
passages have to weigh things up, and weighing things up kept pulling me back to my own habits: neat contrasts, and
endings that sound like verdicts. I threw away more endings than anything else. It was also hard to tell their
confidence apart from my slogans, since their own IPS finishes on a neat line of its own, and I am not sure I always
got that right.
