## Passage 1. How certain is certain?

Laura asked for a "high degree of certainty". In our IPS we went one step further and promised that, barring a US
default, none of her ten payments would depend on a share price. That's a big thing to promise – so it's only fair
that we try to break it ourselves.

The obvious attack is the market, and it fails. Each payment is already owned, in a Treasury that matures just before
its January, so a crash in 2030 changes a price on a screen and nothing else. Held to the end, the bonds pay what they
say.

The attack that actually worried us was quieter. Bonds pay interest along the way, and that money has to sit somewhere
until it's needed. One of ours, the February 2037 bond, matures ten and a half months before the payment it funds. If
cash earned very little in those gaps, a plan we'd called certain would be quietly leaning on future interest rates.
Not a share price, but still a forecast – and we'd told Laura her payments wouldn't rest on forecasts.

So the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes to the payments before the building sees a cent. $2,388 finishes the
ten (the top-up the IPS promised). The other $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, enough that the payments are met even if
the cash waiting between each maturity and each January earns nothing at all. By 2033 the operating reserve should
hold about $444,500 – roughly 111% of what the ten payments need.

Was it worth it? Every one of those dollars could have gone to the building instead, and the building is the part of
this plan Laura will actually see. But a facility contribution can come in smaller than hoped without anyone breaking
a promise. A missed operating payment can't. We'd rather name the cost of that gap now than discover it in 2037.

What's left, we'd rather say than hide: a US default, which every promise made in dollars stands on; iBonds funds
that pay close to, but not exactly, a promised amount; and a 2028 deposit that never comes, which is one of the only
two reasons we'd change the plan.

## Passage 2. Why half?

Half of Laura's shares are promised to no one. It's the least glamorous decision in our plan, and probably the one a
judge is most likely to poke at. Why half – and not a third, or nearly all of it?

Start with what the choice actually changes. Whatever share we keep back, they're the same shares, and they grow by
the same amount. What moves is the line between the money Laura promises co-sponsors and the money that stays hers.
We modelled it: each extra $5,000 she promises at the top of her range costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps, and
her total, at the median, barely moves (within about $1,100 either way). So the split doesn't decide how much there
is. It decides how much of it gets said out loud.

Here's what the two ends look like. Keep only 30% back and she could promise about $10,000 more. That sounds
generous – until you remember what the kept money is for: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a
co-sponsor falling short. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 would need about $11,500 more, and at 30% her
flexibility covers that in only about half of our model futures. At half, it covers it in about 93 of 100 (and in
about 98 of 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983). Keep 70% back, and the cushion grows, but
she promises about $10,000 less, and her range gets narrower.

So half is a choice, not a result. No model picked it, and we won't call it "optimal" – there's no such thing when the
question is how much of an uncertain future to promise to other people. We chose a cushion that does its main job
roughly nine times in ten, and we paid about $10,000 of promise for it.

One honest warning, though. You might hope building costs and shares would move in opposite directions, so the
cushion grows just when it's needed. They don't: building costs more often rise in the years shares fall. Laura's
flexibility can be at its smallest exactly when costs press hardest – which is exactly why we kept a whole half back,
and not a sliver.

## Passage 3. The "copout" argument (Team Reflection)

On 5 October, Darren called our world fund a copout. It's hard to say he was wrong to be annoyed. We'd entered an
investment competition, and our answer to "which shares?" was, more or less, "all of them". He proposed picking
stocks and ETFs instead, and a Taiwan fund (EWT) and gold (GLD) were raised too. A Taiwan fund, for a client building
in Taiwan, has an obvious appeal; gold has the appeal of being there when everything else goes wrong.

We kept VT. But the argument mattered, because it forced us to say out loud what the shares were for. They weren't
there to show off our judgement. The payments were already bought, in Treasuries, and so was the bottom of Laura's
range. The shares had one job left: to decide how generous the top of her facility contribution could be. Once we'd
said that, the question changed from "what will do best?" to "what do we need to be right about?" A Taiwan fund needs
us to be right about Taiwan. A handful of stocks needs us to be right about each one. Gold needs us to be right about
fear. VT needs us to be right about very little – only that the world's companies, on the whole, keep making money.

What it taught us was less about funds than about ourselves. In a competition, the temptation is to make every
decision look clever. Darren's challenge made us notice that the most interesting-looking choice and the best choice
for Laura weren't the same thing, and that defending a plain answer well is harder than defending an exciting one.
When we wrote our trading notes later that month, the VT note gave the answer we didn't quite have on 5 October: VT is
where we allow ourselves to be wrong, because it's the only investment not standing between Laura and a payment
deadline.
