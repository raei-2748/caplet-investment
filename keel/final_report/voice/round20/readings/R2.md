## From memory

1. **If every share became worthless:** all ten $50,000 payments would still be paid, and the building would still get $95,000, the bottom of her range at today's rates. The reason is the order of spending: money for everything promised, or to be promised, is put into Treasuries before any goes into shares. Finance calls this dedication, and her plan extends it to figures she has not yet promised. That is the pencil-and-ink idea: a figure is in ink once Treasuries held for her will pay it.

2. **Why the late-2032 fund and the world share fund:** the team's portfolio is Laura's plan as it will stand after the 2028 deposit, scaled down to $300,000. So it includes the floor (more of IBTM, bought again in 2028), which sets the bottom of the range, and VT, the world share fund, which decides how far above the bottom the top can reach. The ten cost about $302,300, roughly $2,300 more than her first deposit. The longest-dated are bought first, and the 2028 deposit finishes the 2033 piece for about $2,400.

3. **Allocation rule and rebalancing:** the order of spending sets the allocation, not a target mix: each promise is bought before Laura states it. They act on three dates only: January 2027 (the ten), January 2028 (complete them, extra Treasuries, the floor and VT) and January 2031 (half of VT moved into IBTM). Nothing is rebalanced in between because each holding is matched to the date of what it pays.

4. **Concentration and career:** the promised money has one issuer, the US government, spread across dates, and the team accepts that because a second issuer could only add a weaker promise. The biggest single concentration is IBTM, about a quarter of everything she holds in late 2032; it carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, because no Treasury bond was listed maturing close to 2033. Her career reaches the plan at one point, the 2028 deposit. A writer's pay comes later and smaller in a recession (self-employed artists', writers' and performers' receipts fell 7.8% in 2009 while world shares rose). So no shares are bought until that deposit has paid for the ten, and the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller with every payment still met.

5. **How well it was bought:** before ordering, they checked that every bond's yield was within 0.25 points of the official Treasury yields, and checked again at the fill; every bond passed. Eleven trades cost $200 in commissions, about 0.07% of $300,000. On 7 October a click hit the wrong row as the page scrolled, and the platform cancelled an order at once, with no step to confirm it. Since then only Ray places or cancels orders. Their timing was lucky (5 October was the cheapest day for the ten since 2002), but the plan does not depend on it.

   **The one point I would ask the team to make clearer:** what happens to the building's money in the cases they say the payments survive. "$95,000 at today's rates" and "the deposit could arrive three-quarters smaller with every payment still met" both protect the ten payments, but neither says whether the $95,000 floor is fixed yet or could still move. A co-sponsor or a campaign committee hears "at today's rates" as "could be less". So I would want one plain sentence on when the $95,000 stops depending on rates or on the 2028 deposit.
