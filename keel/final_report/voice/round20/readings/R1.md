## From memory

1. All ten $50,000 payments would still be paid, and the building would still get the bottom of her range, about $95,000 at today's rates. The reason is the order of spending: money for anything promised goes into Treasuries before any goes into shares. This is dedication (Leibowitz), stretched to cover figures she will announce later.

2. The team's portfolio is set up as Laura's plan will look after January 2028, scaled down to $300,000. So besides the ten payments' Treasuries it holds the late-2032 fund (IBTM, which carries the 2033 payment and, bought again in 2028, the floor) and the world share fund (VT, which sets how far the top can reach). The ten cost about $302,300, a little over $2,000 more than the first deposit, and the 2028 deposit finishes the 2033 piece for about $2,400.

3. The order of spending decides the allocation, not a target mix. They act only on three dates (January 2027, January 2028, January 2031), each time buying a promise before Laura states it. Nothing is rebalanced because each holding is matched to the date of what it pays.

4. All the promised money has one issuer, the US government, and is spread across dates instead. The team accepts this because a second issuer could only add a weaker promise. The biggest single concentration is IBTM, about a quarter of everything she holds in late 2032, because it carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, and WInS had no Treasury bond maturing close to 2033. Her career reaches the plan only through the 2028 deposit. A writer's income comes later and smaller in a recession (2009: self-employed artists' and writers' receipts fell 7.8% while shares rose), so no shares are bought until that deposit has paid for the ten, and the deposit could be about three-quarters smaller with every payment still met.

5. Before ordering, they checked each bond's yield against official Treasury yields (within 0.25 points), and every bond passed again at its fill. Eleven trades cost about $200 in commissions (about 0.07%). After a mis-click on 7 October cancelled an order with no confirmation step, only Ray places or cancels orders. The timing was also unusually good (5 October was the cheapest day for the ladder since 2002), though the plan doesn't depend on that. The one point I would ask them to make clearer is the 0.25-point check. Say it in basis points and say what it proves: 25 bp only rules out a gross error, and it does not show a good fill. If they mean good execution, give the actual gap, which should be a few basis points.
