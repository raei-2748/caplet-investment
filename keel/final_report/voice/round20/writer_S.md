In October we built your plan in miniature: everything as it will stand once your 2028 deposit is in, scaled down to $300,000. Five Treasury bonds pay for 2038 to 2042, each held to its date. No Treasury bond was listed maturing between February 2031 and February 2036, so iBonds funds carry 2033 to 2036, and 2037 as well, where a fund suited better than the bond on offer. IBTM, which closes in December 2032, does two jobs: your first payment, and the bottom of your range. VT came last, because nothing you've promised rests on it. === S ===
**If every share went to nothing**

If every share Laura owned became worthless, all ten payments would still be paid, and the building would still get $95,000, the bottom of her range at today's rates. The reason is the order of spending: every dollar promised, or to be promised, is bought in Treasuries before any money goes into shares. For the payments, finance calls this dedication (Leibowitz, 1986); Laura's plan extends it to figures she has not yet promised. Her comics are pencilled first and printed only from the ink. In the same way, a figure in this report is in ink once Treasuries held for her will pay it on its date, even if their cash earns nothing more and each fund pays out 0.5% less than it holds; until then it is pencil.

**What the portfolio holds**

Our WInS portfolio is Laura's plan as it will stand after January 2028, scaled down to $300,000 (Table 1), so it holds more than the ten payments' Treasuries – which are all her first deposit buys. IBTM, the fund that closes in mid-December 2032, carries the 2033 payment and, bought again in 2028, the floor, which sets the bottom of her range. VT, a world share fund, decides how far above the bottom the top can reach. At today's rates the ten cost about $302,300, $2,287 more than her first deposit. We buy the longest-dated first, so the piece left over is part of the 2033 payment, the cheapest to finish a year later; the 2028 deposit finishes it for $2,388.

**How it was built**

The order of spending, not a target mix, sets the allocation. Treasury bonds pay for 2038 to 2042, and iBonds funds for 2033 to 2037, because, 2037 aside, WInS listed no bond maturing close enough before them. We act on three dates, each time buying a promise before Laura states it. In January 2027 we buy the ten. In January 2028 we complete them and add $36,774 of extra Treasuries, which put every payment in ink, then buy the floor and VT. In January 2031 we move half of VT into IBTM. Nothing is rebalanced in between, because each holding is matched to the date of what it pays.

**What is spread, and what is not**

VT spreads the shares across 10,185 companies (at 30 September 2026) for a fee of 0.06% a year. The promised money has one issuer, the US government, and is spread across dates instead. We accept that gladly, since a second issuer could only add a weaker promise. The biggest concentration is IBTM, about a quarter of all she holds in late 2032: it carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, because WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing close to 2033. Her career reaches the plan at one point, the 2028 deposit. A writer's pay comes later and smaller in a recession: in 2009, while world shares rose, the receipts of US independent artists, writers and performers working for themselves fell 7.8% (US Census Bureau). So we buy no shares until that deposit has paid for the ten, and it could arrive about three-quarters smaller with every payment still met.

**How well it was bought**

Before ordering, we checked that every bond's yield was within 0.25 points of the official Treasury yields; every bond passed, and passed again at its fill. Our eleven trades cost $200 in commissions, about 0.07% of $300,000. On 7 October a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled, and WInS cancelled an order on the spot, with no step to confirm it; since then only Ray places or cancels orders. The year was rare, though the plan does not need it to be: $300,000 bought all ten payments' Treasuries on about 11 of every 100 trading days since 2000, and 5 October, just before our orders filled, was their cheapest day since May 2002.
