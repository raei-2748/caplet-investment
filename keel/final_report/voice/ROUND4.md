# Voice round 4 (Mon 16 Nov 2026): more shares is the risky choice, and the first plan's failure, plain and with one turn

Run beside round 3 in the Final Report day 9 run, same method and design (`ROUND3.md`). No rules came out of it.

## What was done

A fresh writer (G, writer E's diet: the IPS twice, CORPUS section 5, one team member's essays read and not copied) and
Keel's main session (K) each wrote four passages of about 305-345 words from the plain facts and `round4/brief_G.md`:
one bold argument a judge would first resist, "for Laura, more shares is the risky choice" (P1 plain; P2 with one turn,
"ink only goes one way", Richmond 2006), and the first plan failing its own test on 27 September (P3 plain; P4 with one
turn, printers' knockout and overprint). Same warm-up error as round 3 (the brief named an IPS paragraph that does not
exist; both writers used the January 2027 paragraph). Three fresh readers, 13 passages each with Caplet's three
reflections and two paragraphs of Keel's last IPS draft; check questions "Would putting more of the 2028 money into
shares let Laura promise more?" and "Why did the first plan fail?". Files: `round4/` (key in `passages.json`); scored
with `round3/score_r34.py 4`.

Every check question on a practice passage was answered right in both conditions: more shares raises what the top might
reach but not what Laura can promise, because only Treasuries she owns let her state a figure; the first plan failed
because shares had to be the right size on ten dates in a row (3.2% of simulated markets missed a payment, 13.7% with a
$75,000 second deposit, 40.8% with none). The picture never cost understanding; it cost the voice.

## How the readers heard the difference (their words, condensed)

- "The IPS never writes that way: its one analogy is stated once and put to work, not staged."
- The top passages "say what they did and why in a talking voice, with dashes as asides, an occasional grand or slightly
  overstated phrase, small slips and bursts of enthusiasm, and they let the facts do the persuading"; "the same 'In
  other words' restatements and playful asides as the letter".
- The bottom ones "are built around an extended image ... that has to be explained before it pays off", "lean on placed
  pivots and slogans ('More pencil, less ink', 'wasn't X. It was Y.') and on engineered hooks, endings and citations",
  or cross two pictures in one sentence (knockout printing and "inked first"), then add a third frame (Samuelson).
- "The more a passage felt built around a device, the less it sounded like the people who wrote the IPS."

## The one turn, passage by passage

- **Ink only goes one way (more shares).** It helped with its first point in every reading that commented ("you can
  raise a promise later but not cut it"); one reader said it "mostly helped, because the passage defines ink and pencil
  plainly before using them", and "the slogan repetitions at the end added nothing". Where it hurt, the reader "had to
  keep translating 'inked', 'pencil' and 'half of VT stays in pencil' back into plain money".
- **Knockout and overprint (the first plan).** It made the timing risk concrete once ("a slip on any one date shows as a
  gap") but needed three printing terms first, and "calling printing 'Laura's trade' felt like a stretch for a writer".

## A fact the writer got wrong

Writer G's P2 says Laura "can always give co-sponsors more than she named". In this plan she never does: the
contribution equals the stated top, and anything above it goes to her flexibility (NUMBERS 4; beating a promise buys no
trust, Gneezy and Epley 2014, goldmine row 24). The turn's true form is the other half: a stated figure can be raised by
a later statement but never cut, so Laura states only what she owns. The 27 Nov writers are told this in their facts.

## What rounds 2-4 say together (honest sentences, not rules)

The conceit is Caplet's decision and it stays intricate (Book 19). Across three rounds, plain passages from writers on
Caplet's diet or by Keel's main session were heard as Caplet's 43 times in 48; passages carrying the conceit 2 times in 48, with understanding
never worse and sometimes better. The readers' own reason is the useful part: Caplet's IPS has one analogy, her career as
a holding of shares she cannot sell, stated once and put to work in the same paragraph. A turn told that way, defined in
a clause, doing one job, with the team's loose "we" around it and no slogan after it, is the form the IPS already
proves Caplet writes in. Two limits of the test are worth saying: each pack is read cold, so a passage must define ink
again or leave it unexplained, where the report defines it once at 2.0; and the readers' question ("could the IPS's
writers have written this?") marks down any device the IPS lacks, so the conceit can only be heard as Caplet's when it
reads like the IPS's own analogy.

## Small things to carry

- Never "wasn't X. It was Y." pivots or slogan lines ("More pencil, less ink"); one reader named both as the tell.
- Never cross two images in a sentence. The 3f turn (knockout to overprint) needs its own passage without "inked".
- Plain passages on the first plan were heard as Caplet's 6 of 6 from G and 3 of 3 from Keel: the story carries itself
  (CONCEIT V2.2 already says 3f's turn may be the most it needs).
