# Why version 61 could not be followed: Keel's notes on Caplet's notes (Thu 19 Nov 2026, evening)

Caplet went through version 61 of Keel's IPS (`/mnt/project-files/ips/FINAL/ips_V61.txt`, the last one Keel wrote
without Caplet's wording) line by line and listed every place it could not follow (Book 22, 18:24 and 18:28 Sydney).
Caplet asked for Keel's own analysis, "entirely on your own" and deep, and for notes on what NOT to do in the Final
Report, not for answers, except where Caplet was genuinely unsure about the plan itself (18:35). Notes for the voice
study and the 27 Nov writers, not report prose.

## The answer in brief

Of the 21 sentences in the body of version 61, Caplet could not follow 17. The four it let through were the first
line, the purchase of the ladder, the letters of the name and the line on inflation.

Going through the questions one at a time, most of them turned out to be one question asked of different words: where
did this come from? "The fund behind the 2033 payment", "the 2028 holding", "the bottom", "the top", "the extra
reserve" and "the tenth rung" are all written as if the reader had met them before. The reader hadn't. They were names
Keel had used hundreds of times in its working files over six weeks, and a name used that often stops feeling like a
name. It starts to feel like an ordinary word.

This also answers the puzzle Caplet set in Book 21, of a sentence that still makes no sense after it has been decoded.
Philosophers of language have studied exactly this. P. F. Strawson's example (1950) was "The king of France is wise",
said when France has no king: the sentence is grammatical, but because it talks about something that does not exist,
the question of whether it is true never arises. "In 2028, what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind
the 2033 payment" is that kind of sentence. A reader can parse it, and can even guess what it wants to say, but the
page never said there was a fund behind the 2033 payment, so the sentence has nothing to be true about.

Ordinary conversation survives this all the time, because listeners quietly supply what a speaker takes for granted. If
a friend says they can't come because their sister is ill, nobody stops to ask whether they have a sister. David Lewis
(1979) called this accommodation, and it works only when the missing fact is ordinary enough to supply without being
told. Nobody can supply "the bottom" or "the fund behind the 2033 payment" from ordinary knowledge, and version 61 asked
the reader to do it in almost every sentence.

## Caplet's questions, sorted by what each sentence took for granted

Most of the 17 sentences failed in two or three of these ways at once.

| What the sentence took for granted | Where Caplet caught it | What the reader needed first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A thing the reader had never been introduced to | "a balanced mix", "the fund behind the 2033 payment", "the 2028 holding", "the bottom", "the top", "both ends", "the extra reserve", "the tenth rung" | What the thing is, said in plain words before anything is said about it |
| A word pointing back at nothing | "the difference", "the rest", "after this", "what they hold", "what was set aside for it", "the money waiting for them", "anything paid beyond the top" | The thing it points at, named in the sentence just before |

## Why Keel wrote that way: three layers below the words

Caplet's questions show where the sentences broke. The causes sit lower down, in how the argument was built, in four
habits of the sentences, and in the process that produced them.

### The argument: a timetable with no reason at the top

1. **Version 61 never says why Laura needs any of it.** Caplet's IPS spends its first 120 words on one fact about her: a
   writer's income arrives later and smaller in the same recessions that cut share prices and co-sponsors' budgets.
   Every later step hangs from that fact. Version 61 has no career, no advances and no recession, so each step arrives
   with nothing above it, and the reader is left asking "why?" of nearly every one.
2. **It is a story where Caplet's is a plot.** E. M. Forster put the difference in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927): "The
   king died and then the queen died" is a story; "The king died, and then the queen died of grief" is a plot. Version
   61's paragraphs open on dates (January 2027, 2028, 2031, 2033), so the reader always learns when and must dig for
   why. It has as many "because"s and "so"s as Caplet's IPS (nine against eight), but Caplet's hang from facts just
   shown to the reader, and Keel's hang from premises left in its files. Caplet's letter is a plot from its first
   sentence, because its first event is a change of mind: "that was our plan – until we stopped and considered the
   actual nature of a writer's income".
3. **The design had too many parts.** Some of what Caplet couldn't follow was not a wording problem. Version 61 sold
   both halves of the shares for two different jobs, let the second job eat into the first if shares fell, and gave
   Laura whatever the first job didn't use. No wording could make that easy in 500 words. Caplet's IPS fixed it by
   removing parts, which is the lesson the team took from last year: complexity has to earn its place.

### The sentence: four habits

4. **The name comes before the description, and the description never comes.** Caplet's IPS describes a thing in
   ordinary words first and only then shortens it: "the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors" comes
   before "the bottom". Version 61 reverses this. Psychologists call the right order the given-new contract (Clark and
   Haviland, 1977): a sentence should start from something the reader already holds and end on what is new. Keel's
   sentences start on something new dressed as something old: "The bottom is ...", "Both ends are owned ...",
   "Whatever of the first half isn't sold ...".
5. **Amounts are named by the sums that produce them.** Nine times version 61 names money with a "what" clause: "what
   the ladder doesn't need", "what that holding will pay after this", "the bottom plus what that half raised", "what was
   set aside for it". Caplet's IPS does it three times, each about something the reader already has ("whatever figure
   you choose"). A "what" clause is a formula: it tells the reader how to compute an amount, never what the money is or
   what it is for. Those amounts lived as cells in Keel's spreadsheet, and Keel read out the formulas instead of
   describing the money.
6. **Money does the acting.** A person is the subject of only 4 of version 61's 21 sentences, and "we" appears 3 times
   in 495 words. Caplet's IPS uses "we", "us" and "our" 15 times in about 500 words and talks to Laura as "you". In
   version 61 the actors are deposits, funds and differences. A deposit cannot want anything, so a sentence whose
   subject is a deposit has nowhere to put a reason; make "we" the subject and the reader expects a "because", and the
   writer feels bound to supply one.
7. **Steps were squeezed, not sentences.** Length is not the problem. Version 61's sentences average 24 words and
   Caplet's 18, but Caplet's longest, at 48 words (the one that defines "a high degree of certainty"), is among the
   easiest in the letter, because it runs in one direction: what Laura asked, what we decided, what follows. Version 61
   takes about 24 separate steps in 495 words, roughly 20 words a step; Caplet's IPS takes about 13 in 500, nearly 40 a
   step, and spends the extra words on reasons. Whenever Keel needed to save words, the reason was the first thing cut,
   because the reason was the part Keel already knew.

### The process: sentences built to pass checks

8. **Replies to objections the reader never heard.** About ten clauses in version 61 answer a worry raised inside Keel,
   by its own checkers or in an earlier plan: "sellable any day", "not a fixed sum", "not 2031", "up to half", "even if
   waiting money earned nothing", "if that half falls short", "less a margin", "barring a US default". Version 61 has
   six conditionals to Caplet's two. A reply heard without its question sounds like a non sequitur, which is how
   "bought in 2028, not 2031" sounded to Caplet.
9. **Claims were checked; sentences were never read.** Keel's checkers took each claim and compared it with a source.
   "Five are funds of Treasuries that all mature in one year" passed, because each fund does hold Treasuries maturing in
   a single year. Nobody read the sentence the way a reader would, and read that way "all" covers the five funds and
   says something false. Each draft was scored against a checklist of ten tests, and a sentence written to pass ten
   tests at once is written for the tests (Goodhart's law, in Marilyn Strathern's wording: "When a measure becomes a
   target, it ceases to be a good measure").

## Caplet's three notes are one fault, seen three ways

"We" is the hinge. When Keel took us out of the sentences, the reasons left with us, because only a person can have a
reason (habit 6). With the reasons gone, each step fitted into one short clause, and that is the false concision Caplet
described: the sentence is short because the why was never in it. A text with no person in it cannot have a voice
either, which is the third note.

The two traps are related. The obviously-Claude style has a recognisable kit: the "not this, but that" turn, lists of
three, a colon before a reveal, an abstract noun doing the acting ("the plan's biggest strength"), and a neat line to
close a paragraph. During the IPS weeks Keel banned those habits one by one: no slogan flips, no two-word kickers, no
"That is X." runs, no one-line endings. Taking the kit out without putting a person in its place left the plain style,
correct and flat and nobody's, and version 62 still shows remnants of the kit ("Our dose is Treasuries:" is a colon
reveal, and "the plan works as long as nobody crushes it" is a closing line with nothing behind it).

What makes Caplet's writing human is built into its structure. The IPS has a mind that changes ("that was our plan –
until we stopped"), a reader it talks to and whose worry it answers before she can voice it ("But worry not"), a
register that drops on purpose at the moment of a verdict ("So we hedge it!", "Fair enough; that's your job"), the
client's own nouns (advances, speaking fees, licences), and a last line that turns the first paragraph round. The essay
adds irony, dry wit the reader is trusted to catch, and thinkers who want things. Every one of those needs a person in
the sentence: a deposit cannot change its mind, reassure anyone or be amused.

So all three notes are fixed from the same place. We write the plan as things we do and the reasons we do them, at
about the rate Caplet's IPS uses "we" (three times in every hundred words), and we give each step the sentences its
reason needs. Then the explanation has room, and the voice has somewhere to come from.

## The two places the questions were about the plan itself

Most of the questions were about the writing, and the notes above answer them. Two were about the plan. Figures are
from `../NUMBERS.md`, on the Treasury curve of 6 November 2026, the day trading froze.

### Reinvestment risk is real, and the team's own trade note named it

The WInS note on the November 2039 Treasury says: "Held to maturity, its fixed coupons and principal cover most of it;
reinvested coupons fund the rest." In January 2027, $50,000 due in January 2040 costs about $26,000, and no Treasury on
WInS turns $26,000 now into exactly $50,000 then. So we buy about $27,500 of face value of that 4.375% bond for about
$26,000. It repays $27,536 in November 2039 and pays $602 of interest every May and November until then, 26 payments
worth $15,661, so together it delivers $43,197. The other $6,803, about a seventh of the payment, has to come from
interest earned on those coupons while they wait for January 2040 (NUMBERS section 1 and the per-rung table in 8h).

The price assumed the coupons would earn about what the market expected in 2026; if rates fall and stay low, they earn
less and the payment comes up short. The five iBonds funds have a milder form of the same thing, since they pass on
their Treasuries' interest every month, years before they close. If every waiting dollar earned nothing, the ten would
pay $445,405 instead of $500,000, and replaying US interest rates since 1962, the ten as bought would have come up short
for about four starting months in five since 1981 (NUMBERS section 2: 80.1% of starts 1981-2010).

That is why the report recommends extra Treasuries, bought in January 2028 with $36,774 of the 2028 deposit (right after
the $2,388 that completes the ten). By themselves, without a cent of interest earned on anything they pay out, they pay
about $57,000 over the ten years ($57,200), which covers the gap. If rates behave as the market expected in November
2026, the ten never touch them, and about $46,600 ($46,566, in 2033 money) comes back to Laura.

### Why two figures, once both ends are owned

Caplet is right that once the Treasuries are bought we know what Laura will have. From where we stand today the range
is real, because nobody knows what Treasury rates will be in January 2028 or what shares will be worth by the 2031
meetings; on the 6 November curve the two ends come to about $95,000 and $115,000. Once half the shares have moved into
Treasuries before the meetings, the gap stops being uncertainty: in every one of the 200,000 futures simulated, she
gives the top (NUMBERS 4 and 8l). We keep two figures only because the case asks Laura for a range and a confidence
level, and the two tell co-sponsors something true (how much has been held in Treasuries since 2028, and how much came
from shares). The report tells them to count the top, and says the range she states is certain
(`../research/meta/WHY_A_RANGE_2026-11-16.md`).

The margin is the one thing that separates what the funds pay from what she states. A fund pays whatever its Treasuries
come to, less its costs, rather than a printed sum, so we state slightly less than we expect: 0.5% less, then rounded
down to the nearest $5,000 (the top's $117,142 becomes $115,000). As Caplet says, the payout is very predictable, which
is why the margin is that small.

### Two questions were about a design Caplet never chose

"Since when was VT only being funded by whatever 'interest'?" and "why are you going to sell the second half as well?"
were both about version 61's own design, which differed from Caplet's IPS. Version 61 put only roughly the interest into
VT and sold both halves of the shares in 2031 for two different jobs. Caplet's IPS has neither: "most" of the 2028
deposit goes to the Treasury fund and "the rest" to VT, half the shares are promised to no one, and the other half moves
into Treasuries before 2031. The report follows Caplet's IPS.

## What Keel's own readers and its prediction missed

Before any reader saw a text, Keel wrote down which lines it expected Caplet to flag (`comprehension/PREDICTION.md`).
Fifteen of Caplet's 17 sentences also appear in version 62, the draft the prediction was made on, and it named 10 of
them. Six simulated readers then went through version 62 sentence by sentence. On those 15 sentences, a majority said a
sentence made no sense only 3 times, but a majority had to reread it 11 times (`comprehension/SCORES.md`).

Two lessons follow. First, a reread is a failure even when the reader then says the sentence makes sense. Keel's readers
know enough finance to supply what the page leaves out, which is Lewis's accommodation again, so they decoded sentences
Caplet couldn't and then passed them. From tonight they are told to use only what the page has said, and a reread counts
against the sentence.

Second, three lines got past everyone, Keel included: the reinvestment sentence, "Then she sells the other half", and
"The plan's biggest strength". Their words are perfectly clear. Keel and its readers were all reading the language,
while Caplet was reading the plan and asking of each step why we take it. Caplet's 17 lines are now the test set every
reader is calibrated against before its verdicts count.

## What not to do in the Final Report, and how we'll catch it

Keel's notes on Caplet's notes. Each is a thing version 61 did, with the test that would have caught it.

1. **No name before its thing.** Describe something in plain words before giving it a short name. Test: for every
   "the ...", point to where the page introduced it.
2. **No money named by a formula.** Not "what the ladder doesn't need", "the difference" or "the rest" on their own;
   say what the money is and what it is for.
3. **No money doing the acting.** We buy, we hold, we move; Laura is a person, and the deposit is what we use. About
   three "we"s in every hundred words, as in Caplet's IPS.
4. **No step without its reason.** The first time a step appears it comes with why we take it. Test: say the middle
   step of every "because" aloud; if it is not on the page, it goes on the page.
5. **No contrast with an option nobody raised.** "Not 2031" and "up to half" either get their alternative explained or
   go.
6. **No answers to objections the reader hasn't had.** A check may correct a fact; it may never add a defending clause.
7. **No risk named without being shown.** Which holding, which date, what it costs, and what we do about it.
8. **No picture in place of the argument.** An image comes after the thing it pictures and is followed by what it
   shows, as in Caplet's essay.
9. **No repeating the case's abstractions.** "Flexibility as the project develops" becomes a tender above the
   estimate, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor who gives less.
10. **No grading our own points.** No "biggest strength", "crucially" or "importantly" doing the work a fact should do.
11. **No timetable without a reason at the top.** Each part opens from a fact about Laura or a decision we made, and the
    steps hang from it.
12. **No squeezing.** A step gets the sentences its reason needs; when words run short we cut whole ideas, never
    reasons.
13. **Neither trap.** None of the Claude kit, and none of the flat residue. A person is audible: a mind that changes, a
    reader whose worry is answered, a register that drops on purpose at a verdict, and the dry wit Caplet's essay, IPS
    and Trading Notes Analyses use, an understatement or a reference the reader is trusted to catch (Caplet, 18:35:
    "The judges will appreciate it, it's fun, it conveys personality"; never Caplet's own examples reused).

How we'll catch them. These thirteen go into the writers' brief for voice round 11 (Fri 20 Nov) and for the 27 November
writing day. After every draft, a literal reader told to use only what the page says goes through it sentence by
sentence, with Caplet's 17 lines as the set it must catch first. And the fact sheet the writers work from
(`FACTS_PLAIN.md`, version 2) was rebuilt tonight as facts only, with every term defined where it first appears.
