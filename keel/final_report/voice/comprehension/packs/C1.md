# Reader pack C1 (comprehension study, Thu 19 Nov 2026)

A fact sheet written so that writers can describe the client's plan without reading the planners' working files. Writers will build report sentences from it. Judge each sentence as a writer would meet it: can you understand it, and does it make sense?

**Your role.** You read as a strictly literal reader. You take every word at its ordinary everyday meaning and refuse to repair what the writer left out. If a sentence says a payment 'costs' something, a range 'is owned', or money 'goes into' a fund at a time when it does not yet exist, you report what it literally says, and then say whether that literal claim makes sense.

**The case, in brief (what every reader of these drafts already knows).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator
with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000
into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her
book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay
$50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments, the "operating payments" or "operating commitment")
to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money
aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards
the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she
tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is.
Overpromising would hurt her credibility. Teams in a high-school investment competition run by Wharton advise her: in
early November 2026 each team submitted a 50-word pitch and a 500-word investment policy statement (IPS). Teams could
use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds. WInS is the competition's trading simulator.

## How to read

You are one of several independent readers. You are not on the writer's side and you are not their colleague. Read
the way an intelligent person reads a document they were handed: once, at a normal pace, in order. Do not open any
other file in the repository, do not look anything up, and do not try to find out who wrote what.

1. **First, read each text straight through once**, as it appears under "The texts", without the numbers. Note where
   you stalled, went back, or gave up.
2. **Then go sentence by sentence** through the numbered list and fill one table row per sentence:
   - **Follow**: 1 = understood the first time; 2 = had to read it again or hold something in mind to get it;
     3 = had to rebuild the chain of reasoning, or still unsure what it says.
   - **Sense**: once you have understood it (or done your best), does what it says make sense?
     Y = yes, it is clear what it claims and the claim holds together.
     N = no. Then give the fault in a few words of your own, e.g. "doesn't follow" (the because/so does not connect),
     "impossible as stated" (describes something that cannot happen, e.g. in the wrong order in time), "circular or
     empty" (true of anything, says nothing), "wrong kind of thing" (gives something a property it can't have),
     "points at nothing" (an it/they/that/the ones with no clear owner), "unexplained term", "answers a question nobody
     asked", or anything else that fits better.
     ? = cannot judge.
   - **Plain paraphrase**: what the sentence says, in your own everyday words, in one line. If you had to guess, write
     "guess:" first. If the paraphrase needs words the sentence did not give you, put them in [brackets].
   - **Why** (only when Follow is 2-3 or Sense is N or ?): one short line, in your own words.
3. **Do not be charitable.** If you find yourself working out what the writer must have meant, the sentence did not
   say it: score Follow 2 or 3, write what you worked out as the paraphrase, then judge whether that meaning makes
   sense. Equally, do not invent faults: a sentence that is clear and holds together gets 1 and Y, however plain or
   however elaborate its vocabulary. Long words, formal register and complex sentences are not faults in themselves;
   only failing to be understood, or failing to make sense once understood, is a fault.
4. **At the end**, write three short sections in your own words:
   - **The sentences that made least sense** (up to eight, by number): for each, what exactly is wrong with it, as you
     would explain it to the writer.
   - **What the clearest text does that the others don't** (name it by its letter): a few sentences, with examples.
   - **Any pattern** you noticed in the sentences that failed.

Write your answers to the file named in your instructions, as Markdown: one section per text, each with its table
(columns: # | Follow | Sense | Fault | Plain paraphrase | Why), then the three closing sections.

## The client and the case
- Laura Gao, a Wharton graduate who left tech to write and illustrate books (*The Wuhan I Know*, the graphic memoir
  *Messy Roots*). She is planning a creative residency in Taiwan for artists, writers and designers, opening in 2033.
- Money in: $300,000 at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028 (from book advances, speaking and
  licensing, so it depends on her career going well). Nothing else comes in or goes out before 2033.
- Money out: ten payments of $50,000, one at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042, to run the residency. These must
  be met "with a high degree of certainty", from the portfolio alone.
- After that she wants to give as much as she responsibly can towards the building (the "facility contribution"),
  while keeping flexibility. In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders) a range in dollars and how confident she is.
  Overpromising would damage her credibility.
- Caplet's plan (the IPS, frozen): the ten payments are bought with Treasuries in January 2027; the 2028 deposit first
  finishes that job, then most of it buys a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032 (the bottom of her range), and the
  rest buys VT, a world share fund (which sets the top). Half the shares are promised to no one; the other half moves
  into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. The IPS names only two reasons to change the plan (l.17): a missed deposit or a Treasury
  default; "A fall in the market is not one of them." (Struck Wed 18 Nov evening, coherence reader 10, L1 and H2: "Only two
  things would change the plan:"; the two reasons are not the only ways a promise can fail, card Q109, NUMBERS 8u.)

## The building and the range
- The late-2032 Treasury fund (the floor) pays about $102,000 in mid-December 2032 at today's rates. The bottom of the
  range is lower, $95,000, because it counts that payout as if no more interest were earned, less 0.5%, rounded down:
  the fund's payout is not fixed (Trading Note 2). VT, half of which moves into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings,
  decides how far above the bottom the range reaches (coherence reader 6, Mon 16 Nov, M5).
- On today's figures, the range she would state in 2031 is $95,000 to $115,000 (she states it in $5,000 steps, each
  end rounded down). The bottom is set when the floor is bought in January 2028 and stays put after that ("the bottom
  will remain constant", IPS l.15); before then the rate on the day decides it. The top, $115,000, is her facility
  contribution and the figure co-sponsors count; the bottom is the part held since January 2028. Once both ends are
  owned, the range she states is certain; never put "certain" in the same sentence as the figures, and never
  "secured" for either end (funder test, Mon 16 Nov evening; coherence reader 6 and bond desk 4, M1).
- Her flexibility (the half of the shares promised to no one) is about $21,200 in January 2033, centrally; about
  $28,700 if you add whatever the funds pay above the top of the range. In a bad 1-in-20 future it is about $9,600.
- It is meant for three things the IPS names: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor
  falling short. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 would need about $11,500 more; her flexibility covers that
  in about 93 of 100 model futures, and in about 98 of 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983.

## Why half (the trade-off the IPS chose)
- Keep 30% of the shares back instead, and she could promise about $5,000 more (one $5,000 step; $10,000 before the Tue 17 Nov evening re-size), but her flexibility would cover a
  10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half the futures.
- Keep 70% back, and she would promise about $10,000 less, with more flexibility and a narrower range.
- Each $5,000 more of promised top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps. The total she ends up with barely changes
  (within about $1,100 either way at the median). So the split decides what is promised and what stays hers, not how
  much there is. Half is the middle of that trade, chosen by the team; it is not "optimal" and no model chose it.
- Building costs rise about four years in five whatever shares do, so her flexibility is not a "natural hedge". But a
  share slump followed by a cost burst can catch her: world shares bought at the start of 2000 were down about 41% at
  the 2003 meetings, and Taiwan's building costs then rose about 18% in Taiwan dollars before the contribution (cards
  Q66, Q85; NUMBERS 8q; coherence reader 6, Mon 16 Nov, L10; never "cushion").

## The conceit Caplet chose for the report: pencil and ink (Sat 14 Nov; developed further Sun 15 Nov night)
- Laura is a graphic novelist and illustrator. Comic pages are drawn in pencil first, which can be rubbed out, then
  inked; only the ink is printed. In the report, ink is a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it
  on its date, and pencil is a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet (her future income, the 2028 deposit before it
  arrives, the value of her shares before half of them move, interest on cash waiting, every forecast). She owns the
  shares; no Treasury pays their value. Laura only states a figure once it's inked. That is the IPS's own rule:
  "whatever figure you choose is money you already own". Say "owned" when you define it: most readers hear "inked" as
  "signed".
- Caplet's own words about the conceit: "It is intricate. It involves strikingly dissimilar parallels. It offers a
  startling new perspective." So it is not a word dropped in now and then: it is worked through, each turn showing
  something new. The turns you can use (all checked):
  - **One eraser, three lines.** A recession can shrink three pencil lines in the same downturn (never "rub out": IPS l.9 says
    "later and smaller"): her book advances,
    share prices, and co-sponsors' budgets (not always in the same months, and never every co-sponsor). That's why the
    promised money can't sit in pencil (coherence reader 6, Mon 16 Nov, M4).
  - **Finished before it's offered.** Comic publishers want several issues finished before a series is offered to
    shops ("three completed issues", Image, 2015). Laura's ten payments are bought in January 2027, six years before
    the first one goes out.
  - **The one line that waited.** From January 2027 to January 2028 the interest cash earns while it waits was the one
    pencil line in the payments; the extra Treasuries in January 2028 ink it. What comes back from them is worth about
    $46,600 in 2033 money if rates hold, all of it hers by 2042; if cash waiting earns nothing, none beyond the 0.5%
    margin for fund costs (never "most comes back"; coherence reader 8, M8). Publishers do something similar: they hold back part of an author's
    royalties against returns and release it once the risk passes.
  - **Ink only goes one way.** You can always add ink, but you can't take it off cleanly (a mistake is painted over and
    the original keeps the record). So every end of the range is rounded down, anything above the top goes to her
    flexibility, and half the shares stay pencil on purpose: co-sponsors never see them. Each $5,000 more she states
    costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps.
  - **Trim, bleed and safe area.** Printers run the art past the line where the page is cut, because the blade wanders,
    and keep the words well inside it. The stated top is the cut line; the 0.5% held back and the rounding down are the
    bleed; the bottom sits safely inside.
  - **Non-photo blue.** Artists drew construction lines in a light blue that the printing camera couldn't see. The
    report's models, the 7.5% and every median are like that: the judges see them, the co-sponsors in 2031 never will.
  - **The printed page.** Laura's words to co-sponsors have no comics words in them at all, because the printed page
    only shows ink.
  - **Erase every pencil line and the page still reads.** From January 2028, when the only pencil left is the shares (dated Wed 18 Nov evening, coherence reader 10, M4: the 2028 deposit is pencil until it arrives), if the shares went to zero, the ten payments wouldn't change
    and the range would be one figure, the bottom (no payout figure here, as in SKELETON and CONCEIT since Tue 17 Nov).
  - **Dot gain.** A press always prints a little darker than the file, so printers lighten the file first and the print
    lands where they want it. The funds' fee, the 0.5% held back and the rounding down are taken out before the range is
    stated, so the money comes out lighter than the figure she names only if the funds' other costs pass the room
    held back under the top (the 0.5% margin plus the rounding down, about 2% of what the fund pays, in all, never a year, on today's figures (Wed 18 Nov evening, bond desk 5, M1); never "the margin" for the whole room; "about 3%" before the Tue 17 Nov evening re-size, coherence
    reader 8, M2) (coherence reader 6, M1; bond desk 4, H1, Mon 16 Nov).
  - (Retired Tue 17 Nov after voice round 6: "let the ink dry" and the key plate needed decoding; do not use them. The
    plain fact stays: the top is stated only once the shares have moved, and a top stated a year early would be more
    than the move could buy in about 11 of 100 model futures.)
  - **Registration marks.** Printers put marks outside the page, check them on every pass, and cut them off. The things
    the team watches (the deposit, late payments, a fund closing early, the Taiwan dollar) are like that: checked, never
    in Laura's words.
  - (Struck Tue 17 Nov evening, history only, never offered to writers: the seal, which makes ink heard as signed;
    knockout and overprint, and pencils printed beside the inks, which call the first plan pencil; the key plate, retired
    by voice round 6. Coherence reader 8, M7. The plain facts stay: the first plan's danger was one date, 1 January 2033,

  - **In a market, a click is ink** (Section 2, how the plan was bought, not the Team Reflection): on 7 October a click
    landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled, and the order was gone with no step to confirm it.
- The dates: most of each payment is inked in January 2027, when the ten Treasuries are bought ("held to the end,
  these pay what they say", IPS l.13). The rest is inked in January 2028 by the first $39,162 of the 2028 deposit (the

### The conceit Caplet chose for the report: pencil and ink (Sat 14 Nov; developed further Sun 15 Nov night)

- **F66** Laura is a graphic novelist and illustrator.
- **F67** Comic pages are drawn in pencil first, which can be rubbed out, then inked; only the ink is printed.
- **F68** In the report, ink is a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on its date, and pencil is a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet (her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the value of her shares before half of them move, interest on cash waiting, every forecast).
- **F69** She owns the shares; no Treasury pays their value.
- **F70** Laura only states a figure once it's inked.
- **F71** That is the IPS's own rule: "whatever figure you choose is money you already own".
- **F72** Say "owned" when you define it: most readers hear "inked" as "signed".
- **F73** Caplet's own words about the conceit: "It is intricate.
- **F74** It involves strikingly dissimilar parallels.
- **F75** It offers a startling new perspective."
