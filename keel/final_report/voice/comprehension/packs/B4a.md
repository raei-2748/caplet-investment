# Reader pack B4a (comprehension study, Thu 19 Nov 2026)

Short passages drafted for a competition team's Final Report about the same client (the team evaluates its own plan, recommends figures, and writes words the client could say to co-sponsors). A few passages are the team's earlier short reflections on its own trades. They come from different writers. Each passage is read on its own, as if it were a page of the report you had just turned to.

**Your role.** You read as a sharp final-year school student who writes top-grade analytical English essays and argues for a living in class. You know the case below well but no finance beyond it. You are impatient with sentences that make you work for nothing, and you are not intimidated by a formal or elevated register.

**The case, in brief (what every reader of these drafts already knows).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator
with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000
into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her
book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay
$50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments, the "operating payments" or "operating commitment")
to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money
aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards
the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she
tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is.
Overpromising would hurt her credibility. Teams in a high-school investment competition run by Wharton advise her: in
early November 2026 each team submitted a 50-word pitch and a 500-word investment policy statement (IPS). Teams could
use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds. WInS is the competition's trading simulator.

## How to read

You are one of several independent readers. You are not on the writer's side and you are not their colleague. Read
the way an intelligent person reads a document they were handed: once, at a normal pace, in order. Do not open any
other file in the repository, do not look anything up, and do not try to find out who wrote what.

1. **First, read each text straight through once**, as it appears under "The texts", without the numbers. Note where
   you stalled, went back, or gave up.
2. **Then go sentence by sentence** through the numbered list and fill one table row per sentence:
   - **Follow**: 1 = understood the first time; 2 = had to read it again or hold something in mind to get it;
     3 = had to rebuild the chain of reasoning, or still unsure what it says.
   - **Sense**: once you have understood it (or done your best), does what it says make sense?
     Y = yes, it is clear what it claims and the claim holds together.
     N = no. Then give the fault in a few words of your own, e.g. "doesn't follow" (the because/so does not connect),
     "impossible as stated" (describes something that cannot happen, e.g. in the wrong order in time), "circular or
     empty" (true of anything, says nothing), "wrong kind of thing" (gives something a property it can't have),
     "points at nothing" (an it/they/that/the ones with no clear owner), "unexplained term", "answers a question nobody
     asked", or anything else that fits better.
     ? = cannot judge.
   - **Plain paraphrase**: what the sentence says, in your own everyday words, in one line. If you had to guess, write
     "guess:" first. If the paraphrase needs words the sentence did not give you, put them in [brackets].
   - **Why** (only when Follow is 2-3 or Sense is N or ?): one short line, in your own words.
3. **Do not be charitable.** If you find yourself working out what the writer must have meant, the sentence did not
   say it: score Follow 2 or 3, write what you worked out as the paraphrase, then judge whether that meaning makes
   sense. Equally, do not invent faults: a sentence that is clear and holds together gets 1 and Y, however plain or
   however elaborate its vocabulary. Long words, formal register and complex sentences are not faults in themselves;
   only failing to be understood, or failing to make sense once understood, is a fault.
4. **At the end**, write three short sections in your own words:
   - **The sentences that made least sense** (up to eight, by number): for each, what exactly is wrong with it, as you
     would explain it to the writer.
   - **What the clearest text does that the others don't** (name it by its letter): a few sentences, with examples.
   - **Any pattern** you noticed in the sentences that failed.

Write your answers to the file named in your instructions, as Markdown: one section per text, each with its table
(columns: # | Follow | Sense | Fault | Plain paraphrase | Why), then the three closing sections.

## The texts (read each straight through once)

### Passage P401

The big capital funders a building campaign needs most, public arts programmes and large foundations, write their rules so that they pay last, once every other dollar is confirmed. Two funders that each insist on paying last can wait for each other indefinitely. Field experiments say a lumpy project, one that is worth nothing until fully paid for, gets built far more often when someone goes first: seed money announced at two-thirds of the goal, against one-tenth, raised what others gave nearly sixfold (List and Lucking-Reiley, 2002). So someone has to go first without conditions, and for most givers that is the over-promise the case warns about, a pledge from money not yet in hand. Laura can go first without that risk, because by the 2031 meetings her $115,000 is already held for her in Treasuries that pay out in December 2032. Her condition then lets every other pledge stay conditional without anyone waiting on anyone: if pledges to the building's total are not all made by December 2032, no co-sponsor has to pay.

### Passage P402

Accountants book a likely loss as soon as it is likely, but a gain only once it is realised: 'Probability is not a factor in the recognition of gain contingencies' (KPMG's handbook on the US standard), and lenders, whose claims are capped, are the reason the rule exists (Watts, 2003). Old bookkeeping manuals kept a matching habit: entries in ink, running totals in pencil until the account was closed, then 'write the footings in black ink'. Laura's plan keeps the same books looking forward, in three places. A likely cost is taken out before any figure is stated: a 0.5% margin for fund costs beyond their fee (the bond funds of this kind that have already ended cost about 0.27%). A likely gain is counted as nothing: every payment is sized to be paid even if waiting cash earns no interest. And the likeliest gain of all, her shares' growth, counts for nothing until January 2031, when half of them buy Treasuries before the meetings: a top stated even one month before that move would be more than the move buys in about 3 futures in 100.

### Passage P403

Some years, WInS lists no Treasuries maturing – so we purchased iShares iBonds (like IBTM for 2032) instead. Defined-maturity, investment-grade, liquid, cheap and transparent about yields, these behave a lot like bonds when held to their end – so we'll simply ignore price noise in the interim. Together, our T-Bonds and iBonds ensure each of Laura's payments rests on a dedicated instrument (not merely a forecast). The catch is, their terminal payout is a net asset value, as opposed to a promise of par – but in return we gain a level of liquidity which no odd-lot bond offers.

### Passage P404

Laura's ten payments are paid by ten Treasury holdings, one ending before each January, and the interest those holdings pay before 2033 has to wait, unspent, for years. The plan sizes the ten so that every payment is made even if all that waiting money earns nothing, and in January 2028 it buys about $36,800 of extra Treasuries for that alone; without them, the ten as bought in January 2027 would be about $54,600 short on that test. It looks like an amateur's caution. It is the oldest test in the trade: Martin Leibowitz described a dedicated bond portfolio in 1986 as 'totally passive management' with 'no reinvestment'. The trade's usual ways of passing it are closed to Laura. Zero-coupon Treasuries, which pay nothing until their end, are banned in this competition. Immunisation, the other classic method, needs trading whenever rates move, and the plan's policy allows no trade for a market move. With zeros banned and trading ruled out, buying extra ordinary Treasuries is the only way left to pass the old test.

### Passage P405

Illinois's prepaid tuition plan sold families a promise: pay now, and the state pays the child's university tuition later. Its own disclosures named the three things the promise rested on: 'continued Contract sales within projections', 'a projected annual net return on Fund investments', and 'tuition/fee increases being within projected amounts'. All three stayed open for as long as it kept selling contracts; in 2024 it moved most of its money into Treasuries matched to its payments. Laura's plan carries the same three: new money is her 2028 deposit, from her career; returns are her shares; the bill is what the ten Treasuries cost before they are all bought, plus building costs. But they land differently. Returns never reach a promise she has made: no fall in share prices can break a payment or a figure she has stated. New money and the price of the ten both close for good in January 2028, when the last Treasuries behind the ten are bought. Building costs reach only the money she keeps unpromised. What remains is what Illinois never listed: a US default, and the funds' running costs beyond the small margin held for them.

### Passage P406

Laura keeps half her shares promised to no one, for the three uses her investment policy names: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, and a co-sponsor falling short. A reader may take this for a building's contingency fund, which the case tells teams not to size. The cost engineers' own definition says it is something else. AACE International's standard says a project's contingency 'usually excludes' escalation and currency effects, which are priced apart. Taiwan's culture ministry tells a grantee whose costs rise to raise the shortfall itself, and that it 'may not ask for more'; in England's arts lottery building programme about two-thirds of overruns fell on the organisations. So what Laura holds back is money no building budget's contingency and no grant was going to carry: the owner's share that the profession leaves outside contingency by definition, not a second contingency.

### Passage P407

We know Treasury bonds (including this one – maturing 2037) won't create wealth, but they will remove uncertainty, through duration-matching. In other words, we take Laura's requested 'high degree of certainty' to mean a near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that. Every year the bonds sit untouched is a good year – one less year of Laura's money being exposed to anything the restless markets might decide to do. Notably – Laura's payments are fixed in nominal dollars, so a nominal Treasury matches the liability perfectly, meaning we don't need TIPS, which would hedge against a nonexistent inflation risk.

### Passage P408

Britain's pension regulator lets a pension scheme take more investment risk when its payments are far off, but only as far as the employer behind it, its 'sponsor', can be relied on to pay in more if things go wrong. Its own warning example is a house builder whose scheme holds property: the sponsor and the investments share 'common drivers', so one slump hits both. Laura's plan has a sponsor too, her own writing income, which pays the $150,000 deposit of January 2028. The team's policy says a writer's income comes 'later and smaller' in the same recession in which shares fall: the regulator's house builder, in Laura's world. And she has only one call on that sponsor, in January 2028, and none after it. By the regulator's logic she should stop leaning on her sponsor as soon as its money is in, and the plan does: the first money of that deposit completes the ten Treasuries behind her payments, and from then on they need nothing more from her. The UK code asks for less risk in such a case; the plan takes none, which is the team's own step, not the code's.

### Passage P409

When Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 that she will give $115,000 towards the residency's building, each of them hears something else: what is left to raise, the building's total less her figure. That remainder is their target. In a laboratory study of groups that had to reach a threshold together to avoid a loss (Barrett and Dannenberg, 2012), groups that knew the threshold exactly avoided the loss in 18 of 20 cases; when the threshold was uncertain, the loss came in 16 of 20, while uncertainty about the size of the loss did no such harm. Fundraising's own counting rules already refuse to count a pledge whose conditions may fail. So a figure Laura stated while her shares could still fall would be a target moving under every co-sponsor. She states her range only once both ends are Treasuries she owns, and names the top as her figure, so every co-sponsor's target is exact: the residency's total less $115,000.

### Passage P410

An investment bank that offers a company a fixed sum for its new shares in a 'bought deal' takes the shares onto its own books and carries their price risk until it resells them. A university's gift office does the same from the other side: shares given to it count at what they fetch when sold, and many offices sell them the day they arrive. The two rules agree: a share counts only once it has been turned into something whose value is fixed, and the one exception is whoever chooses to keep the price risk. Laura's plan cuts her world shares along exactly that line. In January 2031, before any co-sponsor hears a figure, half of them move into the Treasury fund that pays out in late 2032, the fund that already holds the bottom of her range. That half is the bought deal, and the gift office's sale done before the gift. The other half stays in shares, promised to no one: the exception both rules name, money whose owner carries the price risk, which is why it never counts in her range. After the move a 40% fall in world shares changes neither end of the range; only that kept half falls.

### Passage P411

On the plan's own measure (the ten payments valued on each day's Treasury rates, always the same distance ahead), $300,000 bought all ten on 10.9% of days since 2000, and on exactly one day from June 2007, the last run of such days before 2026, to August 2026: Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have cost $470,217, more than both of Laura's deposits together. Monday 5 October 2026, two trading days before the team's orders filled, was the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002. Commissions came to $200, against a $37,625 swing in what the ten cost within 2026 ($325,563 on 27 February, $287,938 on 5 October). So the case is set in a rare year, and the plan is built to need the year, not the day: whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them and buys the extra Treasuries, and that keeps all ten paid even if cash waiting earns nothing unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027.

### Passage P412

VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong – because it's the only investment not standing between Laura and a payment deadline. This ETF owns thousands of companies, diversified across countries and sectors, which means we can avoid paying the price for the vain illusion that we can pick winners, instead buying the entire market's growth premium at an expense ratio of just 0.06%, for a forecast return of about 7.5% a year (as per JPMorgan). The T-Bonds and iBonds already cover the operating commitments, so VT answers a different question – how generous can the facility contribution be?
