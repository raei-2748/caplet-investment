# Reader pack A4 (comprehension study, Thu 19 Nov 2026)

Four drafts of the same competition entry's pitch and investment policy statement, written by different writers for the same client. They recommend broadly the same plan. Read each as a document in its own right.

**Your role.** You read as a strictly literal reader. You take every word at its ordinary everyday meaning and refuse to repair what the writer left out. If a sentence says a payment 'costs' something, a range 'is owned', or money 'goes into' a fund at a time when it does not yet exist, you report what it literally says, and then say whether that literal claim makes sense.

**The case, in brief (what every reader of these drafts already knows).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator
with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000
into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her
book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay
$50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments, the "operating payments" or "operating commitment")
to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money
aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards
the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she
tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is.
Overpromising would hurt her credibility. Teams in a high-school investment competition run by Wharton advise her: in
early November 2026 each team submitted a 50-word pitch and a 500-word investment policy statement (IPS). Teams could
use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds. WInS is the competition's trading simulator.

## How to read

You are one of several independent readers. You are not on the writer's side and you are not their colleague. Read
the way an intelligent person reads a document they were handed: once, at a normal pace, in order. Do not open any
other file in the repository, do not look anything up, and do not try to find out who wrote what.

1. **First, read each text straight through once**, as it appears under "The texts", without the numbers. Note where
   you stalled, went back, or gave up.
2. **Then go sentence by sentence** through the numbered list and fill one table row per sentence:
   - **Follow**: 1 = understood the first time; 2 = had to read it again or hold something in mind to get it;
     3 = had to rebuild the chain of reasoning, or still unsure what it says.
   - **Sense**: once you have understood it (or done your best), does what it says make sense?
     Y = yes, it is clear what it claims and the claim holds together.
     N = no. Then give the fault in a few words of your own, e.g. "doesn't follow" (the because/so does not connect),
     "impossible as stated" (describes something that cannot happen, e.g. in the wrong order in time), "circular or
     empty" (true of anything, says nothing), "wrong kind of thing" (gives something a property it can't have),
     "points at nothing" (an it/they/that/the ones with no clear owner), "unexplained term", "answers a question nobody
     asked", or anything else that fits better.
     ? = cannot judge.
   - **Plain paraphrase**: what the sentence says, in your own everyday words, in one line. If you had to guess, write
     "guess:" first. If the paraphrase needs words the sentence did not give you, put them in [brackets].
   - **Why** (only when Follow is 2-3 or Sense is N or ?): one short line, in your own words.
3. **Do not be charitable.** If you find yourself working out what the writer must have meant, the sentence did not
   say it: score Follow 2 or 3, write what you worked out as the paraphrase, then judge whether that meaning makes
   sense. Equally, do not invent faults: a sentence that is clear and holds together gets 1 and Y, however plain or
   however elaborate its vocabulary. Long words, formal register and complex sentences are not faults in themselves;
   only failing to be understood, or failing to make sense once understood, is a fault.
4. **At the end**, write three short sections in your own words:
   - **The sentences that made least sense** (up to eight, by number): for each, what exactly is wrong with it, as you
     would explain it to the writer.
   - **What the clearest text does that the others don't** (name it by its letter): a few sentences, with examples.
   - **Any pattern** you noticed in the sentences that failed.

Write your answers to the file named in your instructions, as Markdown: one section per text, each with its table
(columns: # | Follow | Sense | Fault | Plain paraphrase | Why), then the three closing sections.

### Text H

**Pitch (50 words):**

Laura's ten $50,000 operating payments are fixed, so we pay for them up front: her first $300,000 buys Treasuries that mature one before each January from 2033. Her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that set her minimum facility contribution, and only the interest those would earn goes into world shares.

**Investment policy statement (500 words):**

From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty. Part of what's left can help build the facility.

We call the plan Caplet XR, after extended-release tablets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled "do not crush". Ours is ten Treasury holdings, one redeemed before each January: X for ten, R for redemption. Crushing one means selling it before it matures, even for the building.

Pension funds cover fixed payments either by matching their bonds' average timing to the payments' and rebalancing as rates move, or by buying one bond per payment and holding it. We do the second, which never needs rebalancing. In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds with fixed end dates, each maturing before a payment. A Treasury bond held to maturity pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do. So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares.

Shares are called safe over ten years, but time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for a recovery. Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same downturns that hit share prices, so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice.

The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, which sets her minimum facility contribution. What remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless. We would rather she owned the shares. Nor do we copy portfolio insurance, which sells shares as they fall and helped deepen the 1987 crash. Our plan changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a credible range. The bottom is that fund's payout, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year. The other half stays hers and liquid, as a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.

The ladder's main weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest; if rates fall and stay low, it can fall short. Before Laura names her range, we price that shortfall as if every dollar waiting for a payment earned nothing, and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first, and if it can't cover the gap, she names a lower range.

From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside become her operating reserve, paying each January's $50,000 until 2042. Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.

**Investment policy statement (500 words):**

We call our plan Caplet XR. It’s named after extended-release caplets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled ‘do not crush’. Our ‘dose’ is Treasuries – Laura will, under our strategy, own the exact T-Bonds, T-Notes and Term Treasury ETFs she’ll need to fund her operating commitment. We chose Treasuries because her $50,000 payments are fixed and must be met with a high degree of certainty, and Treasuries pay fixed amounts on fixed dates, backed by the US government. At current Treasury prices her 10 operating payments would cost about $300,000 – which would generate roughly $200,000 in interest, therefore covering the $500,000 required in total – and whilst yields could of course look different by January 2027, we use these numbers as a guideline (and our plan is not dependent on them).

In January 2027 we will use her $300,000 deposit to buy a ladder of 10 Treasury holdings (one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042), and the 2028 deposit will fund any which the 2027 one couldn’t, if necessary. Each holding is bought so that what it repays, with its interest, meets that year’s $50,000. X is the Roman numeral for 10, and our XR (10 Rungs) will not be crushed: we won’t sell them before they mature. Bond prices will rise and fall before then, but a holding kept to maturity still pays what it was bought to pay, so those swings don’t affect the payments.

The ladder is also her operating reserve. It is complete by January 2028 at the latest, five years before the first payment, so in 2033 there is nothing left to set aside or rearrange. From then on it releases its dose on schedule, like the caplet: one rung matures just before each January and pays that year’s $50,000, until the tenth pays the last one in 2042.

Any funds from the 2027 and/or 2028 deposits not needed by the ladder will go into a world index fund (VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF). This is the money for her facility contribution in 2033 and for the flexibility she wants as the project develops. Because no operating payment depends on it, this money can take the risk of shares, which over long periods have grown faster than bonds. That suits Laura, who has taken thoughtful risks throughout her career. One fund holding thousands of companies across the world spreads that risk widely, costs little, and can be sold on any trading day when the money is needed. Shares can fall, and the fund could be worth less in 2033 than it cost, but a fall would reduce only what she can contribute.

Our plan’s biggest strength, and our interpretation of ‘high degree of certainty’, is that – barring a US default – no operating payment is ever at risk, and they certainly never depend on a share price. Like the caplet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.

### Text M

**Pitch (50 words):**

Laura’s career is already a giant stock she can’t sell. So we hedge it! We’ll fund the 10 operating commitments with a Treasury ladder, immune to markets. Yet another Treasury fund will decide the floor of her facility contribution, and a world stock ETF will determine the ceiling.

**Investment policy statement (500 words):**

Dear Laura,

You’d think that someone with your taste for thoughtful risk and 6 years to wait would usually own shares. So, naturally, that was our plan – until we stopped and considered the actual nature of a writer’s income. In good years the advances come, and the speaking fees, and the licences. In a recession they come later and smaller, and during that same period, shares fall and co-sponsors cut their budgets. In effect your career is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell. And so, we believe, your portfolio should be a counterbalance to that – definitely not a contributor.

You asked that the payments be met with a ‘high degree of certainty’, so we decided to take that one step further – barring a US default, none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price, or on anybody else’s money, when they fall due. The money for the operating commitment will go straight into Treasuries, because it’s fixed and non-negotiable, and only money you haven’t promised yet goes into shares, where it can grow.

In January 2027 we’ll use your first deposit to buy 10 Treasuries (T-Bonds or iShares iBonds Term Treasury ETFs, whichever works best for each year), each maturing just before it’s needed. Held to the end, these pay what they say – no price fluctuations for us to fear. At current yields, this would cost roughly $300,000 – in other words, your entire first deposit. But worry not – our plan isn’t dependent on yields staying put! Even if yields fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up being above $300,000, we’ll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit.

The building is a different matter. Most of the 2028 deposit will fund a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, whose payout is the bare minimum you’ll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt. The rest will go into a world share fund of thousands of companies – VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF – and its returns will help determine your maximum facility contribution. That means markets will be able to increase or decrease the top of your planned band as we get closer to 2031, but the bottom will remain constant, built on the ever-reliable Treasuries. Importantly for you, half of the value of the shares will be promised to no one. That is your flexibility – for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short. Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries, so whatever figure you choose is money you already own.

From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve. There are only 2 possible reasons we would change our plan – a missed deposit or a Treasury default. A fall in the market is not one of them.

You take risks on the page. Fair enough; that’s your job. With these promises, ours is to take none.

Yours,
Caplet
