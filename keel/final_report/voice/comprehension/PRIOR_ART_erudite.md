# Prior art: learned and clear on one reading (Thu 19 Nov 2026)

Notes for the team's voice study, not report prose. Nothing here is to be pasted into the Final Report.

The question: what makes prose that is elevated and learned still clear on the first reading, especially prose about
money? Each entry gives the citation, what the source says or shows, one short quotation (25 words or fewer) and where it
was read, then what the writer of a 9-12 page investing report could take from it. Where I read a source only through
someone else's quotation or summary, the entry says so. Read on the web on 19 Nov 2026.

---

## Part A: books and essays on style

### 1. Thomas and Turner, Clear and Simple as the Truth

Thomas, F.-N., & Turner, M. (2011). *Clear and simple as the truth: Writing classic prose* (2nd ed.). Princeton
University Press. (Original work published 1994)

**What it says.** A style is a stand on truth, presentation, scene, cast, thought and language, not a set of skills. In
classic style the motive is truth, the purpose is presentation, writer and reader are intellectual equals, and the model
is conversation. Prose is a window: the reader looks through it at the subject, not at the words. Classic style is
not plain style. Plain style treats the truth as simple and shared; classic style assumes the truth is rarely simple and
makes distinctions, which is where its refinement comes from. Classic prose leaves out three kinds of hedge (of process,
of liability, of worth), allows browsing but not skimming because every word carries a nuance, and simplifies a
qualification only when it does not change the main point. The book's "Museum" holds examples. One is the bird field
guide, written for a reader in the field, not in a library.

**Quotation.** "The guide, assuming this scene, cannot be written in a style that requires study or re-reading if it
expects to be attended to." (Opening of "The Museum", 1994 edition; read in the chapter abstract on JSTOR,
j.ctt1m3nx1d.6. The plain/classic contrast and the three hedges come from Pablo Stafforini's chapter summary,
stafforini.com, 2013. The definitions come from Mark Turner's excerpt at markturner.org/csx.html.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** The elevation is in the thinking: distinctions, nuance, a confident
stand. Ornament is not where it comes from. The clarity comes from the writer having finished thinking before writing,
so the reader gets the result, not the search for it.

**For the report.** The field guide fits the judges' case: a judge reads each report once, in the field, among many.
Classic style also permits an elevated vocabulary, as long as each word shows the subject more exactly. A sentence that
makes the reader reread has failed this stand, however learned it is.

### 2. Pinker, The Sense of Style, chapter 2

Pinker, S. (2014). *The sense of style: The thinking person's guide to writing in the 21st century*. Viking.
Chapter 2, "A Window onto the World: Classic Style as an Antidote for Academese, Bureaucratese, Corporatese, Legalese,
Officialese, and Other Kinds of Stuffy Prose".

**What it says.** Pinker adopts Thomas and Turner's classic style for writing to a general reader. The writer has seen
something the reader has not yet noticed and turns the reader's gaze toward it. Writing simulates two natural acts,
talking and seeing. His example of classic style on a hard subject is Brian Greene explaining multiple universes. The
plainness there is the product of work: paring an argument to its essentials, putting it in order, and finding
analogies that are familiar and accurate. He lists the habits that break the illusion: metadiscourse, signposting,
compulsive hedging, apologising, professional narcissism, clichés, mixed metaphors, metaconcepts (*level*,
*perspective*, *framework*), zombie nouns and unnecessary passives. Classic style is a stance. Good writers know the
truth is hard to reach but do not flaunt that anxiety in every sentence.

**Quotation.** "The guiding metaphor of classic style is seeing the world." (Read in the chapter 2 excerpt on Penguin
Random House Canada's site. The list of habits was read in the *Scientific American* interview, 30 Sep 2014, and in
two chapter summaries.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** The reader is treated as competent, so the writer leaves out
condescending explanation and defensive hedging, and both leave room for content. Abstraction is turned back into
agents doing things.

**For the report.** Keep the scene in view: a writer showing a judge something about Laura's money that the judge has
not yet seen. Words about the writing ("this section will", "in terms of our framework") take the judge's eyes off
the money.

### 3. McCloskey, Economical Writing

McCloskey, D. (1985). Economical writing. *Economic Inquiry, 23*(2), 187-222.
McCloskey, D. N. (2019). *Economical writing: Thirty-five rules for clear and persuasive prose* (3rd ed.). University of
Chicago Press.

**What it says.** An economist writes for economists. Clarity is the one real rule, and the reader decides whether it
has been met: clarity is social, and the reader, like the consumer, has the last word (she quotes Popper writing as if
someone were reading over his shoulder). Writing is thinking. Avoid boilerplate and five-dollar words; *concept*, for example, is a
vague Latinate word where *idea* would do. Avoid elegant variation. Her example is a paper on economic development that
used eight different names for that one thing in two pages. Express parallel ideas in
parallel form so the reader knows what is coming. Her list of economists who write well includes Galbraith, Solow,
Schelling, Hirschman, Kindleberger and Tobin.

**Quotation.** "The way to make it clear is to use one word to mean one thing." (Rule 19, "Avoid Elegant Variation",
1985 article; read in the article PDF hosted at sfu.ca and on deirdremccloskey.com. The 2019 table of contents was read
on the University of Chicago Press site.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Her own prose is witty and allusive (Fowler, Strunk and White, Popper,
Joyce), yet each allusion brings a rule or a reason with it. The learning shows in what she can cite, and it never
blurs a term.

**For the report.** A report with defined terms (a conceit, "the top", "the margin") lives or dies by this rule: once a
term is chosen it stays the same word for the same thing. Elegant variation in a learned register reads as sophisticated
and leaves the reader unsure whether two words mean one thing or two.

### 4. Sword, Stylish Academic Writing, and "Zombie Nouns"

Sword, H. (2012). *Stylish academic writing*. Harvard University Press.
Sword, H. (2012, July 23). Zombie nouns. *The New York Times* (Opinionator).

**What it says.** Sword surveyed 1,000 articles from leading journals in ten disciplines, analysed 500 closely and read
100 style guides. Fewer than 10% met her three tests of engagement, accessibility and craft. Her colleagues described
stylish prose as lively, concrete and written with conviction. The stylish writers she found in every discipline used
engaging titles and openings, concrete examples and case studies, a conversational but authoritative voice, and
verb-driven sentences. They also treated conventions as choices, not rules. In "Zombie Nouns" she shows how
nominalisations (nouns made from verbs and adjectives) take over sentences. They push out active verbs, drain
adjectives and replace people with abstractions. She does not ban them.

**Quotation.** "At their best, nominalizations help us express complex ideas." ("Zombie Nouns"; read in a PDF copy
hosted by Louisiana State University's writing programme. The study's figures come from Matthew Reisz's report in
*Times Higher Education*, 26 Apr 2012, and Sword's own post on the LSE Impact blog, 2012.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Learned abstractions earn their place when a concrete case sits beside
them to cash them out.

**For the report.** An abstract noun such as *liability*, *duration* or *sufficiency* can stand when it names the
idea exactly, as long as a concrete instance (a dated payment, a dollar figure) appears close by. A sentence whose
subject and object are both abstractions, joined by a weak verb, is the zombie case.

### 5. Orwell, "Politics and the English Language"

Orwell, G. (1946). Politics and the English language. *Horizon, 13*(76), 252-265.

**What it says.** Modern prose is assembled from ready-made phrases instead of chosen words, and it hides meaning or
the lack of one. Orwell names the faults (dying metaphors, operators, pretentious diction, meaningless words) and shows
them by "translating" a verse of Ecclesiastes into official English. Bad writers, he says, think Latin and Greek words
grander than Saxon ones. A scrupulous writer asks four questions of every sentence: what am I trying to say, what words
will express it, what image or idiom will make it clearer, and is that image fresh enough to have an effect? The sixth
rule tells the writer to break the other five rather than write anything barbarous.

**Quotation.** "What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about." (Read in
a scan of the essay on archive.org.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Orwell objects to grand words that hide meaning. He has no objection to
learned words as such. His third question admits images only when they make the point clearer, which is the test
between an allusion that explains and one that decorates.

**For the report.** For any elevated word, ask whether the meaning chose it or the wish to sound elevated did. The six
rules can be pushed too far (Orwell breaks some himself). The four questions are the useful part.

### 6. Williams, Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace

Williams, J. M., & Bizup, J. (2014). *Style: Lessons in clarity and grace* (11th ed.). Pearson. (12th ed. 2017.)

**What it says.** Clarity is a matter of where things sit in a sentence. Readers understand a sentence best when its
main characters are the grammatical subjects and their important actions are the verbs, not nominalisations. Familiar
information comes first and new, complex information last. The later lessons, "Shape" and "Elegance", show how a long
sentence stays readable. It is extended after its main clause with resumptive, summative and free modifiers rather
than by nesting clauses inside one another, and grace comes from balance, rhythm and a stressed ending.

**Quotation.** "make main characters the subjects of your verbs; make those characters' important actions your verbs."
(Summing-up of the clarity lessons; read in a copy of the 11th edition's opening lessons and its list of ten principles.
I did not read the "Shape" and "Elegance" lessons in full; the list of modifier types comes from the principles list.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Williams separates length from difficulty. A long sentence with a clear
subject and verb up front, extending rightward one step at a time, is easy to read. A shorter one built around a
nominalisation can be harder.

**For the report.** Elevated and long is allowed, but the sentence should name its character (Laura, the fund, the
Treasury) and the action early, then grow at the end.

### 7. Gopen and Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing"

Gopen, G. D., & Swan, J. A. (1990). The science of scientific writing. *American Scientist, 78*(6), 550-558.

**What it says.** Readers take meaning partly from where words sit, not only from what they say. They expect the
grammatical subject to be followed soon by its verb. The opening of a sentence (the topic position) should give
perspective and link back to what came before. The end (the stress position) is where readers put the emphasis, so it
should hold the new and important material. When a writer breaks these expectations, readers spend on untangling the
structure the energy they should spend on the content. Improving the structure of the writing improves
the thought, and the reverse also holds.

**Quotation.** "It is a linguistic commonplace that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of
a sentence." (Read in the reproduction at wstomv.win.tue.nl.) Gopen later expanded the method in book form; this note
rests only on the 1990 article.

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Gopen and Swan explain why some long, learned sentences read once and
others need three passes. In the clear ones, every clause arrives where the reader expected it, and the weight falls
at the end.

**For the report.** This is the most practical diagnostic for the "opaque" drafts. In a sentence that needs rereading,
check whether the subject is far from its verb, whether the old information comes last, and whether the stress
position is wasted on a qualifier or a citation.

### 8. Lucas, Style

Lucas, F. L. (1955). *Style*. Cassell. (Reissued as *Style: The art of writing well*, Harriman House, 2012 and 2020.)

**What it says.** Lucas, a Cambridge classicist, treats style as an extension of character and organises much of the
book under one heading, "Courtesy to Readers". Under it come clarity, brevity and variety, followed by chapters on
simile and metaphor, rhythm, irony, and planning and revision. He warns that brevity, like clarity, has limits, and he
asks writers to check each phrase for exaggeration said for effect.

**Quotation.** "Do I really mean that? Have I said it for effect, though I know it is exaggerated?" (Read as quoted in
the Wikipedia article on the book. I read no full chapter. Anthony Campbell's review, 2000, quotes Lucas's image of a
clear word as a finger-post pointing at its object.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Lucas writes in a learned register (classical quotation, foreign
phrases, which he later translated) and treats it as a form of courtesy. His test against writing for effect speaks
directly to the "performed" drafts.

**For the report.** Ask Lucas's question of every sentence that sounds good: was it said for effect? A neat reversal or
a rounded ending usually was.

## Part B: writers on money and economics admired for being learned and clear

### 9. Bagehot, Lombard Street

Bagehot, W. (1873). *Lombard Street: A description of the money market*. Henry S. King.

**What it shows.** In chapter 1 Bagehot explains his title. He calls the book *Lombard Street*, not the Money
Market, because he wants to deal, and to be seen dealing, with concrete realities. He notes the belief that the money market can only be
spoken of in very abstract words and rejects it. The chapter ends with the book's whole case in plain words: money will
not manage itself, and Lombard Street has a great deal of it to manage.

**Quotation.** "it is the writer's fault if what he says is not clear." (Chapter 1; read on Project Gutenberg, ebook
4359.)

**What critics say.** Woodrow Wilson's essay on Bagehot ("A literary politician", *The Atlantic Monthly*, Nov 1895)
praises "the wit that illuminates and the knowledge that refreshes". (Read as quoted on the Langport heritage page on
Wilson as Bagehot's admirer; I did not read the full essay.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** He names a place you could walk down instead of an abstraction, and he
holds the writer, not the subject, responsible for difficulty. His learning (history, banking practice) supplies the
particular case for each general claim.

**For the report.** Where a draft writes "the fixed-income component", write what the thing is (the Treasuries that pay
the 2033 instalment). The judge can picture it.

### 10. Keynes, The General Theory, chapter 12

Keynes, J. M. (1936). *The general theory of employment, interest and money*. Macmillan. Chapter 12, "The State of
Long-Term Expectation".

**What it shows.** Chapter 12 explains, with figures from everyday life, why markets price what other people will
think. Professional investment is likened to a newspaper competition in which entrants pick the faces they expect
others to pick. Passing a loss on to the next player is likened to the children's games of Snap, Old Maid and Musical
Chairs. Decisions to invest rest in the end on animal spirits. There is the remark that worldly wisdom teaches it is better for reputation to fail conventionally
than to succeed unconventionally. The beauty-contest passage is long, but it climbs one degree at a time, and each
clause arrives where the counting leads the reader to expect it.

**Quotation.** "anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be" (Chapter 12, section 5; read on
marxists.org.)

**What critics say.** Samuelson (1946, *Econometrica, 14*(3), 187-200) judged the book as a whole "badly written,
poorly organized", with flashes of insight among tedious algebra (read as quoted on the Falkenblog, 2012; the
citation was checked on the Econometric Society's site). Tyler Cowen (*Marginal Revolution*, 30 Apr 2009, read): "This
is the best chapter in the book and one of the most important economics essays of all time."

**What makes register high and sense clear.** The same learned author is opaque where he writes in definitions and
algebra and clear where his subjects are people doing things: investors, speculators, players. Each analogy carries a
step of the argument; remove the beauty contest and the third degree of expectation cannot be explained.

**For the report.** Keynes's chapter is the strongest proof that a high register about markets can be read once. The
lesson lies in its subjects and its analogies. Its vocabulary is not the model.

### 11. Buffett: the SEC handbook preface and the shareholder letters

Buffett, W. E. (1998). Preface. In Office of Investor Education and Assistance, *A plain English handbook: How to create
clear SEC disclosure documents*. U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission.
Buffett, W. E. (2002). Chairman's letter [on the year 2001]. In *Berkshire Hathaway Inc. 2001 annual report*.

**What it says and shows.** In the preface Buffett writes with one person in mind. He pretends to be talking to his
sisters and states his goal: to give them the information he would want from them if their positions were reversed. He
says he does not need to be Shakespeare, only to have a sincere desire to inform. He also says that, reading
disclosure documents for decades, he often concluded nothing was being said, and in some cases suspected the issuer did
not want to be understood. In the 2001 letter an image appears as a reason within an argument. Having argued that an
insurer should test every link in a reinsurance chain against a catastrophe in a bad economy, he adds: "After all, you
only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out." (Read in the copy of the 2001 letter at
midgardfinance.com.)

**Quotation.** "Though highly intelligent, they are not experts in accounting or finance." (Preface; read in the
handbook PDF hosted by the Utah Division of Securities.)

**What makes register high and sense clear.** The imagined reader is intelligent and outside the trade, which rules out
both jargon and condescension. His images come from ordinary life and arrive after the argument they summarise, with a
connective ("After all") that makes them a premise.

**For the report.** A Wharton judge is Buffett's reader: highly intelligent, not necessarily expert in Treasury
ladders. Write the sentence you would want if the positions were reversed.

### 12. Howard Marks's memos

Marks, H. (2011). *The most important thing: Uncommon sense for the thoughtful investor*. Columbia Business School
Publishing.
Marks, H. (2015, September 9). *It's not easy* [Memo to Oaktree clients]. Oaktree Capital Management.

**What it shows.** Marks has written client memos since 1990. The 2011 book is built from them, and Buffett's
endorsement of it says the memos are the first thing he opens and reads (read on the NetGalley listing). "It's Not Easy"
opens with a scene: a 2011 lunch at which Charlie Munger said investing is not supposed to be easy. It then teaches
"second-level thinking" through paired examples that share a frame. Each first-level thought is quoted as an investor
would say it, and the second-level version repeats the frame and adds what the crowd believes and what the price
already holds. The memos lean on attributed quotation (Munger, Buffett, Galbraith) as evidence.

**Quotation.** "First-level thinking says, 'It's a good company; let's buy the stock.'" (Read in the memo PDF on
oaktreecapital.com.) On the museum's acquisition of the memos in October 2025, Marks said he had always aimed to make
complex ideas clear and accessible (Museum of American Finance press release, 14 Oct 2025).

**What makes register high and sense clear.** The ideas are learned: market efficiency, consensus already in the price,
behavioural error. They are shown as thoughts a person has, in quotation marks, so the reader sees the reasoning instead
of a label for it. The parallel frame makes the one difference visible.

**For the report.** The paired frame works here because the second half adds information that the first half lacked.
A pair whose second half only inverts the first is the "neat reversal" the leader rejected.

### 13. Bernstein, Against the Gods

Bernstein, P. L. (1996). *Against the gods: The remarkable story of risk*. John Wiley & Sons.

**What it shows.** The introduction opens with a question (what separates modern times from the thousands of years
before?) and answers it with the book's thesis: the mastery of risk. It then tells that history through the thinkers
who achieved it, from games of chance and Pascal to modern portfolio theory. The allusion in the title is the thesis
itself. Treating the future as more than the gods' whim is what "against the gods" means, and the Prometheus comparison
in the introduction repeats the point.

**Quotation.** "the notion that the future is more than a whim of the gods and that men and women are not passive before
nature." (Introduction; read in the O'Reilly preview of the Wiley edition.)

**What critics say.** The review excerpts collected by Powell's Books (read there) call the book readable (*Barron's*, *The
New York Times*), and *The Australian* wrote: "There's a growing market for savants who can render the recondite
intelligibly." *Kirkus* (18 Oct 1996, read) found it dense but
compelling.

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Bernstein carries mathematics through people and dates. Each idea
arrives with a person attached and a problem that person was trying to solve, and the classical allusion is an
argument, not a garnish.

**For the report.** A learned reference (Neyman, Lintner, the UCC, Perold and Sharpe) reads clearly when it brings a
person and a problem with it. A bare name dropped in for credit does not.

### 14. Galbraith

Galbraith, J. K. (1978, March). Writing, typing, and economics. *The Atlantic Monthly*.
Galbraith, J. K. (1990). *A short history of financial euphoria*. Whittle Direct Books.

**What it says and shows.** In the 1978 essay Galbraith says that complexity and obscurity have professional value in
economics: they work like a guild's apprenticeship rules. He says a first draft is a very primitive thing for anyone who
is not certifiably a Milton, that later drafts make better writing possible, and that one cannot be wholly clear about
something one does not understand. In *A Short History of Financial Euphoria* the irony sits in the last words of the
sentence: "The world of finance hails the invention of the wheel over and over again, often in a slightly more unstable
version." (Read as quoted in Novel Investor's notes on the book.)

**Quotation.** "In the case of economics there are no important propositions that cannot be stated in plain language."
(Read as quoted in Timothy Taylor's *Conversable Economist* post of 25 Aug 2015. The drafts and understanding lines were
read as quoted in a commentary at butwhatfor.beehiiv.com. I did not read the Atlantic scan itself.) McCloskey's 1985
list of economists who write well includes him.

**What makes register high and sense clear.** Galbraith's elevation is in the irony and the cadence, not in obscure
words. His understatement does its work in the stress position, where Gopen and Swan say the reader puts the weight.

**For the report.** Galbraith gives the judges' likely reaction to obscurity: it reads as guild protection, not depth.
Irony, if used, is one qualifier at the end of a factual sentence. It is never a set piece.

## What elevated-but-clear prose does, sentence by sentence

1. **The learned word is the narrowest word available, and it keeps one meaning to the end.** Bernstein's thesis, the
   mastery of risk, is set up on the first page and then delivered by the whole book. Orwell's "let the meaning choose the word"
   and McCloskey's one word for one thing describe the same habit: the high register comes from precision, not from
   synonyms.

2. **The grammatical subject is someone or something that acts, even when the topic is abstract.** Keynes's chapter 12
   is clear and much of the rest of the book, by Samuelson's account, is not. In chapter 12 the subjects are
   professional investors, speculators and players of Old Maid, which is Williams's principle of characters as
   subjects and actions as verbs.

3. **The long sentence starts with what the reader already holds and ends on the one new thing.** Its length comes from
   steps added after the main clause, not from clauses nested inside it. Keynes's beauty contest counts upward degree
   by degree, and Galbraith saves "slightly more unstable" for the stress position (Gopen and Swan; Williams on
   modifiers).

4. **The figure or allusion carries a step of the argument and could not be cut without loss.** Without the newspaper
   competition the third degree of expectation is hard to state. Without the gods the title of Bernstein's book has no
   meaning. Buffett's tide is offered as a reason ("After all") for a stress test he has just argued. A decorative
   figure fails Orwell's third question, whether it makes the point clearer.

5. **The particular stands where the category would have gone.** Bagehot names a street instead of "the money market".
   Marks quotes the thought ("It's a good company; let's buy the stock") instead of naming "naive reasoning". Thomas and
   Turner's field guide presents the bird, not the class of birds. Sword's stylish writers put a case beside each
   abstraction.

6. **The claim stands without hedges of process, liability or worth.** The qualification that remains is the one that
   changes the claim, stated once, where it applies. Thomas and Turner's classic stance and Pinker's point that good
   writers conceal their anxiety for clarity's sake describe this; the result reads as confident without being
   arrogant.

7. **The writer addresses one imagined reader, intelligent and outside the trade, as an equal.** Buffett's sisters and
   Thomas and Turner's symmetry of writer and reader set the register. It is elevated because the reader is assumed
   to be capable of fine distinctions, not because the vocabulary keeps the reader out.

8. **Abstract nouns appear where the idea is truly abstract, with a concrete instance close by.** Sword allows that
   nominalisations at their best express complex ideas. Her stylish writers, like Bagehot, then turn the abstraction
   into a case the reader can see.

9. **Nothing is said for effect; each sentence says what the writer means, at the strength the writer believes it.**
   Lucas asks "Have I said it for effect?" and Orwell names insincerity as the great enemy of clear language. Admired
   passages on money also end where the matter ends: Bagehot's chapter closes on his case for the book, not on a turn.

10. **A parallel pair repeats its frame so that one difference shows, and the second half adds information.** Marks's
    first-level and second-level sentences share their opening words so that the eye goes to what changes, as McCloskey
    says parallel form lets the reader know what to expect. The pair informs because the second half adds the crowd's
    belief and the price. A pair that only inverts its first half is a performance.
