## The sentences that made least sense

1. **P410.7**: This contradicts sentence 3 of the same passage. There the "exception" was someone who keeps the price risk, which suggests their shares still count. Here the kept-risk half "never counts". It also says "both rules name" the exception, but the gift-office example named none. Choose one meaning of "exception" and use it in both places, or drop the word.
2. **P410.3**: The two examples are practices, not rules. "The one exception is whoever chooses to keep the price risk" makes a person the exception to a rule about when shares count, and never says whether shares count or don't for that person. State the rule and its exception as two plain statements about shares.
3. **P411.5**: "Whatever the ten cost in January 2027" is withdrawn at the end by "unless yields fall about 3.6 points before January 2027". Either it holds whatever the cost or it has a limit, not both. "Built to need the year, not the day" is a slogan put where the reason should be. Split the sentence and give the limit as the limit.
4. **P401.6**: "Her condition" arrives right after we were told she goes first without conditions. The reader can't tell whose condition the December 2032 deadline is, or whether her own money is released too if the total isn't reached. Introduce the deadline as a new thing and say whom it binds.
5. **P410.6**: Saying the half "is the bought deal" names it but explains nothing, and the name fits badly: in a bought deal a bank carries the risk, while here nobody does. Say what the conversion does (fixes the value before any figure is stated).
6. **P404.8**: Ruling out two named methods doesn't make the third the "only way left". Other bonds or other mixes go unconsidered. "Extra ordinary" also reads as "extraordinary" on a normal pass.
7. **P409.6**: It states a range but then names the top as "her figure", leaving the reader asking what the bottom is for. "Every co-sponsor's target is exact" also holds only if the building's total is fixed, which nothing here says. Her side of the subtraction can be exact; the target can't be called exact.
8. **P406.6**: The evidence says contingency "usually excludes" escalation and currency, and the conclusion turns that into "by definition". The first of her three uses, ordinary building overruns, is what contingency normally does cover, and a co-sponsor falling short isn't touched by either source.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**P408** is the clearest of the longer arguments. Each sentence carries one step, and the parallel is built from a mechanism the reader can check, not asserted as an identity. It gives the regulator's rule, then the reason the rule exists ("common drivers, so one slump hits both"), then the matching piece of Laura's world ("her own writing income, which pays the $150,000 deposit"), then the fact that settles it ("only one call on that sponsor"). Only then does it draw the conclusion. It also says where the parallel stops: "the plan takes none, which is the team's own step, not the code's". Compare P410, which declares "That half is the bought deal" and leaves the reader to make the fit work. P412 is also clean, but it is short and makes only one inference.

## Any pattern

- **Conclusions overreach in the last sentence.** Most failures are the final or near-final sentence of a passage (P401.6, P404.8, P405.9, P406.6, P409.6, P410.7, P411.5). The evidence is fine; the closing "so" claims more than it supports.
- **Absolute words the evidence doesn't reach:** "the oldest", "the only way left", "by definition", "whatever", "exactly", "never counts". In each case the preceding evidence supports a weaker word.
- **Parallels asserted, not shown.** "A matching habit" (P402.2), "does the same from the other side" (P410.2), "is the bought deal" (P410.6) and "the exception both rules name" (P410.7) all claim an identity between two fields without naming the shared mechanism. P408, which names it, works.
- **Things referred to as if already introduced:** "her condition", "the small margin held for them", "the funds", "this one", "their". These come in passages read on their own, where the reader never met them.
- **The informal trade notes (P403, P407) fail differently.** They fail on loose connectives ("in other words" introducing a new claim) and on statements true whatever happens ("every year ... is a good year"), not on overreaching conclusions.
