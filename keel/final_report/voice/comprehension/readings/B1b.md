## The sentences that made least sense

1. **P102.12 (twin: P106.13).** Two sentences earlier you said a US default and a missing 2028 deposit are how the ten payments can fail. Then you say the IPS's two reasons, which are those same two events, are "not ways a promise fails". As written, the same events sit on both lists and are denied on one of them. If you mean that the IPS list is about what you *do* (change the plan) while the failure list is about what *happens*, say the same events serve two jobs. Don't deny the overlap.
2. **P104.13 (twin: P108.12).** "The IPS's two reasons to change the plan" are never named on the page. The reader is asked to accept that they "belong on a different list" without being shown what they are, and "a different list" is never said to be different from what.
3. **P104.6 (twin: P106.6).** The conclusion is that all ten payments are made even with shares at zero. It rests on "the shares are the only pencil left". This page never defines pencil, ink or the page, and never states the premise that actually carries the argument: every payment is already held in Treasuries. The picture stands in for the reason. One plain clause would do the work, as P102.1's "all owned in Treasuries from January 2028" does.
4. **P102.7 (twin: P106.8).** One clause introduces four new things: "fund costs beyond the fees", "the room under it", "the late-2032 fund" and "cash waiting earning nothing". I could rebuild a likely meaning (a cushion of about 2% that survives even zero interest on idle cash). But the page never says what cash is waiting or for what, so the "even with" qualifier can't be checked.
5. **P101.5 (also P107.5, P110.5, P111.6).** "A fall in shares before the move lowers what she can state, never what she has stated." The passage says she buys before any co-sponsor meeting, so before the move she has stated nothing, and the second half is true of nothing. The point you want is about a fall *after* she states: it can't touch the stated figure because that figure is held in Treasuries. "The move" is also never named on the page. P107.5 and P111.6 add a second, different reason ("the ten payments never rest on it") under the same colon, as if it explained "promises nothing she doesn't own".
6. **P107.8.** "Co-sponsors work the same way": the same way as what? The sentence before says Laura can only ever raise a figure, and capping grants is the opposite. The example really supports keeping spare money for overruns, which is how P101.8 ("Funders expect that") and P111.9 ("points the same way") use it.
7. **P105.3.** If the bonds "remove uncertainty" (sentence 1), the money in them isn't exposed to markets, so a year passing removes no exposure. And "every year the bonds sit untouched is a good year" is true of every year, so it says nothing.
8. **P109.4.** The "in return we gain liquidity" trade-off doesn't fit its own passage. Sentence 1 says the funds were bought because no Treasury bond existed for those years, so there was no bond to trade against. Sentence 2 says you hold to the end and ignore price moves, so the liquidity is a benefit you have said you won't use. "Net asset value", "par" and "odd-lot" are also left unexplained.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**P111.** Every "because" is followed by the actual reason in plain words: "It's meaningful because it's $115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in, owned before she asks anyone and announced first." The next sentence ties the evidence back to that reason with "exactly that way".

It also gives each sentence one job and introduces a thing before using it. Sentence 2 says one fund pays the gift and sentence 3 says when she buys it, so no "it" changes owner mid-sentence (compare P107.2 and P110.2: "pays it: Laura buys it").

It states the one-way rule as a plain cause, with no picture: "She can always state more later but never take a stated figure back, so her figure is rounded down ...". P101.6 and P107.7 say the same thing through "ink" and "pencil", which the page never defines.

It names the flexibility directly ("Her flexibility is the other half of the shares, about $21,200") rather than "that half". Its comparison says what it compares: co-sponsors' practice "points the same way", where P107.8's "work the same way" claims a likeness that isn't there.

Its one slip is sentence 6, with its unexplained "the move" and the empty half of the contrast. Among the risk passages, P108 is the plainest. But it drops the premise that P102.1 states (the payments are held in Treasuries), which leaves "No fall in share prices can." unsupported.

## Any pattern

- **Words that point to another page.** The failing sentences lean on things defined elsewhere in the report: "the move", "the top", "the late-2032 fund", "the room under it", "cash waiting", "the IPS's own condition", "the case's five areas", "the IPS's two reasons". Read as a page on its own, each one points at nothing. The risk passages (P102, P104, P106, P108) suffer most, because they compress material explained elsewhere.
- **Pictures doing the work of reasons.** Where the image appears (pencil, ink, the page, rubbing out), it tends to carry the reason instead of illustrating a reason already given. With a plain gloss beside it (P101.6's dash) the sentence holds. When the image is the only reason (P104.6, P106.6), it fails.
- **Two lists that share their items.** Distinctions between lists ("exits" versus "ways a promise fails") fail in two ways. Either the same items are on both lists and the sentence denies the overlap (P102, P106), or the items are never named (P104, P108).
- **Contrasts that lose their timing.** A contrast squeezed into one clause ("lowers what she can state, never what she has stated") loses its timing and becomes empty for the time it names.
- **Conclusions one step past their reason.** "Breaks nothing" goes one step further than its reason ("not a promise"). The versions that say "nothing promised" or "anything she has stated" (P102.8, P106.9's ending) hold. P106.9 drops the reason itself.
- **A short sentence that borrows its verb.** "No fall in share prices can." works only directly after the "could" it borrows from. With a sentence about her income in between, it first attaches to the wrong verb ("delays", "pushes those back").
