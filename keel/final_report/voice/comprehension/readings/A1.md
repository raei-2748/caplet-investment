## The sentences that made least sense

1. **R10.** "The fund behind the 2033 payment" comes from nowhere: you told me about ten holdings, not a fund. In "enough to return about all of it", the "it" sits right next to "the 2033 payment", so the first reading is that you buy enough to return the 2033 payment, which is already covered. Say what is bought, with what money, and what it pays back: "buys enough of the Treasury fund ending December 2032 that it pays back roughly the whole leftover sum."
2. **R12.** "It is bought in 2028": the share fund was the last thing named, so "It" reads as the shares. Nobody suggested 2031, so "not 2031" answers a question I wasn't asking. The because-chain is missing its middle step (waiting until 2031 would leave the money in shares meanwhile). You also say "the residency can't depend on shares" when you mean her contribution to the building.
3. **R20.** In "the money waiting for them", "them" can only be the ladder and the extra reserve. The payments you mean aren't in the sentence.
4. **R9.** It announces "the first meaning of XR" without saying X = ten and R = rungs, when the only meaning we had been given was extended-release. You spell out the second meaning in R14 ("an extra reserve"), so spell out the first too.
5. **R7.** The heavily-borrowed-company comparison only helps a reader who already knows how debt makes shares swing. For me it explains something unclear with something unfamiliar.
6. **R3.** You can't buy the bottom of a range, and "shares that pay for its top before she names it" hides a sale three years later. In a 50-word pitch I couldn't rebuild it.
7. **R6.** "Balanced mix" is never defined, and you leave me to make the step from "the payments are fixed" to "every gain or loss would fall on the last third". The point is good, but I had to make it myself.
8. **R11.** "Because it costs less than it returns" works only once R10 has been rebuilt. "It" is used twice without a fresh noun, so the clearest idea in R depends on its least clear sentence.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text K** is the clearest sentence by sentence. Each sentence carries one step, and it explains a new word where it first appears: "X is the Roman numeral for 10, and our XR (10 Rungs)", with crushing defined on the spot ("we won't sell them before they mature"). It gives the reason inside the claim ("We chose Treasuries because...", "Because no operating payment depends on it, this money can take the risk of shares"). It repeats nouns ("the ladder", "the 2028 deposit") instead of leaning on "it" and "that". It shows the sum when it gives a number (K8: $300,000 plus $200,000 of interest covers the $500,000). It also tells things in time order: buy in 2027, complete by 2028, pay out from 2033. Part of that clarity comes from leaving out the hardest part, though: K never mentions the 2031 range or how confident Laura can be, which the case asks for. Of the texts that do cover the range, H is clearer than R. It gives one figure per sentence (H23 the bottom, H24 the top, H25 the half she keeps), and H24's "a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year" is the best-explained reason in all four texts. M is nearly as clear as K, and it is the only one that makes her career the reason for the whole plan.

## Any pattern

Almost all the failures are in R, and they share three habits. First, pronouns and short labels point back across sentences to something that has a different name each time: "the fund behind the 2033 payment" becomes "it", then "the 2028 holding", then "that holding"; there is "the first half" and "the other half", and "them" with nothing to attach to. Second, a mechanism gets squeezed into a word that can't literally carry it: "buys the bottom", "both ends are owned", "sells ... for XR's second meaning". Third, because/so clauses leave out the middle step (R6, R12), or wordplay is asserted instead of shown (R9). The other texts' weak spots are smaller. A finance word is dropped in without a gloss ("hedge" and "ladder" in M's pitch, "capital-protected note" and "option" in H18, "balanced mix" and "heavily borrowed company" in R6-R7), or "that fund" and "the Treasuries" are used when two or three things could fit (H23, M26). The pitches suffer most, because M's and R's compress what their bodies later explain. None of the failures comes from long words or formal register: K8 is the longest sentence in the set and it still lands.
