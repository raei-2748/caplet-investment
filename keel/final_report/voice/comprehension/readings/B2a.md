## The sentences that made least sense

1. **P204.6** ("Short of a default, that would take fund costs beyond the fees passing about 2%..."). Every noun phrase refers back to something you never gave me: "that", "the fees", "the two Treasury funds", "the top", "that room", "rounding down". I can build a guess (there's a 2% cushion, part margin, part rounding), but I can't check it against anything on the page. Introduce the margin, the top and the rounding before you lean on them, or cut the sentence.
2. **P209.4** ("Besides a default, those extra costs would have to pass about 2%... because rounding down left room beyond the margin"). This has the same unexplained terms as P204.6, and the opening makes "a default" sound like one of the costs. The "because" holds only if I already know what was rounded down and what the margin is.
3. **P204.10** (and its twins **P206.9**, **P209.8**, **P211.10**). "The case's five areas" is followed by four items. The reader counts. "Timing is the 2028 deposit" is also an equation, not a sentence. Name the five areas, or say four, and give each item a verb that explains it.
4. **P211.6** ("falling shares shrink what she can state, never what she has"). Read literally this is false: falling shares obviously shrink what she has. You mean what she has already stated, so say "stated".
5. **P206.3 / P211.3**. The "because/since a downturn delays a writer's income first" clause is dropped into the middle of a list, so the next item ("or fund costs...") reads as part of the reason. Put the reason in its own sentence, or after the whole list. "Waiting cash" also needs one plain clause of explanation.
6. **P207.4** ("their terminal payout is a net asset value, as opposed to a promise of par..."). There are five unexplained terms in one sentence. The payoff it names (liquidity) also clashes with the previous sentence: if you'll hold to the end and ignore prices, why does being easy to sell matter?
7. **P211.9** ("The worst two-year spans since 1991 rose by more, and if it runs out, nothing promised breaks"). Periods don't rise, costs do. "It" is two sentences from its owner. A warning and a reassurance are joined by a bare "and", so the reader can't tell which one you want them to hold.
8. **P204.7** ("Falling shares before the move only lower what she can state"). "The move" appears nowhere else in the passage. I can't tell what move, when, or why its timing matters.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**P208** is the clearest, with P203 a close twin. It opens by telling me who is speaking and what the thing is ("I'm opening an artists' residency in Taiwan in 2033"), then gives every sentence one fact with its own amount and date: "$50,000 each January from 2033 to 2042", "$95,000... since January 2028, and the rest since January 2031, when my shares moved into Treasuries." It never uses a term it hasn't earned. Where a phrase could be vague it adds the clause that settles it: "my own $115,000 comes first *and doesn't depend on anyone else*" removes the doubt that "comes first" leaves in P201.7. "Fixed in US dollars, so it won't rise" gives the real reason, where P202.6's "in US dollars, so" does not. It also explains its odd details with a reason ("one year at a time, which is how Taiwan's funders give"). The only slip is "the offer", which it never named.

## Any pattern

- The failures cluster in the analytic "how each promise fails" passages (P204, P206, P209, P211), which each read as if I had just read the previous section. They use definite articles for things I've never met ("the fee", "the margin", "the top", "the move", "waiting cash", "rounding down", "the meetings", "the two Treasury funds").
