## The sentences that made least sense

1. **R12** ("It is bought in 2028, not 2031 ..."). The nearest thing "it" can be is the world share fund in the sentence before, and with that reading the sentence says the shares are bought early *because* the residency can't depend on shares, which contradicts itself. Name the thing: "The Treasury fund behind the bottom is bought in 2028 ...".
2. **R10** ("what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, enough to return about all of it ..."). It talks about "the fund behind the 2033 payment" as if we had met it, but R8 only spoke of "holdings". "All of it" has three possible owners (the payment, the fund, the leftover money). The sentence also packs four steps into one breath: what is left, what it buys, what that returns, and what that sets. Split it, and say "the leftover" instead of "it".
3. **R9** ("Those ten rungs are the first meaning of XR"). The text never says what X or R stand for, so I can't check that ten rungs are a "meaning" of XR. R14's "second meaning" (presumably eXtra Reserve) works only if the reader spells it out for themselves. If the double meaning matters, state it once in plain words. If it doesn't, cut it.
4. **R3** ("The rest buys the bottom of her 2031 range and shares that pay for its top before she names it"). You can't buy a range's lower end, and shares pay for nothing until they are sold. The order (buy now, sell later, name after) has to be rebuilt by the reader. Say what is bought and what is later sold.
5. **H18** ("Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless"). Two trade terms arrive with no gloss and "this" has no clear owner. As a result, the next sentence ("We would rather she owned the shares") has no visible contrast. Either explain the option in half a line or drop the comparison.
6. **M26** ("From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve"). By then there are three lots of Treasuries. Read as all of them, the building money becomes part of the reserve. Write "the ten Treasuries".
7. **M25** ("whatever figure you choose is money you already own"). "Whatever" claims too much, since a figure above the bottom plus the moved half would not be owned. Write "the figure you give".
8. **R6** ("so in a balanced mix every gain or loss would fall on the last third"). The conclusion is reachable, but the reader has to build the bridge. "Balanced mix" is unexplained, and the missing clause is "because the payments can't shrink, every rise or fall in the whole lands on what's left".

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text K** is the clearest. Every sentence carries one step, and every pronoun points at something named in the sentence just before (K13-K15: "The ladder is also her operating reserve. It is complete by January 2028 ... From then on it releases its dose"). It defines its coinage at the moment it uses it ("X is the Roman numeral for 10"), where R uses its coinages without defining them. It names the one worry a lay reader would have and answers it in the same sentence (K12: bond prices move, but a holding kept to the end still pays). Its figures add up visibly (K8: $300,000 plus $200,000 of interest makes the $500,000), and it says plainly what a loss would cost (K21: "a fall would reduce only what she can contribute"). Part of its clarity comes from leaving out the hard parts, though: K never explains how the 2031 range is set or what happens if waiting money earns less, and those are the passages where R and H stumble. Of the texts that do tackle them, H is much easier to follow than R. H24 gives a reason anyone can follow ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year").

## Any pattern

- **Figures treated as objects.** "Buys the bottom of her range", "both ends are owned", "shares that pay for its top": a figure or a range end is talked about as if it were the money behind it.
- **Pointers that skip a nearer candidate.** R12's "It", R10's "all of it", H23's "that fund" and M26's "the Treasuries" each have a closer or competing owner than the intended one.
- **Things referred to as already known when they were never introduced.** "The fund behind the 2033 payment", "the 2028 holding", "XR's first meaning".
- **Trade words dropped in without a plain gloss.** "Balanced mix", "capital-protected note", "option", "hedge", and "ladder" in the pitches.
- **A chain of steps squeezed into one sentence**, so the reader has to carry a quantity over from the sentence before (R10 into R11, R14 into R16). The failures cluster in R's account of how the range is set, the most intricate part of any of the texts. In nearly every case the fix is one named noun or one connecting clause, not a new idea.
- **Words of scope that stretch a true claim** ("whatever figure", "the Treasuries" meaning all of them) appear in M.
