## The sentences that made least sense

- **R10.** The reader has never met "the fund behind the 2033 payment", because the ladder was introduced as "Treasury holdings" with no fund named. "All of it" has no clear owner. And the subject ("what the ladder doesn't need buys ...") says all the leftover money goes into that fund, yet the next sentence sends part of it into shares. Name the fund when it first appears, say "part of the leftover", and say "enough to pay back the whole leftover sum".
- **R12.** "It is bought in 2028" follows straight after the world index fund, so the reader takes "it" to be the shares, and then the reason (the residency can't depend on shares) points the wrong way. Even with "it" taken as the Treasury fund, "her facility contribution, resting on the US government instead" is only true of the bottom in 2028, because the top still rests on shares until 2031.
- **R3.** "Buys the bottom of her 2031 range" gives a number the property of something you can buy. "Shares that pay for its top before she names it" describes a sale three years later without saying so. A pitch reader can't rebuild that.
- **R9.** "Those ten rungs are the first meaning of XR" claims a meaning without giving it (X for ten? R for rungs?), and R4 had just said the name came from "extended-release". The reader is told there is a code but isn't given the key.
- **M18.** The reassurance "we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit" leans on the income the same letter has just called a share holding that comes "later and smaller" in a recession. The fallback for the payments is the very risk the letter said to counterbalance, so "simply" and "our plan isn't dependent on yields" don't follow from it.
- **M2.** "So we hedge it!" A hedge gains when the thing hedged loses. Nothing in the plan does that: it only refuses to add the same risk, and it still buys a share fund. The letter's own later word, "counterbalance ... not a contributor", is the honest claim.
- **R14.** It is sound once rebuilt, but the sentence puts a label ("XR's second meaning") where the reader needs the plain thing, and it asks the reader to hold "up to half" and "enough to meet every payment" together without saying which limit binds.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text K.** Each sentence makes one move and carries its own reason inside it: "Because no operating payment depends on it, this money can take the risk of shares"; "a holding kept to maturity still pays what it was bought to pay, so those swings don't affect the payments". When it uses its image it decodes it on the spot ("will not be crushed: we won't sell them before they mature"; "Our 'dose' is Treasuries"), so the caplet never stands in for a reason. It keeps one name for each thing ("the ladder", "a world index fund"), where R coins new labels for the same objects ("the fund behind the 2033 payment", "the 2028 holding", "XR's second meaning"). Its dates run forward in order (2027, 2028, 2033, 2042) and no pronoun reaches back more than one sentence. The cost is scope: K says little about how the 2031 range is built, which is exactly where R and H spend their hardest sentences. M is nearly as easy to follow and argues its "why" best, but it slips twice (M2, M18). H is clear step by step but asks for more outside knowledge (pension-fund matching, capital-protected notes, portfolio insurance).

## Any pattern

The failures cluster where a text packs a mechanism into fewer words than the mechanism has steps, and that happens most in R and in the pitches. Four habits recur. First, pronouns and back-references with no owner in the same sentence ("it", "all of it", "them", "after this", "that holding"). Second, labels used as if the reader already knew them, often names from the conceit ("XR's first/second meaning") put where a plain noun would do. Third, shorthand that gives a figure the property of a thing: you "buy" a bottom, "own" an end, "repay" a sum. Fourth, a result stated before the pieces that produce it (R10 before R11; the pitch R3 before the whole IPS). Separately, M shows an argument failure rather than a reading one: its strongest premise (her income behaves like shares and shrinks in recessions) quietly undercuts two later claims (the pitch's "hedge" and the 2028 deposit as the ladder's safety valve). No sentence failed because of long words or formal register. The sentences that worked, in every text, take one step at a time with the "because" or "so" inside the sentence.
