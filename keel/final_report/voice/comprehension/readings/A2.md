## The sentences that made least sense

1. **R12** ("It is bought in 2028, not 2031 ..."). The sentence before ends on "one world index fund", so "It" reads
   as the share fund. Read that way, the reason you give (the residency can't depend on shares) is an argument
   against buying it. Name the thing: "The floor fund is bought in 2028". Also, until 2031 the top of her contribution
   still rests on shares, so only the bottom rests "on the US government instead". Say "the bottom of her
   contribution".
2. **R10** ("enough to return about all of it by December 2032"). "It" could be the leftover money, the 2033 payment
   or the fund. The reader can't tell how big the bottom of the range is until the next sentence explains the
   difference. Write "enough to pay back roughly the whole leftover by December 2032".
3. **R9** ("the first meaning of XR"). You never say what X and R stand for. The reader has to guess from "ten rungs".
   Say it once in plain words (as H6 and K11 do) before using it.
4. **M2** ("So we hedge it!"). The plan doesn't hedge her career: it keeps share risk out of the payment money and
   still buys a share fund for the top. Your own M11 ("a counterbalance ... not a contributor") is the accurate
   claim. A finance reader will mark "hedge" as wrong.
5. **M15** ("these pay what they say"). "These" includes the iBonds ETFs from the sentence before, and a term fund has
   no promised payout at its end. Limit the claim to the bonds, or say the funds pay close to a known amount.
6. **K12** ("a holding kept to maturity still pays what it was bought to pay"). This is the same problem: the Term
   Treasury ETFs you list in K6 don't have a fixed end payout. H11 shows the safe wording ("A Treasury bond held to
   maturity ...").
7. **R14** ("XR's second meaning, an extra reserve ..., enough to meet every payment"). The pun asks the reader to
   decode a label in the middle of a mechanism. "Enough to meet every payment" reads as if the extra reserve alone
   pays all ten, when you mean it fills the reinvestment gap.
8. **R20** ("the money waiting for them"). The only plural nouns "them" can grab are the ladder and the extra reserve.
   You mean the payments, which aren't named in the sentence.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text H.** Every sentence names its subject outright ("The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder", "The bottom is
that fund's payout, rounded down"), so nothing hangs on an "it" reaching back across another noun. It gives the
reason inside the sentence that makes the decision: H24's "because a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after
a bad year" and H14's "so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice". It explains its name once and in full (H6, "X
for ten, R for redemption") and never asks the reader to decode it again. It limits its claims exactly: H11 says "A
Treasury bond held to maturity", not "these", and H23 builds in "rounded down" instead of treating a fund's payout as
exact. It also places the plan against known alternatives in one line each (duration matching against one bond per
payment, the capital-protected note, portfolio insurance), so a finance reader knows what it is by what it isn't. K is
just as easy sentence by sentence, but it covers less (no range mechanism, no reinvestment risk), so part of its
clarity comes from leaving the hard parts out. R covers the same mechanism as H, in compressed steps linked by
pronouns.

## Any pattern

The failures cluster in R and in the most intricate stretch of the mechanism (the 2028 purchase, the 2031 move, the
reinvestment top-up). They come in four kinds:

- **Reference.** A pronoun or a back-pointer ("it", "them", "that half", "the 2028 holding", "after this") carries a
  referent from one or two sentences back, sometimes across a nearer noun that grabs it first (R10, R12, R16, R20).
- **Shorthand.** A figure stands in for the money behind it ("buys the bottom", "shares that pay for its top", "both
  ends are owned"). This is readable once, but it piles up.
- **Labels.** The reader is asked to decode a label mid-argument (XR's first and second meanings). The same name worked
  when it was explained once up front (H6, K11).
- **Overreach.** The sense failures are a different kind: a property of a Treasury bond stretched to cover the funds
  (M15, K12), or a technical word used loosely (M2's "hedge").

Tone did not cause failures by itself: M's exclamations and the caplet framing in R, H and K were all understood.
Compression and unclear reference did.
