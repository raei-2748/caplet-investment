## The sentences that made least sense

1. **R10.** Too many loose references in one sentence. "The fund behind the 2033 payment" appears for the first time (the ladder was described as "holdings", not funds), and "enough to return about all of it" has two possible owners for "it": the 2033 payment or the leftover money. It also says the leftover money buys the fund, then the next sentence spends part of that same money on shares. Name the fund, say "about as much as was left over", and say only part of the leftover is spent on it.
2. **R11.** "The difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund." The difference between what the holding costs and what it repays is interest paid in 2032; it cannot be invested in 2028. What actually goes into shares is the cash left unspent, which is the same size as the interest. Make the cash the subject and keep the interest as its measure, as Text H's H17 does ("What remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into...").
3. **R12.** "It is bought in 2028, not 2031" follows straight after the shares, so "It" reads as the share fund, when the reason given (the residency can't depend on shares) shows it must be the Treasury holding. Nobody had proposed 2031 as a date, so "not 2031" answers a question the reader never had. Name the holding and say why waiting would be worse.
4. **R3.** "The rest buys the bottom of her 2031 range and shares that pay for its top." A range's bottom is a number and can't be bought; shares don't "pay for" a figure. The sentence leaves out the Treasuries that do the work. Say "buys Treasuries that set the bottom".
5. **R14.** She "sells up to half her shares for XR's second meaning". A meaning isn't something you buy, and the second meaning's letters are never given. Literally, the extra reserve on its own is "enough to meet every payment", when it means enough, with the ladder, to cover the shortfall if waiting money earns nothing.
6. **R18.** "Both ends are owned before anyone hears them." Ends of a range are figures; she owns Treasuries, not figures. Then "those Treasuries" points only at the funds in R17, although the bottom rests on the 2028 holding.
7. **H2.** "Only the interest those would earn goes into world shares." In 2028 that interest doesn't exist yet ("would earn"), so it can't be invested, and the pitch never says where the money for shares comes from. H17 later says it properly.
8. **M25.** "Whatever figure you choose is money you already own." Taken literally, any figure she picks would be owned money, which can't be true above the range. Say "the figure you choose, up to the top, is backed by Treasuries you already own".

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text K.** Each sentence carries one step, and the reason for the step sits in the same sentence ("We chose Treasuries because her $50,000 payments are fixed..."; "Because no operating payment depends on it, this money can take the risk of shares"). Its numbers add up on the page: $300,000 plus about $200,000 of interest covers the $500,000 (K8), so the reader is never asked to take a "cost" on trust. Its label is spelled out at first use ("X is the Roman numeral for 10 ... XR (10 Rungs)"), where R speaks of "the first meaning of XR" without ever saying what X and R stand for. Its pronouns reach back one sentence at most ("It is complete by January 2028", "From then on it releases its dose"). And it never makes a number do the work of money: holdings are bought, money goes into a fund, and only holdings are owned. The cost is that K is clearest partly because it does least. It says nothing about the 2031 range, a floor for the building or reinvestment, which are exactly where the other texts' sentences break, so a reader finishes K not knowing what Laura would tell co-sponsors. Text M is nearly as easy to follow, but it overstates (M11, M25, M26).

## Any pattern

- **Figures treated as things.** Buying "the bottom" (R3), "both ends are owned" (R18), "whatever figure ... is money you already own" (M25), Treasuries that "repay" a sum nobody borrowed (R1, R5). The writer shortens "the Treasuries that will pay this figure" to "the figure", and the sentence then says something impossible.
- **Money moved before it exists.** Interest that "would" be earned (H2) or "the difference" between cost and payout (R11) going into shares on the day of purchase. The sentence that works (H17) makes the leftover cash the subject and uses the interest only as a measure of its size.
- **Stretched pointers and late labels.** "It" (R12), "all of it" (R10), "them" (R20), "that fund" (H23), "the Treasuries" (M26); names used before they are defined or never defined at all (XR's first and second meanings, "the 2028 holding", "kept shares", "spare money", "the money set aside").
- **Where the failures fall.** The ladder passages are clear in all four texts. Nearly every failure is in the 2031 range and reinvestment passages, and the more steps a text packs into 500 words, the more it fails: R most, then H. R's failed sentences each carry two or three steps joined by "because", "so" or a colon.
- **Absolute words that later sentences undo.** "Definitely not a contributor" (M11) before a share fund is bought; "whatever figure" (M25); "never needs rebalancing" (H9) before the ladder's reinvestment weakness and the move of shares into Treasuries (H26-H27).
