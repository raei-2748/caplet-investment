## The sentences that made least sense

1. **R12.** "It is bought in 2028, not 2031" has no clear "it". The last thing named is the world index fund, and reading it that way, the sentence says shares are bought early because the residency can't depend on shares. Name the thing ("the Treasury fund behind the bottom"). Also say why 2031 was ever a possibility, because nobody suggested it.
2. **R10.** "Enough to return about all of it" could mean the leftover money or the 2033 payment. "The fund behind the 2033 payment" appears without introduction, and the sentence packs three steps (buy more, size it, set the bottom) into one. Say plainly that you buy just enough of the fund to pay back the whole leftover by December 2032.
3. **R9.** "Those ten rungs are the first meaning of XR" assumes I already know that X is ten and R is rungs. Nothing in the text says so. I could only guess.
4. **R3.** "Shares that pay for its top before she names it" asks me to picture shares paying for a figure that hasn't been named. The missing step is that the shares are sold and turned into Treasuries first. In a 50-word pitch I can't rebuild that.
5. **H2.** "Only the interest those would earn goes into world shares" reads as though interest will be moved into shares as it's paid. H17 later shows it is a sum about that size invested at the start. As written, the pitch misled me about how my money moves.
6. **R20.** "The money waiting for them": the only things "them" can grammatically refer to are the ladder and the extra reserve, and money waiting for those is nonsense. You mean the payments, so say "the payments".
7. **R16.** After two pages telling me Treasuries pay fixed sums, "a fund's payout isn't fixed" arrives in a subordinate clause with no explanation. It makes me wonder whether the funds in my ladder are fixed either. This deserves its own sentence, or none.
8. **H18.** "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note": "this" is a structure built over three sentences and never named, and the note and the option are both unexplained. I hadn't asked what banks sell. If the comparison matters, tell me what I gain by not buying the bank's version.

## What the clearest text does that the others don't

**Text M** is the clearest. It gives the reason before the machinery: M8-M10 builds "your career is a holding of shares you can't sell" in three short steps I could follow first time. Only then does it say what goes where. Each holding arrives with its job in the same sentence: the late-2032 fund is "the bare minimum you'll be able to promise", the share fund "will help determine your maximum", and the kept half "is your flexibility" for named things (overruns, the Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short). Mechanisms are put in everyday words ("Held to the end, these pay what they say"; "whatever figure you choose is money you already own"). Things keep one name, and there is no wordplay on the letters of a name to decode. Its only slips are small: "Yet another" in the pitch and the loose "the Treasuries" in M26. Text K is just as plain line by line, but it never tells me how my 2031 range is set, which is the thing I most need from it. H and R cover more of the plan (the reinvestment risk, the extra reserve), but that is where they lost me.

## Any pattern

The failures cluster where the plan is most intricate: the 2028 money and the 2031 steps, mostly in R and H. Four things recur. First, a pronoun or "that ..." phrase reaches back across sentences, often to something given a new name on the way: "It", "all of it", "them", "that fund", "the 2028 holding", "kept shares" / "spare money" / "the first half". Second, the name is made to carry new meanings (XR as ten rungs, redemption, extra reserve) without saying how the letters map, and each time it interrupts the money step. Third, future money is spoken of as if it were here now (interest the Treasuries "would earn" going into shares, "the difference"). That skips the one step that makes the idea work: the Treasury fund is sized to give back the whole sum. Fourth, some sentences answer a question I hadn't asked (why not 2031, what banks sell, where the name comes from in a pitch). The more steps a sentence packs in (R3, R10, R12), the more likely I had to rebuild it. Sentences with one holding and one job each (most of M and K) almost never failed.
