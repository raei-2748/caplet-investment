# Prior art: why a sentence that parses can still make no sense (Thu 19 Nov 2026)

These are notes for the team's voice study, not report prose. Nothing here is meant to be copied into the Final Report.

The complaint: "even after reading it 3 times and understanding the logical chain, the sentence simply does not make any sense." The five sentences that drew it, numbered for use below:

- **E1** "Our dose is Treasuries: before anyone relies on a sum Laura commits to, she owns the ones that will repay it."
- **E2** "Because it costs less than it returns, the difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund, so the shares risk only that interest."
- **E3** "Both ends are owned before anyone hears them, and in 2033 she gives what those Treasuries pay, up to the top."
- **E4** "Like the caplet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it."
- **E5** "X is the Roman numeral for 10, and our XR (10 Rungs) will not be crushed: we won't sell them before they mature."

How the sources were read: each quotation below was read in the source or in a full-text copy at the address given. Where only a secondary copy was available, the note says so. No quotation runs past 25 words.

---

## 1. The problem, classified first

Reading research splits understanding into three levels: the words and grammar (the "surface"), the network of ideas the text states and how they link (the "textbase"), and a working picture of the situation the text describes (the "situation model"). Kintsch (1988) and Zwaan and Radvansky (1998) set out this split; Section 2 cites them.

| Level | What a reader does | Do E1-E5 pass? |
|---|---|---|
| Surface: words and grammar | Parse the sentence | Yes. Every word is common and every sentence is grammatical. The leader parsed them. |
| Textbase: what each phrase points to, and how clauses link | Find what "it", "the ones", "both ends" and "those Treasuries" refer to; name the link behind each "and", colon and "because" | Partly. E2's "it" has no noun to point to. E3's "both ends", "those Treasuries" and "the top" have none in the sentence. E5's "and" joins a fact about Roman numerals to a promise about bonds. |
| Situation model: a picture of who does what, when, why, with what | Run the plan in the head: money in, bonds bought, years pass, money out | No. E2 puts money into a fund before that money exists. E3 has numbers being "owned". E1 has Treasuries "repaying" a gift. E4 and E5 lean on an image (crushing a pill) that maps one verb and nothing else. |

**Classification.** This is a coherence failure, not a syntax or vocabulary failure. The sentences fail at the textbase and situation-model levels, and the cause is on the writer's side: the writer's private knowledge (what the deposit is for, what "XR" means on a pill bottle) fills gaps that the reader cannot fill. A readability formula, which counts only word and sentence length, would pass all five; that point is my inference, not a cited finding.

**Why "understanding the logical chain" is not the same as "making sense."** The leader rebuilt the chain, but did it with his own knowledge of the plan; that is textbase repair done from outside the sentence. "Making sense" is the next level: every word has to fit the world (a number cannot be owned) and every link has to be true (the interest does not exist yet when the fund is bought). Britton and Gülgöz (1991) show the two can come apart. Their readers recalled a text and still built the opposite picture of what it said.

## 2. Sources, grouped by field

### A. Cognitive psychology of reading comprehension

**Chomsky, N. (1957). *Syntactic structures*. Mouton.**
- **What it shows.** Grammaticality and meaning are separate. "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is well formed and makes no sense.
- **Quotation (p. 15).** "Yet (1), though nonsensical, is grammatical, while (2) is not grammatical." I read this only in a secondary source, the Wikipedia article "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously", which cites p. 15. I did not read the book.
- **How it applies.** It is the baseline: passing the grammar test proves nothing about sense. It does not explain the five examples, because each of their phrases is locally meaningful. What fails is how the phrases fit together and fit the world.

**Bransford, J. D., & Johnson, M. K. (1972). Contextual prerequisites for understanding: Some investigations of comprehension and recall. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 11*(6), 717-726. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9**
- **What it shows.** It tested passages built from ordinary words in normal English sentences. Without the right context, people rated them hard to understand and recalled little. With the context given first (a picture, or a title such as "washing clothes"), comprehension and recall roughly doubled. The same context given afterwards hardly helped. In Experiment I (out of 7), comprehension was rated 2.30 with no context, 6.10 with context before and 3.30 with context after. Recall (out of 14 ideas) was 3.60, 8.00 and 3.60.
- **Quotation (Experiment I discussion).** "All Ss presumably knew the lexical meanings of the words and were familiar with the sentence structures used in the passage." Read in the full-text PDF at pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/bransford72.pdf.
- **How it applies.** This is the cleanest experimental proof that a sentence can parse and still not be understood. It explains E4 and E1. A reader who does not already hold the frame "an extended-release caplet must not be crushed" cannot understand E4, and E5 supplies the frame only afterwards. The "context after" result says explaining a sentence later does not rescue it.

**Kintsch, W. (1988). The role of knowledge in discourse comprehension: A construction-integration model. *Psychological Review, 95*(2), 163-182. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.2.163**
- **What it shows.** Comprehension builds layered representations: a linguistic level, a propositional "text base", and a situation model in which the text's content is merged with the reader's knowledge. Inferences are produced freely and then pruned to what coheres.
- **Quotation (p. 163).** The top level is "a level at which the text itself has lost its individuality and its information content has become integrated into some larger structure". Read in the APA full text, psycnet.apa.org/fulltext/1988-28529-001.html.
- **How it applies.** It supplies the vocabulary for the classification above. E1-E5 can be parsed into propositions, but those propositions do not merge into one consistent structure.

**Kintsch, W., & Rawson, K. A. (2005). Comprehension. In M. J. Snowling & C. Hulme (Eds.), *The science of reading: A handbook* (pp. 209-226). Blackwell.**
- **What it shows.** It is a plain summary of the same model. Texts always leave gaps, and readers fill them with inferences from knowledge. A reader who gets only what is explicit understands shallowly.
- **Quotation.** "Texts are almost never fully explicit, so there are always gaps left to be filled in by the reader." Read in the chapter PDF at sites.pitt.edu/~perfetti/PDF/Kintsch%20&%20Rawson.pdf. The handbook's contents page shows the chapter starting on p. 209.
- **How it applies.** Gaps are normal. The trouble in E1-E5 is that the reader has nothing to fill them with. E2's missing premise (the deposit equals the sum the bonds will repay) and E5's "XR" can be filled only from the writer's files.

**Zwaan, R. A., Langston, M. C., & Graesser, A. C. (1995). The construction of situation models in narrative comprehension: An event-indexing model. *Psychological Science, 6*(5), 292-297. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00513.x**
- **What it shows.** Readers index each event on five dimensions and must update the picture whenever one of them shifts.
- **Quotation (abstract).** "events are the focal points of situations conveyed in narratives and are connected in memory along five dimensions: time, space, protagonist, causality, and intentionality". Read in the abstract on JSTOR (40063035) and APA PsycNet. The full text is paywalled.
- **How it applies.** The model was built for stories. Applying it to plan sentences is my extension, but a plan sentence is a short story about money over years, so the dimensions fit.
  - **E2 breaks time and cause.** The interest is earned later, yet it "goes into" a fund now. The real cause, cash left over today because the bonds cost less than the sum they repay, is never stated.
  - **E3 shifts every dimension in one sentence.** The time shifts from before 2031 to 2033. The actor shifts from an unnamed owner to "anyone" (the co-sponsors) to "she". The causal link is a bare "and".
  - **E1 puts four events in one clause** (commits, relies, owns, repays) with no order given.

**Zwaan, R. A., & Radvansky, G. A. (1998). Situation models in language comprehension and memory. *Psychological Bulletin, 123*(2), 162-185. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.123.2.162**
- **What it shows.** It reviews fifteen years of evidence that understanding a text means building a coherent model of the situation. It also reviews work in which a causal connective ("because") strengthens the reader's representation, while a non-causal link makes readers work to supply the missing inference.
- **Quotation (p. 163).** Many researchers argue that "the construction of a coherent situation model is tantamount to the successful comprehension of a text". Read in the full-text PDF at sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/Cognitive/Readings/Zwaan_Radvansky_1998.pdf.
- **How it applies.** It is the review behind the split in Section 1. Its point about connectives cuts both ways for E2. "Because" tells the reader to build a cause, so a false "because" damages the picture more than a vague "and" would.

**Haviland, S. E., & Clark, H. H. (1974). What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in comprehension. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 13*(5), 512-521. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4**
**Clark, H. H., & Haviland, S. E. (1977). Comprehension and the given-new contract. In R. O. Freedle (Ed.), *Discourse production and comprehension* (Chapter 1). Ablex.**
- **What it shows.** A definite phrase ("the beer", "the ones", "those Treasuries") tells the reader that its referent is already known. The reader searches memory for that referent and attaches the new information to it. If no direct antecedent is there, the reader has to build a bridge, which takes measurably longer. "Horace got some picnic supplies out of the car. The beer was warm." took about 180 ms longer than the version that mentioned beer first.
- **Quotations.**
  - Haviland and Clark (1974), abstract: "a target sentence with a definite noun phrase presupposing existence took less time to comprehend when its Given information had a direct antecedent". Read on ScienceDirect and in the Stanford PDF.
  - Clark and Haviland (1977): "there is no direct antecedent in the context sentence, and so the listener must build a bridge". Read in the Stanford PDF, web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/; the OCR there reads "direcl".
- **How it applies.** Every "given" phrase in the examples asks for a bridge, and in several the bridge cannot be built from the text.
  - **E2:** "it ... it ... its" appears before any noun at all.
  - **E3:** "Both ends", "those Treasuries" and "the top".
  - **E1:** "the ones ... repay it".
  - **E5:** "them", plural, after the singular "XR".

**Britton, B. K., & Gülgöz, S. (1991). Using Kintsch's computational model to improve instructional text: Effects of repairing inference calls on recall and cognitive structures. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 83*(3), 329-345. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329**
**Also: Kintsch, W. (1998). *Comprehension: A paradigm for cognition*. Cambridge University Press (pp. 310-311, on this study).**
- **What it shows.** The authors used Kintsch's model to find every point in a 1,000-word history text where the reader had to supply an inference ("Hanoi" after "North Vietnam"). They repaired each one by stating the link. Recall rose sharply, and readers' grasp of how the key ideas related came to match the experts'. Readers of the original could recall facts and still held the opposite view of the main point.
- **Quotations.**
  - Britton and Gülgöz (1991), abstract: "free recall of the principled revision was much increased over that of the original version". Read on Ovid and in ERIC.
  - Kintsch (1998, p. 310): "whereas the text emphasized the failure of the operation Rolling Thunder, in the students' judgments Rolling Thunder was linked to success, instead." Read in the book PDF at andymatuschak.org/files/papers/.
- **How it applies.** It shows the fix works and is mechanical: find each gap and state the link. It also warns that a reader who can repeat E2 can still come away with the wrong mechanism, namely that the plan invests interest it has already earned.

### B. Linguistics and philosophy of language

**Hobbs, J. R. (1979). Coherence and coreference. *Cognitive Science, 3*(1), 67-90. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0301_4**
- **What it shows.** A text is coherent only if each part is linked to the next by a recognisable relation (elaboration, explanation, contrast, and so on). Shared reference is not enough. Readers often resolve pronouns as a by-product of finding that relation.
- **Quotations.**
  - p. 67: "John took a train from Paris to Istanbul. He likes spinach." This "is not coherent, even though 'he' can refer only to John."
  - p. 69: "Coherence thus plays a role beyond sentence boundaries analogous to the role played by grammaticality within sentences."
  - Read in the paper PDF at isi.edu (people-hobbs).
- **How it applies.**
  - **E5 is the spinach example.** "X is the Roman numeral for 10" and "our XR will not be crushed" share a symbol but no relation.
  - **E1's colon promises an elaboration of "dose"** and delivers a rule about timing instead.
  - **E3's "and" joins two clauses with no relation to name.**
  - **E2's pronouns cannot be resolved** because no relation pins them down.

**Sanders, T. J. M., Spooren, W. P. M. S., & Noordman, L. G. M. (1992). Toward a taxonomy of coherence relations. *Discourse Processes, 15*(1), 1-35. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539209544800**
- **What it shows.** Readers infer a small set of relations between clauses. The relations differ by a few basic features: causal or additive, positive or negative, about events or about claims, and the order of the parts. Readers are sensitive to these features, and connectives signal them.
- **Quotation (abstract).** "Inferring coherence relations, such as cause-consequence and claim-argument, is a necessary condition for a discourse representation to be coherent." Read in the OpenAlex abstract.
- **A second definition, read only at second hand.** The paper defines a relation, at p. 2, as "an aspect of meaning of two or more discourse segments that cannot be described in terms of the meaning of the segments in isolation". I read this only as quoted by Hoek and colleagues (ACL Anthology 2019.dnd-10.4); the Utrecht PDF did not load.
- **How it applies.** It gives the writer a checklist of relations to name. E2 marks a causal relation with "because" and "so", but the cause it marks is not the real one: the bond's cost being lower than its return does not put money in a fund today.

**Grice, H. P. (1975). Logic and conversation. In P. Cole & J. L. Morgan (Eds.), *Syntax and semantics: Vol. 3. Speech acts* (pp. 41-58). Academic Press.**
- **What it shows.** Listeners assume a speaker is cooperative, meaning truthful, as informative as needed, relevant and clear, and they draw inferences on that assumption. Grice himself allows that the clarity maxim may cover how a remark connects to the remarks around it.
- **Quotations.**
  - p. 46: under Relation, "a single maxim, namely, 'Be relevant.'"
  - p. 46: under Manner, "the supermaxim—'Be perspicuous'—and various maxims such as: 1. Avoid obscurity of expression. 2. Avoid ambiguity."
  - p. 51: Manner might apply to "the connection of what is said with adjacent remarks". The OCR in that copy reads "acljacent".
  - Read in the reprint PDF at sfu.ca/~jeffpell/Cogs300/GriceLogicConvers75.pdf and the ILLC copy.
- **How it applies.** Each maxim catches a different example.
  - **Relation, E5.** Why is the reader told the Roman numeral for 10? A cooperative reader hunts for a reason and finds a pun.
  - **Quantity, E4.** "Works as long as nobody crushes it" is true of almost anything, so it informs almost nothing.
  - **Quality, E4.** The plan is not "named after" a caplet; the team is.
  - **Manner.** It covers all five.

**Sperber, D., & Wilson, D. (1995). *Relevance: Communication and cognition* (2nd ed.). Blackwell. (1st ed. 1986.)**
**Wilson, D., & Sperber, D. (2004). Relevance theory. In L. R. Horn & G. Ward (Eds.), *The handbook of pragmatics* (pp. 607-632). Blackwell.**
- **What it shows.** Readers weigh what an input gives them (new conclusions) against the effort it costs. Every utterance carries a presumption that it is worth the effort.
- **Quotations.**
  - Sperber and Wilson (1995), Preface to the Second Edition: "Human cognitive processes, we argue, are geared to achieving the greatest possible cognitive effect for the smallest possible processing effort." Read in the book PDF hosted at monoskop.org.
  - Wilson and Sperber (2004): "other things being equal, the greater the processing effort required, the less relevant the input will be". Read at dan.sperber.fr (the chapter text).
- **How it applies.** Three readings is a large effort. When the payoff is "the plan works if no one breaks it" (E4) or a pun (E5), the reader judges the sentence not worth it, and the judgement spreads to the writer. This is the economic form of the leader's complaint.

**Ryle, G. (1949). *The concept of mind*. Hutchinson.**
- **What it shows.** A category mistake treats something as if it belonged to a logical type it does not belong to. The example is a visitor who, after seeing the colleges and libraries, asks to see "the University".
- **Quotation (p. 16 in the Hutchinson printing).** The visitor spoke "as if 'the University' stood for an extra member of the class of which these other units are members". Read in the scanned book at debracollege.dspaces.org and in the CMU PDF. Page numbers differ between printings.
- **How it applies.** Each example gives a subject or object a role its category cannot take.
  - **E3: "Both ends are owned."** The ends of a range are figures. She owns the Treasuries that pay them.
  - **E1: Treasuries "repay" a sum Laura "commits to".** Repaying needs a prior debt to the payer. A gift is not a loan.
  - **E5: "rungs ... mature".** Rungs belong to ladders; bonds mature.
  - **E2: "the shares risk only that interest".** What is at risk is the cash put into the shares.
  - **The point.** Each mistake is small, and readers sense the mismatch even when they cannot name it. That is the "does not make sense" feeling after the chain has been rebuilt.

### C. Metaphor and analogy

**Gentner, D. (1983). Structure-mapping: A theoretical framework for analogy. *Cognitive Science, 7*(2), 155-170. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3**
- **What it shows.** An analogy works by carrying relations (causes, orders, constraints) from a familiar base to a target, not surface attributes. Under the systematicity principle, readers carry over a connected system of relations in preference to an isolated one. A comparison that carries neither attributes nor relations is an anomaly. Gentner's example is "Coffee is like the solar system" (Table 1, p. 161).
- **Quotations.**
  - p. 161: "Finally, a comparison with neither attribute overlap nor relational overlap is simply an anomaly."
  - p. 162: analogy "conveys a system of connected knowledge, not a mere assortment of independent facts".
  - Read in the paper PDF at archive.org (gentner_dedre/gentner1983.pdf).
- **How it applies.**
  - **E4 maps one isolated verb, "crush", and no system.** The base relation is that crushing an extended-release pill releases the whole dose at once. That relation would have to map to "selling a bond early fixes its price before it pays". The sentence states neither half, and most readers do not know the base relation.
  - **E5 maps a typographic coincidence ("X" = 10).**
  - **E1's "dose" maps nothing the sentence states.** By Gentner's table, E4 sits close to "Coffee is like the solar system".

**Glucksberg, S., & Keysar, B. (1990). Understanding metaphorical comparisons: Beyond similarity. *Psychological Review, 97*(1), 3-18. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.97.1.3**
- **What it shows.** "A is B" metaphors ("my job is a jail") are understood as class-inclusion statements. The vehicle ("jail") names a category of things that confine, and it is also that category's best example.
- **Quotation (abstract).** "the metaphor vehicle (e.g., 'jail') refers to that category, and at the same time is a prototypical exemplar of that category". Read in the APA PsycNet abstract and the UChicago MDL page.
- **How it applies.** For E1's "Our dose is Treasuries" to work, "dose" would need to be the prototype of some known category that Treasuries could join. It is not; "a measured amount of medicine" offers nothing Treasuries share. "Jail" works because everyone knows what jails typify, and "dose" typifies nothing relevant.

**Bowdle, B. F., & Gentner, D. (2005). The career of metaphor. *Psychological Review, 112*(1), 193-216. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.112.1.193**
- **What it shows.** Novel figures are processed as comparisons, and only conventional ones as categories. Novel figures are understood faster, and preferred, in simile form ("A is like B"). A novel metaphor in "A is B" form sends the reader looking for a category that is not there.
- **Quotation.** "The metaphor form initially invites an inappropriate comprehension strategy—it invites searching for a category that does not exist." Read in the paper PDF at groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/BowdleGentner05.pdf.
- **How it applies.** E1 uses the costly form for a brand-new figure. E4 does use the simile form ("Like the caplet"), which is right for a novel figure, but the comparison still fails Gentner's test because only one relation carries over.

### D. The writer's side: why the writer cannot see the gap

**Camerer, C., Loewenstein, G., & Weber, M. (1989). The curse of knowledge in economic settings: An experimental analysis. *Journal of Political Economy, 97*(5), 1232-1254. https://doi.org/10.1086/261651**
- **What it shows.** It named the "curse of knowledge": people who know more cannot reliably reproduce the judgements of people who know less. Market incentives reduced the bias but did not remove it.
- **Quotation (abstract).** "Better-informed agents are unable to ignore private information even when it is in their interest to do so". Read in the abstract at the University of Chicago Press and CaltechAUTHORS.
- **How it applies.** It is the cause behind all five examples. The writer knows that "XR" is pharmacy shorthand for extended release, that the team is named Caplet, and that the 2028 deposit equals the sum the bonds repay. Knowing those things, the writer cannot read the sentence as someone who doesn't.

**Newton, E. L. (1990). *The rocky road from actions to intentions* [Doctoral dissertation, Stanford University].**
- **What it shows.** In the "tappers and listeners" study, tappers drummed well-known songs and predicted that listeners would name about half. Listeners named 3 of 120, or 2.5%. I read the numbers in secondary summaries: Stanford Magazine (2007) on Heath and Heath's *Made to Stick*, and Castellion's 2008 review of that book. I confirmed the dissertation's title and date on its scanned title page (gwern.net) but did not read its results chapter.
- **Quotation.** Heath and Heath (2007), *Made to stick*, Random House, as quoted in Stanford Magazine: tappers "have been given knowledge (the song title) that makes it impossible for them to imagine what it's like to lack that knowledge".
- **How it applies.** E5 is a tapped song. The writer hears "extended release, do not crush" when writing "XR". The reader hears two letters.

**Hinds, P. J. (1999). The curse of expertise: The effects of expertise and debiasing methods on prediction of novice performance. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 5*(2), 205-221. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205**
- **What it shows.** Experts predicted novices' difficulty worse than intermediates did. Prompts meant to reduce the bias did not help.
- **Quotation (abstract).** "those with more expertise were worse predictors of novice performance times and were resistant to debiasing techniques intended to reduce underestimation". Read in APA PsycNet.
- **How it applies.** The drafting team that wrote E1-E5 had read every file. Asking it to "imagine a fresh reader" is the kind of debiasing that failed in this study. The test has to use a real cold reader.

**Keysar, B., & Henly, A. S. (2002). Speakers' overestimation of their effectiveness. *Psychological Science, 13*(3), 207-212. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00439**
- **What it shows.** Speakers were warned that their sentences were ambiguous and still overestimated how often listeners got their meaning. Listeners were right about 61% of the time. Overhearers who knew the intended meaning, but had not said the sentence, did not overestimate.
- **Quotation (abstract).** Speakers "underestimate the ambiguity of their own utterances and overestimate the extent to which their disambiguating cues make their intention transparent". Read in the full-text PDF at voices.uchicago.edu.
- **How it applies.** Writing the sentence is what produces the illusion that it is clear. That is why the writer of E3 sees "both ends" as obvious.

**Flower, L. (1979). Writer-based prose: A cognitive basis for problems in writing. *College English, 41*(1), 19-37. https://doi.org/10.2307/376357**
**Flower, L. S. (1981). Revising writer-based prose. *Journal of Basic Writing, 3*(3), 62-74.**
- **What it shows.** First drafts tend to be "writer-based". They follow the writer's own path of discovery and use terms loaded with private meaning. Reader-based prose has to be made deliberately, usually by revision.
- **Quotations.**
  - Flower (1981, p. 63): writer-based prose is "prose in which the writer is essentially talking to himself". Read in the PDF at wacclearinghouse.org/docs/jbw/v3n3/flower.pdf.
  - Flower (1979, p. 19): its source is the writer's "failure to transform private thought into a public, reader-based expression". I read this only as quoted by Writing Commons.
- **How it applies.** "XR", "dose", "caplet", "rungs", "both ends" and "the top" are private names for things the reader has not been given. E5 also follows the writer's route to the idea (Roman numeral, then pun, then promise) rather than the reader's need, which is the promise first.

**Pinker, S. (2014). *The sense of style: The thinking person's guide to writing in the 21st century*. Viking. (Chapter 3, "The Curse of Knowledge".)**
**Pinker, S. (2014). Why academics stink at writing. *The Chronicle Review*. https://stevenpinker.com/files/pinker/files/pinker_2014_why_academics_writing_stinks.pdf**
- **What it shows.** It applies the curse of knowledge to prose. Writers who are expert in a subject think in "chunks" and labels they have coined, skip steps that seem obvious, and cannot see their own opacity. The remedy is to show drafts to real readers.
- **Quotations.**
  - The book, chapter 3: "The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose." Read in a book PDF copy.
  - The 2014 essay: the cure "requires showing a draft to a sample of real readers and seeing if they can follow it". Read at stevenpinker.com.
- **How it applies.** It is the writing-craft version of the research in this section. It explains why the drafting team produced E1-E5 and why only a cold reader will catch the next one.

### E. Writing guidance built on the above

**Gopen, G. D., & Swan, J. A. (1990). The science of scientific writing. *American Scientist, 78*(6), 550-558.**
- **What it shows.** Readers expect particular things in particular places. They expect the "old" information that links back at the start of a sentence, and the person or thing whose story it is in the topic position. They expect the action in the verb and the new, emphatic point at the end. When the writer leaves out the linking information, the reader must construct it.
- **Quotation (section "Perceiving Logical Gaps").** "Often this happens when the connections are so clear in the writer's mind that they seem unnecessary to state". Read in the retyped full text at userpages.cs.umbc.edu/nicholas/601/Gopen-Swan_ScientificWriting.pdf.
- **A principle from the closing list.** "Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb." Read in the Yale PDF.
- **How it applies.**
  - **E2 opens with "Because it"**, which is neither old information nor a character.
  - **E1's topic is "Our dose"**, which is new to the reader and not a character.
  - **E4's action is buried** in the condition "as long as nobody crushes it".
  - **E5 ends on "before they mature"**, which is the right point, after 20 words spent on the Roman numeral.

**Williams, J. M., & Bizup, J. (2017). *Style: Lessons in clarity and grace* (12th ed.). Pearson.**
- **What it shows.** Readers judge prose clear when the subjects name the main characters, the verbs name their actions, and each sentence opens with something familiar.
- **Quotations (the book's "Ten Principles for Writing Clearly").** "Use subjects to name the characters in your story"; "Use verbs to name their important actions"; "Open your sentences with familiar units of information". Read in the archive.org full text of the 12th edition.
- **How it applies.** Here is who or what is the grammatical subject in each example.
  - **E1:** "Our dose", an abstraction.
  - **E2:** "the difference", an abstraction.
  - **E3:** "Both ends", in the passive with no agent.
  - **E4:** "the plan".
  - **The real characters are missing as subjects.** Laura, the co-sponsors, the bonds and the fund appear as subjects in only one clause of five sentences: "she gives" in E3.

**Sword, H. (2012, July 23). Zombie nouns. *The New York Times* (Opinionator). https://archive.nytimes.com/opinionator.blogs.nytimes.com/2012/07/23/zombie-nouns/**
- **What it shows.** Nouns made from verbs and adjectives ("nominalizations") push people and actions out of sentences.
- **Quotations.** Such nouns "cannibalize active verbs, suck the lifeblood from adjectives and substitute abstract entities for human beings". Of an example sentence: "Yet it fails to tell us who is doing what." Read in the NYT archive and the LSU reprint.
- **How it applies.** The examples have few classic "-tion" nouns, but they do the same thing with short abstract nouns: "dose", "difference", "interest", "ends", "top". Each stands where a person doing something should be. E2 is the clearest. "The difference ... goes into one world index fund" hides the person who buys the fund and the moment she buys it.

**Plain Language Action and Information Network. (2011). *Federal plain language guidelines* (Rev. 1, May 2011). https://www.plainlanguage.gov**
- **What it shows.** The U.S. government's plain-writing rules, from the law and technical-writing tradition, include using the same term for the same thing throughout (section "Use the same term consistently for a specific thought or object").
- **Quotation (p. 45).** "Using a different term may cause the reader to wonder if you are referring to the same group." Read in the guidelines PDF at fai.gov.
- **How it applies.** Across E1-E5 the same holding is called "Treasuries", "dose", "the ones", "XR", "10 Rungs" and "them". A reader who meets "XR" in E5 can fairly ask whether it is something other than the Treasuries in E1 and E3.
- **Not used.** Fowler's older warning against "elegant variation" makes the same point, but I read it only at second hand (Wikipedia), so it is left out.

## 3. A test a writer can run on every sentence

There are eight questions, each tied to the sources above. Run them in order; the first three take seconds. A sentence that fails any one of them goes back for rewriting.

**T1. Can I point to what every "it", "they", "the ...", "those ..." and "both ..." means?** (Haviland & Clark 1974; Clark & Haviland 1977; Britton & Gülgöz 1991)
- **How to run it.** For each pointing word, put a finger on the earlier words it stands for, in this sentence or the one before. If the reader would have to build the link from outside the text, name the thing instead.
- **Catches E2** ("it ... it ... its" before any noun), **E3** ("Both ends", "those Treasuries", "the top"), **E1** ("the ones ... repay it") and **E5** ("them" after "XR").

**T2. Who does what?** (Williams & Bizup 2017; Gopen & Swan 1990; Sword 2012)
- **How to run it.** Underline the subject and the main verb. Is the subject a person, or a thing that acts (Laura, the co-sponsors, the bonds, the fund)? Is the main action in the verb, not hidden in a noun such as "dose", "difference", "interest" or "ends"?
- **Catches E1** ("Our dose is"), **E2** ("the difference ... goes"), **E3** ("Both ends are owned", which has no agent) and **E4** ("the plan works").

**T3. Can I put a year on every event, in order?** (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser 1995; Zwaan & Radvansky 1998)
- **How to run it.** List the events the sentence describes with a date and an actor for each, then read the list in date order. Does anything use money or a fact before it exists? Does the actor change without being named?
- **Catches E2** (interest that arrives at maturity is invested at the start), **E3** (owned, heard, given: three times and three actors, none marked) and **E1** (commits, relies, owns, repays, in no stated order).

**T4. Can I name the link?** (Hobbs 1979; Sanders, Spooren & Noordman 1992; Grice 1975, Relation)
- **How to run it.** For every "and", "so", "because" and colon, write the relation in one word: cause, result, example, contrast or then. Is it true? If no word fits, the two halves are "He likes spinach". If "because" names a false cause, the sentence is worse than one with no connective.
- **Catches E5** ("X is the Roman numeral for 10, and ..."), **E1** (the colon after "dose" promises an explanation of "dose" and gives a timing rule), **E2** ("Because it costs less than it returns" is not why money goes into the fund) and **E3** (a bare "and").

**T5. Can that subject really do or have that?** (Ryle 1949)
- **How to run it.** Read subject, verb and object literally. Can an end of a range be owned? Can a Treasury repay a gift? Can a rung mature? Can shares "risk" interest? If not, name the real owner, payer or thing at risk.
- **Catches E3** ("ends are owned", "hears them"), **E1** ("repay it"), **E5** ("rungs ... mature") and **E2** ("the shares risk only that interest").

**T6. Does the image carry a system of relations, from a base every reader knows, given before it is used?** (Gentner 1983; Bowdle & Gentner 2005; Glucksberg & Keysar 1990; Bransford & Johnson 1972)
- **How to run it.** Write the mapping in two columns: in the base, X causes Y; in the plan, X' causes Y'. There must be at least one relation that carries over, not just one shared word, and the reader must already know the base without our files. The mapping must come before or with the image, never in a later sentence. A brand-new image works better as "like" than as "is".
- **Catches E4** (one verb, "crush", carries over; most readers do not know why caplets must not be crushed; the plan is not named after a caplet; the explanation arrives one sentence late, in E5), **E5** (the "XR" pun) and **E1** ("Our dose is Treasuries" sends the reader looking for a category that does not exist).

**T7. Is any word a private code, and has a cold reader said the sentence back?** (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989; Newton 1990; Hinds 1999; Keysar & Henly 2002; Flower 1979; Pinker 2014)
- **How to run it.** List every coined name, acronym or nickname: XR, caplet, dose, rungs, ends, top. Each one is defined at first use or cut. Then give the sentence, with only the text before it, to someone who has not seen the files, and ask them to say in their own words who does what and when. The writer's sense that it is clear counts for nothing: speakers overestimate, and experts resist debiasing.
- **Catches E5** ("XR"), **E4** ("caplet"), **E1** ("dose") and **E3** ("ends", "the top").

**T8. Is it worth the reader's effort, and is each thing kept under one name?** (Grice 1975, Quantity and Manner; Sperber & Wilson 1995; Wilson & Sperber 2004; Federal Plain Language Guidelines 2011)
- **How to run it.** What does the reader know after this sentence that they did not know before? Is that worth the reading time it costs? Does the sentence give an existing thing a new name?
- **Catches E4** ("works as long as nobody crushes it" says almost nothing) and **E5** (the Roman numeral fact). Across E1-E5 it catches one set of bonds under six names: Treasuries, dose, the ones, XR, 10 Rungs, them.

## 4. Notes for the team's voice study

- **The test is against unmapped images, not against images.** By Gentner's test, a figure works when every reader knows the base and at least one causal relation carries over. Pencil and ink is an example: pencil can be rubbed out and ink cannot, just as a figure no owned bond pays can still change and a figure an owned bond pays cannot. The caplet, "dose" and "XR" fail because the base is private or nothing carries over. This fits the voice rounds' finding that one turn is heard when it is glossed in its own sentence: the gloss is the mapping, given first.
- **Watch for E3's category mistake in current wording.** Wherever a figure, rather than a bond, is the grammatical thing that is "owned", T5 applies. "A figure owned in Treasuries" carries its own repair ("in Treasuries"); "both ends are owned" did not.
- **Run T7 with real cold readers.** Hinds (1999) and Keysar and Henly (2002) say self-checks by the writer, or by anyone who has read the files, are the least reliable check. The blind reads the voice programme already runs are the right instrument; adding "say it back in your own words" turns them into a sense test as well as a voice test.
