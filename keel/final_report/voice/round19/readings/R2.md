## From memory

1. From January 2028, the ten payments rest on Treasuries held for her: a bond or a Treasury fund for each year, plus extra Treasuries. With those, every payment is met even if the interest earns nothing while it waits. It cost roughly $39,000 of the 2028 deposit, about a quarter of it. A couple of thousand dollars finished the set of ten, and about $36,800 bought the extras. If rates go as expected, most of that (about $46,600) comes back to her by 2042.
2. The interest arrives years before the payment it is meant to help pay, and nobody knows what it will earn in the meantime. If rates fall and stay low, the waiting money earns less. No new money comes in after 2028. So a gap found in 2033 could only be filled from money promised to the building, or by resting a fixed payment on shares. The zero-interest bonds a lottery would use are banned for her, and that is why she needs the extras.
3. Only two things: the 2028 deposit not arriving by 31 March 2028, or a Treasury payment more than five business days late. No market price changes the plan.
4. (a) The ten payments: only a 2028 deposit far smaller than planned (about three-quarters smaller) or no deposit at all, or the US failing to pay. A smaller deposit's loss falls first on the building money and her shares, not on the payments. (b) The top figure: the Treasury fund behind it losing more than about 2% of what it pays to costs beyond its fee. Laura makes up the gap from her flexibility, as her signed letter says. (c) Her flexibility: a rise of more than about 25% in the cost of her share of the building (building costs plus the Taiwan dollar), assuming shares earn 7.5%; history has gone past that four times since 1991, at 28% or more in two years. Past that point, the other funders, a smaller design or a later start take the loss. A share fall also shrinks it directly.
5. A share fall can shrink her flexibility, and before 2031 it can lower the top she will be able to state. It cannot change the ten payments, and after January 2031 it cannot change the figure she has signed for. **My point for the team:** say plainly what a co-sponsor can book on the day the letter arrives, and what stands behind it. If both ends of the range are already owned, why is there a range? If the signed top can fall short, the make-up comes from her flexibility, and that money is still in shares. A co-sponsor's finance committee will ask exactly that.
