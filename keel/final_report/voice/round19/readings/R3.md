## From memory

1. From January 2028 the ten payments rest on Treasuries held for her: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds, one ending just before each 1 January payment, plus extra Treasuries so that every payment is met even if the money paid out early earns nothing while it waits. The cost was the first roughly $39,000 of the 2028 deposit, about a quarter of it: a couple of thousand to complete the ten and about $36,800 for the extra Treasuries. If rates turn out as today's prices imply, the extras aren't needed and about $46,600 comes back to her by 2042. (The earlier letter had this extra reserve bought in 2031 by selling up to half her shares, which is a different timing from the report's.)
2. Because the interest the bonds pay arrives years before the payment it helps to pay, and nobody knows what it will earn in between; if rates fall and stay low, the waiting money earns less. Zero-coupon Treasuries (STRIPS) would avoid this, as the California Lottery does, but they are banned. And after 2028 no new money comes in, so a gap found in 2033 could only be closed with money promised to the building or by resting a fixed payment on shares.
3. Only two things, neither a market price: no 2028 deposit by 31 March 2028, or a Treasury payment more than five business days late.
4. (a) The ten payments: they still hold even if the 2028 deposit is about three-quarters smaller, because they're bought first; the floor (bottom) and her shares take that loss. Beyond that, only a late Treasury payment, a US default in effect. (b) The top: it falls short only if IBTM's costs beyond its fee eat more than about 2% of what it pays; Laura makes up the difference herself from her flexibility, as her signed letter says. (c) Her flexibility: it covers about a 25% rise in the cost of her share of the building if shares earn the 7.5% assumed; past that, the other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the rest. History has beaten it: four times since 1991, building costs and the Taiwan dollar together raised the cost 28% or more within two years. It is also the only part a share fall touches.
5. A fall in share prices can shrink her flexibility (the part still in shares, promised to no one) and, before January 2031, how large a top she can state. It cannot change the ten payments or, after January 2031, her signed figure, because Treasuries held for her pay both on their dates. The point I'd ask the team to make clearer: what the top actually rests on and how it could fall short. Say in plain words what IBTM is, what its "costs beyond its fee" are, why a Treasury fund can pay out less than its Treasuries, and roughly how many dollars that risk is, so a reader knows why she signs a letter promising to cover it.
