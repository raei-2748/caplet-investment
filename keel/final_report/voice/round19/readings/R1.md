## From memory

1. From January 2028 the ten payments rest on Treasuries held for her: a ladder of ten bonds and term funds, one ending before each January payment, plus extra Treasuries. These are sized so that every payment is met even if the interest waiting to be used earns nothing. The cost was the first part of the 2028 deposit, about $39,000 (about a quarter of the deposit). Roughly $2,400 of that completes the ladder and roughly $36,800 buys the extra Treasuries. If rates turn out as today's prices imply, about $46,600 of it (in 2033 money) comes back to her by 2042.
2. The interest arrives years before the payment it serves, and no one knows what it will earn while it waits. Zero-coupon Treasuries (STRIPS), which would remove that problem, are banned. There is also no new money after 2028, so a gap found in 2033 could only be closed with money meant for the building, or by making a fixed payment depend on shares.
3. Only two things, neither of them a market price: no 2028 deposit by 31 March 2028, or a Treasury payment more than five business days late.
4. (a) The ten payments come up short only if the 2028 deposit is more than about three-quarters smaller than planned (or the Treasury doesn't pay). Up to that point the floor and her shares take the loss, because the payments are bought first. (b) The top falls short if the 2032 fund's (IBTM's) costs beyond its fee take more than about 2% of what it pays. Laura bears that loss: her signed letter has her make up the difference from her flexibility. (c) Her flexibility falls short if her share of the building gets more than about 25% dearer (building costs and the Taiwan dollar together), assuming shares earn 7.5%. It also shrinks if shares fall. Beyond that, the other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the loss. History has passed 28% four times since 1991.
5. A fall in share prices can change her flexibility and, before January 2031, how high a top she can state. It cannot change the ten payments, or, after January 2031, the figure she has signed. The point I would ask the team to make clearer is what stands behind the signed top. It rests on a Treasury fund whose payout isn't fixed, and any shortfall is covered from flexibility that is still in shares. So the team should say in one sentence how far the fund's expected payout sits above the signed figure, and why "no fall in share prices can change it" still holds.
