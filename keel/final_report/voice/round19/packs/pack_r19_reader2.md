# Reader pack 2 (voice round 19, Wed 25 Nov 2026)

A short letter from an earlier round of the competition, then three short passages about the same client. Each is read on its own, as if it were a page you had just turned to.

**Your role.** You read as a consultant who runs capital campaigns for arts buildings: you raise money from foundations, companies and government, and you know how donors judge a founder's promises. You are not an investment specialist. You read each page once.

**The case, in brief (what you know before you read).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000 into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay $50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments) to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is. Overpromising would hurt her credibility. A team of school students advises her; it may use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds.

## How to read each text

You are one of several independent readers, not on the writer's side. Do not open any other file, do not look
anything up, and do not try to find out who wrote what. Use only the page and the case above. Read each text straight
through once at a normal pace, from the plain version, then go to its numbered sentences and fill one table row per
sentence:

- **Follow**: 1 = understood the first time; 2 = had to read it again or hold something in mind to get it; 3 = had to
  rebuild the reasoning, or still unsure what it says.
- **Sense**: once understood, does what it says make sense? Y, N with the fault in a few words, or ? cannot judge.
- **Note**: only when Follow is 2-3 or Sense is N or ?: one short line on what is missing or wrong.

For **Passage 3 only**, add a **Paraphrase** column: what the sentence says, in your own everyday words.

Do not be charitable, and do not invent faults: a clear sentence that holds together gets 1 and Y however plain or
elaborate. Long words and long sentences are not faults in themselves.

When you have finished the last table, **do not look back at any text** and answer the five questions at the end of the
pack from memory.

Write your answers as Markdown to the file named in your instructions: one section per text (`## Letter`,
`## Passage 1`, `## Passage 2`, `## Passage 3`), each with its table (columns: # | Follow | Sense | Note, and for
Passage 3: # | Follow | Sense | Paraphrase | Note), then `## From memory` with the five answers.

## The letter

Under Caplet XR, before anyone relies on a sum Laura commits to, she owns the Treasuries that will repay it. A balanced mix would suit her record of thoughtful risks. But at current Treasury prices her ten operating payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and they are fixed, so in a balanced mix every gain or loss would fall on the last third. That third would swing like the shares of a heavily borrowed company.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of ten Treasury holdings, one repaying before each January from 2033 to 2042, and the 2028 deposit buys any it can't afford. The X and R stand for those ten rungs, and none is sold before it repays. Five are funds of Treasuries that all mature in one year, sellable any day but paying out what they hold, not a fixed sum. The payments are fixed in dollars, so inflation affects only what the facility will cost.

In 2028, what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, enough to return about all of it by December 2032, which sets the bottom of her range. Because it costs less than it returns, the difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund, so the shares risk only that interest. It is bought in 2028, not 2031, because the creative residency in Taiwan can't depend on shares: its co-sponsors, grants and fees can shrink in the same recessions that hit shares, and her facility contribution, resting on the US government instead, must hold when they do.

The ladder also carries reinvestment risk: interest arrives years before the payment it serves, so money waits, earning less if rates fall and stay low. So in 2031, before she names a range, she sells up to half her shares for an extra reserve of Treasuries, enough to meet every payment even if waiting money earned nothing. If that half falls short, the 2028 holding's payout makes up the rest.

The bottom is what that holding will pay after this, less a margin because a fund's payout isn't fixed. Then she sells the other half for Treasury funds repaying before 2033; the top is the bottom plus what that half raised. Both ends are owned before anyone hears them, and in 2033 she gives what those Treasuries pay, up to the top.

Whatever of the first half isn't sold, and anything paid beyond the top, stays hers, liquid and promised to no one, so she keeps room as the project develops. In 2033 her operating reserve is already in place: the ladder, the extra reserve and the money waiting for them. The plan's biggest strength, and what we mean by a high degree of certainty, is that, barring a US default, no payment, and no figure once named, depends on a share price. The tenth rung, with what was set aside for it, meets the last $50,000 in 2042.

## Passage 1

*A paragraph from a team's letter to Laura.*

In January 2027 we’ll use your first deposit to buy 10 Treasuries (T-Bonds or iShares iBonds Term Treasury ETFs, whichever works best for each year), each maturing just before it’s needed. Held to the end, these pay what they say – no price fluctuations for us to fear. At current yields, this would cost roughly $300,000 – in other words, your entire first deposit. But worry not – our plan isn’t dependent on yields staying put! Even if yields fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up being above $300,000, we’ll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit.

## Passage 2

*From a team's Final Report on Laura's plan: the ten-year commitment and the operating reserve.*

From January 2028, all ten payments are paid even if the money the Treasuries pay out early earns nothing while it waits. We hold them to that because the interest arrives years before the payment it helps to pay, and nobody knows what it will earn in between. The price is the first $39,162 of the 2028 deposit, about a quarter of it at today's rates: $2,388 completes the ten, and $36,774 buys extra Treasuries. If later rates turn out as today's prices imply, the extras are never needed, and about $46,600, in 1 January 2033 money, comes back to Laura by 2042.

The California State Lottery's investments must "mature in amounts sufficient to meet required prize payouts on dates immediately prior" to them – Laura's rule, in other words. But the Lottery holds zero-coupon Treasuries, which leave no interest to reinvest; Laura may not (STRIPS are banned), and the $36,774 is what that ban costs her.

From 2033 the operating reserve is a schedule, not a pot. Each payment has its own Treasury bond or fund ending just before its 1 January, and the reserve changes only as each pays out and is spent. We keep it apart from the building's money, and finish it in 2028, because nothing new comes in after that: a gap found in 2033 could be closed only with money promised to the building, or by resting a fixed payment on a share price.

## Passage 3

*From the same report: assumptions, limits and what to watch.*

Only two things would change the plan, and neither is a market price: no 2028 deposit by 31 March 2028, or a Treasury payment more than five business days late. The ten payments survive a 2028 deposit about three-quarters smaller: they are bought first, so the floor and Laura's shares take the loss. The top would fall short if IBTM's costs beyond its fee took more than about 2% of what it pays; her signed letter has her make up the difference from her flexibility. Her flexibility covers a rise of about 25% in the cost of her share of the building, if shares earn the 7.5% assumed; past that, the residency's other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the rest. History has gone past it: four times since 1991, building costs and the Taiwan dollar together made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years. Table 3 watches the early signs: the deposit, each Treasury payment, a fund's notice of an early end, the Taiwan dollar and building costs.

The funds forced to sell in Britain's 2022 gilt crisis had borrowed against their gilts; Laura borrows nothing, so no fall in prices can make her sell. No fall in share prices can change the ten payments or, after January 2031, her signed figure: both are in ink – Treasuries held for her will pay them on their date – and only her flexibility, still in shares and promised to no one, stays in pencil.
