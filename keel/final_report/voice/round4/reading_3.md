# Blind reading, round 4, reader 3

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. These are the things I listened for. The letter is warm and a little
rough. It talks straight to Laura, its sentences run on ("You'd think that someone with your taste for thoughtful risk
and 6 years to wait..."), and it gets excited ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not ... !"). It reaches for slightly grand
words ("ever-reliable Treasuries", "immune to markets"), makes confident promises, and allows itself one or two neat
lines, mostly at the end. Neat lines are rare and they sound sincere. It never explains things with an extended image.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Short, even, plain explanation that restates the IPS's ladder in report form. Nothing in it is beyond the
  team, but nothing sounds like them either: it is cleaner and flatter than the letter, with none of its bumps or
  enthusiasm.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan uses one bond per payment held to the end, so
  every payment is bought in January 2027 with Treasuries and none depends on shares.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. Each payment has its own Treasury or Treasury fund held to maturity,
  which pays what it promised whatever rates or markets do.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens with a polished hook ("an argument we expect a judge to resist, because we resisted it ourselves"),
  then asks a question and answers it in fragments ("Because of what that money is for."). It closes on a reframe ("isn't
  really a crash ... It's standing in front of co-sponsors..."). The "Nice!" feels like a borrowed exclamation used ironically: the
  IPS's exclamations are sincere. It reads as a skilled essayist doing a lively student, not the students themselves.
- **Meaning:** Putting three-quarters of the leftover 2028 money in shares adds about $7,000 at the median but costs
  $14,000 in a bad future and shrinks the range Laura can state, while all-Treasury has its own costs, so the plan's
  split is a deliberate middle.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. More shares would raise what she keeps on average but shrink what she can
  promise: the range drops from $95,000-$115,000 to $30,000-$85,000 and misses $95,000 in about 62 futures in 100.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Very compressed and controlled. Each sentence carries a mechanism, and there are crafted lines ("a number
  said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). It sounds
  like an experienced editor condensing the plan, without the IPS's warmth or looseness.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half the shares (moved into Treasuries
  first). Before naming it, any shortfall from reinvesting the ladder's interest at low rates is priced, given a margin,
  and covered from her kept shares; if that is not enough, the range comes down.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. Before the 2031 range is named, the shortfall from reinvesting interest
  at low rates is priced at that day's rates plus a margin and moved from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her
  flexibility, and if necessary the range, absorbs it, not the payments.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is argued like a paper. It cites an illustrator with a reference, and it stacks parentheses of method
  ("200,000 simulated futures, 6 November curve; benchmarks only...") and numbers. Every finding is then translated
  back into ink and pencil. It sounds like a careful, academic outsider, nothing like the chatty letter.
- **Meaning:** A promise, like ink, is easy to raise and messy to cut, so the plan states only what Laura owns in
  Treasuries. More shares would add a little at the median but shrink the range she can state and raise the chance of
  falling short, and going all-Treasury costs flexibility.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Shares are not owned Treasuries, so they cannot be promised: three-quarters in
  shares shrinks the statable range to $30,000-$85,000 (62 in 100 under $95,000), and all shares leaves no bottom at all.
- **Image:** It helped with the first point (you can raise a promise later but not cut it). After that it got in the
  way, because I had to keep translating "inked", "pencil" and "half of VT stays in pencil" back into plain money while
  also reading the numbers.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** More conversational than 4 ("We tested it.", "Strangely enough"), but it is built around slogan turns ("the
  question isn't really 'how much growth?' – it's 'how much can she ink?'", "More pencil, less ink.") and a one-line
  twist at the end. The IPS's writers have a neat closing line, but not this many placed ones.
- **Meaning:** Shares count as pencil until half move into Treasuries, so putting more of the 2028 money in shares
  shrinks the range Laura can promise, while putting it all in Treasuries costs her total and flexibility. More shares
  would make her promise smaller.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Promises can only be made from what she owns in Treasuries; three-quarters in
  shares cuts the statable range to $30,000-$85,000 and misses $95,000 in about 62 futures in 100.
- **Image:** It mostly helped, because the passage defines ink and pencil plainly before using them and the reframe ("how
  much can she ink?") is clear. The slogan repetitions at the end added nothing.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves in a straightforward order: the objection, what the money is, the numbers, a fair word for the other
  extreme, then the conclusion. The asides are human ("that's the appeal", "To be fair"). The last sentence
  ("we'd rather hand her a number she'll never have to walk back than an extra $7,000 at the median") sounds like a team
  standing behind its choice. It is more polished than the IPS, but the same people could write it for judges.
- **Meaning:** More shares in the 2028 money would add about $7,000 at the median and double her flexibility, but it
  costs her a promisable figure (range $30,000-$85,000, frequent misses); all-Treasury costs flexibility and total; the
  plan's split avoids overpromising.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Shares add to what she keeps, and only Treasuries she owns add to what she can
  promise. With more shares, the range she can state falls and she misses $95,000 far more often.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's own sound: one long, slightly tangled sentence that piles a reason onto a reason. It uses
  a grand phrase ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), an eager "just 0.06%" and "(as per JPMorgan)", and a
  confident opener ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong"). It is a little unpolished in the way the letter is.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong because no payment depends on it. It buys the whole world
  market cheaply instead of picking winners, and decides how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. The Treasuries already cover the payments, and VT only answers how generous the
  facility contribution can be, so a 30% fall changes the top of the contribution, not the plan.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The reflection itself sounds human ("It took us a while to see why it failed", "The other surprise was how
  little our own argument had mattered"). But it is organised around a long borrowed printing image, and it keeps
  landing on pivot lines ("A crash wasn't really the problem – timing was.", "So the fix wasn't better shares. It was
  to..."). It feels shaped by a writer who plans effects. The IPS's writers rarely do.
- **Meaning:** The first, mostly-shares plan failed its own test because each payment depended on share values being
  right on ten particular dates. The fix was to buy the payments first in Treasuries, and the team learned to ask what
  a decision changes before arguing about it.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It relied on shares being worth enough on each of ten payment dates, and in
  testing it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets (13.7% if the 2028 deposit was $75,000, 40.8% if it never
  came).
- **Image:** Mixed. The idea that registration means timing did make clear that the risk was the dates, not the
  average. But I needed a paragraph of printing terms to get there, and "print the payments over everything" stayed
  vague.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens on a confessional hook ("We'd love to say we got Laura's plan right the first time. We didn't...").
  It then stacks three devices: the printing image, "inked first" from another image, and the Samuelson citation, each
  delivered smoothly. It sounds like a polished essay built from the team's facts, not the team's own unhurried
  explaining.
- **Meaning:** The first plan, three-quarters shares trusting ten years of growth, failed its test before any trade,
  because each payment needed shares to be the right size on the right date. Buying the payments first fixed it, the
  belief has a name (time diversification), and the small effect of the share-weight debate taught the team to weigh
  decisions by what they change.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It counted on shares being worth enough on ten separate dates, and in testing it
  missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets, far more if the 2028 deposit fell short.
- **Image:** It got in the way. The printing image needs explaining, and then "inked" from a different image arrives in
  the same paragraph, so I was juggling two pictures instead of following the point.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It reads like a team telling its own story plainly. It opens with "Our first plan was the obvious one", the
  same move as the IPS's "So, naturally, that was our plan", and it has small chatty touches ("Our instinct, it turns
  out, even has a name", "back in 1963"). It ties the 3.2% back to Laura's "high degree of certainty". A couple of
  sentences are rhetorical set-ups ("That sounds small, until you remember..."), so it is not certain.
- **Meaning:** The team's first, mostly-shares plan missed payments in testing, especially if the second deposit fell
  short, so they switched to buying the payments first. They learned that their share-weight debate barely mattered,
  and that time does not remove risk from dated payments (Samuelson).
- **Check question (The first plan):** It assumed ten years of share growth would carry ten dated payments, but it
  missed at least one in 3.2% of tested markets, against a client who asked for a high degree of certainty.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Earnest and plain ("We genuinely believed 10 years was long enough"), with small repetitions ("before a
  single trade" twice) and the IPS's habit of writing "10" in digits. It owns the mistake without much staging. The
  Samuelson sentence and "The second lesson was more humbling" are a little textbook, but the whole could be the team
  writing a reflection.
- **Meaning:** The first plan would have failed: it missed payments in testing because each payment depended on share
  values on a given date. The team rebuilt it in a fixed order (payments, bottom, top, what she keeps), learned the
  name of the belief it had leaned on, and learned to weigh decisions by what they change.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It trusted shares to be worth enough on ten dated payments, and in simulation it
  missed at least one in 3.2% of markets, 40.8% if the 2028 deposit never came.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the letter's mix of eagerness and slightly showy knowledge. One run-on sentence lists five
  adjectives and then "so we'll simply ignore price noise", and "The catch is" is followed by a concession and a "but in
  return". It writes "T-Bonds and iBonds" the way the IPS does, and it sounds like the same students explaining their
  choices.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS listed no Treasury maturing in a given year, the team bought iShares iBond funds instead. They
  behave like bonds held to maturity, so each payment rests on a dedicated instrument, at the cost that the fund pays
  out its net asset value rather than par, in exchange for liquidity.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes, on the passage's logic. Each payment rests on a dedicated T-Bond or
  iBond held to its end, not on a forecast, though the iBonds pay their net asset value rather than a promised par
  amount.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds most like the letter's writers. It is confident to the point of over-claiming ("a near-ironclad
  guarantee, and we provide exactly that", "matches the liability perfectly"), and it uses the letter's slightly grand
  personification ("anything the restless markets might decide to do") and odd but human punctuation ("Notably –").
  Its tone is reassuring, like the IPS's "take that one step further".
- **Meaning:** Treasuries do not create wealth but remove uncertainty. Held untouched and matched to the payments, they
  meet Laura's "high degree of certainty", and because her payments are fixed in dollars, ordinary Treasuries match
  them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The payments are fixed in nominal dollars and each is matched by a
  Treasury held untouched to its date, so falling rates do not change what they pay.
- **Image:** None.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest (13, 7, 12) are a little rough and very eager, like the IPS. Their sentences run on and
pile up reasons, the vocabulary is slightly showy ("vain illusion", "near-ironclad", "restless markets"), and the claims
are confident and reassuring. Not one of them sets up an effect. The middle group (10, 11, 6, 1) is plain report prose
that carries the numbers and owns mistakes simply; the same people could write it, just more carefully. The lowest
(5, 8, 9, 4) are built around an extended image (ink and pencil, knockout and overprinting) that has to be explained
before it pays off. They lean on placed pivots and slogans ("More pencil, less ink", "wasn't X. It was Y.") and on
engineered hooks, endings and citations, so they read like a practised essayist arranging the team's facts rather than
the team explaining its plan.
