# Blind reading, round 4, reader 1

Read the IPS twice (second time slowly), then the 13 passages. What I heard in the IPS: a letter that talks to Laura
the way bright, slightly excitable students talk. It has short bursts of enthusiasm ("So we hedge it!", "But worry
not"), dashes used like spoken asides, the odd grand phrase ("the ever-reliable Treasuries", "definitely not a
contributor"), and plain "we'll simply" reassurance. It argues by stating a position and then explaining it, not by
building images. Its one big analogy (her career is a stock she can't sell) is a single idea, said and used, not drawn out.

### Passage 1
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It sounds like the IPS writers talking about their own trade. It opens on a confident one-liner, explains it after a dash, and reaches for a slightly grand phrase ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners") next to a casual "as per JPMorgan", which is the same mix of flourish and student plainness as "the ever-reliable Treasuries". It ends on a question it then answers, the way the IPS does.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to go wrong, because the Treasuries already cover the payments; it is a cheap, diversified fund whose job is to decide how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (Monitoring):** No. VT is the only holding not standing between Laura and a payment, so a 30% fall only changes how generous the facility contribution can be, not the plan.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 2
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The first paragraph could be the team, but the passage then turns into a carefully built printing lesson (knockout, overprint, registration) that it maps point by point onto the plan. That is a crafted writer's device, and it closes on a polished turn ("print the payments over everything") rather than the IPS's plain explanation. It sounds like a skilled essayist borrowing the team's facts.
- **Meaning:** The all-shares first plan failed in simulation because every one of ten dated payments needed shares to be big enough on the right date; buying the payments in Treasuries first fixed it, and the 50/60/70% argument turned out to matter far less than that order.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It needed shares to be the right size on ten fixed dates in a row, and in simulation it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets (13.7% with a $75,000 second deposit, 40.8% with none), while a Treasuries-first plan never missed.
- **Image:** It helped a little with the "one slip on any date shows" idea, but I had to learn a printing term first, and calling printing "Laura's trade" felt like a stretch for a writer, so on balance it got in the way more than it helped.

### Passage 3
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clear and correct but sounds like a textbook or a careful analyst: short declarative sentences, no asides, no enthusiasm, no speaking to anyone. Nothing in it is unlike the team's ideas, but nothing in it sounds like the people who wrote "worry not".
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan uses the buy-one-bond-per-payment-and-hold method, so the first $300,000 buys Treasuries maturing before each January payment and no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes, on this passage's logic: each payment has its own Treasury held to maturity, which pays what it promised whatever rates or markets do. (The passage does not say what happens to interest that has to be reinvested.)
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 4
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This has the IPS's exact temperament. It concedes something ("won't create wealth") and then answers it, adds a slightly theatrical touch ("anything the restless markets might decide to do"), makes a confident promise ("we provide exactly that") and puts in a "Notably –" aside. It is a little overstated in places ("a nonexistent inflation risk"), which is also very like the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury doesn't grow wealth but removes uncertainty by matching a fixed nominal payment, which is why nominal Treasuries, not TIPS, are the right match for Laura's "high degree of certainty".
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes, the passage says, because each fixed nominal payment is matched by a nominal Treasury held untouched to its date, so lower rates don't change what it pays.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 5
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is tight, cool and very controlled. Clipped sentences, colons and a neat inversion ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does") make it read like an experienced editor's summary. The line about "a number said aloud" is good, but it is quieter and more polished than the IPS's chatty, exclamation-prone voice.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 the range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half the shares (moved into Treasuries first), and before naming it the team prices any shortfall from reinvesting coupons at low rates and covers it from Laura's kept shares, lowering the range if that is not enough.
- **Check question (Operating reserve):** Yes. The passage admits the ladder relies on reinvesting interest, but before the 2031 range is named that shortfall is priced at the day's rates, plus a margin, and covered by moving her kept shares into Treasuries (and the range comes down if needed), so her spare money absorbs it, not the payments.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 6
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It reads like the IPS writers' own trade explanation: a practical "so we did X instead", a breathless run of adjectives, a "we'll simply ignore" that echoes the IPS's "we'll simply complete it", and an honest "The catch is" with a jargon payoff (NAV vs par, odd-lot). The tense slips ("purchased", then "we'll") feel like real student writing, not imitation.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS lists no Treasury for a year, iBond ETFs stand in; they behave like bonds held to maturity, so each payment still has its own instrument, at the cost of paying out net asset value rather than a promised par but with more liquidity.
- **Check question:** None fits closely.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 7
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is an honest, slightly earnest student reflection ("We genuinely believed", "more humbling", "doesn't dress it up") that uses the IPS's numerals ("10 years", "10 payments") and its "In other words" habit. It is more measured than the IPS and repeats "before a single trade", but it sounds like the same people looking back.
- **Meaning:** The first, three-quarters-shares plan failed its own simulation test before any trade, because of payment timing rather than crashes; the team rebuilt it payments-first, learned the belief was the time-diversification fallacy, and learned that its 50/60/70% debate mattered little.
- **Check question (The first plan):** Because each of the 10 payments needed share values to be big enough on its exact date, and simulations showed it missing at least one payment in 3.2% of markets (far more if the 2028 deposit fell short), while a Treasuries-first plan never missed.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 8
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It argues the way the IPS does (raises the obvious objection, grants its appeal, then "The catch is everything else"), with "In other words" and "To be fair" in a conversational register. It is heavier with numbers than the IPS and a little more analyst-like, but the closing line about a number she'll never have to walk back sounds like the team's own conviction.
- **Meaning:** Simulations show that putting more of the leftover 2028 money into shares adds a little to what Laura keeps on average but shrinks the range she can state and raises the chance of missing it, so only Treasuries add to what she can promise.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Three-quarters in shares adds about $7,000 at the median and more flexibility, but the statable range drops to $30,000-$85,000 and misses $95,000 in about 62 of 100 futures; shares add to what she keeps, only Treasuries add to what she can promise.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 9
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The argument is carried by an image brought in from outside, with a citation to an illustrator, and "ink" quickly becomes a verb the passage leans on in every paragraph. "Pencil" turns up without ever being explained, and some sentences are knotted ("The usual instinct, saving for a building 5 years away, is shares"). It feels like a device laid over the team's numbers rather than the team thinking out loud.
- **Meaning:** Because a promise can be raised but not cut, Laura should only state what she owns in Treasuries; simulations show more shares give a slightly bigger median but a smaller, less reliable range, while all-floor states more but leaves her less overall.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. More shares means less of the money is owned in Treasuries by 2031, so the range she can state shrinks (to $30,000-$85,000 with three-quarters in shares) and falls short of $95,000 in 62 of 100 futures.
- **Image:** Half and half. "Ink only goes one way" made the one-way nature of a promise clear at once, but "pencil" was never defined and the constant "inks/inked" made the numbers harder to follow, so in the end it got in the way.

### Passage 10
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It reads like a smooth compression of the first-plan story with several devices stacked on it: a stock opener ("We'd love to say..."), the printing knockout, the "inked" image and Samuelson, all in one short space. Each piece is competent, but the whole sounds assembled by a practised writer, not spoken by the IPS's authors.
- **Meaning:** The first plan, mostly shares, failed in simulation because ten dated payments each needed shares to be big enough on the day; the team switched to buying the payments first in Treasuries, a choice that mattered far more than its debate over share percentages.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It made every one of 10 dated payments depend on share values being right on the day, and simulations showed at least one missed payment in 3.2% of markets (13.7% and 40.8% if the second deposit shrank or vanished), while a Treasuries-first plan never missed.
- **Image:** It got in the way. The knockout picture alone might have helped, but mixing it with "inked first" and then Samuelson meant two metaphors and a theory competing in one paragraph.

### Passage 11
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is the clearest of the ink-and-pencil passages, because it defines both words, but it is built like a magazine essay. It speaks over Laura's head to "a judge", sets up a reframing ("the question isn't really... it's..."), uses a slogan ("More pencil, less ink.") and ends on a twist ("Strangely enough, they'd make it smaller"). The IPS doesn't move in rehearsed turns like these.
- **Meaning:** If ink means owned in Treasuries and pencil means waiting on a market, then more shares means less Laura can promise in 2031: a bit more on average, but a much smaller and less reliable range.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Three-quarters in shares adds about $7,000 at the median but cuts the statable range from $95,000-$115,000 to $30,000-$85,000 and leaves her under $95,000 in about 62 of 100 futures, because only money owned in Treasuries can be promised.
- **Image:** It helped more than in Passage 9, because ink and pencil are defined before they are used, but the slogan lines made it feel like the image was the point rather than the plan.

### Passage 12
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is warm, conversational and candid ("That sounds small, until you remember what's being missed", "Our timeline puts it bluntly", "showed back in 1963"), and it reaches back to Laura's own "high degree of certainty" the way the IPS does. It is smoother and less excitable than the IPS, but it sounds like real people telling what happened and what surprised them.
- **Meaning:** The obvious first plan, mostly shares, failed its own simulation test, especially if the second deposit fell short; the team found its share-mix debate barely mattered, recognised the time-diversification fallacy, and adopted the payments-first order the report rests on.
- **Check question (The first plan):** It counted on time to make shares safe for ten dated payments, but in simulation it still missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets (13.7% with a $75,000 second deposit, 40.8% with none), while buying the payments in Treasuries first never missed.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 13
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It sounds closest of the "more shares" passages to the IPS's speaking voice: it sets up the instinct generously, has a casual "VT is sitting right there", a "Nice!" that recalls "worry not!", and plain admissions ("we resisted it ourselves"). Addressing "a judge" and the polished last line are less like the letter, and the "Nice!" may be a touch forced, but the movement (instinct, test, numbers, what it means) is the team's.
- **Meaning:** More shares would add a little to what Laura keeps on average but shrink and weaken the range she can promise in 2031, so the plan's split is a deliberate middle between all-shares and all-floor, aimed at never naming a number the market later takes back.
- **Check question (More shares):** No. Three-quarters in shares adds about $7,000 at the median, but the range shrinks to $30,000-$85,000 and she lands under $95,000 in about 62 of 100 futures (about 3 under the plan); shares add to what she keeps, not what she can promise.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### What separates the top from the bottom
The passages I ranked highest sound like the same people as the IPS: they say what they did and why in a talking voice, with dashes as asides, an occasional grand or slightly overstated phrase, small slips and bursts of enthusiasm, and they let the facts do the persuading. The middle passages (7, 8, 12, 13) keep that candour and argument shape but are smoother and more numbers-led, as if the same team had been edited. The lowest either sound like nobody in particular (3, and in a cooler way 5) or are built around a borrowed image (knockout printing, ink and pencil) carried through with slogans, reframings and twist endings. The IPS never writes that way: its one analogy is stated once and put to work, not staged.
