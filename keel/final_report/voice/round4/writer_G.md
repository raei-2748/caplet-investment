## Warm-up

The submitted IPS has no paragraph that opens "We'll start with the 10 payments", so I took the paragraph that does
that job: the one opening "In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit to buy 10 Treasuries".

Notes, a few words per sentence:
1. Jan 2027, first deposit, 10 Treasuries (T-Bond or iBond, whichever suits the year), each matures just before needed.
2. Held to the end = pay what they say; prices nothing to fear.
3. Today's yields, about $300k = the whole first deposit.
4. "worry not" – not reliant on yields staying put!
5. Yields fall, ladder > $300k, simply finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 your first deposit will buy 10 Treasuries – a T-Bond where there is one for that year, an iShares
iBonds fund where there isn't – each maturing just before the payment it's for. Held to the end, they pay what they
say, so there's no price movement for us to worry about. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is to say
all of your first deposit. But don't worry – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the
ladder ends up costing more than $300,000, we'll simply finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: I turned their loose "whichever works best for each year" into a tidy either/or that explains
availability, which is the over-explaining habit, and "which is to say" is more bookish than their "in other words". I
also flattened the bit of theatre that makes the original theirs: "nothing for us to fear" became "worry about", and
"worry not" became a plain "don't worry".

=== P1 ===
The obvious objection to our plan is that Laura holds too few shares. She has years to go, a building to fund, and
shares grow – so surely more of the 2028 deposit belongs in VT? But for Laura, more shares is the risky choice: it
buys a little more on average and costs her what she most needs in 2031, a figure she can promise.

The money in question is about $111,700, left from the 2028 deposit after the top-up. Our plan puts about 55% of
the $150,000 into the Treasury fund maturing in late 2032 and the rest into VT. Half of VT moves into Treasuries before
the 2031 meetings, the range is only what she then owns in Treasuries, and the other half is her flexibility. The plan
is frozen; we simply ran it against the alternatives in 200,000 simulated futures (6 November curve).

Ours states $95,000 to $115,000 and contributes $115,000 at the median, $95,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future, and under
$95,000 in about 3 in 100. Put three-quarters of the $111,700 in shares. Everything Laura has beyond
the 10 payments rises by about $7,000 at the median, and her flexibility more than doubles, from about $29,000 to
$66,000 – that's the appeal. The catch is everything else. A bad 1-in-20 future leaves her about $14,000 worse off, the
range she can state drops to $30,000–$85,000, and the contribution misses $95,000 in about 62 futures in 100. All
shares is worse again: no floor, $0 to $70,000, and a miss in 79 in 100.

To be fair, all-floor isn't free either: it states a single $130,000 in about 95 futures in 100, but leaves her less
in total than our plan in about 63, with only about $9,000 of flexibility.

In other words, shares add to what she keeps; only Treasuries add to what she can promise. The case warns that
overpromising would damage her credibility – and we'd rather hand her a number she'll never have to walk back
than an extra $7,000 at the median.

=== P2 ===
The usual instinct, saving for a building 5 years away, is shares, and plenty of them – Laura has time, and shares
grow. Our plan does close to the opposite with the 2028 deposit, because for Laura, more shares is the risky
choice.

The reason is that ink only goes one way. As one illustrator put it, adding ink is easy, whereas taking it
away "is a lot more complicated (and messy)" (Richmond, 2006). Laura's promise in 2031 behaves exactly like that. She
can always give co-sponsors more than she named; giving less is the messy kind, and the case says overpromising
would damage her credibility. So the plan inks only what she owns. Both ends of the range are
rounded down, anything above the top goes to her flexibility, and half of VT stays in pencil on purpose. More shares
simply means more pencil – and less she can ink.

The model agrees (200,000 simulated futures, 6 November curve; benchmarks only, as the plan is frozen). About
$111,700 is split after the top-up: about 55% of the $150,000 deposit into the late-2032 Treasury fund, the rest into
VT, half of which is inked before the 2031 meetings. That states $95,000 to $115,000 and contributes $115,000 at the
median, $95,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future and under $95,000 in about 3 in 100, with about $29,000 of
flexibility.

With three-quarters in shares, her median total beyond the 10 payments gains about $7,000 and her flexibility reaches
about $66,000. In exchange, her bad 1-in-20 total loses about $14,000, the range she can ink shrinks to
$30,000–$85,000, and 62 in 100 come in under $95,000. All shares leaves nothing to ink at the bottom: $0 to
$70,000, and 79 in 100 short.

Inking everything has its own price – a single $130,000 in about 95 futures in 100, but less in total than our plan in
about 63, and only about $9,000 of flexibility. So the plan leaves enough in pencil for the shares to grow, and inks
nothing it might need to take back.

=== P3 ===
The first plan we built for Laura would have been a mistake, and our own model told us so before we'd placed a single
trade.

On Sunday 27 September that plan put about three-quarters of the money in shares and trusted 10 years of growth to
cover the 10 payments. We genuinely believed 10 years was long enough for shares to carry them. Then we tested it. In
our simulations that day, it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets – in 13.7% if the 2028 deposit came in at
$75,000, and in 40.8% if it never came at all. A plan that bought the 10 payments in Treasuries first never missed one.
Our timeline for that day doesn't dress it up: "first model of our original plan ... We changed strategy."

What we'd underestimated wasn't a crash so much as timing. With three-quarters in shares, every payment needed share
values to be the right size on the right date, 10 times over, and one bad date was enough to miss. So we dropped the
plan, before a single trade, and rebuilt it in a fixed order: the payments first, then the bottom of the range, then
the top, then whatever Laura keeps.

The belief we'd leaned on turns out to have a name – the time-diversification fallacy. Paul Samuelson (1963) showed
that someone who would refuse one favourable bet cannot rationally accept a long run of them. In other words, 10 years
of growth was never going to make 10 dated payments safe by itself, however long it felt at the start.

The second lesson was more humbling. We had argued over whether the growth money should be 50%, 60% or 70% shares, and
in our simulations that whole choice moved the median outcome by about $2,000 – while putting the payments first was
worth the whole case. It taught us to weigh a decision by what it actually changes.

=== P4 ===
We'd love to say we got Laura's plan right the first time. We didn't, and the first version is worth showing.

On Sunday 27 September our plan put about three-quarters of the money in shares and trusted 10 years of growth to cover
the 10 payments; we believed 10 years was long enough for shares to carry them. Before any trade, we tested it. In our
simulations that day, it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets, in 13.7% if the 2028 deposit came in at
$75,000, and in 40.8% if it never came. Buying the 10 payments in Treasuries first never missed one. Our timeline
reads: "first model of our original plan ... We changed strategy." And we did, before a single trade.

Printing has the best picture we've found of what went wrong. In a knockout, the colour underneath leaves a hole for
the black to fall into – and if the sheet slips even slightly, a thin white gap shows. Overprinting lays the black straight on top of the colour, so no gap can open. Our
first plan was a knockout. Its danger wasn't a crash: with three-quarters in shares, each payment needed share values
to be the right size on the right date, 10 times over, and a slip on any one date would show as a missed payment. The
fix wasn't better shares. It was to print the payments over everything – inked first, in Treasuries she owns, where no
slip can show – and only then the bottom of the range, the top, and what Laura keeps.

The belief underneath has a name, the time-diversification fallacy: Paul Samuelson (1963) showed that someone who would
refuse one favourable bet cannot rationally accept a long run of them. And the question we'd argued over – 50%, 60% or
70% of the growth money in shares – moved the median by about $2,000 in our simulations, while printing the payments
first was worth the whole case. That gap taught us to weigh a decision by what it changes.

=== NOTES ===
Next to the IPS and the Trading Notes, these read a notch more orderly than the team: they keep a dash and a "the catch
is" or "in other words" in hand, but the team's writing lets a sentence run on with a little excitement and reaches for
a bigger word than it strictly needs, and my P1 and P3 tidied that eagerness into argument. The figures carried most
of the weight in P1 and P2, which made both close to the team's way of explaining by showing the numbers, but also
made them denser than anything Caplet wrote at that length. The ink turn in P2 did real work, because the idea that ink
can be added but not cleanly taken off explains in one move why the ends are rounded down, why anything above the top
goes to her flexibility and why half the shares stay out of the promise, none of which P1 could say without stopping
to argue it. The knockout in P4 helped less: it needs four sentences of printing before
it pays off, and on a second read it slowed the reflection down more than it sharpened the "10 dates, not one crash"
point that P3 makes plainly.
