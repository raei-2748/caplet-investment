=== P1 ===
Here's an argument we expect a judge to resist, because we resisted it ourselves: for Laura, more shares would be the risky choice.

The instinct is easy to understand. She has years before the building needs money, shares beat bonds over long stretches, and VT is sitting right there. So why does most of the 2028 deposit go into a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032 and earns, at best, what Treasuries earn?

Because of what that money is for. In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a figure she has to stand behind – and the only figures she can stand behind are ones she owns in Treasuries. So we ran the alternative. Take the roughly $111,700 left after the top-up and put three-quarters of it in shares, instead of about a quarter. At the median, she ends up with about $7,000 more in total – $198,000 against $191,000. Nice! But in a bad 1-in-20 future she has about $14,000 less, the range she can state shrinks from $95,000–$115,000 to $30,000–$85,000, and her contribution lands under $95,000 in about 62 futures out of 100, against about 3 under our plan. Go all the way to shares and the floor disappears altogether.

The shares, in other words, add to what Laura keeps, not to what she can promise. That doesn't make the opposite extreme free, either – putting all of it in the floor states a bigger single figure, $130,000, but leaves her with less overall in about 63 futures out of 100, and only around $9,000 of flexibility for overruns. Our split sits between the two, and we chose it knowing what each side costs.

So the risk we're steering around isn't really a crash – Laura's payments wouldn't notice one. It's standing in front of co-sponsors with a number the market later takes back.

=== P2 ===
Any illustrator will tell you that ink only goes one way. You can always add more; taking it off is another matter – as one put it, it 'is a lot more complicated (and messy)'. We think that's the most useful thing Laura's own trade can teach her about the 2031 range, and it settles an argument a judge might reasonably start: shouldn't someone with years to go hold more shares?

In our plan, ink means a figure she owns in Treasuries, paying on its date; pencil is anything still waiting on a market. Shares are pencil until half of them move into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. So the question isn't really 'how much growth?' – it's 'how much can she ink?'

We tested it. Take the roughly $111,700 left after the top-up and put three-quarters in shares instead of about a quarter. At the median she ends up with about $7,000 more overall ($198,000 against $191,000) – but in a bad 1-in-20 future, $14,000 less. Worse, the range she can actually state drops from $95,000–$115,000 to $30,000–$85,000, and her contribution falls under $95,000 in about 62 futures out of 100, compared with about 3 under the plan. More pencil, less ink.

And because ink can't be taken back, the plan only ever adds it. Every end of the range is rounded down, anything above the top goes to her flexibility rather than to a co-sponsor, and half the shares stay in pencil on purpose – nobody is ever promised them. The other extreme isn't free: put it all in the floor and she inks a bigger single figure ($130,000), but ends up with less overall in about 63 futures out of 100 and only about $9,000 of flexibility.

So more shares wouldn't make Laura's promise bigger. Strangely enough, they'd make it smaller.

=== P3 ===
Our first plan was the obvious one. On 27 September we put about three-quarters of Laura's money into shares, reasoning that ten years was surely long enough for growth to carry ten payments of $50,000.

Then we tested it – before placing a single trade – and it failed its own test. In our simulations it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets. That sounds small, until you remember what's being missed: a payment Laura asked us to meet with a 'high degree of certainty'. And it got much worse the moment we stopped assuming her second deposit arrived in full – 13.7% if it came in at $75,000, and 40.8% if it never came at all. A plan that bought the ten payments in Treasuries first never missed one. Our timeline puts it bluntly: 'first model of our original plan ... We changed strategy.'

What surprised us most was what we'd been arguing about. We had spent real time on whether the growth money should be 50%, 60% or 70% shares – and that whole debate was worth about $2,000 at the median. Payments first was worth the entire case. Ever since, we've tried to ask what a decision actually changes before arguing about it.

Our instinct, it turns out, even has a name: the time-diversification fallacy. Paul Samuelson showed back in 1963 that if you'd refuse one favourable bet, you can't rationally accept a long run of the same bet – more years spread the risk over more dates, they don't make it disappear. We'd assumed time would do the work that only a matching Treasury can.

The order that came out of that Sunday has held since – payments first, then the bottom of Laura's range, then the top, then what she keeps – and every later choice in this report was built on top of it.

=== P4 ===
On 27 September our plan was about three-quarters shares, with ten years of growth expected to carry ten payments of $50,000. We tested it before a single trade, and it failed its own test: in our simulations it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of markets – 13.7% if the 2028 deposit came in at $75,000, and 40.8% if it never came. Buying the ten in Treasuries first never missed one. 'We changed strategy,' says our timeline, and we did, that day.

It took us a while to see why it failed, and Laura's trade explains it better than we can. In printing, a knockout leaves a hole in the colour for the black to drop into – and if the sheet slips even slightly, a thin white gap shows around the edge. Overprinting lays the black straight on top of the colour, so no gap can open at all. Our first plan was a knockout. On average the shares would have grown plenty. The danger was registration: each payment needed share values to be the right size on the right date, ten dates in a row, and a slip on any one of them would show. A crash wasn't really the problem – timing was.

So the fix wasn't better shares. It was to print the payments over everything: bought first, in Treasuries, where a slip in the market can't open a gap.

The other surprise was how little our own argument had mattered. We'd debated whether the growth money should be 50%, 60% or 70% shares, and that debate was worth about $2,000 at the median. Payments first was worth the whole case. Since then we've tried to ask what a decision changes before we argue about it – and the order that came out of that Sunday (payments, then the bottom of the range, then the top, then what she keeps) is the one every later page of this report sits on.
