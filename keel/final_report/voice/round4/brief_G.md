## Topic one (P1 plain, P2 with one turn): one bold argument a judge would first resist
The argument: **for Laura, more shares is the risky choice.** A judge's first instinct: a client with years to go and a
building to fund should hold more shares, because shares grow. The team's answer is that more shares would buy a little
more on average and cost her what she can promise. Checked facts (model figures, 200,000 simulated futures, 6 Nov 2026
curve; the plan itself is frozen and is not being changed, these are benchmarks):
- What is being split is the money left in January 2028 after the top-up, about $111,700. The plan puts about 55% of
  the $150,000 deposit into the Treasury fund that matures in late 2032 (the floor; "most of the 2028 deposit", as the
  IPS says) and the rest into VT. Half of VT moves into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings; the range is only what is
  then owned in Treasuries; the other half is her flexibility.
- The plan: stated range $95,000 to $115,000; facility contribution $115,000 at the median, $95,000 in a bad 1-in-20
  future, under $95,000 in about 3 of 100; her flexibility about $29,000 at the median; all the money left to Laura
  beyond the ten payments about $191,000 at the median, $159,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future.
- Three-quarters of that money in shares instead: stated range $30,000 to $85,000; contribution $85,000 at the median,
  $60,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future, under $95,000 in about 62 of 100 futures; her flexibility about $66,000; all money
  about $198,000 at the median, $145,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future.
- All of it in shares: no floor; range $0 to $70,000; contribution under $95,000 in about 79 of 100 futures; all money
  about $202,000 at the median, $134,000 in a bad 1-in-20 future.
- So three-quarters shares adds about $7,000 at the median to everything she ends with, takes about $14,000 off a bad
  1-in-20 outcome, and cuts what she can promise in public from $115,000 to $85,000 at the median. The shares add to
  what she keeps; only Treasuries add to what she can promise.
- The other side, so the argument is fair: putting all of it in the floor states a higher single figure ($130,000) in
  about 95 of 100 futures, yet leaves her less in total than the plan in about 63 of 100, and keeps only about $9,000
  of flexibility. The plan sits between the two.
- The case says overpromising would damage her credibility with co-sponsors. Never use "optimal".
- **The turn for P2: "ink only goes one way."** In inking you can always add ink, but you cannot take it away cleanly:
  as one illustrator put it, adding is easy, while taking ink away "is a lot more complicated (and messy)" (Lori
  Richmond, 2006). So the plan states only what it owns, rounds every end down, sends anything above the top to her
  flexibility, and keeps half the shares in pencil on purpose. More shares means more pencil and less she can ink.

## Topic two (P3 plain, P4 with one turn): our first plan failed its own test (Team Reflection)
Written as the team looking back, in "we". Checked facts (that day's model, always dated; say "in our simulations"):
- On Sunday 27 September the team's first plan put about three-quarters of the money in shares and trusted ten years
  of growth to cover the ten payments.
- It was tested before any trade. It missed at least one payment in 3.2% of simulated markets; in 13.7% if the 2028
  deposit came in at $75,000; in 40.8% if the deposit never came. A plan that bought the ten payments in Treasuries
  first never missed one.
- The share weight the team had argued over (50%, 60% or 70% of the growth money) moved the median outcome by only
  about $2,000.
- The team's own timeline says it plainly: "first model of our original plan ... We changed strategy." The plan was
  dropped before a single trade and rebuilt: payments first, then the bottom of the range, then the top, then what she
  keeps.
- What changed: before, the team believed ten years was long enough for shares to carry the payments. After, the order
  of claims was fixed, and the team learned to weigh a decision by what it changes: the weight argued over was worth
  about $2,000; payments first was worth the whole case.
- One piece of learning you may use (one only): the belief that "over ten years shares will win" has a name, the
  time-diversification fallacy. Paul Samuelson (1963) showed that someone who would refuse one favourable bet cannot
  rationally accept a long run of them.
- Leave Laura's career idea out of this passage (it belongs elsewhere). Do not repeat the IPS's sentences.
- **The turn for P4: knockout to overprint.** In printing, a knockout leaves a hole in the colour for the black to fall
  into, and if the sheet slips even slightly a thin white gap shows. Overprinting lays the black on top of the colour,
  so no gap can open. The first plan's danger was not a crash: with three-quarters in shares, each payment needed share
  values to be the right size on the right date, ten times over, and a slip on one date shows. The fix was not better
  shares; it was printing the payments over everything, bought first, where no slip can show.
