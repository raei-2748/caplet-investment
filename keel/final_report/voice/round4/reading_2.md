# Blind reading, round 4, reader 2

Read first: the IPS, twice (the second time slowly). Then the 13 passages in pack_r4_reader2.

What I heard in the IPS: a letter that is warm, quick and a little playful ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not", "Fair
enough; that's your job"). The writers are confident to the point of over-reach ("the ever-reliable Treasuries", "no
price fluctuations for us to fear"), and some phrasing is slightly bumpy and student-like ("so we decided to take that
one step further"). They like a spaced dash for a turn, "In other words" or "In effect" to restate, numerals for small
counts ("10 Treasuries", "6 years", "2 possible reasons") and real instrument names. One idea leads each paragraph. The
only big image is a concept (her career as a stock she can't sell), not a drawn-out metaphor. It ends on a neat
reversal.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It starts in a plain, honest student voice, then turns into a carefully built printing metaphor (knockout,
  overprint, registration) that the writer clearly enjoys running. The IPS writers explain with a concept and move on.
  This writer stops to teach a craft term and then maps the plan onto it, which sounds like a practised essayist more
  than the people who wrote "But worry not".
- **Meaning:** The team's first plan was three-quarters shares and failed its own test because every payment needed
  shares to be worth enough on a set date. So the payments were bought first in Treasuries, and the team learned that
  its debate over the share mix had hardly mattered.
- **Check question (the first plan):** It missed at least one payment in 3.2% of simulated markets (13.7% if the 2028
  deposit was $75,000, 40.8% if it never came), because each payment needed share values to be right on its own date,
  while buying the payments in Treasuries first never missed one.
- **Image:** It helped somewhat: "a slip on any one date shows as a gap" made the timing risk concrete. But I had to
  learn three printing terms first, and "print the payments over everything" blurred the picture again.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens with a stock confessional move ("We'd love to say we got it right... We didn't") and then piles
  three frames into four paragraphs: the printing picture, an "inked" image dropped into it, and Samuelson's fallacy.
  It ends on a tidy moral. It reads as assembled from parts, not written forward by one person with one point.
- **Meaning:** The first share-heavy plan failed its test on payment timing. The fix was to buy the payments first in
  Treasuries. The belief behind the first plan was the time-diversification fallacy, and the share-mix debate was worth
  little next to that.
- **Check question (the first plan):** Three-quarters in shares missed at least one payment in 3.2% of simulated
  markets (far more if the 2028 deposit fell short), because each payment depended on share values on one date, while
  Treasuries bought first never missed.
- **Image:** It got in the way. The printing picture is then crossed with "inked first, in Treasuries she owns", so I
  was switching between two pictures within a sentence, and then a third frame (Samuelson) arrived.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It sounds exactly like the IPS writers on a specific trade. It is confident, slightly over-eager ("a
  near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that", "anything the restless markets might decide to do"), mixes
  textbook jargon ("duration-matching", "nonexistent inflation risk") with a flourish, and uses the same "In other
  words" and quoted 'high degree of certainty' as the letter. Its bumpy rhythm and the "Notably –" are a student's, not
  an editor's.
- **Meaning:** This 2037 Treasury is held for certainty, not growth. Because Laura's payments are fixed in nominal
  dollars, a nominal Treasury held untouched matches them, so TIPS aren't needed.
- **Check question (operating reserve):** On this passage's account, yes: the payments are fixed nominal amounts matched
  by Treasuries held untouched, so a fall in rates doesn't change what they pay. The passage doesn't say what happens
  to coupon interest that has to be reinvested, though, and that is where low rates would bite.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clipped and aphoristic, with short declaratives and polished turns ("a number said aloud can't quietly
  be halved after a bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). It reads like a skilled editor's
  summary. The IPS writers are chattier and warmer and never this terse. The content also sits awkwardly with the
  letter's "Held to the end, these pay what they say", because this passage calls the ladder's reliance on reinvested
  interest its "one weakness".
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the rounded-down floor to that plus the half of her shares moved into
  Treasuries. Any shortfall from reinvesting the ladder's interest at low rates is priced and covered from her kept
  shares before she names the range, and if that isn't enough, the range comes down.
- **Check question (operating reserve):** Yes. The shortfall from reinvesting at very low rates would be priced before
  the 2031 range is named and covered by moving her kept shares into Treasuries (lowering the range if needed), so the
  payments come before her spare money and her promise.
- **Image:** No extended image.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves like the IPS's own opening ("Our first plan was the obvious one... Then we tested it"): natural and
  candid, with "surely" and dashes in the same places the letter uses them. It is smoother than the IPS, and a couple of
  turns are stock explainer moves ("That sounds small, until you remember...", "it turns out, even has a name"), so it
  sounds like the team on a good, well-edited day.
- **Meaning:** The team's first, share-heavy plan failed its own test, especially if the 2028 deposit fell short. The
  team learned that time doesn't remove risk, that its share-mix debate barely mattered, and to put the payments first.
- **Check question (the first plan):** Relying on about three-quarters shares meant at least one payment missed in 3.2%
  of simulated markets (13.7% or 40.8% if the 2028 deposit was cut or never came), while buying the payments in
  Treasuries first never missed.
- **Image:** None to speak of.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain, correct and efficient, and echoes the IPS closely ("each maturing just before", "pays exactly
  what it promised"). But it has none of the letter's warmth, asides or bumps. It could be the team in a tight
  explanatory section, or a careful adult writer, and nothing in it decides between the two.
- **Meaning:** Like a pension fund, the plan covers each fixed payment with its own Treasury (bond or fixed-end fund)
  bought in January 2027, maturing just before the payment and held to the end, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question (operating reserve):** On this passage's account, yes: each payment is a Treasury held to maturity,
  which pays what it promised whatever rates or markets do. The passage doesn't mention any interest that would have
  to be reinvested.
- **Image:** None (the pension-fund comparison is a brief reference, not an image).

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the same voice as Passage 3 and the IPS: a run of adjectives ("defined-maturity, investment-grade,
  liquid, cheap and transparent about yields"), "we'll simply ignore price noise", a frank "The catch is", and a bit of
  jargon worn proudly ("terminal payout is a net asset value", "odd-lot bond"). It is earnest, a little clunky and
  confident, which fits the letter's writers.
- **Meaning:** In years where WInS listed no Treasury maturing, the team bought iShares iBond funds, which behave like
  bonds held to term. Their payout is a net asset value rather than a promised par, and in exchange they are far more
  liquid.
- **Check question (operating reserve):** Mostly: each payment rests on its own instrument held to its end, so falling
  rates don't threaten it. But in the iBond years the payout is a net asset value, not a promised par, so the passage
  stops short of saying the amount is exact.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is crafted and lively, but the shape is a writer's: open on an illustrator anecdote, define the metaphor,
  run the numbers through it, land on "More pencil, less ink", then end on a reversal line ("Strangely enough, they'd
  make it smaller"). The IPS does end on a neat turn, but it never carries one image across a whole argument like this.
- **Meaning:** Only figures owned in Treasuries ("ink") can be promised. More shares ("pencil") would add a little at
  the median but shrink the range Laura can state and raise the chance of a low contribution, so more shares would make
  her promise smaller.
- **Check question (more shares):** No. Three-quarters in shares would cut the range she can state from
  $95,000-$115,000 to $30,000-$85,000, and her contribution would fall under $95,000 in about 62 futures in 100 rather
  than about 3, because shares aren't owned in Treasuries when she names the figure.
- **Image:** It mostly helped. "Ink is owned in Treasuries, pencil is still waiting on a market" is easy to hold, and
  "ink only goes one way" explains the rounding down. The illustrator detour and the "More pencil, less ink" slogan felt
  staged.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain, honest narration with the IPS's habit of numerals ("10 years", "10 payments"), a little
  repetition ("before a single trade" twice) and a few easy phrases ("doesn't dress it up", "more humbling"). It sounds
  like thoughtful students reflecting rather than performing, though it is more even-tempered than the letter.
- **Meaning:** The first plan trusted 10 years of shares to carry 10 dated payments and failed the team's own
  simulation. The team rebuilt it payments-first and learned about the time-diversification fallacy and about weighing
  decisions by their effect.
- **Check question (the first plan):** With three-quarters in shares, every payment needed share values to be right on
  its date, so it missed at least one payment in 3.2% of simulated markets (more if the 2028 deposit fell short), while
  Treasuries bought first never missed.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the IPS's conversational energy: it addresses the reader's instinct, "VT is sitting right there", a
  "Nice!" that matches the letter's "So we hedge it!", and an ending that turns the risk on its head the way the
  letter's last line does. The numbers are clear and the argument moves forward without a scaffold of imagery. It is a
  bit more polished than the IPS, but the person sounds the same.
- **Meaning:** More shares would be the riskier choice for Laura, because only figures she owns in Treasuries can be
  promised in 2031. Shares add to what she keeps, not to what she can promise, and the plan's split is chosen knowing
  what each extreme costs.
- **Check question (more shares):** No. Putting three-quarters of the $111,700 in shares adds about $7,000 at the
  median, but the range she can state shrinks from $95,000-$115,000 to $30,000-$85,000 and her contribution lands under
  $95,000 in about 62 futures in 100, because shares can't be promised until they are in Treasuries.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It argues like a team defending its own choice: a clear objection, the numbers laid out, then "that's the
  appeal. The catch is everything else", "To be fair" and the IPS's "In other words". The volume of figures makes it
  read more like an analyst's paragraph in places, but the turns and the closing preference ("a number she'll never
  have to walk back") sound human and like this team.
- **Meaning:** Simulations show that more shares raise the median and her flexibility slightly but sharply cut what
  Laura can promise co-sponsors, while all-floor costs her total wealth and flexibility. Shares add to what she keeps;
  only Treasuries add to what she can promise.
- **Check question (more shares):** No. Three-quarters in shares would drop the stated range to $30,000-$85,000 and
  miss $95,000 in about 62 in 100 futures (all shares, 79 in 100), because only what she owns in Treasuries can be
  promised.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The opening sentence is awkward, and from then on the ink-and-pencil vocabulary is pressed into every
  technical clause ("the range she can ink", "nothing to ink at the bottom", "inks nothing it might need to take
  back"). That feels like a device being applied on purpose, not a voice. The citation and the heavy run of figures
  make it read like a careful but mechanical rewrite.
- **Meaning:** Because a stated promise can be raised but not cut, the plan commits only what Laura owns in Treasuries.
  The simulations show that more shares would add a little to her total but shrink and weaken the range she can state.
- **Check question (more shares):** No. More shares mean less she owns in Treasuries when she names the range: it
  would shrink to $30,000-$85,000 with 62 in 100 futures under $95,000, and all shares would leave nothing at the bottom
  ($0 to $70,000).
- **Image:** It got in the way. Once the metaphor replaces plain words inside the statistics, I had to translate "ink"
  back into "owned in Treasuries" at every figure.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's voice almost exactly. It opens with a confident, slightly cheeky line ("VT is where we
  allow ourselves to be wrong"), has a long, eager sentence carrying "the vain illusion that we can pick winners", "as
  per JPMorgan" and an expense ratio, and closes on a question about Laura's generosity. It is a little breathless and
  very sure of itself, like the people who wrote "the ever-reliable Treasuries".
- **Meaning:** VT, a cheap, diversified world share fund, is the one holding allowed to fall, because the Treasuries
  already cover the payments. Its job is to decide how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (monitoring):** No. A 30% fall hits only VT, which stands behind no payment: it lowers how generous
  the facility contribution can be, while the Treasuries already cover the payments, so nothing in the plan needs to
  change.
- **Image:** None.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like eager, confident students. They are a little over-reaching in their words
("restless markets", "vain illusion", "we provide exactly that"), slightly bumpy, and attached to real instruments and
one idea at a time, with the same "In other words" restatements and playful asides as the letter. The middle passages
(5, 9, 10, 11, 6) are clear and human but smoother than the IPS, as if the same people had been edited. The lowest
passages are engineered: an extended metaphor carried through every sentence or crossed with a second one, a citation
and an anecdote stacked on the argument, or clipped aphorisms in a tidy summary voice. None of them has the uneven,
sincere energy of a letter that says "But worry not". The more a passage felt built around a device, the less it
sounded like the people who wrote the IPS.
