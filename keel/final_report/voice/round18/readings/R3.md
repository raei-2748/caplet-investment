## From memory

1. **How much, and when?** $115,000 (US dollars) for the building, paid in January 2033. She signs a letter for it in 2031.

2. **Could it be lower?** In US dollars, as she puts it, no: her letter fixes the sum at $115,000, and if the fund holding the Treasuries pays out less she makes up the difference herself from money she kept back. What I remember is a range of $95,000 to $115,000, with $95,000 as the part that never depended on shares, so if her own kept-back money were not there, I would expect it could fall towards $95,000. In my own currency it could be lower: the Taiwan-dollar amount depends on the exchange rate in January 2033, so if the US dollar weakens the NT$ we receive is smaller.

3. **The $95,000 in her words:** the part held for her in US Treasuries since January 2028, the part that never depended on her shares (the bottom of her range).

4. **How sure, and why believe her?** Very sure. She says neither figure depends on shares since January 2031, so no market can change them, and Treasuries are guaranteed by the US government. A funder should believe her because the money already exists before she signs: she shows her broker's statement of the account holding the Treasuries (what they pay by December 2032 beside today's value), gives a signed, dated pledge letter, and commits without waiting for anyone to match her. That is the contrast with Lehman's pledge to Spelman, which was paid from money the bank did not yet have, and the college got only $3 million of $10 million.

5. **Figure I would write down: $115,000 (US dollars), committed, payable January 2033.** For our own NT$ budget I would plan it at a cautious exchange rate, since the NT$ value floats. The one thing I would still ask before committing ministry money: proof of the money she says she kept back to cover any shortfall in what the fund pays (how much, and where it is held), because the broker's statement shows only the Treasuries, and the shortfall promise is what turns the range into a fixed $115,000.
