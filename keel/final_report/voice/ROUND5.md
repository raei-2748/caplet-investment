# Voice round 5 (Mon 16 Nov 2026, evening): the portfolio as built, and the assumptions, plain and with one turn

Run in the Final Report evening research run (brought forward from Tue 17 Nov). Same design as rounds 3 and 4
(`ROUND3.md`, `ROUND4.md`), with the change those rounds asked for: the readers were given the definition of pencil and
ink in their brief, as the report gives it once at 2.0, so no passage had to define it again. No rules came out of it.

## What was done

Two fresh writers wrote the same two topics, each twice (common brief `round5/writer_common_r56.md`):
- **Writer H** read writer E's diet (the IPS twice, CORPUS section 5 twice, one team member's essays, read and not
  copied; a script found no run of six or more words shared with the essays).
- **Writer J** read the IPS twice and CORPUS sections 3 and 5 twice, and no essays: a test of whether the essays matter.

Topic one was Section 2 beside Table 1 (P1 plain; P2 with one turn, "the table as a page today: every Treasury row ink,
VT the only pencil line"). Topic two was the assumptions and limits in the case's five areas (P3 plain; P4 with one turn,
"erase every pencil line and the page still reads"). The warm-up now quotes the IPS's real paragraph (line 13).

Three fresh readers each read 13 passages in a different order: the eight practice passages, Caplet's three Trading
Note reflections and two paragraphs of Keel's last IPS draft. Check questions: "Why does the $300,000 competition
portfolio hold a Treasury fund for the facility and a world share fund, when the IPS spends the whole 2027 deposit on
the ten payments?" and "If world shares returned 4% a year instead of 7.5%, would the top Laura states in 2031 change
much?" Files: `round5/` (key in `passages.json`; packs built outside the repo by `build_packs_r56.py`; scored with
`score_r56.py 5`).

## What they found

| Writer and condition | Heard as possibly Caplet's | Average place (1 = most like Caplet, of 13) |
|---|---|---|
| Caplet's own reflections | 9 of 9 ("Surely" 9 times) | 2.0 |
| Writer J, plain | 6 of 6 | 5.2 |
| Writer H, plain | 6 of 6 | 5.8 |
| Writer J, one turn | 6 of 6 | 8.5 |
| Writer H, one turn | 2 of 6 | 10.5 |
| Keel's last IPS draft | 0 of 6 | 12.5 |

Plain 12 of 12; one turn 8 of 12, against 2 of 24 in rounds 3 and 4. Every check answer on a practice passage was right
in both conditions (all 24). The image helped understanding in 10 of the 12 one-turn readings and got "a little in the
way" in two, both on the same passage (H's P2, below).

## How the readers heard the difference (their words, condensed)

- The top practice passages "answer an objection before anyone raises it, and answer it cheerfully" ("The catch is",
  "Yes, 89.55% of the book is in one issuer, and that's on purpose"), "own the awkward parts", and keep "the letter's
  bounce: an exclamation mark, 'simply', 'after all'".
- The lower ones were "polished and assured, with a writerly frame", "smoothed by a more practised hand", or "careful
  and correct, but tight-lipped and often passive ('is assumed to land', 'Rates are taken')", with "clauses squeezed
  together" in a last paragraph that "crams the margins and the limits".
- On J's turn in P4: "'Erase every pencil line – the shares gone to zero – and the page still reads' made the stress
  test concrete at once, and the gloss between the dashes" did the explaining; "the one image is used as a stress test,
  not as decoration".
- On J's turn in P2: "'it turns out to be sorted already' makes the image do real work, splitting the table into what is
  owned and what is not"; one reader: it "adds little beyond Treasuries against shares".
- On H's turn in P2: "'That's how 89.55% of the book ends up with one issuer' made the image sound like the cause of the
  concentration"; another: the passage "treats the image as the reason for the one-issuer" choice.

## What the round says (honest sentences, not rules)

With the definition given once beforehand, a single turn can be heard as Caplet's: writer J's two turns were accepted
by all three readers. The turns that worked glossed themselves in the same sentence (the dash after "erase every pencil
line"), were the only place the point was made, and sat in passages that stayed active and talking. The turns that
failed did one of two things: H's P4 made a point the plain version had already made in plain words (H's own note: the
turn "mostly restated the zero-shares test"), and H's P2 let the image stand as the reason for a decision, when the
reason is the matching rule (money due on a date is matched by the Treasury of that date). One-turn passages still rank
below plain ones on average (9.5 against 5.5), so the picture costs some voice even when it is accepted. Writer J, who
read no essays, did at least as well as writer H on every measure, so the essays are not needed in a writer's diet.

## Small things to carry

- A turn says what the image shows and then the plain reason, in that order; it never stands in for the reason.
- Put the turn where the point is made for the first time in the passage, not after a plain sentence that made it.
- Keep the "we" active around a turn; the passive sentences around H's turns were marked down before the image was.
- Every passage ran 20-60 words over its 340-word aim, and the readers' complaint about crammed last paragraphs was
  about the assumptions topic, which carries seven checked facts: on 27 Nov 3e's paragraph needs fewer figures, which is
  also what all three of Monday's blind judges asked (`research/panel/2026-11-16_judge_*.md`).
