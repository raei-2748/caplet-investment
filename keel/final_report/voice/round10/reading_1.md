# Voice round 10, reader 1

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. I read it as the work of a team that writes the way it talks. It addresses
Laura directly, likes a spaced dash and the odd exclamation ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not"), and makes one confident
turn per paragraph ("In effect your career is a large holding of shares, and one you cannot sell"). It is happy to be a
little loose ("naturally", "simply", "Fair enough; that's your job") and explains *why we did it* more than *what the
rule is*.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It moves in short legal clauses that assume the reader already knows the plan's private vocabulary ("the room
  under it", "fund costs beyond the fees", "Section 2"). It sounds like a careful auditor writing a note to file, not a
  team talking to a client. "No fall in share prices can." follows a sentence about why the deposit is named, so it
  lands a beat late and I had to look back to see what it answers.
- **Meaning:** The ten payments can fail only through a US default or a missing or much smaller 2028 deposit, never
  through falling shares. A stated figure fails only if its Treasuries pay less or late, or if fund costs go past a
  small margin. The flexibility is a limit and not a promise.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or arrive late through a US default, or a 2028
  deposit that never comes or comes far smaller (it rests on advances, speaking fees and licences, which a downturn
  delays). A fall in share prices could not. A stated figure would fall short if the Treasuries behind it paid less or
  late, or if fund costs beyond the fees ate through the room under it, about 2% of what the late-2032 fund pays.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Plain, orderly and clear, and it explains a choice by setting it beside what others do, the way a student who
  has read around would. It has none of the IPS's warmth or swing, though. It reads like a textbook paragraph the team
  could have written on a sober day.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan buys one Treasury per payment and holds it
  to maturity, so no payment depends on shares and nothing needs forecasting or trading.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** It answers only the "could not" half: markets and share prices cannot
  touch a payment, because each Treasury held to maturity pays what it promised.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Formal and stately, with no contractions, two citations packed into one sentence and a slogan flip ("a
  size, not a promise"). It sounds like a grant officer or an academic writing about Laura, not a team writing for her.
  The ink sentence is the liveliest part, but it sits inside an otherwise stiff paragraph.
- **Meaning:** Recommend about $115,000, paid by one late-2032 Treasury fund bought in 2028 and again in 2031. It is
  meaningful because it is her own money and comes first, and responsible because nothing is stated before it is owned.
  About $21,200 of shares stays free for overruns.
- **Check question (facility contribution):** About $115,000 (about NT$3.77 million on 6 Nov 2026 rates). It is
  meaningful because it is her own money, $115,000 of $450,000, owned before she asks anyone and given first, which is
  how practice judges a founder's gift. It is responsible because nothing is stated before it is owned, so falling shares
  lower only what she can still state. It is rounded down, and any excess goes to her flexibility. She keeps the other
  half of her shares, about $21,200, for building-cost overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short.
- **Image:** It helped a little. "Ink only goes one way" made the one-way nature of a stated figure easy to hold, though
  "stay pencil on purpose" needed the definition fresh in mind.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is the most spoken of the facility passages ("We'd recommend", "whose money it is and when it shows up",
  "hers before she asks anyone, and named first"). It sounds like the IPS team explaining to someone across a table. The
  citations still weigh down the middle, but the voice comes back afterwards.
- **Meaning:** The same recommendation of about $115,000, sized on the day trading froze and paid by one late-2032
  Treasury fund. It is meaningful because it is her own money and comes first, and responsible because she promises
  nothing she does not yet own. The other half of the shares is her flexibility.
- **Check question (facility contribution):** About $115,000, sized on 6 Nov 2026. It is meaningful because of whose
  money it is and when it arrives: hers, $115,000 of $450,000, before she asks anyone. It is responsible because she
  promises only what she owns, the ten payments never rest on it, and falling shares only lower what she can still
  state. About $21,200 stays unpromised, for overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short.
- **Image:** It helped. The ink line is glossed at once in plain words ("she can always state more later, never take a
  stated figure back"), so the picture and the point arrive together.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is careful and even, but it reads like a structured risk register ("For the ten payments, failing means
  ... For a stated figure, failing means ..."). The one flourish, the pencil sentence, runs long and ornate next to the
  plain lines around it. It sounds like a polished outsider imitating the team rather than the team.
- **Meaning:** Payments fail only through a US default or a missing 2028 deposit, never falling shares. A stated figure
  fails only through default or fund costs beyond a 2% margin. Flexibility can run out without breaking a promise, and
  the share-return assumption and inflation barely matter to what is promised.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or be late through a US default or a 2028
  deposit that never comes or comes far smaller. Falling shares could not, even to zero. A stated figure would fall
  short through the same default, or fund costs beyond the fees past about 2% of what the late-2032 fund pays.
- **Image:** It helped me picture the point that shares going to zero leaves every payment intact. But "the page still
  reads" is one turn too many in a long sentence, and it slowed me down.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's own energy: a bold opening claim ("where we allow ourselves to be wrong"), a phrase with
  character ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), "just 0.06%", "as per JPMorgan" and a closing question. It

- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong, because no payment depends on it. It buys the whole world

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Of the risk passages, this is the one with a person in it ("It's the risk we'd point to first", "the IPS says

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It reads like the team explaining a real purchase: the slightly informal sequence ("Some years ... so we

- **Meaning:** Where no Treasury bond matured in the right year, the team bought iBonds funds instead. They behave like

- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** Partly: a payment resting on an iBonds fund could come in a little short,

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the IPS's conviction and its tics: a concession opening ("We know ... won't create wealth, but"), "In
  other words", a romantic line about "the restless markets", and a confident jab at TIPS. Slightly overstated in the way

- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury bond removes uncertainty rather than creating wealth. Because Laura's payments are fixed

- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** Partly: inflation could not make a payment fall short, because the

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Plain, short sentences in a clear order, with one good human line ("a number said aloud can't quietly be
  halved after a bad year"). It is tidier and more aphoristic than the IPS ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise

- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half the shares, moved into Treasuries before
  it is named. Before naming it, any shortfall from reinvesting bond interest at lower rates is priced and covered from

- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short if rates fall and stay low, so reinvested

### What separates the highest from the lowest

The passages I ranked highest sound like people defending choices they made themselves. They are a little loose and
enthusiastic, with an honest concession ("The catch is", "We know ... won't create wealth"), a phrase with some
personality, and sentences that run on the way talk does. The middle group keeps that spoken feel through contractions
and connective phrases ("the risk we'd point to first", "whose money it is and when it shows up"), even when the
content is technical. The lowest passages are compressed and formal. They use private shorthand the reader has to
decode ("the room under it", "fund costs beyond the fees"), slogan pairs ("a size, not a promise"), stacked citations
and risk-register structure, so they sound like an auditor or a grant writer describing Laura rather than the team
talking to her.
