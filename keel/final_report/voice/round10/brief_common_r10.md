## Topic one (P1 plain; P2 one turn): assumptions and limits, failure first
Wharton asks the report to "clearly identify important assumptions, uncertainties, and limitations", and the case names
five areas: investment performance, the timing of cash flows, outside funding, inflation, and flexibility. The part is
built around three things Laura's plan promises or keeps, and for each says what failing would mean, what could make it
fail, and (for two of them) one figure. Then the five areas in a sentence or two, and then, as a separate point, the
IPS's two reasons to change the plan. At most two figures in the whole passage: the two given below. Each note is its
own short paragraph; one risk per sentence.

1. **The ten payments** ($50,000 each January, 2033 to 2042, for the residency's running costs). Most of each is owned
   in Treasuries from January 2027; all ten from January 2028, when the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them.
   The standard they meet: paid even if cash waiting between a bond's end and its January earns nothing, with a small
   margin held back for fund costs beyond the funds' fee. They fail only if a payment is short of $50,000, or late, on
   its 1 January. What could do that: a US default (the IPS's own condition, named once; watched as any Treasury payment
   more than five business days late); no 2028 deposit, or one far smaller than planned (say "(Section 2)" and give no
   figure). Why that deposit is the risk to name: it comes from income paid on contract dates (advances, speaking fees,
   licences), which a downturn delays first. Give that reason its own sentence, never a clause inside the list. Fund
   costs beyond the fee bigger than the margin while cash waiting earns too little. Late rather than short: a broker
   failing while the account holds more than the broker-failure insurance covers. What cannot: any fall in share prices.
2. **A figure Laura has stated to co-sponsors** (the bottom of her range, owned from January 2028; the top, owned once
   half her shares move into Treasuries in January 2031, before the meetings). It fails if the Treasuries she owns for
   it pay less than the figure, or pay late: what would break is her word to co-sponsors. What could do that: the same
   default, and fund costs beyond the fees bigger than the room left under the figure (the margin of 0.5% held back,
   plus the rounding down to a round figure). **Figure:** those costs would have to pass about 2% of what the one
   Treasury fund behind the top pays (the fund that pays out in late 2032, bought in January 2028 and bought again with
   the moved shares in January 2031), even if cash waiting earns nothing. If that fund ended early, the trading costs of
   moving to another sit inside the margin, and at today's market rates nothing is short (say this once, with no
   probability). What cannot: a missed deposit (then no range is stated, since nothing is stated before it is owned); a
   fall in yields before January 2028, which lowers the bottom she will be able to state, never one she has stated; a
   fall in share prices, which before the move lowers the figure she can state and breaks nothing.
3. **Her flexibility** (half the shares, promised to no one): a limit, not a promise. It runs out if its uses together
   outrun it, and that breaks no promise. Its uses, in the IPS's order: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan
   dollar (both tested, not forecast), a co-sponsor falling short (not sized), whose budget can fall in the same downturn
   as shares. It shrinks if shares fall before 2033; the honest limit is a share slump before the meetings followed by a
   jump in costs. **Figure:** if shares earn the return assumed, her flexibility plus anything the fund pays above the
   top, about $28,700, covers a rise of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of the part of a building budget her $115,000
   pays for; the worst two-year spans since 1991 rose by more. That is a limit she meets, never a change of plan.
4. **The five areas, briefly:** investment performance: the return assumed for world shares (J.P. Morgan's, given with
   the range's table, not here) barely moves the top she states; what shares do before the meetings sets it. Timing:
   the 2028 deposit in full at the start of 2028 (note 1). Outside funding: none for the payments or the bottom;
   co-sponsors' money is note 3's. Inflation: the payments are fixed in dollars, so inflation decides what they buy,
   never whether they are paid; building costs are note 3's. Flexibility: note 3.
5. **The IPS's two reasons to change the plan, a separate list** (a missed deposit, a Treasury default): they are the
   exits for when the plan cannot be carried out, not the ways a promise can fail, and a fall in the market is not one
   of them. Never write "only two things can break the plan".

**P2's turn, the same for both writers: erase every pencil line.** Its one place is as the gloss of the line that shuts
the first note's door, in the same sentence or the one straight after it and nowhere else: put the shares at zero (rub
out every pencil line) and the page still reads, all ten payments made. It is true from January 2028, when the only
pencil left is the shares. Pencil stays a figure (the shares, the top before the move), never a whole plan; never call
the January 2027 purchase, the IPS or the Trading Notes pencil.

## Topic two (P3 plain; P4 one turn): the facility contribution and her flexibility
Wharton asks for "a recommended amount for the facility contribution, along with a clear rationale for how it remains
both meaningful and responsible, and how flexibility can be preserved". The case's words for what she wants: a
"meaningful" contribution that stays "responsible" and keeps "flexibility as the project develops". This part gives one
figure and argues it; the range of figures, and how sure Laura can be of them, comes in the next part, so leave the
range out. At most four figures in the passage: $115,000, $450,000, and two of your choosing from below.

1. **The recommendation, one figure:** about $115,000, on market rates of 6 November 2026, the day trading froze. It is
   paid by one Treasury fund that pays out in late 2032: bought in January 2028 with part of the 2028 deposit, and
   bought again in January 2031 with half of her shares, before the meetings with co-sponsors. Say the two buys in plain
   words. In Taiwan dollars, once, as a dated size and not a promise: about NT$3.77 million at 32.768 to the US dollar on
   6 November 2026.
2. **Why it is meaningful:** $115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in (say the dollar figure beside the $450,000; never
   "about a quarter", which another part uses for something else); owned before she asks anyone; announced first. The
   evidence, in one sentence: practice judges a founder's or board member's gift by its size against her own means and
   by its coming first, not by its share of a campaign's goal (BoardSource; Kresge Foundation), and in a laboratory a
   leader's example raised others' giving only when they could see the leader's means (Levati and others, 2007). Never
   "lead gift" (that is judged by a share of a goal the case does not give).
3. **Why it is responsible:** nothing is promised before it is owned, so the ten payments never depend on it, and a fall
   in shares before the move lowers what she can state, never what she has stated.
4. **Her flexibility:** the other half of the shares, promised to no one: about $21,200 on those market rates, about
   $28,700 with what the fund pays above $115,000. It is for the IPS's three uses, in the IPS's own order: building
   costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short. One test you may use: it covers a 10%
   stronger Taiwan dollar on the contribution in about 93 of 100 simulated futures. Why keep it unpromised, from
   co-sponsors' own practice: funders cap their grants and owners pay the overruns (Taiwan's programme for venues leaves
   anything over the approved budget to the owner), and a lead donor in fundraising guides is the one who "decides
   later" to give again when a campaign falls short (Klein, 2001), whom no one should count on in advance.
5. **The trade, one clause if it fits:** each $5,000 more promised would cost about $5,300 of what she keeps. Half was
   the IPS's choice (Caplet's), and $115,000 follows from it; never "optimal", never "the model chose half".
6. **What the design is, one clause if it fits:** a buy-and-hold split of the 2028 money: no rule moves money between
   shares and Treasuries as markets move, and the one move, before the 2031 meetings, is fixed in advance. Never "CPPI",
   "portfolio insurance" or "capital-protected"; never "a natural hedge" (a stronger Taiwan dollar has come with a dearer
   building, not a cheaper one).

**P4's turn, the same for both writers: ink only goes one way.** New ink can always be added to a page; taking it away
is far messier. Use it once, where it does its job, glossed in the same sentence: that is why each figure she states is
rounded down, why anything the fund pays above it goes to her flexibility, and why half the shares stay pencil on
purpose (she can always state more later; she can never take a stated figure back). Then carry on plainly.
