Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit to buy ten Treasuries – T-Bonds or iShares iBonds funds, whichever suits each year – and each one matures just before its payment is due. Held to the end, they pay what they say, so there are no price swings for us to fear. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is to say your whole first deposit. But worry not – the plan doesn't depend on yields staying where they are! If yields fall and the ladder costs more than $300,000, we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: I reached for tidier joins ("which is to say", "so there are") where the original lets a dash do the work, and "whichever suits" is a shade stiffer than "whichever works best". I also lost the loose, spoken bits ("in other words", "ends up being"), which are exactly what make the original sound like students talking to someone they like rather than a writer smoothing a paragraph.

=== P1 ===
The ten payments fail only if one is short, or late, on its 1 January. A US default could do that. So could a 2028 deposit that never comes, or comes far smaller (Section 2). That deposit is built from advances, speaking fees and licences paid on contract dates, and those are what a downturn delays first. No fall in share prices can.

A figure Laura has stated to co-sponsors fails if the Treasuries behind it pay less, or late – and then what breaks is her word. The same default could do it. So could fund costs beyond the fees, but only past about 2% of what the late-2032 fund behind the top pays out.

Her flexibility is a limit, not a promise, so running it dry breaks nothing. If shares earn the return assumed, it and whatever the fund pays above the top would cover a rise of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of what her contribution builds; the worst two-year spans since 1991 rose by more.

Of the case's five areas, two need a line here: the return assumed for shares barely moves the top she states, and inflation decides what the payments buy, never whether they're paid.

The IPS's two reasons to change the plan are a separate list: exits for when it can't be carried out, not ways a promise fails.

=== P3 ===
We recommend a facility contribution of about $115,000, on market rates of 6 November 2026 – roughly NT$3.77 million at that day's exchange rate, a size, not a promise. One Treasury fund paying out in late 2032 carries it: Laura buys it in January 2028 with part of her second deposit, and again in January 2031 with half her shares, before she meets a single co-sponsor.

It's meaningful because it is hers and comes first: $115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in, owned before she asks anyone. Practice judges a founder's gift by its size against her own means and its coming first, not its share of a campaign's goal (BoardSource; Kresge Foundation), and in the laboratory a leader's example lifted others' giving only when they could see the leader's means (Levati et al., 2007).

It's responsible because nothing is promised before it is owned, so a fall in shares before the move lowers what she can state, never what she has stated. The figure is rounded down, anything the fund pays above it goes to her flexibility, and half her shares stay unpromised on purpose – she can always add later if the building needs it, but never take a stated figure back.

That half, about $21,200, is for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short. Funders expect that: they cap their grants and leave overruns to the owner, as Taiwan's programme for venues does.

=== NOTES ===
Next to the IPS these read cooler: at about 240 words there was no room for the team's spoken asides ("worry not", "Importantly for you"), so the warmth sits mainly in the short lines ("No fall in share prices can.", "before she meets a single co-sponsor") and in the dashes. The pencil turn in P2 earned its place because it comes straight after the short line and says why that line is true (from January 2028 the shares are the only pencil left), rather than saying it again; "the page" may need half a beat to land. The ink turn in P4 was harder: it has to carry three consequences in one sentence, so it leans towards a list with an image on top, and plain P3 says the same thing in fewer words. Getting under the limit cost whole ideas, not squeezed sentences: in P1 and P2 the broker-failure risk, a fund ending early and the three uses of her flexibility; in P3 and P4 the donor who "decides later" (Klein, 2001) and the trade behind half (about $5,300 of what she keeps for each $5,000 more promised).
