# Voice round 10, reader 3

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. What I hear in it: six students talking to one person they like and
respect. Their sentences are loose and run on, with asides in dashes and parentheses. They are a little showy ("So we
hedge it!", "But worry not", "the ever-reliable Treasuries", "no price fluctuations for us to fear"). They set something
up and then turn it ("You'd think ... until we stopped and considered"). The content is plain, but the people behind it
are audible, and they will overreach a little for effect.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This reads like a document assembling its case: a figure, a slogan ("a size, not a promise"), then "It is
  meaningful because ... It is responsible because ..." with citations doing the work. Without contractions and with
  "half her shares stay pencil on purpose", it sounds like a careful analyst, not the students who wrote "worry not".
- **Meaning:** Laura should state about $115,000, paid by one late-2032 Treasury fund she owns before any meeting. It
  is meaningful because it is her own money and it comes first, and responsible because nothing is stated before it is
  owned. About $21,200 stays unpromised for overruns.
- **Check question (facility contribution):** The figure is about $115,000 on 6 November 2026 rates (about NT$3.77
  million). It is meaningful because it is hers, owned before she asks anyone, and large against her $450,000; practice
  (BoardSource, Kresge) and a lab study (Levati et al.) say a founder's gift is judged that way. It is responsible
  because she states only what she owns: a share fall before the move lowers what she can state, never what she has
  stated, and the figure is rounded down. The unpromised half, about $21,200, is for building costs that overrun, a
  stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short.
- **Image:** Mixed. "A stated figure never comes off the page" helped me see why the figure is rounded down, but
  "stay pencil" used as an adjective felt like a label I had to translate.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain and orderly: two ways to do a thing, then "We do the second", then the step. It has none of the
  IPS's play, but a team switching into report mode could write it, and its last sentence repeats the IPS's "pay what
  they say" idea in a calmer way.
- **Meaning:** Like a pension fund, Laura's plan buys one Treasury per payment and holds it to maturity, so no payment
  depends on shares.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits, partly):** It says only what cannot make a payment fail: market moves and
  share prices, since a Treasury held to maturity pays what it promised. It does not say what could.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's voice in a different setting: confident and a little breathless, with flourishes like
  "near-ironclad guarantee", "the restless markets might decide to do" and "Notably –", plus a slight overreach at the
  end ("a nonexistent inflation risk"). It sounds like a student explaining a trade they are proud of.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury bond doesn't build wealth, but it removes uncertainty. Because Laura's payments are
  fixed in nominal dollars, a nominal Treasury matches them and TIPS aren't needed.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits, partly):** Neither markets nor inflation can make a payment fall short,
  because the bond is held and the payments are nominal. It names nothing that could.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is built like a checklist turned into prose. It goes "For the ten payments, failing means ... For a stated
  figure, failing means ...", and "Two of the case's five areas need a word" sounds like a box being ticked. The clipped
  "No fall in share prices can." leans on the sentence before it, and the image sentence is long and tangled.
- **Meaning:** Only a US default or a missing or much smaller 2028 deposit could make a payment fail. Shares can't,
  even at zero. A stated figure fails only through default or fund costs beyond about 2%. The flexibility is a limit,
  not a promise.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or arrive late through a US default, or a
  2028 deposit that never comes or comes far smaller (her advances, fees and licences are what a downturn delays
  first). A fall in share prices could not, even to zero. A stated figure would fall short only if the Treasuries
  behind it paid less or late (default), or if fund costs beyond the fees went past about 2% of what the late-2032 fund
  pays.
- **Image:** It helped. "Rubbing out every pencil line, and the page still reads" made the point concrete, but the
  "because ... the only pencil left" tail came too late and made me reread.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The content is the same as 4, without the image and a little plainer. It still moves in the same formal
  pattern ("fails only if ... could do that ... So could ...") and uses inside phrases like "the case's five areas".
  It is clear, but it sounds like a document listing conditions, not people talking to Laura.
- **Meaning:** Only a US default or a missing 2028 deposit can break a payment, and shares cannot. A stated figure
  breaks only through default or fund costs past about 2%. Running the flexibility dry breaks no promise.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or arrive late through a US default, or a
  2028 deposit that never comes or comes far smaller. A fall in share prices could not. A stated figure would fall
  short if the Treasuries behind it paid less or late, or if fund costs beyond the fees passed about 2% of the
  late-2032 fund's payout.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It carries the same content as 4 and 5, but people are speaking: "It's the risk we'd point to first", "the
  IPS says as much", "a recession pushes those back". These echo the IPS's own account of a writer's income. It ends
  by naming the two reasons plainly, as the IPS did.
- **Meaning:** Payments fail only through a US default or a missing 2028 deposit (the bigger worry), never through
  shares. A stated figure is safe unless fund costs eat the roughly 2% room under it. The flexibility's weak spot is a
  share slump followed by a cost jump.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or arrive late through a US default, or
  (the risk they point to first) a 2028 deposit that never comes or comes far smaller, because advances, fees and
  licences are pushed back in a recession. Share prices could not, even at zero. A stated figure would fall short if
  the Treasuries for it paid less or late, or if fund costs beyond the fees ate past the room under the figure, about
  2% of the late-2032 fund's payout, even with waiting cash earning nothing.
- **Image:** It helped. The aside in dashes ("rub out every pencil line, since ... they're the only pencil left") let me
  picture the page surviving with all ten payments, and it was easier to follow than in Passage 4.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the closest to the IPS. It has the confident opener ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong"),
  a long sentence that keeps going, a slightly grand phrase ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), the
  ticker-level detail with "(as per JPMorgan)", and a rhetorical question to end. It is the same students who wrote
  "a world share fund of thousands of companies".
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong, because it carries no payment. It is a cheap, diversified way
  to buy the market's growth, and it decides how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question (facility contribution, partly):** No figure is given. VT's growth sets how generous the
  contribution can be, while the Treasuries carry the payments. It doesn't say what is kept unpromised.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It does the same job as 1, 9 and 12, but it sounds like someone talking: "We'd recommend", "What makes it
  meaningful is whose money it is and when it shows up", "she promises nothing she doesn't own yet". The citations
  sit inside a conversational sentence instead of propping it up.
- **Meaning:** About $115,000 is recommended, paid by one late-2032 Treasury fund bought before any co-sponsor hears a
  figure. It is meaningful because it is hers and comes first, and responsible because nothing is promised before it
  is owned. The other half of the shares, about $21,200, is her flexibility.
- **Check question (facility contribution):** The figure is about $115,000, sized on 6 November 2026. It is meaningful
  because it is $115,000 of her own $450,000, owned and named first, which is how practice and the lab evidence judge a
  founder's gift. It is responsible because the ten payments never rest on it and falling shares lower only what she
  can state, never what she has stated. The figure is also rounded down. The unpromised part, about $21,200, is for
  building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short.
- **Image:** It helped. The gloss in dashes ("she can always state more later, never take a stated figure back") meant
  I didn't have to decode "ink only goes one way".

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is short, plain and concrete, and has one or two lines with real feeling ("a number said aloud can't
  quietly be halved after a bad year"; "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). It sounds like the IPS's
  "whatever figure you choose is money you already own" said again more tightly. It is a little more polished and
  aphoristic than the IPS's looser sentences.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 Laura gives a range from an owned floor to a top that half her shares buy in Treasuries. If
  low rates leave the ladder short of reinvested interest, that gap is covered from her kept shares first, and the range
  comes down only if they can't cover it.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** Rates falling and staying low could leave the ladder short, because it
  counts on reinvesting interest. The gap is priced and moved from her kept shares into Treasuries before she names a
  range. A stated figure would fall short only if that gap were bigger than her spare money, in which case the range
  comes down before it is stated.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds like the same students explaining a choice they had to make: "The catch is,", "we'll simply

- **Meaning:** Where WInS had no Treasury bond for a year, the team used iBond funds, which act like bonds held to the

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest (7, 13, 3) sound like students explaining their own trades. They have long, loose
sentences with asides, a slightly showy word or two ("the vain illusion", "restless markets", "The catch is"), and
occasional overreach. These are the habits that make the IPS sound like people. The middle passages (8, 6, 10, 11, 9)
carry report content but keep a speaking voice: "we'd", "really", "before she meets a single co-sponsor". The
bottom ones (5, 1, 4) read like a checklist turned into prose. Each paragraph opens on a defined term followed by
"fails if / could / So could", and they use clipped lines that lean on the sentence before ("No fall in share prices
can."), inside references ("the case's five areas", "a size, not a promise") and the image as a label ("stay
pencil"). They are clear and correct, but I can't hear the people who wrote "But worry not" in them.
