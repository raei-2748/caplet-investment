# Writer S, voice round 10

## Warm-up

Notes on the IPS paragraph, one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027, first deposit, 10 Treasuries, bonds or iBonds whichever suits each year, each matures just before it's needed.
2. Held to the end, pay what they say, no price swings to fear.
3. About $300k at today's yields = the whole first deposit.

5. Yields fall, ladder costs more than $300k, finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:

In January 2027 your first deposit will buy 10 Treasuries – T-Bonds or iShares iBonds funds, whichever suits each
year – each one maturing just before its payment is due. Held to the end, they pay exactly what they say, so their
prices in between needn't worry us at all. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is your whole first
deposit. Don't worry – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ladder costs more than
$300,000, we'll just finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: I made the deposit the subject instead of "we'll use", so the team stopped doing things in its own
sentence, and I tidied away the connectors ("in other words", "for us to fear", "But worry not") that make the original
sound like people talking. The flat "just finish it" also lost the original's "simply complete it", which is a touch
grander and more sure of itself.

=== P1 ===
The ten payments, all owned in Treasuries from January 2028, fail only if one is short, or late, on its 1 January. A US
default could do that – the IPS's own condition. So could a 2028 deposit that never comes, or comes far smaller (Section
2). We name that deposit because it's paid from advances, speaking fees and licences on contract dates – the money a
downturn delays first. No fall in share prices can.

A figure Laura has stated fails if the Treasuries behind it pay less, or late, and then it's her word that breaks. Fund
costs beyond the fees could, but only by eating through the room under it – about 2% of what the late-2032 fund pays,
even with cash waiting earning nothing.

Her flexibility is a limit, not a promise: it can run out without breaking anything she has stated. What would really
stretch it is a share slump before the meetings, then a jump in costs. If shares earn the return assumed, her
flexibility and what the fund pays above the top cover a rise of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of her share of the
building; the worst two-year spans since 1991 rose by more.

The case's fifth area, inflation, decides only what the fixed payments buy, never whether they're paid.

The IPS's two reasons to change the plan – a missed deposit, a Treasury default – are exits for when the plan can't be
carried out, not ways a promise fails.

=== P2 ===
Laura's ten payments fail if one arrives short, or late, on 1 January. A US default could break them; the IPS says as
much. So could a 2028 deposit that never arrives, or arrives far smaller (Section 2). It's the risk we'd point to first:
advances, speaking fees and licences are paid on contract dates, and a recession pushes those back. No fall in share
prices can. Put the shares at zero – rub out every pencil line, since from January 2028 they're the only pencil left –
and the page still reads, all ten payments made.

A stated figure fails if the Treasuries she owns for it pay less, or late, and it's her word that breaks. Fund costs
beyond the fees could do it, but only past the room under the figure – about 2% of what the late-2032 fund pays, even if
cash waiting earns nothing.

Her flexibility is a limit, so running dry breaks nothing promised. Its weak spot is a share slump before the meetings
followed by a cost jump. At the assumed return, her flexibility plus what the fund pays above the top would cover a rise
of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of her share of the building, though the worst two-year spans since 1991 rose
further.

The case's fifth area, inflation, changes what the fixed payments buy, not whether they arrive.

The IPS's two reasons to change course, a missed deposit and a Treasury default, are exits for when the plan can't go
on, not ways a promise fails.

=== P3 ===
We recommend a facility contribution of about $115,000, on market rates of 6 November 2026, the day trading froze. One
Treasury fund maturing in late 2032 pays it. Laura buys it in January 2028 with part of the 2028 deposit, then buys more
of it in January 2031 with half her shares, before she meets a single co-sponsor.

It's meaningful because it's $115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in, owned before she asks anyone and announced first.
Practice judges a founder's gift exactly that way, by its size against her own means and its coming first, not its share
of a campaign's goal (BoardSource; Kresge Foundation), and in a laboratory a leader's example raised others' giving only
when they could see the leader's means (Levati et al., 2007).

It's responsible because nothing is promised before it's owned: the ten payments never lean on it, and falling shares
before the move lower what she can state, never what she has stated.

Her flexibility is the other half of the shares, about $21,200 on those rates, kept for building costs that overrun, a
stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short. She can always state more later but never take a stated figure
back, so her figure is rounded down, whatever the fund pays above it joins her flexibility, and that half stays
unpromised. Co-sponsors' own practice points the same way: funders cap their grants and owners pay the overruns, as in
Taiwan's own programme for venues.

=== P4 ===
We'd recommend about $115,000 for the building, sized on the market of 6 November 2026, the day trading froze. A single
Treasury fund maturing in late 2032 pays it: Laura buys it in January 2028 with part of her second deposit, and again in
January 2031 with half her shares, before any co-sponsor hears a figure.

What makes it meaningful is whose money it is and when it shows up – $115,000 of the $450,000 she puts in, hers before
she asks anyone, and named first. That's the test practice sets for a founder's gift, its size against her own means and
its coming first rather than its share of a campaign's goal (BoardSource; Kresge Foundation), and in the laboratory a
leader's example lifted others' giving only when they could see what the leader had (Levati et al., 2007).

It's responsible because she promises nothing she doesn't own yet: the ten payments never rest on it, and falling shares
before the move lower what she's able to state, never what she's stated.

The other half of the shares, about $21,200 on those rates, is her flexibility, for overrunning building costs, a
stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short. Ink only goes one way – she can always state more later, never
take a stated figure back – so her figure is rounded down, whatever the fund pays above it joins her flexibility, and
half the shares stay pencil on purpose. Co-sponsors work the same way: funders cap their grants and owners pay the
overruns, as in Taiwan's own programme for venues.

=== NOTES ===
Next to the IPS these are steadier and less playful than the team is: fitting three notes, the case's areas and the
IPS's two reasons into about 240 words left no room for the letter's asides, so the assumptions part reads closer to a
checklist than to quick bursts, and to stay near length I cut whole ideas (the broker failure, the Taiwan-dollar size
of the contribution, Klein's donor who "decides later"). In P2 the turn helped: as the gloss straight after "No fall in
share prices can." it says what the plain version leaves implied, that by 2028 the shares are the only thing left that
can move, though its sentence is the longest in the part. In P4 the turn carried the "state more later, never take
back" point in fewer words than P3 needed and sounded a little more like the team, sure of itself and pleased with the
idea; the sentence after it, on co-sponsors' own practice, felt closest to the IPS's voice in both versions.
