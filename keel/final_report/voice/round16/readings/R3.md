## From memory

1. "In ink" means the money is already covered: Treasuries she holds will pay that exact amount on that date, so it is not a hope. "In pencil" is anything that could still change, like a future book advance or what her shares happen to be worth. She only states figures that are in ink.

2. She tells co-sponsors a range: $95,000, held in Treasuries since January 2028, and up to $115,000, since January 2031 when she moved half her shares into Treasuries. She signs for the $115,000. Behind the $50,000 a year for running costs (2033 to 2042, about $615,000 with the building money) stand Treasuries held since January 2028. The guarantee behind each figure is the US government, not share prices.

3. Yes. Owning is having the money (the Treasuries); signing is promising in writing to give it. Owning shows the money exists, but not that she'll give it. A funder's gift office can only record a written commitment with an amount, a purpose and a date, so it needs the signature as well as the holdings. The Lehman example shows what happens with a signature but no money held.

4. Yes: "ink". Earlier it meant money that Treasuries held for her will pay. In the pledge part it also meant her signature, "the other kind of ink". It helped a little because the passage says so outright and ties it to "a funder can count", but it blurred the neat rule that only Treasury-backed figures are ink. I also noticed "owned" and "held for her" being used almost interchangeably.
