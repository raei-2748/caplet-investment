## From memory

1. "In ink" means the money is already covered. Treasuries held for her will pay that exact figure on that date, so it cannot disappear. "In pencil" means nothing yet backs it, such as a future book advance or the value of her shares. She only states figures that are in ink.

2. She gives a range for the building. The bottom is $95,000, held in Treasuries since January 2028. The top is $115,000, backed by Treasuries since January 2031, when she moved half her shares into them. She signs for the top. She also commits $50,000 a year for running costs in 2033 to 2042, held in Treasuries since 2028, about $615,000 in total. The statement is a written commitment made in 2031, with payment in January 2033.

3. Yes. Owning the money shows it exists. Signing is a promise to hand it over. A funder's gift office can count a signed pledge, but it cannot count money that merely sits in someone's account. The Lehman and Spelman example shows what happens when a pledge is not backed by money held at the time.

4. Yes, "ink". At first it meant money that Treasuries held for her will pay. Then "a signature is the other kind of ink" made it mean a written promise as well. I took it as "a firm, countable commitment". It helped a little, because the passage said outright that it was a second meaning. It also got in the way, because the first definition no longer held without that extra clause.
