## From memory

1. "In ink" means the money for a figure is already sitting in Treasuries held for her that will pay it on its due date. "In pencil" is a figure nothing she holds will yet cover, like a future book advance or her shares' value. She only states figures that are in ink.

2. She gives a range for the building: $95,000 as the low end, held in Treasuries since January 2028, and up to $115,000 as the top, which also rests on Treasuries bought by January 2031 after she moved half her shares into them. She says she signs for the $115,000. Alongside that she says the $50,000 a year running costs for 2033-2042 are also held in Treasuries, about $615,000 in total.

3. Yes. Owning the money shows she can give; signing is a promise to give, in writing. A funder's gift office can count a signed pledge but not a bank balance, and a pledge without money behind it can fail (the Lehman and Spelman example), so a funder wants both.

4. Yes: "ink". Early on it meant money that Treasuries held for her will pay. In the last passage a signature is called "the other kind of ink", meaning a written promise to give. I took it as "something that can no longer be rubbed out". It was explained in the text, so it helped a little, but it blurred the first meaning: ink was about her ability to pay and then became about her commitment to pay.
