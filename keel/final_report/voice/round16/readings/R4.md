## From memory

1. A figure is "in ink" when Treasuries that someone holds for Laura will pay exactly that amount on the date it is due. If nothing like that backs it yet (a future book advance, or what her shares happen to be worth), it is only pencil. She only says out loud the figures that are in ink.

2. She gives co-sponsors a range for the building: $95,000 as the bottom, held in Treasuries since January 2028, and up to $115,000 as the top, held in Treasuries since January 2031 after she moved half her shares. She signs for the top. She also commits $50,000 a year for running costs from 2033 to 2042, held in Treasuries since 2028, about $615,000 in total.

3. Yes. Owning shows the money exists; signing is the promise to give it. A funder's gift office can book only a written pledge, and the Lehman and Spelman example shows what a pledge looks like when the money is not already in hand. So the funder cares about both: the money, so the promise can be kept, and the signature, so they can count on it.

4. No word changed its meaning in a way I noticed. "Pledge" is used for Lehman's and then for Laura's, but the passage says outright that they run in opposite directions, and I think that helped. "Ink" does not appear in the last passage; it is replaced by "held" and "signed", and I read those as the same idea, not a shift.
