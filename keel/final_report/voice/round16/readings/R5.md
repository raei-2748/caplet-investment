## From memory

1. "In ink" means the dollar figure is already backed by Treasuries that she holds and that will pay that amount on that date. "In pencil" means there is no such Treasury behind it yet, like a hoped-for book advance or what her shares might be worth. She only says a figure out loud once it is in ink.

2. She tells co-sponsors a range for the building: $95,000, held in US Treasuries since January 2028, up to $115,000, held since January 2031 after she moved half her shares into Treasuries. She signs for the top, $115,000, and pays it in January 2033. On top of that, $50,000 a year for the running costs from 2033 to 2042, held in Treasuries since 2028, about $615,000 in total. Behind each figure are Treasuries that the US government guarantees, not share prices.

3. Yes. Owning is having the money held in Treasuries, which shows it exists. Signing is the written promise (amount, purpose, date) that she will give it. A funder's gift office counts the signed pledge, not money in an account, so owning without signing counts for nothing with the funder. Lehman had pledged a lot and then failed to pay.

4. I would say "commit". In the Letter it was about relying on a sum she commits to, an internal choice backed by Treasuries. In Passage 3 "I commit to it in writing" means a signed pledge to others. I took it as the signed promise there. It helped little, since P3.1 had already separated holding from signing, but it blurred the earlier use where commit seemed to mean only that she intends to pay. A smaller one: "held for her" (Passage 2) became "mine" in P3.7, which I read as the same thing.
