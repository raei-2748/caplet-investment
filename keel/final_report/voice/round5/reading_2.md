# Blind reading, rounds 5-6, reader 2 (13 passages)

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. I hear its writers as a group that is confident, a bit showy and eager to
reassure. They raise a worry so they can wave it away ("You'd think...", "But worry not –", "So we hedge it!"). They take
Laura's words and push them one step further. They hinge sentences on dashes, and some sentences run on a little. They
like a strong word ("ever-reliable", "immune to markets") and end on a punchy turn ("ours is to take none").

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The voice explains its own book with casual verbs ("shrunk", "pulls double duty") and a little defensiveness ("actually bought, not just described"), then settles into the numbers. It is tidier than the IPS, but it sounds like the same practical, slightly breezy group.
- **Meaning:** The competition book is Laura's whole two-deposit plan scaled to $300,000, so it holds the floor fund and VT; it was built before the reserve was sized; IBTM is both the 2033 payment and the stand-in floor fund; WInS's listings forced most of the fund/bond split; and trading hit targets set in advance.
- **Check question (portfolio as built):** The book is not the 2027 deposit alone. It is the whole plan, both deposits, shrunk to $300,000, and the team wanted every instrument the strategy relies on actually bought. So the 2028 money's floor fund (IBTM standing in) and VT appear, split about 73/27 as in the plan.
- **Image:** It helped. Sorting the rows into ink and pencil gave me the reason the 89.55% in one issuer is fine before the passage stated it, though calling a whole holding "pencil" stretches the definition (which is about figures) a little.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The pivots ("That's why ... at all", "The catch is", "Yes, ... and that's on purpose", "And the buying...") have the IPS's habit of answering an objection before anyone raises it, and answering it cheerfully. The numbers are tighter than in the IPS, but the person talking sounds the same.
- **Meaning:** Same as Passage 1 without the image: the book is the scaled whole plan; IBTM does two jobs; WInS forced the split and only 2037 was a real choice; the one-issuer weight is deliberate; and trading met its preset targets.
- **Check question (portfolio as built):** The $300,000 book is the whole plan for both deposits, scaled down, so the floor fund and VT from the 2028 deposit are in it. The team wanted every instrument it relies on bought and tested, and since the book predates the reserve, VT is a bigger share (8.84%) than in the plan.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It opens by admitting the book "doesn't look like the IPS", then asks and answers its own question, the way the IPS opens on "You'd think..." before it turns. The one long, slightly overloaded "Because..." sentence and the digit in "10 payments" feel like the same hands. "The one line not yet settled" stands in plainly for the image.
- **Meaning:** Same material as Passages 1 and 2, with IBTM's dollar figure and which holding pays which years. It is the only version that calls the bond test a "price band" where the others say yield within a quarter point of the curve.
- **Check question (portfolio as built):** This passage answers most directly: the book is Laura's whole plan scaled to $300,000 of its $450,000, so the floor fund and VT bought with the 2028 money had to be in it, "bought, not assumed".

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It is loose, eager and a bit showy: a pile of adjectives, "so we'll simply ignore price noise", then "The catch is, ..." followed by a trade-off "but in return". That is the IPS's rhythm and dash habit, with a student's pleasure in terms like "terminal payout" and "odd-lot".
- **Meaning:** Where WInS listed no Treasury bond for a year, the team bought iBonds Treasury funds, which behave like bonds held to their end; they pay net asset value rather than par, but they are more liquid than small bond lots.
- **Check question (portfolio as built):** This question fits only partly. The passage explains why some payment years sit in funds rather than bonds, but not why the book holds a facility fund and VT. From this passage alone I could not answer.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the same enthusiastic, slightly over-reaching voice as the letter. "A near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that" pushes Laura's words one step further, as the IPS does with "high degree of certainty". "Restless markets" and the dash after "Notably" add to it. The loose terms ("duration-matching" for simply holding to maturity) sound like a student, not an editor.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury will not grow wealth but removes uncertainty; every untouched year is a good year; and because the payments are fixed in nominal dollars, nominal Treasuries match them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question:** Neither fits.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is the same material as Passages 1-3, smoothed by a more practised hand: "in miniature", "leans on", "WInS made most of that call for us", "The execution held to targets". It is fluent and even, and it loses the slight bumpiness and eagerness the IPS has. Still plausible as theirs after heavy editing.
- **Meaning:** The book is the whole plan scaled to $300,000, which is why it holds the floor fund and VT; it was built before the reserve was sized; IBTM is the largest holding for two jobs; WInS forced the split; and trading held to preset targets.
- **Check question (portfolio as built):** The book is the plan for both deposits in miniature, so it holds what the 2027 deposit alone never would. The floor fund and VT were bought at real prices rather than assumed, split about 73/27 as in the plan.
- **Image:** It mostly helped, as in Passage 1. But "That's how 89.55% of the book ends up with one issuer" made the image sound like the cause of the concentration, which muddled it for a moment.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is a calm, textbook explanation that opens on pension-fund practice, in short even sentences with no turn and no enthusiasm. It sounds like a good explainer for a general reader, not the team that wrote "So we hedge it!". A sentence of it could be theirs; the whole does not sound like them.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds meet fixed payments, Laura's plan uses one bond per payment held to maturity, so the 2027 money buys a ladder maturing before each January payment and no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question:** Neither fits. The passage describes the plan, not the competition book.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It opens with a frank admission ("looks the most exposed") and then says "Yet in our model it hardly matters", the IPS's move of raising a worry to dismiss it. It is long and dense with figures, but the "we" stays active and opinionated throughout. The fronted "Two safety margins we chose on purpose" is a little stylised.
- **Meaning:** The 7.5% share assumption hardly moves the top (4% to 10% gives about $116,000-$119,500); what shares actually do before 2031 matters ($95,000-$140,000); with shares at zero the payments and the roughly $103,000 floor stand. The passage also covers deposit timing, no outside funding, the Taiwan cost and currency risk stated as a limit, two deliberate margins and the remaining limits.
- **Check question (assumptions):** No, not much: from 4% to 10% the top before rounding moves only from about $116,000 to $119,500, less than one $5,000 step. The top is set by what shares actually do before the 2031 meetings, not by the long-run rate assumed. The passage is clearer that it doesn't matter than about why.
- **Image:** It helped. "Erase every pencil line – the shares gone to zero – and the page still reads" made the stress test concrete at once, and the gloss between the dashes told me exactly what erasing meant.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It covers the same ground as Passages 8, 10 and 11, but compressed and in the passive ("is assumed to land", "Two margins are chosen, not forecast", "the model being a model"). The last paragraph crams the margins and the limits into one semicolon list. It reads like a capable editor's summary of the team's section rather than the team talking.
- **Meaning:** The 7.5% assumption barely moves the top; actual markets before 2031 set it; with shares at zero the payments and floor stand; plus deposit timing, no outside funding, the Taiwan cost-and-currency limit, two margins and the remaining limits.
- **Check question (assumptions):** No. Anywhere from 4% to 10% the top only moves from about $116,000 to $119,500, inside a single $5,000 step, because what shares actually do before the 2031 meetings sets the top.
- **Image:** It helped, as in Passage 8, though the explanation ("if the shares went to zero") comes after the colon, so I read the image a beat before I knew what it meant.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It asks the reader's question out loud ("So is the top of Laura's range resting on the hopeful end? In our model, hardly."). "The facility is another matter" echoes the IPS's "The building is a different matter". It is the same confident, slightly playful person, though with more passives than the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The share assumption looks fragile but barely moves the top; actual markets before 2031 set it; even at zero shares nothing else moves; plus deposit timing, no outside money, the Taiwan cost-and-currency limit, two deliberate margins and the remaining limits.
- **Check question (assumptions):** No, hardly. Between 4% and 10% the top before rounding moves only from about $116,000 to $119,500, less than one $5,000 step, because the top is set by what shares actually do before the 2031 meetings. The rhetorical question made this the easiest version to remember.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The exclamation mark, "simply", "after all" and the run of short fragments at the end ("The model is a model.") recall the IPS's bursts ("So we hedge it!", "Fair enough; that's your job."). It is a little rough in places ("Treasury rates are simply as the bond market priced them", "sized as half the shares, as the IPS says"), and that roughness reads as human.
- **Meaning:** The 7.5% assumption barely moves the top; actual markets before 2031 set it ($95,000-$140,000); plus deposit timing, no outside funding, the Taiwan cost-and-currency limit, two margins and four plain limits. It does not run the zero-shares test.
- **Check question (assumptions):** No, it would barely change. From 4% to 10% the top runs only from about $116,000 to $119,500, less than one $5,000 step, because what shares actually do before 2031 sets the top.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The sentences are short, clipped and careful, with one crafted flip ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does") and none of the dashes, eagerness or "we'll simply" of the IPS. Where the IPS boasts that the ladder doesn't depend on yields staying put, this passage names a weakness and a cut to the whole range. It sounds like a more guarded, lawyerly mind.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 Laura states a range from the floor (rounded down) to the floor plus half her shares, moved into Treasuries first. Because the ladder relies on reinvesting interest, any shortfall priced at that day's rates is covered from her kept shares before she names the range, and if that is not enough, the whole range comes down.
- **Check question (assumptions, loosely):** By this passage the top is half of what the shares are actually worth when she names it, so slower growth would lower the top by half of whatever value was not earned, not by a fixed assumed rate. The passage gives no figure.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It starts with a confident one-line idea ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong"). Then one long, happily overloaded sentence carries "vain illusion", "growth premium" and "(as per JPMorgan)", and the passage ends on a question. That is the IPS's mix of punch, showiness and a slightly loose join.
- **Meaning:** VT is the only holding not tied to a payment: a cheap world index fund that avoids stock-picking and is expected to earn about 7.5% a year. It decides how generous the facility contribution can be while the Treasuries cover the payments.
- **Check question (portfolio as built, partly):** It says why a world share fund exists at all: to size the facility contribution while the Treasuries cover the payments. It does not say why the $300,000 book holds it when the 2027 deposit all goes to the ladder.

### What separates the highest from the lowest

The passages I ranked highest sound like teenagers who are pleased with their idea and a little showy about it. They use big words with confidence ("vain illusion", "near-ironclad", "terminal payout", "restless markets"), hinge sentences on dashes, let a sentence run on and close on a turn or a question, and push Laura's own words further rather than hedging them. The middle group carries that voice under the discipline of many figures, and the conversational pivots keep it recognisable ("The catch is", "Yes, ... that's on purpose", "hardly", an exclamation mark). The lowest are even and clipped, more passive and more cautious: a textbook explainer (7), an editor's summary (9), and a guarded mind that names weaknesses where the IPS reassures (12). They are good prose, but nobody in them sounds excited about the plan.
