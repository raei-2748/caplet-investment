# Writer J, voice round 5

## Warm-up: IPS line 13, notes then rebuild

Notes, one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027 – first deposit – buy 10 Treasuries (bonds or iBonds funds, whichever suits each year) – each matures just before it's needed.
2. Held to the end, pay what they say – price swings: nothing to fear.
3. Cost at current yields ~$300k – i.e. the whole first deposit.
4. Reassure – plan doesn't need yields to stay put (exclamation).
5. Yields fall, ladder costs more than $300k – finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 we'll spend your first deposit on 10 Treasuries – T-Bonds or iShares iBonds funds, whichever suits each year – each one maturing just before the payment it's for. Held to the end, they pay what they say, so whatever their prices do in between doesn't bother us. At today's yields that costs about $300,000 – your whole first deposit, near enough. But don't worry – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ladder ends up costing more than $300,000, we'll just finish it with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: I went shorter and more casual ("near enough", "doesn't bother us", "just finish it") and lost the team's slightly formal connectives – "in other words", "we'll simply complete it" – which sit beside the chattiness and are half of what makes it theirs. I also dropped "no price fluctuations for us to fear", which has more character than my flatter version, and I cut "the total cost of the ladder ends up being above", where the team happily lets a sentence run long the way people talk.

=== P1 ===
Table 1 is Laura's whole plan, both deposits, scaled to the $300,000 we were given to trade ("scaled to the model portfolio", as our trade notes put it). That's why it holds a fund for the building and a world share fund at all, when the IPS spends the whole first deposit on the ten payments: every instrument the strategy relies on got bought and tested. The catch is that we built it before we'd sized the operating reserve, so it holds neither the 2028 top-up nor the extra Treasuries, and it splits what's left of the 2028 money the way the plan does – about 73% to the floor fund, 27% to VT. Hence VT at 8.84% of the book, against about 6.6% of the plan.

IBTM, the largest row, does two jobs: it pays the 2033 payment and stands in for the floor fund, the bottom of her facility contribution. Around it, the mix was mostly forced on us. WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036, and no iBonds Treasury fund ends between 2037 and 2043 – so the payments of 2033 to 2036 had to sit in funds, and 2038 to 2042 in bonds. Only 2037 was our choice: IBTR, over a 4.5% bond maturing in February 2036.

Yes, 89.55% of the book is in one issuer, and that's on purpose. For money that must be paid on a set date, the matching asset is the Treasury of that date; spreading it around would only add ways to come up short. The diversifying is done in VT, across about 10,000 companies.

And the buying met targets set before trading began: all ten Treasury holdings at exactly their target units, every bond's yield within a quarter of a percentage point of the curve, cash never below its $1,000 floor, $200 of fees in all. VT went last, from the cash left over – 167 units against a target of 164. The $1,309 row is that $200 plus interest the bonds already owed when we bought them; it comes back with each bond's next interest payment.

=== P3 ===
We assume world shares earn 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's long-term figure for global shares – in the upper third of fifteen houses' published figures, whose middle is about 6.5%. In our model, though, that number barely matters: anywhere from 4% to 10% a year, the top Laura can state runs from about $116,000 to $119,500 before rounding, less than one $5,000 step! What sets the top is what shares actually do before the 2031 meetings, and across the markets we tested, that runs from $95,000 to $140,000. Treasury rates are simply as the bond market priced them on 6 November 2026.

On timing, we assume the 2028 deposit arrives in full at the start of 2028, as the case has it. If nothing has arrived by 31 March 2028, that's the first of the IPS's two reasons to change the plan; whatever does arrive runs in the same order. Outside funding: none, for the ten payments or the bottom of the range. Co-sponsors' money is expected year by year, in Taiwan dollars.

Inflation can't touch whether the payments are made – they're fixed in dollars – only what they buy. Taiwan's building costs rose about 3.3% a year over the five years to September 2026, and building costs and the Taiwan dollar together rose more than 28% in 1 in 20 two-year spans since 1991: about $32,200 on a $115,000 top, a little more than her whole flexibility (about $29,800). It's a limit, not a reason to change anything. Her flexibility, after all, is sized as half the shares, as the IPS says, not to any cost estimate.

Two safety margins are there on purpose, and they're margins, not forecasts: the payments are sized so all ten are paid even if cash waiting earns nothing after January 2028, and each end of the range is cut by 0.5% for the funds' costs beyond their 0.07% yearly fee.

And the limits. A US default, the IPS's own condition. The iBonds funds pay what their bonds are worth, not a fixed sum. The model is a model. And the plan secures the money, not the site or the people.

=== NOTES ===
Without the essays I leaned on the Trading Note reflections for rhythm ("The catch is", "Yes,", contractions, one exclamation), so the passages probably sound more like those reflections than like the IPS letter's warmth; the brief's density of figures still pushed each passage towards a tidy four-paragraph run, which is more finished than the team's quick bursts. In P2 the turn did one real job, letting the 89.55% in one issuer read as the obvious consequence of a table that sorts itself, but I had to drop the "owned and paying on its date" gloss so it wouldn't define ink a second time. In P4 the turn sat most naturally right after the $95,000 low end of the share range, so the worst case arrived as the next step rather than a set piece; the one strain is that "every pencil line" is wider than "the shares gone to zero", and a careful reader might notice. On balance the turn helped in P4 and was neutral in P2, and P4 runs about 40 words longer than P3 while P2 matches P1.
