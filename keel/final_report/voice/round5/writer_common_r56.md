# Voice rounds 5 and 6: what every writer does (Mon 16 Nov 2026, evening)

You are practising the voice of six Sydney high-school students (team "Caplet") who are writing a Final Report for the
Wharton Global High School Investment Competition. Their client is Laura Gao, a graphic novelist planning an artists'
residency in Taiwan. The team asked (Book 17): "you must START with OUR voice and style and tone, and move forward from
there. Literally write like we do." They want much more voice practice before the report is written (Book 19).

## 1. Read, whole, slowly, before writing anything
Your brief names your reading. Read it as writing by people, not as a list of features. Do NOT open any other file in
`keel/` (Keel's working files pull the voice away); the only facts files are below.

## 2. The facts
- `/home/user/caplet-investment/keel/final_report/voice/FACTS_PLAIN.md` is the main source of facts and figures. Its
  section on the conceit ("pencil and ink") lists the turns, all checked.
- Your brief adds checked facts for your two topics. Never invent a number, a date, a quotation or an event. You may

## 3. What the last four rounds found (descriptions, not rules)
- The team's real writing is heard as people "sure of their plan, warm towards Laura, and plainly enjoying the idea:
  explaining in quick bursts, with asides and turns on a dash, reaching a little further than they need to". Writing
  that lost was "too finished": staged set pieces, neat reversals, rounded endings, a guided-tour structure.
- Passages that STACKED images, or built a turn up and then named it, were heard as "a skilled adult writer working a
  conceit". In rounds 3 and 4 the readers said why: the IPS has one analogy (her career as a stock she can't sell), and
  it is "stated once and put to work, not staged". What they marked down: a turn built and then named, a slogan or
  closing aphorism, a second reference beside the turn, two images crossed in one sentence, and "ink" used before it
  was defined. The image never cost understanding; it cost the voice.
- The team does not hedge: Treasuries may be called guaranteed, locked, set in stone or risk-free, and nothing needs
  "barring a US default" attached (Book 19). Accuracy still matters: never state a false fact.
- The report is written about Laura and to the judges' questions, but never addresses "the judges" or "you, the reader".

## 4. This round's question
Each topic is written TWICE, so the readers can hear the difference:
- **plain**: no conceit at all;
- **with one turn**: exactly ONE turn of the pencil-and-ink conceit, the one your brief names. This round the readers
  are given the definition before they read (ink = a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on
  its date; pencil = a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet), as the report gives it once, early on. So do NOT
  define ink or pencil again and do NOT build up to the turn: use it the way the IPS uses its one analogy, in a sentence
  or two where it does a job, then carry on in plain words. No second image, no craft aside, no pun, no closing line
  that repeats it. The two versions carry the same facts and argument, so the turn is the only difference.

## 5. Warm-up (write it in your output file, short)
Take the IPS paragraph that starts "In January 2027 we’ll use your first deposit" (line 13 of the IPS file). Write a
few words of notes per sentence. Put the IPS away. Rebuild the paragraph from your notes alone. Compare in two
sentences: where did yours drift?

## 6. The four passages (about 280-340 words each)
Each is a part of a longer Final Report, which is not a letter: it is an in-depth analysis of the plan, written for
Wharton judges, in the team's voice. It evaluates and explains why; it never just repeats the IPS. Your brief names the
topics. Order: P1 topic one plain; P2 topic one with its turn; P3 topic two plain; P4 topic two with its turn. Write P2
fresh, not as an edit of P1.

## 7. Output
Write everything to the output file named in your brief: the warm-up, then the four passages, each starting on its own
line with `=== P1 ===`, `=== P2 ===`, `=== P3 ===`, `=== P4 ===` and nothing else on that line, then `=== NOTES ===`
with three or four honest sentences on how the passages felt next to the team's writing, and whether the turn helped
or got in the way.
