2. Held to the end – pay what they say – no price swings to fear.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 your first deposit will buy 10 Treasuries – a T-Bond or an iShares iBonds fund, whichever suits each year – and each one matures just before its payment is due. Hold them to the end and they pay what they say, so the prices in between don't frighten us. At today's yields that comes to about $300,000, which is the whole of your first deposit. But don't worry – nothing here needs yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ladder costs more than $300,000, the 2028 deposit simply finishes it.

Comparison: mine made the deposit do the buying ("your first deposit will buy") where theirs has the team doing it ("we'll use your first deposit"), so the people quietly dropped out of the first sentence. I also lost "in other words" and "worry not" – the small restatement and the bit of play – which left it tidier and a little flatter than theirs.

=== P1 ===
Table 1 is our competition book at cost, on the original $300,000, and at first glance it doesn't look like the IPS. The IPS spends the whole 2027 deposit on the 10 payments – so why does this book also hold a Treasury fund for the facility and a world share fund? Because it's Laura's whole plan, both deposits, scaled to $300,000 of its $450,000, so every instrument the strategy needs was bought, not assumed (our trade notes call it 'scaled to the model portfolio'). It was built before the operating reserve was sized, so it holds neither the 2028 top-up nor the extra Treasuries, and it splits what's left of the 2028 money as the plan does – about 73% to the floor fund, 27% to VT. That's why VT is 8.84% of the book but only about 6.6% of the plan.

Every row but VT is a Treasury Laura would already own, paying out on a set date; VT is the one line not yet settled. So 89.55% of the book sits with one issuer, on purpose: for money that must be paid on a date, the matching asset is the Treasury of that date. The diversifying is done in VT, across about 10,000 companies.

IBTM is the biggest row, $97,490 (32.50%), because it does two jobs – the 2033 payment, and a stand-in for the floor fund. IBTO to IBTR pay 2034 to 2037, and the five bonds pay 2038 to 2042. Most of that was forced: WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036, and no iBonds Treasury fund ends in 2037-2043. Only 2037 was a real choice – we took IBTR over a 4.5% bond maturing in February 2036.

We set targets before trading and met them: every Treasury holding at exactly its target units, every bond inside a quarter-point price band, cash never under its $1,000 floor, $200 of fees in all. VT went in last, from the cash left – 167 units against a target of 164. The $1,309 row is those fees plus interest already owed on the bonds, which comes back with each one's next interest payment.

=== P3 ===
Of the case's five areas, investment performance looks the most fragile. We assume world shares earn 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's 2027 long-term figure, in the upper third of 15 houses' forecasts (their middle is about 6.5%). So is the top of Laura's range resting on the hopeful end? In our model, hardly. Anywhere from 4% to 10% a year, the top before rounding moves only from about $116,000 to $119,500, less than one $5,000 step. What sets it is what shares actually do before the 2031 meetings: $95,000 to $140,000 across the markets we tested. Even with the shares at zero, nothing else moves – the 10 payments are unchanged, the floor fund still pays about $103,000, and the range is one figure, $95,000. Treasury rates are taken as the market priced them on 6 November 2026.

The 2028 deposit is assumed to arrive in full at the start of 2028; if nothing has come by 31 March 2028, that's the first of the IPS's two reasons to change the plan, and a partial deposit runs the same order. No outside money is assumed for the payments or the bottom of the range, and co-sponsor money is expected year by year, in Taiwan dollars.

Inflation decides what the payments buy, never whether they're paid – they're fixed in dollars. The facility is another matter: Taiwan's building costs and the Taiwan dollar together rose more than 28% in 1 in 20 two-year spans since 1991 – about $32,200 on a $115,000 top, a little more than her whole flexibility (about $29,800 in our model). Since her flexibility is half the shares, not a cost estimate, that's a limit we state, not a reason to change.

Two margins are deliberate, not forecasts: all 10 payments are covered even if waiting cash earns nothing after January 2028, and each end of the range is cut 0.5% for the funds' costs beyond their 0.07% fee. The limits: a US default (the IPS's own condition), iBonds funds that pay what their bonds are worth, a model that is only a model, and a plan that secures the money but not the site or the people.
