## From memory

**A. The plan's argument in three sentences.**
My first deposit buys US Treasuries for all ten $50,000 payments six years early. The reason is that my second deposit comes from my career, and a recession could make it late and small at the same moment shares fall and co-sponsors cut back. The second deposit then goes in a fixed order: first it finishes the payments, adding extra Treasuries in case cash sitting between coupons earns nothing; then it buys more of a Treasury fund that closes in late 2032, which sets the lowest building figure I can name (about $95,000); and last it buys world shares. In 2031 half the shares are sold into that same fund, which sets the top (about $115,000). The thread through all of it is pencil and ink: I only say a figure once Treasuries held for me will pay it. So the range I give co-sponsors isn't a best guess and a worst guess. It's certain, and its two ends mark the two dates each part was paid for. The half of the shares I keep is my flexibility, for currency moves and building-cost overruns.

**B. The three things I remember most, strongest first.**
1. Pencil and ink: a comic page is drawn twice and only the ink gets printed. My money is built the same way, and I state nothing still in pencil.
2. $450,000 in and about $615,000 promised, with most of the difference being interest the US government pays because the Treasuries are bought years early.
3. Leaving the payments to shares would mean "betting on my career twice". Close behind: the insurance that hands back its premium with interest if nothing goes wrong.

**C. Moments where something earlier paid off later.**
- ¶2 to ¶18: the question of why to give a range for a figure they call certain. The answer is that the two ends aren't guesses. They are two dates, January 2028 for the bottom and January 2031 for the rest. This was the strongest click.
- ¶1 to ¶6: where the extra $165,000 comes from. $500,000 of payments costs only about $302,000 because each Treasury pays interest for years first.
- ¶7 to ¶12 and ¶25: "It will matter again" about the fund for 2032 (IBTM). The floor is more of that same fund, and later it turns out to carry the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, which is why its own risks get a paragraph.
- ¶8 to ¶10: "the only money that can close that gap is money that has not arrived yet" leads straight into the 2028 deposit buying the extra Treasuries.
- ¶5 to ¶24: my career, the risk the whole plan was built around, comes back with the deposit arriving smaller or not at all.

**D. Questions the piece raised and never answered.**
- Why does about $37,000 of extra Treasuries close a gap of about $54,000? I guess they earn interest before they are needed, but the page doesn't say so.
- Why buy the longest-dated payments first, so that the bit left over falls on 2033?
- Why is my real plan limited to what a competition trading simulator lists? Reading as Laura, that line broke the spell. And why does the 2037 payment also need a fund?
- If a Treasury payment were more than five business days late, what would the plan change to? Why five days?
- Why does the top stay at $115,000 even if shares grow only 5% a year?
- The shortfall with no 2028 deposit at all (about $57,000) is a little bigger than the one with cash earning nothing (about $54,000). Why the difference?
- Flexibility's third job, a co-sponsor falling short, never gets a number or a test the way the currency and building costs do.
- "No building before the money is in hand": whose rule is that, mine or the residency's?
- What is the "government-backed protection" if the broker fails, and how long would the delay be?

**E. Sentences that announced or promised a point instead of making it.**
- "It will matter again." (¶7)
- "It is spent in one order, and the order is the argument." (¶9)
- "Here is the first answer to the opening puzzle." (¶6)
- "The second puzzle is timing." (¶2). The first puzzle was never called a puzzle.
- "Then there is the risk the whole plan was built around ..." (¶24). This one is a softer signpost.

## Last

If I were busy, I'd most likely stop at ¶20-¶21: by ¶19 my question (what do I say, and how sure can I be) is answered, and what follows reads like an appendix of risk figures, with ¶22 repeating ¶20 and ¶25 written for a fund manager.

The one change that would help a reader like me most: put the risks after ¶19 into one shorter stretch, each opening with what I (or the residency) would do if it happened, and cut the fund mechanics in ¶25 to one plain sentence.
