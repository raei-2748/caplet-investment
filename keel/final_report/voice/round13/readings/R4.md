## From memory

**A. The plan's argument in three sentences.**
All of Laura's promises fall due years after her only two deposits, so the plan never lets her announce a figure until she already holds the US government bonds that will pay it. The first deposit buys bonds and bond funds for the ten yearly payments; the second finishes them, adds a cushion so they still work if the interest earns nothing, buys a fund maturing in late 2032 that fixes the lowest building figure, and puts what is left in a world share fund. Just before she speaks to the co-sponsors in 2031, half the shares are turned into more of that 2032 fund, which fixes the top (about $115,000, with about $95,000 of it held since 2028), and the other half stays hers as flexibility for things like building costs and the Taiwan dollar.

**B. The three things I remember most, strongest first.**
1. Pencil and ink: a comic page is pencilled and then inked, only the ink is printed, and Laura only says out loud a figure that is "inked", meaning bonds she holds will pay it. The ending brought it back: co-sponsors see only the ink.
2. Why the first, share-heavy plan was dropped: a writer's income already behaves like a big pile of shares she can't sell, so loading the portfolio with shares doubles the bet.
3. Selling the shares before the 2031 meetings, not after: a figure named too early might have to be cut later, and you can paint over ink but the co-sponsors keep the record, which is exactly the credibility damage the case warns about.

**C. Moments where something earlier paid off later.**
- ¶3 to ¶28: the pencil-and-ink picture set up at the start closes the piece (co-sponsors, like her readers, only see the ink; the pencil underneath is hers to redraw). Clicked as the reason for the picture.
- ¶2 to ¶26 (I think): the worry about a writer's income comes back as the test that the 2028 deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller and the payments would still be met. "So that is why the plan was built around the payments first."
- ¶6 to ¶10: the "catch" that a fund's payout isn't fixed turned into the 0.5% margin.
- ¶9 to ¶10: the shortfall if interest earns nothing (about $445,000 of $500,000) is what the extra Treasuries are sized for, and those plus the ten become the operating reserve the case asks for. That was a real click with the case.
- ¶14 to ¶25: buying the floor first means even if shares went to nothing the range just shrinks to the $95,000 bottom.
- ¶17 to ¶23: "half the shares" puzzled me at ¶17, and the later paragraph on keeping half back picked it up (though see D).
- The case's "overpromising hurts credibility" paid off in ¶18.

**D. Questions the piece made me ask and never answered.**
- Why exactly half of the shares is sold in 2031. The piece says the split is a choice and that it barely changes the total, but not why half rather than a third or all of it.
- Why buying the longest-dated payments first is the right order (why the 2033 piece is the right one to leave unbought).
- What "came up short in 80 of every 100 starting months since 1981" means: short of what, and reinvested at what rates? Why would it be so often short?
- Why a more cautious 5% share growth gives the same $115,000 top. I guessed the rounding to $5,000s, but it wasn't said.
- How the floor's roughly $102,000 becomes $95,000: how much of the drop is the "interest earns nothing" step and how much the margin and rounding?
- Why $5,000 more of stated top costs about $5,300 of flexibility rather than $5,000.
- What Laura would actually do if the 2032 fund ended early or her broker failed.

**E. Sentences that announced or promised a point instead of making it.**
- The one saying her own trade (illustration) has the right picture for what replaced the old plan: it promised the picture before giving it (it then delivered quickly).
- "Keeping half back is a choice, and the model prices it": announced a justification that then priced the trade but didn't justify half.
- The opening of the career paragraph, saying her career could do exactly what the plan was designed around: a set-up line.
- The line calling IBTM "the last thing to watch": announced a section rather than making a point.
- The rhetorical "What does that certainty cost?" and "Why a range at all, then?": set-ups, though both were answered.

## Last

Where I would most likely stop if busy: at ¶19, because after ¶18's strong point the printers' picture and the "2% in all" aside felt like the piece circling a point already made, and the dense tests from ¶23 to ¶27 come after it.

The one change that would most help a reader like me: early on, one plain sentence on what "the cash paid along the way earns nothing" means and why it is the cautious test, since that condition comes back in almost every paragraph (¶3, ¶9, ¶10, ¶12, ¶14, ¶17, ¶19, ¶26, ¶27) and I never fully knew why it was the right worst case.
