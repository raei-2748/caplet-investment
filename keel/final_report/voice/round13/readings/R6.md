## From memory

**A. The plan's argument in three sentences.**
All of my money comes in during 2027 and 2028, but the promises run from 2031 to 2042, and my income from writing could shrink in the same downturn that hits shares, so I should only tell anyone a figure once Treasuries already held for me will pay it. The first deposit buys Treasuries for the ten yearly payments. The second finishes them and adds a cushion so they hold up even if the interest earns nothing more, then buys a Treasury fund that ends in late 2032, which sets my lowest building figure (about $95,000), and puts what is left into a world share fund. Just before the 2031 meetings, half of those shares go into the same 2032 fund, so the top (about $115,000) is already held when I name it, and the other half stays mine as a buffer for costs, the currency or a co-sponsor giving less.

**B. The three things I remember most, strongest first.**
1. Pencil and ink. A comic page is pencilled, then inked, and only the ink is printed, so I state a figure only once it is "inked" in Treasuries. Being an illustrator, I got it at once, and the ending came back to it.
2. If the interest earned nothing, the ten payments would come to only about $445,000 of the $500,000, and that would have happened in most historical start dates. So there is an extra block of Treasuries (roughly $37,000) that may never be needed and comes back to me. It is like a publisher holding back part of the royalties against returns, which I know from my own contracts.
3. My career already behaves like a big holding of shares I can't sell, so a share-heavy plan would double my risk. That is why the first plan was dropped.
(Close behind: the printer's cut line, with $115,000 as the words kept inside it and about 2% of room for the blade; and the pledge sentence, "$115,000, all held before I say it, $95,000 of it since 2028".)

**C. Moments where something earlier paid off later.**
- ¶3 to ¶9/¶10: the ink definition said "even if the cash paid out along the way earns nothing more". Now I see why: reinvestment is not guaranteed and would have come up short most of the time, so the extra Treasuries cover it.
- ¶6 to ¶10: the funds' payout "isn't a fixed sum", and that is why there is a 0.5% margin, against about 0.27% seen in the funds that have already ended.
- ¶14 to ¶19: why the figure is rounded down. It is the room left for the blade, about 2%.
- ¶19 to ¶27: that same 2% is the line that fund costs would have to cross before the top is at risk.
- ¶14 ("more of IBTM") to ¶27: IBTM was already held for the 2033 payment, so one fund carries the payment, the bottom and the top.
- ¶15 to ¶25: no promise rests on the shares, so if they went to nothing the range would just shrink to $95,000 and the payments would be untouched.
- ¶2 to ¶26: the career risk they designed around. The 2028 deposit could come in about three-quarters smaller and the payments would still be met, which matches the roughly $39,000 the payments needed from it.
- ¶8 to ¶9: why "the interest" was still in pencil after 2027.

**D. Questions the piece raised and never answered.**
- Why half? ¶23 says the split is a choice and prices it, but never says why half and not 60/40 or 40/60.
- Why is buying the longest-dated payments first the right order (¶7)? What is gained by leaving the gap in the 2033 payment?
- If a cautious 5% a year still gives a $115,000 top, why does the top reach $115,000 in only about 57 of 100 futures?
- Why would an early end of IBTM "during 2031" leave the top about $4,600 short, and why only 2031?
- Why does about $46,600 come back from about $36,774 of extra Treasuries? (I guessed interest, but the page doesn't say.)
- The bottom is "never measured again", yet IBTM is on the watch list. What does Laura do if the fund's costs ever pass the 2%?
- Is the 2031 sale date fixed even if shares have just crashed that month?
- Is the pledge letter legally binding, and what does she do with her flexibility if nothing goes wrong?

**E. Sentences that announced or promised a point instead of making it.**
- The one-line paragraph saying the rest of the 2028 deposit buys two things in a set order (¶13).
- The line saying my own trade has the right picture for what replaced the first plan (¶3). It promises the picture before giving it, though the picture then arrives.
- The line saying keeping half back is a choice and the model prices it (¶23). It promises an argument for the split and gives only the price.
- The opener naming IBTM as the last thing to watch (¶27). It is mild, but it announces.
- ¶16 as a whole: it sums up the order I had just read rather than adding much.
