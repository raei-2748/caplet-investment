## From memory

**A. The argument in three sentences.**
Laura never states a dollar figure until US Treasuries she already holds will pay it on its date (counted cautiously, with no interest earned on the cash they pay out along the way and a small allowance for fund costs), so each date in the plan locks in a promise before she says it. The 2027 deposit buys the ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years early, which is cheap because Treasuries pay interest the whole time; the 2028 deposit, the last money she will ever receive, then finishes and insures those payments, buys a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032 to fix a cautious floor for the building (about $95,000), and puts the small rest into a world share fund. In January 2031 half the shares go into that same Treasury fund, which fixes the top (about $115,000 on today's figures), so the range she gives co-sponsors is not a best and worst guess but two dates on which money was locked in, and the half of the shares she keeps is her room for a stronger Taiwan dollar, building overruns or a co-sponsor falling short, while her own career, the risk the plan was built around, can shrink by about three-quarters without touching the payments.

**B. What I remember most, strongest first.**
1. The rule behind the comic-page picture: pencil until a Treasury will pay it, ink after, and she only speaks in ink.
2. The opening sum: $450,000 goes in and about $615,000 is promised, mostly because of interest on Treasuries bought years before they are needed.
3. The range's two ends being two dates (the bottom held since 2028, the rest of the top since 2031) rather than two guesses, so the range can be called certain.
(A close fourth: a share-heavy plan would bet on her career twice, because her second deposit and share prices fall in the same recession.)

**C. Payoffs.**
- ¶1 and ¶6: the $615,000 puzzle clicked when ten payments worth $500,000 cost about $302,000 bought early.
- ¶2 and ¶17-18: why wait (the top is pencil until the 2031 move) and why a range for a certain figure (the ends mark dates).
- ¶7 and ¶12/¶25: "it will matter again" about the 2032 Treasury fund paid off when that fund turned out to carry the 2033 payment, the floor and the top, and its not-quite-fixed payout became the last risk section.
- ¶8 and ¶10: the "cash waiting" gap that only future money can close is what the extra Treasuries from the 2028 deposit close.
- ¶10 and ¶24: the first slice of the 2028 deposit is roughly a quarter of it, so that is why the deposit can come in about three-quarters smaller and the payments still hold.
- ¶5 and ¶24: the career risk set up early is the one tested last.
- ¶1 and ¶15: "only a small part is a bet on shares" is backed by the top staying the same at a much lower share return.

**D. Questions it never answered.**
- If the stated range is certain, what happens when the 2032 fund pays less than the top in the cases the last risk paragraph lists, and who covers the gap (her kept shares, presumably, but it is not said)?
- Why keep half the shares rather than more than half? The case given only shows why not to keep less.
- Why so little in shares overall (roughly $30,000 of $450,000), and what does that cost her against a more balanced plan? The "insurance refunds its premium" point counts Treasury interest, not what the money might have earned in shares.
- The shortfall with no 2028 deposit (about $57,000) differs from the zero-interest shortfall given earlier (about $55,000); I assume the difference is the unbought sliver grown to 2033, but it is not said.
- The historical replay says the payments would often have come up short, but by how much?
- Why does the 2037 payment also rest on a fund, and why were the longest-dated payments bought first?
- Why is a Treasury payment five business days late the second trigger, and what would "changing the plan" actually involve?
- Does the leftover from the extra Treasuries (the refund of about $46,000) count as part of her flexibility?

**E. Sentences that announced instead of making a point.**
- The one saying the deposit is spent in one order and the order is the argument.
- "It will matter again" about the 2032 fund.
- The one saying both answers come from how she makes her books (it does pay off at once, so mild).
- The signposts that open ¶6 (here is the first answer) and ¶24 (the risk the plan was built around) and ¶25 (last, the funds).
