## From memory

**A. The plan's argument in three sentences.**
Laura's first deposit buys government bonds for all ten $50,000 payments years ahead, because the interest earned over those years does most of the paying, and because her second deposit comes from her career, which can shrink in the same recession that sinks shares and co-sponsors. The second deposit then finishes the payments with a cushion in case waiting money earns no interest, buys more of one Treasury fund to set a cautious bottom for the building money, and puts what is left in a world share fund. In 2031 half the shares are moved into that same Treasury fund before she meets anyone, so every figure she states is already paid for: the range is "certain", its two ends mark the two dates they were paid for, and the shares left over are hers to absorb surprises.

**B. The three things I remember most, strongest first.**
1. Pencil and ink: a comic page is drawn in pencil, then inked, and only ink gets printed; Laura only says a number once Treasuries held for her will pay it.
2. The range's two ends are not a best guess and a worst guess but two dates: the bottom was paid for in 2028, the rest of the top in 2031. That is why a "certain" figure can still be a range.
3. The insurance that refunds its premium with interest: if waiting money does earn interest, the extra Treasuries come back to her (something like $46,000).
(Close behind: if shares went to nothing, the range would shrink to $95,000 and the payments would not change by a dollar.)

**C. Moments where something earlier paid off later.**
- ¶1/¶2 to ¶6: the "extra $165,000" puzzle. So that is why $500,000 of payments cost only about $302,000: each bond is bought years early and earns interest the whole time.
- ¶2 to ¶17 and ¶18: the timing puzzle. So that is why she waits until 2031 (the top is pencil until the shares are moved), and why a figure called certain is still a range (the ends are two dates).
- ¶5 to ¶24: the career risk the plan was built around comes back; the deposit could arrive much smaller and the payments would still be met.
- ¶7 ("It will matter again") to ¶12 and ¶25: the 2033 fund (IBTM) turns out to hold the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, which is why the fund risks are what they watch.
- ¶3 to ¶26: what is left in pencil is hers to rub out, at no cost to anyone else.

**D. Questions the piece raised and never answered.**
- Why does the extra cushion cost less (about $37,000) than the shortfall it covers (I remember about $55,000)? Presumably because it is bought years ahead and earns interest, but it is never said.
- Why is the top the same $115,000 whether shares grow 7.5% or 5% a year? I assume the rounding down to $5,000 steps, but I had to guess.
- Why does each extra $5,000 of top cost about $5,300 of what she keeps, and not $5,000, if the split "does not change how much there is"?
- Why was waiting money short so often after 1981 and rarely before? I assume falling interest rates, but the page does not say.
- Two different "no interest" shortfalls (one in ¶8, a slightly bigger one when the deposit never arrives): what is the difference?
- Where does "a Treasury payment more than five business days late" as a reason to change the plan come from? It appears once with no explanation.
- Why 0.5% for fund costs, and why buy the longest-dated payments first?

**E. Sentences that announced or promised a point instead of making it.**
- "It will matter again." (¶7, about IBTM): a teaser, and it made that paragraph feel like detail with no payoff yet.
- "It is spent in one order, and the order is the argument." (¶9)
- "Here is the first answer to the opening puzzle." (¶6), though the answer follows straight away.
- "Both answers come from how Laura makes her books." (¶3), a promise that is mostly kept in the same paragraph.
