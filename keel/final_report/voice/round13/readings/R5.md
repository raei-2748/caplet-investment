## From memory

**A. The argument in three sentences.**
Every figure Laura promises is promised years before the money for it exists, and her income as a writer already behaves like a big share position she cannot sell, so the plan never lets a promise rest on shares: it buys Treasuries first and lets her state a figure only once Treasuries held for her will pay it. The 2027 deposit buys Treasuries and dated Treasury funds for the ten payments; the 2028 deposit finishes them, adds a block of extra Treasuries so the payments survive reinvestment at zero and some fund costs, buys the 2032 Treasury fund that fixes a bottom of about $95,000, and puts what is left in a world share fund. In January 2031, before she meets the co-sponsors, half the shares move into that same 2032 fund, so the top she names (about $115,000 on today's figures) is fully held when she names it, and the other half stays as unpromised flexibility for building-cost, currency and co-sponsor surprises.

**B. What I remember most, strongest first.**
1. The rule itself: buy first, state second, told as pencil and ink (ink being a figure that Treasuries already held will pay on its date).
2. Dropping the share-heavy first plan because a writer's income falls in the same downturn as shares, and the comparison of roughly 1 run in 31 missing a payment against none.
3. The reinvestment test: with waiting money earning nothing the ten as first bought pay only about $445,000 of the $500,000 and would have come up short in most starting months since the early 1980s, which is why the extra Treasuries (about $37,000) exist.
(Close behind: the top reaches $115,000 in only a little over half the simulated futures from today, and the bottom being fixed in 2028, three years before anyone hears it.)

**C. Pay-offs.**
- ¶2 to ¶26: the career risk set up at the start comes back as a stress test; the deposit can arrive about three-quarters smaller and the payments still hold. So that is why the payments are bought out of the first deposit.
- ¶8 to ¶10: the "interest" left in pencil in 2027 is exactly what the extra Treasuries ink in 2028; and the $2,388 plus the extras make the first slice of the 2028 deposit.
- ¶14 to ¶17: the top is worked out "the same way" as the bottom, so the three cautious steps I held in ¶14 paid off.
- ¶3 to ¶18: stating only what is in ink is why the sale happens before the meetings; naming a figure early and cutting it later is the credibility damage the case warns about.
- ¶6 to ¶10 to ¶27: the fund payout not being fixed explains the 0.5% margin and comes back as the first thing to watch on the 2032 fund.
- ¶15 to ¶22: the share fund nothing depends on becomes, after the 2031 move, the flexibility kept for what goes wrong after she speaks.

**D. Questions the piece raised but did not answer.**
- Why exactly half of the shares? The piece shows the split barely changes the total, but not why half, or whose preference it is.
- How is the model built (how rates and shares are simulated together, what volatility)? "200,000 futures" is all I got.
- Why would an early end of the 2032 fund leave the top up to about $4,600 short if waiting money earns nothing, and why does that vanish at market rates?
- Why replay interest rates from 1981 and not earlier?
- What does Laura say to co-sponsors about confidence? The suggested words give the figure and its history but no stated confidence.
- If the 2028 deposit comes in partly (say half), what happens to the floor and the bottom she can state?
- When exactly is the building contribution paid, and does the 2032 fund's December payout just sit until then?

**E. Sentences that announced instead of making the point.**
- ¶3's opening, that her own trade "has the right picture" for the new plan, announces the image before giving it.
- ¶13's one-line signpost (the rest buys two things in a set order).
- ¶27's opening, naming the 2032 fund as "the last thing to watch", before the reasons.
- The two rhetorical questions (what certainty costs, why a range at all) are answered at once, so they read more as signposts than as points.

## Last

The one change that would help a reader like me most: give the from-today odds (91 and 57 in 100) where $115,000 first appears in ¶17, so the top is never read as settled before ¶20, and cut the printers' parallel in ¶19 to keep one image for the whole piece.
