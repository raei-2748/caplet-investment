# Reader pack 3 (voice round 13, Sun 22 Nov 2026)

One long piece drafted for a competition team's Final Report about a client's money. Read it as a section of a report you had been handed.

**Your role.** You read as the client in the case: a writer and illustrator with a business degree, reading about your own money and your residency, attentive but not a finance professional.

**The case, in brief (what you know before you read).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000 into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay $50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments) to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is. Overpromising would hurt her credibility. A team of school students advises her; it may use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds.

## How to read

You are an independent reader, not the writer's colleague. Do not open any other file, look anything up, or try to find out who wrote it. Use only the page and the case above.

1. **Read the piece once, straight through, at a normal pace, in order.** As you go, keep a one-word mark for each numbered paragraph: **with it** (following and wanting to go on), **drifting** (following but no longer sure why you are being told this), or **lost** (lost the thread of the argument). Do not go back while reading.
2. **Then, before you look back at the piece**, answer from memory, in your own words, without quoting:
   - **A.** The plan's argument in three sentences.
   - **B.** The three things you remember most, in order of how strongly.
   - **C.** Any moment where something said earlier paid off later ("so that is why ..."): give the two paragraph numbers if you can, and what clicked.
   - **D.** Any question the piece made you ask that it never answered.
   - **E.** Any sentence that announced or promised a point instead of making it.
3. **Now look back** and fill one table row per paragraph: ¶ | Mark (from step 1) | Follow (1 = understood first time; 2 = needed a second read or had to hold something in mind; 3 = had to rebuild the reasoning or still unsure) | Why (Y = for every step or claim in it you could tell a friend why, from the page alone; N = at least one step or claim has no reason on the page) | Note (one line: for drifting, lost, 2-3 or N, what happened).
4. **Last, two lines:** where in the piece you would most likely have stopped reading if you were busy, and why; and the one change that would most help a reader like you.

Write your answers as Markdown to the file named in your instructions, in this order: `## Marks while reading` (¶1: mark, ...), `## From memory` (A-E), `## Paragraph by paragraph` (the table), `## Last`.

## The piece

**¶1** Laura Gao puts $450,000 into her portfolio, in two deposits a year apart, and nothing else ever goes in. On today's figures she will commit about $615,000: ten operating payments of $50,000, and $115,000 towards the residency's building. The extra $165,000 is not a trick, and only a small part of it is a bet that shares do well. Most of it is interest the US government will pay on Treasuries bought years before the money is needed.

**¶2** The second puzzle is timing. We can work out the $115,000, roughly, today. Yet Laura will not say it to anyone until 2031, and when she does, the case asks for it as a range. Why wait four years to announce a figure, and why give a range for a figure we are prepared to call certain?

**¶3** Both answers come from how Laura makes her books. She draws graphic novels, and a comic page is drawn twice: first in pencil, which can be rubbed out, and then in ink. Only the ink is printed. We built her money the same way. A dollar figure is in ink once Treasuries held for her will pay it on its date, counted as if the cash they pay out along the way earned nothing more, and after a small margin for fund costs. Until then it is in pencil: her future income, her shares, any interest not yet earned, and every forecast in this piece. The plan's one rule is that Laura states a figure only once it is in ink, and each of its dates inks something.

**¶4** [2027: the payments, six years early] A Treasury is a bond issued by the US government, which guarantees it. Held to the end, it pays exactly its stated interest and its face value, on fixed dates. In January 2027, six years before the first payment, Laura's first deposit buys ten of them, one for each payment, each paying out before the 1 January it pays for.

**¶5** Why buy the payments first, and so early? Partly because they are the one fixed thing in the case, and must be met 'with a high degree of certainty'. Mostly because of where her money comes from. The second deposit is book advances, speaking fees and licensing. In a recession that money comes later and smaller, and in the same downturn share prices can fall and co-sponsors can cut their budgets. A plan that left the payments to shares would be betting on her career twice. Our first plan, mostly shares, did exactly that, until we thought about how a writer is actually paid.

**¶6** Here is the first answer to the opening puzzle. Ten payments worth $500,000 cost about $302,300 at Treasury prices of 6 November 2026, because each is bought six to fifteen years before it falls due, and a Treasury pays interest the whole time. That is $2,287 more than the first deposit. We bought the longest-dated payments first, so the sliver left unbought is part of the 2033 payment. The 2028 deposit buys it a year later, for $2,388; we call that the top-up.

**¶7** Five of the ten are not single bonds but Treasury funds. Laura's plan may use only what WInS, the competition's trading simulator, lists, and WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036. So the payments of 2033 to 2036 had to rest on iShares iBonds funds, and 2037's does too. Each holds only Treasuries maturing in one calendar year, closes on about 15 December of that year and pays out what its Treasuries are then worth – close to a bond, but not a fixed sum. The 2033 payment rests on IBTM, a Treasury fund that holds only Treasuries maturing in 2032. It will matter again.

**¶8** So after January 2027 most of each payment is in ink, but two pieces are still pencil. One is the top-up. The other is quieter. Each Treasury pays interest along the way, and two bonds pay out months before their payment is due. The ten reach $50,000 each only if all of that money earns interest, at the rates implied by today's Treasury prices, while it waits for its 1 January. We call it cash waiting. If cash waiting earned nothing, the ten as bought would come up about $54,600 short. Nor is that far-fetched: replaying US interest rates since 1962, the ten as bought would have come up short in 80 of 100 starting months from 1981, and about 1 in 6 before then. The only money that can close that gap is money that has not arrived yet.

**¶9** [2028: the last money in] The 2028 deposit of $150,000 is the last money Laura's portfolio will ever receive, so every promise she makes has to be paid for from it. It is spent in one order, and the order is the argument.

**¶10** First, $39,162 finishes the payments. $2,388 is the top-up. The other $36,774 buys extra Treasuries: more of the same bonds and funds that pay the later years, sized so that every payment is met even if cash waiting earns nothing at all, and even if every Treasury fund pays out 0.5% less than its Treasuries are worth. From January 2028, then, all ten payments are in ink: Treasuries held for Laura will pay them in full. Together, the ten and the extra Treasuries are the operating reserve we recommend Laura set aside in 2033.

**¶11** Fair enough to ask whether that is too much: about a quarter of the second deposit insures against a world in which waiting money earns nothing. But unused insurance here is not lost. If rates turn out as November 2026's prices implied, the payments never touch the extra Treasuries, and their money and its interest come back to Laura by 2042: about $46,600, valued at the start of 2033. Few insurers refund the premium with interest when nothing goes wrong.

**¶12** Second, $81,244, a little over half the deposit, buys more of IBTM, the fund that closes in mid-December 2032 and already holds the money for the 2033 payment. At today's rates this purchase pays about $102,000 in December 2032. We call it the floor, because it sets the lowest figure Laura can state to co-sponsors. That figure, the bottom, is worked out cautiously: count as if the interest IBTM pays out earns nothing more, take 0.5% off for the fund's costs beyond its fee, and round down to a whole $5,000. The $102,000 becomes $95,000. It is worked out once, in January 2028, and never again. The bottom is in ink three years before co-sponsors hear it.

**¶13** Third, the last $29,594 buys VT, the Vanguard Total World Stock ETF: one fund holding shares in thousands of companies around the world. Shares come last in the order because no promise depends on them, and they get three years to grow before 2031. If we are going to be wrong about something, we would rather it be here.

**¶14** By the end of January 2028, then, the payments and the bottom are in ink. The top is not, and cannot be yet. It depends on what the shares are worth, and shares stay pencil until something turns them into Treasuries.

**¶15** [2031: why the top waits] In January 2031, before Laura meets any co-sponsor, half the value of her shares is sold and the money buys more IBTM. The top applies the bottom's cautious rule to the floor and the moved shares together: interest counted as earning nothing more, 0.5% off, rounded down once. On today's figures, with world shares growing 7.5% a year (J.P. Morgan's 2027 assumption, above the middle of published forecasts), the top is $115,000. At 5% a year the top is still $115,000, and at 3.1% one $5,000 step lower.

**¶16** Between 2028 and 2031 markets can move the top but never the bottom. If shares fell to nothing, the range would shrink to a single figure, the $95,000 bottom, and the ten payments would not change by a dollar.

**¶17** Why not name the top sooner? Because before the move it is pencil: no Treasury held for her would pay it yet. Our model simulates 200,000 futures for interest rates and share prices. Had Laura named a top in January 2030, a year early, the move would have bought less than she had named in about 11 of every 100 simulated futures. Seen from today, the top she will be able to state is at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures, and at least $115,000 in about 57. That uncertainty is the whole reason she waits. Once the shares have moved, nothing about the figure she states is uncertain, because the Treasuries that will pay it are already held for her.

**¶18** So what does she say? We recommend one figure to plan around, the top, with its history attached: '$115,000, all of it held in Treasury funds before I say it, $95,000 of it since January 2028.' The case asks for a range, and this is one, but of a particular kind. Its two ends are not a best guess and a worst guess. They mark two dates. The bottom is the part no market could move after January 2028; the rest of the top has been held since January 2031. Asked how confident we are that her contribution falls inside it, we do not answer with a percentage: the range she states is certain, because Treasuries already held for her, through IBTM, will pay both ends.

**¶19** She states it in US dollars, with its Taiwan-dollar value on a named day, and offers a signed pledge letter: holding the Treasuries shows the money exists, and a signature says it will be given. Her words state the whole commitment: ten payments of $50,000, held in Treasuries since January 2028, and $115,000 for the building, about $615,000 in all. From January 2028 she can already tell the residency's own planners 'at least $95,000', because that much is in ink from then.

**¶20** [Half, and why] What goes to the building above the bottom comes down to one choice: how much of the shares to move in 2031. We chose half, and not because half is optimal. The split decides how Laura's money divides between what is promised and what stays hers, not how much there is: each extra $5,000 of top costs about $5,300 of what she keeps, at the median. Keep only 30% back, and she could state a top about $5,000 higher, but what she kept would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half of simulated futures, against about 93 in 100 with half kept. Half sits in the middle of a nearly one-for-one trade, and we chose the middle because what she keeps has real work to do.

**¶21** [Promised to no one, and what could test it] In mid-December 2032 IBTM pays out; the top goes to the building, and anything above it is hers. So is the half of the shares that stays in VT, promised to no one. We call that half her flexibility, and it stays in pencil for good: she owns the shares, but no Treasury pays their value. If shares earn the 7.5% assumed, it is worth about $21,200 at the start of 2033, or about $28,700 counting what IBTM pays above the top. In 1 simulated future in 20 it is about $9,600 or less. It has three jobs: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, and a co-sponsor falling short.

**¶22** The building is paid for in Taiwan dollars, and Laura's money is in US dollars. If the Taiwan dollar strengthened 10% between the 2031 meetings and the contribution, the same building would cost about $11,500 more on her $115,000. Her flexibility alone covers that in about 93 of 100 simulated futures, and it would have covered the Taiwan dollar's actual two-year moves since 1983 in about 98 of 100.

**¶23** The harder test is building costs and the currency moving together. In four separate episodes since 1991 they made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years: about $32,200 more on her $115,000, more than all her flexibility. Past that point the residency's other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the rest, along with a plain rule: no building before the money is in hand.

**¶24** Then there is the risk the whole plan was built around, Laura's own career. A recession could make the 2028 deposit smaller, late or absent. At today's rates it could arrive about three-quarters smaller and all ten payments would still be met, even with cash waiting earning nothing. With no deposit at all and cash earning nothing, the ten would fall $57,455 short, and nothing would buy the floor or the shares. A missed deposit is one of only two reasons we would change the plan; the other is a Treasury payment more than five business days late.

**¶25** Last, the funds themselves. IBTM carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, and a fund pays what its Treasuries are worth, not a fixed sum. With cash earning nothing it could pay less than the top if its costs beyond its fee took more than the roughly 2% of room that the 0.5% margin and the rounding down leave, if it ended early or merged during 2031 (up to about $4,600 short, and nothing at today's market rates), or if the broker holding it failed, where the risk above the government-backed protection is a delay. The five iBonds Treasury funds that have ended so far all paid out in their final December. These are what we watch as 2031 approaches, and not one of them is a share price.

**¶26** Back, then, to the $450,000. Laura puts it in and, on today's figures, commits about $615,000, and at no point does she say a dollar of that before Treasuries held for her will pay it. The first deposit inks what cannot be late. The second finishes that job, inks the bottom and buys the shares. The move in January 2031 inks the top before she says it. What is left in pencil – her flexibility, her income and every forecast above – is hers to rub out, and rubbing it out costs nobody else a dollar.
