# Reader pack 6 (voice round 13, Sun 22 Nov 2026)

One long piece drafted for a competition team's Final Report about a client's money. Read it as a section of a report you had been handed.

**Your role.** You read as the client in the case: a writer and illustrator with a business degree, reading about your own money and your residency, attentive but not a finance professional.

**The case, in brief (what you know before you read).** Laura Gao, a writer and illustrator with a business degree, is planning a creative residency for artists in Taiwan, opening in 2033. She puts $300,000 into an investment portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028; that second sum comes from her book advances, speaking fees and licensing. Nothing else goes in or out before 2033. From the portfolio she must pay $50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042 (ten payments) to run the residency, and they must be met "with a high degree of certainty". At the start of 2033 she sets money aside for those payments (an "operating reserve"), and beyond that she wants to give as much as is responsible towards the residency's building (her "facility contribution") while keeping flexibility as the project develops. In 2031 she tells co-sponsors (other funders of the building) a range in dollars for that contribution, and how confident she is. Overpromising would hurt her credibility. A team of school students advises her; it may use US Treasury bonds, Treasury funds, shares and share funds.

## How to read

You are an independent reader, not the writer's colleague. Do not open any other file, look anything up, or try to find out who wrote it. Use only the page and the case above.

1. **Read the piece once, straight through, at a normal pace, in order.** As you go, keep a one-word mark for each numbered paragraph: **with it** (following and wanting to go on), **drifting** (following but no longer sure why you are being told this), or **lost** (lost the thread of the argument). Do not go back while reading.
2. **Then, before you look back at the piece**, answer from memory, in your own words, without quoting:
   - **A.** The plan's argument in three sentences.
   - **B.** The three things you remember most, in order of how strongly.
   - **C.** Any moment where something said earlier paid off later ("so that is why ..."): give the two paragraph numbers if you can, and what clicked.
   - **D.** Any question the piece made you ask that it never answered.
   - **E.** Any sentence that announced or promised a point instead of making it.
3. **Now look back** and fill one table row per paragraph: ¶ | Mark (from step 1) | Follow (1 = understood first time; 2 = needed a second read or had to hold something in mind; 3 = had to rebuild the reasoning or still unsure) | Why (Y = for every step or claim in it you could tell a friend why, from the page alone; N = at least one step or claim has no reason on the page) | Note (one line: for drifting, lost, 2-3 or N, what happened).
4. **Last, two lines:** where in the piece you would most likely have stopped reading if you were busy, and why; and the one change that would most help a reader like you.

Write your answers as Markdown to the file named in your instructions, in this order: `## Marks while reading` (¶1: mark, ...), `## From memory` (A-E), `## Paragraph by paragraph` (the table), `## Last`.

## The piece

**¶1** Every promise in Laura's plan is made years before it is paid. Co-sponsors hear a figure in 2031 and the ten payments run from 2033 to 2042, yet all the money arrives in two deposits, in 2027 and 2028. A promise like that breaks in the gap between the day it is made and the day the money for it exists. So the plan keeps one habit throughout: Laura buys first and states second.

**¶2** We didn't start there. Our first plan held mostly shares, and would have bought the Treasuries for all ten payments on 1 January 2033 with whatever the money had become. On 27 September 2026, before any trade, we dropped it, because of how a writer is paid. Advances, speaking fees and licences arrive in good years; in a recession they come later and smaller, and in the same downturn share prices can fall and co-sponsors can cut their budgets. Laura's career is already, in effect, a large position in shares that she has no way of selling. A portfolio that leaned on shares as well would add to that risk instead of offsetting it.

**¶3** Laura is an illustrator, and her own trade has the right picture for what replaced it. A comic page is drawn in pencil first, which can be rubbed out, and then inked; only the ink is printed. We use the same two words for money. A dollar figure is in ink once Treasuries held for Laura will pay it on its date, even if the cash they pay out along the way earns nothing more, and after a small margin for costs. Until then it is in pencil: her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the value of her shares, and every forecast. Laura states a figure only once it is in ink. The plan acts on three dates, January 2027, January 2028 and January 2031, and each inks something. Every figure below is at Treasury rates of 6 November 2026.

**¶4** [January 2027: the ten payments] Six years before the first payment is due, the first deposit of $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one for each payment, each paying out before the 1 January it pays for. A Treasury is a bond issued and guaranteed by the US government; held to the end, it pays exactly its stated interest and its face value, on fixed dates. Its price moves in between, which matters only to someone who sells, and we don't intend to.

**¶5** The payments go first because they are the one part of the case that can't bend: fixed in US dollars, fixed in date and never raised for inflation. Fixed dollars on fixed dates is exactly what a Treasury pays, and buying in 2027 settles what they cost instead of leaving it to six years of interest rates.

**¶6** The payments of 2038 to 2042 rest on five Treasury bonds, and those of 2033 to 2037 on Treasury funds, because for most of those years no Treasury bond open to us matured at the right time. Each of those funds is an iShares iBonds fund that holds only Treasuries maturing in one calendar year, the year in its name. It closes around 15 December of that year and pays its holders what its Treasuries are then worth. The catch is that this payout isn't a fixed sum: the fund charges a small fee and can bear costs the fee doesn't cover, such as trading.

**¶7** At 6 November rates the ten cost about $302,300, a little more than the whole deposit. We buy the longest-dated payments first, so the small piece left unbought is part of the 2033 payment, and the 2028 deposit completes it a year later for $2,388.

**¶8** So January 2027 inks most of every payment, not all. Two parts stay in pencil for a year: the $2,388 still to buy, and the interest.

**¶9** [The interest, and what certainty costs] A Treasury pays interest along the way, and some pay out early: the bond behind the 2038 payment matures in February 2037. The ten reach $50,000 each only if all of that money is reinvested until its payment is due, at the rates implied by today's Treasury prices. Those rates are not a forecast, and nothing guarantees them. If the money waiting for its payment earned nothing at all, the ten as bought in 2027 would pay about $445,400 of the $500,000. Replaying US interest rates since 1981, they would have come up short in 80 of every 100 starting months.

**¶10** So after the $2,388, the 2028 deposit's next call is $36,774 of extra Treasuries: more of the same bonds and funds that pay the later payments. They are sized so that every payment is met even if waiting money earns nothing, and even if every fund pays out 0.5% less than its Treasuries are worth. That 0.5% is the margin for the funds' costs beyond their fee; the five iBonds Treasury funds that have already ended showed about 0.27% in all. From January 2028, with the deposit's first $39,162 spent, every payment is in ink. From 2033 these Treasuries, the ten and the extras, are the operating reserve the case asks us to size.

**¶11** What does that certainty cost? The price is the $36,774 of extra Treasuries, money that could have gone towards the building, and it may never be spent. If rates turn out as today's prices imply, the payments never touch the extra Treasuries, and about $46,600 comes back to Laura by 2042, valued at the start of 2033. Publishers do something similar with an author's royalties: they hold part back against returns, the unsold books that shops send back, and pay it out once that risk has passed. The extra Treasuries work the same way, against a risk to the payments rather than to sales.

**¶12** The truer measure is against the plan we dropped, which missed at least one payment in about 1 run in 31 in a model we ran on 27 September. It needed no crash to miss, only several poor years for shares before 2033, found on a date when no more money was coming. Ours, once the 2028 deposit is in, meets every payment even if waiting money earns nothing. Going from 1 in 31 to none costs whatever growth the Treasury money might have had in shares, and for payments that can't bend we think that is worth it.

**¶13** [January 2028: the floor and the shares] The rest of the deposit buys two things, in a set order.

**¶14** First, $81,244, a little over half the deposit, buys more of IBTM, the iBonds fund that holds only Treasuries maturing during 2032 and closes in mid-December that year, just before the residency opens. We call it the floor, because what it pays sets the lowest figure Laura can state. At today's rates the floor pays about $102,000 in December 2032. The bottom of her range comes from it in three cautious steps: count as if the interest it pays out before then earns nothing more, take off the 0.5% margin, and round down to a whole number of $5,000s. That gives $95,000. It is worked out once, when the floor is bought, and never measured again: the bottom is in ink three years before anyone hears it.

**¶15** Second, the remaining $29,594 buys VT, Vanguard's Total World Stock ETF: one fund holding shares in thousands of companies across the world. It is the only money in the plan that no promise depends on.

**¶16** Nothing comes in after this deposit, so every promise has to be paid for out of it: the payments first because they are fixed, the floor next so the bottom is held long before anyone hears it, and shares last because nothing rests on them, which also gives them three years to grow before 2031.

**¶17** [January 2031: the figure she states] Before the 2031 meetings, half the value of Laura's shares is sold and the money buys more IBTM. The top of her range is then worked out exactly as the bottom was, on both purchases of IBTM together: count as if the interest earns nothing more, take off 0.5%, and round down once. Our model simulates 200,000 futures for interest rates and share prices; in its middle case shares grow 7.5% a year (J.P. Morgan's assumption, above the middle of published forecasts), and the top is $115,000. At a more cautious 5% a year it would still be $115,000.

**¶18** The sale comes before the meetings so that the Treasuries behind any figure she names are already held for her when she names it. Naming one sooner would be a gamble: in the simulated futures, a top named in January 2030 would be more than the move could buy in about 11 of every 100. Ink can be added to a page but not cleanly taken off: a mistake gets painted over, and the original art keeps the record. Co-sponsors would keep the record of a figure Laura later had to cut, which is the damage to her credibility the case warns about.

**¶19** The margin and the rounding together leave the top about 2% below what IBTM will pay, even if waiting money earns nothing (2% in all, not 2% a year). Printers run the art past the line where the page will be cut, because the blade wanders, and keep the words well inside it. Here the $115,000 is the words, and the 2% is the room left for the blade.

**¶20** Seen from today, the figure is uncertain: the top she can state reaches at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures, and at least $115,000 in about 57. Share prices up to January 2031 decide where it lands, and nothing after that does. Once the shares have moved, both ends are in ink, and we are as confident in the stated range as in the Treasuries that will pay it.

**¶21** Why a range at all, then? The case asks for one, and this one carries information a single number would hide: the bottom has been held since January 2028, and the gap above it is what three years of shares added. So we recommend that Laura give co-sponsors one figure to plan around, the top, and say where it came from: "$115,000, all of it held in Treasury funds before I say it, $95,000 of it since January 2028." She gives it in US dollars, the currency her Treasuries pay, with its Taiwan-dollar value on a named day, and puts it in a signed pledge letter with the amount, purpose and date. The holdings show the money exists; the letter says it will be given.

**¶22** [Promised to no one, and what could test it] The other half of the shares stays in VT, promised to no one. This is Laura's flexibility, and it stays in pencil for good, on purpose. In the central case it is worth about $21,200 at the start of 2033, and anything IBTM pays above the top is hers as well; in 1 simulated future in 20 her flexibility is $9,600 or less. It is there for what can go wrong after she has spoken: the building costing more, the Taiwan dollar strengthening, a co-sponsor giving less.

**¶23** Keeping half back is a choice, and the model prices it. Each extra $5,000 of stated top would cost about $5,300 of flexibility at the median, while what she gives and keeps together moves by about $1,100 or less, whichever split she picks. The split decides how her money divides between what is promised and what stays hers, not how much there is.

**¶24** The building is paid for in Taiwan dollars, and her money is in US dollars. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger between the meetings and the payment would need about $11,500 more on her $115,000, and her flexibility alone covers that in about 93 of 100 simulated futures. Costs and currency together are the harder test: four times since 1991 they made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years, about $32,200 on her $115,000, more than all her flexibility. Past that point the residency's other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the rest, and nothing should be built before its money is in hand.

**¶25** Shares could fall before 2031, which lowers the top she can state and nothing else. If they fell to nothing, the range would shrink to one figure, the $95,000 bottom, with every payment untouched; after the move, a fall shrinks only her flexibility.

**¶26** Her career could do exactly what we designed around. At today's rates the 2028 deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller and every payment would still be met, even with waiting money earning nothing. If it never came, the payments would fall short on that same cautious count, and nothing would buy the floor or the shares. A missed deposit is one of the two reasons our written policy for Laura gives for changing the plan; the other is a Treasury payment more than five business days late, which would mean a US default.

**¶27** IBTM is the last thing to watch, because this one fund carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top. If waiting money earned nothing, three things could come between it and the top: costs beyond its fee taking more than about 2% of what it pays; the fund ending early, merging or moving its end during 2031, which could leave the top up to about $4,600 short; or her broker failing, where government-backed protection tops up only to $500,000 and the risk above that is delay. At today's market rates an early end leaves nothing short, and the five that have ended all paid out in their final December.

**¶28** By January 2031, then, every figure Laura states is in ink: the ten payments since January 2028, $95,000 for the building since the same month, and the rest of the $115,000 since the shares moved. What remains in pencil is hers, and it is meant to stay that way. Co-sponsors, like her readers, will only ever see the ink, while the pencil underneath stays hers to rub out and redraw as the building takes shape.
