# The three texts the readers read


## Text 1

Laura's whole facility contribution comes from her second deposit, the $150,000 arriving in January 2028. Nothing comes in after it, so we spend it all that month, in order of who relies on it.

The 10 operating payments go first, because they're fixed. In January 2027 we buy each one its own US Treasury bond or Treasury fund, paying out before it falls due; about $39,000 of the 2028 deposit finishes that job and adds extra Treasuries, so all 10 are met even if the interest paid out along the way earns nothing more. Only then does a dollar go to the building.

Next we buy the floor: $81,244 of IBTM, an iShares fund that holds Treasuries maturing during 2032, closes in mid-December 2032 and pays out what its Treasuries are then worth. We give it most of what's left – about 73 in every 100, the same split as our competition portfolio – because the bottom is the part of her range no market can touch once it's bought. At today's rates the floor pays about $102,000. A fund's payout isn't fixed, though, so we count it cautiously: as if none of its interest after January 2028 earns anything more, less 0.5% for costs beyond its fee, rounded down to a whole $5,000. That makes a bottom of $95,000. It looks stingy, and it's meant to – a figure someone builds on should never be more than the fund will pay.

The last $29,594 buys VT, one fund of shares in thousands of companies worldwide. We put shares last because no promise depends on them, which is exactly why they can be shares at all: they're expected to grow faster than Treasuries, and they can fall. Over the three years to 2031, they decide how far above $95,000 the top reaches.

In January 2031, before her first meeting with co-sponsors, we sell half the value of her shares and buy more IBTM. Counted the same cautious way on both purchases, the top is $115,000 on today's figures. Laura is an illustrator, so she knows the rule of a comic page: pencil first, which can be rubbed out, and only the ink is printed. We hold her figures to it: one is in ink once Treasuries held for her will pay it, and she states nothing still in pencil – which is why we move the shares before the meetings.

The other half stays in VT, promised to no one. It's her flexibility, for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar (the building's bills come in Taiwan dollars) or a co-sponsor falling short. Half is our choice, not the answer to a formula, and we modelled what it trades. Keep only 30% back and she could state about $5,000 more, but her flexibility alone would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in about half our simulated futures, not about 93 in 100. The split decides how her money divides between what is promised and what stays hers; it hardly changes how much there is.

From today, $95,000 to $115,000 is a forecast. Until January 2028 even the bottom moves with Treasury yields, and the top with share prices until January 2031. In our 200,000 simulated futures for rates and shares, with world shares growing 7.5% a year in the middle case (J.P. Morgan's assumption, above the middle of published forecasts), she can state a top of at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100, and at least $115,000 in about 57. If VT were worth nothing by 2031, the range would shrink to $95,000 and the 10 payments wouldn't move at all. That's why we'd have her name nothing before January 2031: a top named a year sooner would be more than the move could buy in about 11 of 100 futures.

Once we've moved the shares, no market can change the figure she states, because the Treasuries that will pay it are already held for her. The 0.5% and the rounding leave the top about 2% below what IBTM will pay, even if its waiting interest earns nothing; what's left to watch is the fund itself – costs above that 2%, or an early end, which at worst leaves the top about $4,600 short, covered by her flexibility. So we'd have her give co-sponsors one figure to plan around, naming the bottom once, as the part no market could move after 2028: "$115,000, all of it held in Treasury funds before I say it, $95,000 of it since January 2028."


## Text 2

In a recession, Laura's advances and fees can come later and smaller, share prices can fall and co-sponsors can cut their budgets, all in the same downturn. So we asked how much of her facility contribution she could promise in 2031 that no such year could take back.

It is paid from her second deposit. The first, $300,000 in January 2027, buys ten Treasuries, each maturing just before one of the $50,000 payments. Nothing comes in after the $150,000 of January 2028, so every promise she makes is paid for from it, and we spend it in order of what can least afford to be wrong.

The payments come first, because they're fixed. About $39,000 (on today's figures: Treasury prices at the close on 6 November 2026) finishes the ten and buys extra Treasuries, so that all ten are met even if the interest they pay along the way earns nothing while it waits. From January 2028, no fall in interest rates can send the building's money to the payments.

The floor comes second. We put $81,244 into IBTM, a fund holding Treasuries that mature during 2032; it closes in mid-December that year and pays out what they're worth. On today's figures this purchase pays about $102,000. We count it the way a co-sponsor about to plan a building would want it counted: as if none of the interest it pays out earns anything more, less 0.5% for running costs beyond the fund's fee, rounded down to a whole $5,000. That leaves $95,000, the bottom of her range, worked out once, in January 2028, three years before she says it. The amount follows our IPS, which sent "most" of the deposit here.

The shares come last. The remaining $29,594 buys VT, a fund of thousands of companies worldwide. No promise rests on it, so it's the one place we let markets decide: shares should earn more than Treasuries, and how they do sets how far above the bottom her top reaches. In January 2031, before the first meeting, we'll sell half and buy more IBTM, so that whatever top Laura names is paid by Treasuries already held for her. The other half stays in VT, promised to no one: her flexibility, for a building that costs more than planned, a Taiwan dollar that strengthens, or a co-sponsor who falls short. Half was our choice, not a model's answer. Keep only 30% back and she could state about $5,000 more, but her flexibility would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half of simulated futures, against 93 in 100.

We count the top the same cautious way, on both purchases of IBTM together: $115,000 on today's figures. So in 2031 she states $95,000 to $115,000, and gives co-sponsors the top to plan around. Seen from today it is uncertain: our model runs 200,000 futures, with world shares growing 7.5% a year in the middle case (J.P. Morgan's assumption) and a yearly spread of about 18%. The top comes to at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 futures, and to at least $115,000 in about 57. Once the shares are moved, nothing about it is uncertain: the Treasuries that will pay it are already held, and she can say so in one breath: "$115,000, all of it held in Treasury funds before I say it, $95,000 of it since January 2028."

Each end of the range moves only until we buy it. If shares halve before January 2031, the half we move is worth half as much, and the part of her top above the bottom roughly halves with it; if they fell to nothing, her range would be the bottom alone. After the move, a fall in shares shrinks only her flexibility. Lower yields before January 2027 would make the payments dearer and the floor smaller (1 point lower gives a bottom of $85,000), but once we've bought the floor, rates at 1%, or at zero, leave the bottom standing while the fund runs to its end, because it was counted as if interest earned nothing. A dearer building is met first from her flexibility, about $21,200 at the start of 2033 if shares earn the 7.5% assumed; a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar would take about $11,500 of it. Four times since 1991, building costs and the Taiwan dollar together made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years, about $32,200 on her $115,000 and more than all her flexibility. A gap that size falls to the other funders, a smaller design or a later start, and those work only before building begins, which is why the building waits for the money.


## Text 3

The building is a different matter. Most of the 2028 deposit will fund a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, whose payout is the bare minimum you’ll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt. The rest will go into a world share fund of thousands of companies – VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF – and its returns will help determine your maximum facility contribution. That means markets will be able to increase or decrease the top of your planned band as we get closer to 2031, but the bottom will remain constant, built on the ever-reliable Treasuries. Importantly for you, half of the value of the shares will be promised to no one. That is your flexibility – for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short. Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries, so whatever figure you choose is money you already own.

From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve. There are only 2 possible reasons we would change our plan – a missed deposit or a Treasury default. A fall in the market is not one of them.
