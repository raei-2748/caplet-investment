**Question.** Caplet's FINAL IPS and Keel's last draft (V62, `/mnt/project-files/ips/FINAL/ips_V62.txt`, 6 Nov 07:30)
carry mostly the same plan. Caplet called Keel's drafts incomprehensible and wrote its own. Idea by idea, what did the
human writers do that Keel did not? Read by ear, sentence against sentence. No counting.

## Six ideas, side by side

**A. The central promise**
- Keel: "Our dose is Treasuries: before anyone relies on a sum Laura commits to, she owns the ones that will repay it."
- Caplet: "You asked that the payments be met with a 'high degree of certainty', so we decided to take that one step
  further – barring a US default, none of your 10 operating payments will depend on a share price, or on anybody
  else's money, when they fall due. The money for the operating commitment will go straight into Treasuries, because
  it's fixed and non-negotiable, and only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares, where it can grow."
- What differs: Caplet starts from what Laura asked for, in her words, and says the promise follows from it ("so").
  The reason is a "because" clause, out loud. The contrast ("only money you haven't promised yet") comes with its own
  reason ("where it can grow"). Keel's line is a rule with no reason, and "the ones" makes the reader go back and work
  out what it points to (Treasuries? sums?).

**B. Why not a share-heavy plan**
- Keel: "At current Treasury prices her ten operating payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and they are
  fixed, so in a balanced mix every gain or loss would fall on the last third. That third would swing like the shares
  of a heavily borrowed company."
- Caplet: "You'd think that someone with your taste for thoughtful risk and 6 years to wait would usually own shares.
  So, naturally, that was our plan – until we stopped and considered the actual nature of a writer's income. In good
  years the advances come, and the speaking fees, and the licences. In a recession they come later and smaller, and
  during that same period, shares fall and co-sponsors cut their budgets. In effect your career is a large holding of
  shares, and one you cannot sell."
- What differs: Caplet concedes first ("You'd think"), tells the story of changing its mind, and walks the causal
  chain one step per sentence with time markers ("In good years", "In a recession", "during that same period"). Only
  then does the abstraction arrive ("In effect ..."). The nouns are Laura's (advances, speaking fees, licences). Keel's
  argument is sound but skips its middle step: the reader must already know that fixed claims gear up the leftover
  money to see why "a heavily borrowed company" follows. That is writing for someone who already knows what we know.

**C. The ladder**
- Keel: "In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of ten Treasury holdings, one repaying before each January from
  2033 to 2042, and the 2028 deposit buys any it can't afford."
- Caplet: "In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit to buy 10 Treasuries (T-Bonds or iShares iBonds Term Treasury
  ETFs, whichever works best for each year), each maturing just before it's needed. Held to the end, these pay what
  they say – no price fluctuations for us to fear. At current yields, this would cost roughly $300,000 – in other
  words, your entire first deposit. But worry not – our plan isn't dependent on yields staying put! Even if yields
  fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up being above $300,000, we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit."
- What differs: in Caplet's version people act ("we'll use your first deposit"); in Keel's, money acts ("her $300,000
  buys"). Caplet names the instruments. One property per sentence. It restates on purpose ("in other words, your
  entire first deposit") and answers the worry it has just raised ("But worry not"). Keel packs the same content into
  one sentence whose last clause has an "it" with two possible owners.

**D. Floor and ceiling**
- Keel: "In 2028, what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, enough to return about
  all of it by December 2032, which sets the bottom of her range. Because it costs less than it returns, the
  difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund, so the shares risk only that interest."
- Caplet: "The building is a different matter. Most of the 2028 deposit will fund a Treasury fund maturing in late
  2032, whose payout is the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt. The rest will go
  into a world share fund of thousands of companies – VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF – and its returns will help
  determine your maximum facility contribution. That means markets will be able to increase or decrease the top of
  your planned band as we get closer to 2031, but the bottom will remain constant, built on the ever-reliable
  Treasuries."
- What differs: a five-word sentence announces the change of subject. Each sentence does one job, and "That means"
  hands the reader the consequence. Keel's version has the cleverer idea (the shares are bought with roughly the
  floor's interest, so they risk only the interest) and Caplet dropped it. The idea was not wrong; it could not be said
  plainly in the space Keel gave it, so it read as noise. Lesson for the report: the report has room for that idea,
  in four or five sentences, not one.

**E. Flexibility**
- Keel: "Whatever of the first half isn't sold, and anything paid beyond the top, stays hers, liquid and promised to
  no one, so she keeps room as the project develops."
- Caplet: "Importantly for you, half of the value of the shares will be promised to no one. That is your flexibility
  – for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short."
- What differs: Caplet describes the thing, then names it ("That is your flexibility"), then says what it is for, with
  three concrete examples. Keel's sentence asks the reader to remember a "first half" defined two paragraphs earlier.

**F. The ending**
- Keel: "Like the caplet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it, and the tenth rung, with what
  was set aside for it, meets the last $50,000 in 2042."
- Caplet: "You take risks on the page. Fair enough; that's your job. With these promises, ours is to take none."
- What differs: the ending turns on Laura's own life (a writer takes risks on the page), not on an invented motif. It
  is three short sentences with a conversational hinge ("Fair enough"). The wit is in the idea, not the wording.

## What the humans did, in general terms (to be tested in study 2)
1. **People are the subjects.** "We'll use", "you asked", "we'd change". Keel made money and holdings the subjects
   ("her $300,000 buys", "what the ladder doesn't need buys").
2. **Reasons are said out loud,** in "because", "so", "that means". Keel tucked reasons into appositives and
   participles, or left them out.
3. **One new thing per sentence,** and the next sentence picks up where the last one ended.
4. **Changes of subject are announced** ("The building is a different matter.").
5. **Concession, then the turn** ("You'd think ... until we stopped and considered").
6. **The reader's worry is named and answered** ("But worry not").
7. **Concrete things from the client's world** (advances, speaking fees, licences; overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar).
8. **Things are called what they are** (VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF; iShares iBonds). No coined labels.
9. **Some repetition is welcome** ("in other words, your entire first deposit").
10. **A hard idea is cut, not squeezed.**
11. **Warmth and a little cheek** ("So we hedge it!", "Fair enough; that's your job").

## The comprehension problem: a first diagnosis
Every Keel sentence above that a reader would stumble on has the same fault in it: a pointer the reader can't resolve
on one pass ("the ones", "any it can't afford", "all of it", "that half", "the first half", "after this"). Each pointer
saves a few words by referring back to something said earlier, often clauses or paragraphs earlier. That is
compression, so Ray's suspicion is right in part. But the deeper faults are two others: steps of reasoning left out
because Keel already knew them (B), and money rather than people doing the acting (C, D). Study 2 (Thu 19 Nov) tests
these four suspects (compression, unresolvable pointers, missing steps, abstract subjects) on the sentences Caplet
flagged, and records which fix cures which sentence.

## Rules for the report that follow from this study
- Every sentence has a person or a named thing as its subject, doing a verb.
- No "it", "this", "that", "the rest" or "the ones" whose owner is more than one sentence back. Repeat the noun.
- Every claim that follows from another says so ("so", "because", "that means").
- One mechanism per sentence. A mechanism that needs four sentences gets four.
- Announce each change of subject.
- Use Laura's world for examples, and the case's words for the parts of the plan.
