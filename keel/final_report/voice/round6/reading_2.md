# Voice round 6, blind reader 2

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly and aloud in my head. Here is what I hear in it: a letter. The writers talk
to one person, explain each step as they take it, and let a sentence run on with a dash when the thought keeps going.
They allow themselves some cheek ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not", "Fair enough; that's your job"). Their figures of
speech are quick and are never explained ("a giant stock she can't sell"). Their grammar is a little loose in places, in
the way a confident student's is. They like "simply", "in effect" and "naturally", and they turn a thought with a dash
rather than a colon.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's voice in note form. It makes a confident claim, says "in other words", and then goes one step
  further than the client asked, the same move as "we decided to take that one step further". The commas are a student's
  ("remove uncertainty, through duration-matching"; "Notably – Laura's payments"), and there is a flash of attitude in
  "the restless markets". It sounds like a bright teenager who has just learned what duration-matching is and is pleased
  about it.
- **Meaning:** The Treasury bonds won't grow Laura's money, but because they are matched to her fixed dollar payments
  they take away the uncertainty, so inflation-linked bonds (TIPS) aren't needed.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The first paragraph talks like a person explaining something odd to a friend ("which makes it an odd sort of
  range. Most ranges are a best guess ... but hers isn't"), and that is close to the IPS's habit of stopping to say what
  something really means. The second paragraph then piles four adjustments into one long sentence, and the fragment
  "Never the range, never a payment." sounds more styled than the rest. On the whole the passage sounds like the writers
  on a day when they had too many numbers to carry.
- **Meaning:** Laura leads with $115,000, the figure co-sponsors count. The $95,000 bottom has been owned since January
  2028 and the rest since the 2031 move, so the stated range is certain. Each end is worked out cautiously, and after the
  move only her flexibility can change.
- **Check question (the 2031 range):** A co-sponsor should count the top, $115,000, because that is the facility
  contribution she actually gives. She states a range anyway because the two ends record two moments: the $95,000 she
  has owned since January 2028, and what the 2031 move into Treasuries added. The gap is history, not a margin for error.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the IPS's offhand practicality ("we'll simply ignore price noise in the interim") and its "The catch
  is," turn, and it opens with a slightly ungrammatical compression ("WInS lists no Treasuries maturing") of the kind a
  student writes while thinking fast. The sentence that opens with a list of adjectives ("Defined-maturity,
  investment-grade, liquid, cheap ...") sounds like a teenager showing what they've read, and that matches the IPS's
  bracketed "(T-Bonds or iShares iBonds Term Treasury ETFs, whichever works best for each year)".
- **Meaning:** In years where WInS had no Treasury bond, the team bought iBond Treasury funds, which behave like bonds held
  to maturity. They pay out a fund value rather than a promised par amount, and in return they can be sold more easily.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The passage moves like spoken explanation. "we'd rather not answer with a percentage", "Where we did have to
  be careful was the funds", "never earns another cent of interest", "Before the move it's a different story" are all
  phrases a student says out loud to a teacher, and they suit the IPS's warmth. Two things hold me back. The coinage "two
  inkings with two dates" made me stop, and the doubled "takes off ..., takes off another ..." in the cost sentence reads
  like someone working through a list rather than writing.
- **Meaning:** Laura takes $115,000 into the 2031 meetings. The bottom ($95,000) became hers in January 2028 and the rest
  of the top before the meetings, so the range is certain once both are owned. Each end is cautious about fund costs.
  Before the move a share crash could still cut the top, which is why she waits.
- **Check question (the 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is her facility contribution and the figure
  co-sponsors count. The range exists because its two ends were bought at different times, January 2028 and the 2031
  move, so the gap records when each part became hers, not how unsure anyone is.
- **Image:** It helped a little. "Inked in January 2028" fixed the bottom's date in my mind, but "two inkings" as a noun
  got in the way for a second.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is tidy, clipped and impersonal. It uses short declaratives, a colon, a semicolon, and a polished closing
  turn ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). It doesn't stop to explain the way the IPS does, and it has
  none of the IPS's asides or cheek. It sounds like a careful adult editor summarising the plan, not like six students
  writing to their client.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the owned floor up to a top that includes half the shares moved into Treasuries.
  Any shortfall in the ladder from reinvesting interest at low rates is covered first from Laura's kept shares, and if
  those can't cover it the whole range is lowered.
- **Check question (the 2031 range):** The passage doesn't say which figure a co-sponsor should count. It says the bottom
  is "money she already owns", which made me think the bottom was the safe figure to count. It also doesn't explain why
  there is a range if both ends are owned. Its last line ("the whole range comes down") suggests the range can still
  move, so I came away less sure, not more.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** The opening line ("VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong") has the same confident turn as the IPS's
  opening, and the long middle sentence sprawls ("which means we can avoid paying the price for the vain illusion ...,
  instead buying ...") the way a keen student's sentence does when it has three points to make at once. "(as per
  JPMorgan)" is a schoolroom citation, and ending on a question is the IPS's way of handing the reader the point.
- **Meaning:** VT is the only holding that no payment depends on, so it is where the team takes risk: a cheap, widely
  diversified world share fund with a forecast of about 7.5% a year, which decides how generous the facility contribution
  can be.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Some of it sounds like the writers: the defensive opener ("that's the plan working, not a lack of nerve"), the
  rhetorical "Why 0.25?" and the plain account of the mis-click. But the key-plate comparison forces a long explanation
  between dashes in the middle of a sentence. The fragment "overpaying from the start, or buying something no one trades"
  is clipped to the point of being unclear. "The US open fell at 00:30 ... and Eric fell asleep" stumbles over its own
  "fell". It reads like their voice edited by someone in a hurry.
- **Meaning:** The book is 89.55% Treasuries by design. It met the targets set before trading (ten Treasuries exactly on
  target, VT at 167 against 164, cash above $1,000, $200 in fees), with two rules added along the way: a yield check after
  two stale prices, and only Ray trading after a mis-click. It can't show the 2028 deposit.
- **Check question (buying it):** Before trading, the team set a target number of units for each of the ten Treasury
  holdings and for VT, and a cash floor of $1,000. All ten Treasuries landed exactly on target, VT came in at 167 units
  against 164, cash stayed above the floor, and fees were $200.
- **Image:** This uses a different printing image (the key plate on a press), not pencil and ink. It got in the way: I had
  to read the explanation between the dashes before I could get back to the point, which was simply "we bought the
  Treasuries first".

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Most of it is plain and personable ("Both ends are pitched low on purpose" is good). The sentence where it
  reaches for the image ("Rub out the pencils while the ink is still tacky and it smears") is where I stop hearing the
  IPS's writers: it is elaborate, it needs decoding, and their own figures of speech are quick and plain. The order also
  wanders. The answer on confidence comes last, after the risk, so the passage circles back on itself.
- **Meaning:** The top, $115,000, is what counts. Both ends are owned in Treasuries by the time Laura speaks, and both are
  set cautiously. Before the move a crash could cut the top, and naming it a year early would leave it unowned in about
  11 of 100 model futures, so she waits. After the move only her flexibility can change.
- **Check question (the 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is the money she gives and the number a co-sponsor
  books in its plans. The range only records which part she owned first (the $95,000 since January 2028) and which part
  the 2031 move added, so it is not a margin for error.
- **Image:** It got in the way. Rubbing out pencil while the ink is wet doesn't match the problem described, which is
  stating a figure (inking it) too early, so I had to work out the mapping and still wasn't sure I had it right.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is lively and spoken: "so it was hard to fudge later", "trouble either way round", "Then 7 October." It
  explains both ways a stale price could hurt, as the IPS explains both good and bad years of a writer's income, and
  "it's the right one to miss" has the IPS's confident judgement. It sounds like a student telling the story of the
  trading weeks to a teacher, with the lesson attached.
- **Meaning:** The team set its targets before trading, added a 0.25-point yield-check rule after two stale WInS prices
  and a one-trader rule after a mis-click, and hit every Treasury target exactly. VT, bought last, was the one miss, and
  the book can't show the 2028 deposit.
- **Check question (buying it):** The team set a target number of units for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT bought
  last with leftover cash, and cash never below $1,000. All ten Treasuries landed exactly on target, every bond sat inside
  the yield band, cash stayed above $1,000, fees were $200, and VT ended at 167 units instead of 164.
- **Image:** It helped. "A click is ink" is one quick clause and makes the point that an order can't be undone. It
  stretches the report's definition of ink (a figure owned in Treasuries) to mean simply "permanent", but it reads
  naturally.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves cleanly from what the case asks, to the answer, to how cautious the figures are, to confidence, to
  timing, and it sounds like the IPS's writers throughout. "isn't us hedging our bets" plays on the IPS's own "So we hedge
  it!", "almost stubbornly" has their touch of self-aware attitude, "there's nothing left for markets to decide" echoes
  "immune to markets", and "simply" is their word. The only heavy patch is the costs sentence, which is a lot of numbers
  for one breath.
- **Meaning:** The top, $115,000, is what a co-sponsor counts. The $95,000 bottom has been owned since 2028 and the rest
  since the 2031 move, so the range is certain and set cautiously. Only her flexibility can change after the move, and
  because a top named early could still go unowned, Laura waits until the move is done.
- **Check question (the 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is the facility contribution she hands over and what
  a co-sponsor plans around. She states a range because the gap shows what she owned first ($95,000, since January 2028)
  and what the 2031 move added; it isn't the team hedging its bets.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This sounds most like the IPS of the four implementation passages. It asks itself a question and answers
  it ("did we buy what we said we'd buy? Yes, though not without two scares."), as the IPS does with "So, naturally, that
  was our plan – until ...". It talks to the reader ("That matters more than it sounds"), and its idioms are an
  Australian student's ("all up", "stops a stale one cold", "a market has no undo button"). "The catch is" at the end is
  the same turn as in the shorter notes.
- **Meaning:** The team wrote down the finished book before trading, met it (ten Treasuries exact, cash above $1,000,
  $200 fees, VT 167 against 164) despite two scares, stale prices and a mis-click, each of which produced a rule. The book
  can't show the 2028 deposit.
- **Check question (buying it):** An exact number of units for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT bought last with
  whatever cash was left, and never less than $1,000 in cash. All ten Treasuries hit their targets exactly, every bond sat
  inside the 0.25-point band, cash stayed above $1,000, fees were $200, and VT was the one holding off target at 167
  units against 164.
- **Image:** Not used ("no undo button" does the same job in plain words).

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is conversational and sure of itself ("It wasn't all smooth, mind you", "if it's more than 0.25 points out,
  don't"), and it explains VT's miss as something planned for, which is the IPS's way of turning a weakness into part of
  the design. "Mind you" sounds a shade older than the IPS's writers. The paired line "a fair price never trips it and a
  stale one can't slip through" is a little more crafted than their usual sentences.
- **Meaning:** The book did what the pre-trading list said: ten Treasuries exact, VT at 167 against 164 by design, cash
  above its floor, $200 in fees. Two rules came from stale prices and a mis-click, the book is 89.55% Treasuries on
  purpose, and it can't show the 2028 deposit.
- **Check question (buying it):** A number of units for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT last with leftover cash, and
  never less than $1,000 in cash. All ten Treasuries sit exactly at target, VT came in at 167 units against 164 (the gap
  they expected, since VT absorbs any slack), cash stayed above the floor, and fees were $200.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is a clean, textbook paragraph: two methods set side by side, then "We do the second", with no dashes, no
  asides and no warmth. It is clear and correct, but it sounds like a reference book or an adviser's summary, not like
  writers who open with "You'd think ..." and close with "ours is to take none".
- **Meaning:** Like a pension fund that buys one bond per payment and holds it, the team buys a ladder of Treasuries and
  Treasury funds in January 2027, each maturing just before a payment, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### What separates the highest from the lowest

The passages I ranked highest talk to someone. They explain one thought at a time, let a sentence run on with a dash,
ask and answer their own questions, and carry a little attitude and slightly loose grammar, as the IPS does ("worry not",
"Fair enough"). The middle group has the same voice but loads too many figures into single sentences, so the
explaining stops while the arithmetic happens. The lowest are either tidy and impersonal (13 and 5: clipped declaratives,
semicolons, polished closing lines) or reach for an extended figure of speech that has to be decoded (8's tacky ink,
7's key plate). The IPS's own figures of speech are short, plain and never explained. When the image appeared as one
quick clause it read as theirs (9's "a click is ink"); when it needed unpacking, it didn't.
