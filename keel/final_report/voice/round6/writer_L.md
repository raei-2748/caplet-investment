Notes, one line per sentence of IPS line 13:

Rebuilt from the notes alone:

In January 2027 your first deposit buys 10 Treasuries – T-Bonds, or iShares iBonds funds where a year needs one – each maturing just before the payment it covers. Hold them to the end and they pay what they say; the price can wobble in between and it won't matter to us. At today's yields that costs roughly $300,000, which is the whole first deposit. Don't worry, though – nothing here needs yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ten end up costing more than $300,000, the 2028 deposit simply finishes the job.

Where mine drifted: I made the deposit the subject ("your first deposit buys") where the team has "we'll use", so the people doing the work dropped out of the first sentence, and I explained the iBonds choice ("where a year needs one") instead of leaving the team's looser "whichever works best". I also turned "no price fluctuations for us to fear" into a tidier explanation, and lost a little of its swagger.

=== P1 ===
Before any order went in, we wrote down what the finished book had to look like: a number of units for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT last with whatever cash was left, and never less than $1,000 in cash. Judged against that list, it did what we said it would. All ten Treasury holdings sit at exactly their target units. VT came in at 167 units, not 164, and that's the one gap we'd planned for: it was bought last, so any slack in the bond prices was always going to land on it and nowhere else. Cash never went below its floor; fees came to $200 in all.

It wasn't all smooth, mind you. On 29-30 September two WInS bond prices were stale, about a full percentage point of yield off the real Treasury curve. Buy at a yield that's too low and you've overpaid on day one; too high, and it probably means a bond nobody's trading. So we made a rule – check every bond's yield against the Treasury curve before ordering, and if it's more than 0.25 points out, don't. That band is about seven times the normal day-to-day scatter around the curve, and still wider than the scatter at its 2008 peak, so a fair price never trips it and a stale one can't slip through.

Then 7 October. The US market opened at 00:30 Sydney time, and Eric fell asleep before it. That same day a click landed on the wrong row as the page scrolled, and the order was gone, with no step to confirm it. From then on only Ray placed or cancelled orders. All eleven filled on 7-8 October, every bond inside the band.

The book is 89.55% Treasuries, on purpose: money that has to be paid on a date is matched by the Treasury of that date. What it can't show is the 2028 deposit, since WInS gives us one $300,000 account – so it's Laura's whole plan scaled to $300,000 of its $450,000.

=== P3 ===
Wharton asks for a range Laura could give co-sponsors in 2031, and her confidence in it. On today's figures we'd have her lead with $115,000. That's her facility contribution – what she gives – and the figure co-sponsors count. Below it sits $95,000, the part she's held since January 2028, when the late-2032 Treasury fund was bought; the rest of the top has been held since half her shares moved into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. So when she speaks, both ends are owned in Treasuries, which makes it an odd sort of range. Most ranges are a best guess with room either side to be wrong, but hers isn't: the gap just records what she owned first and what the move added, and neither end is a margin for error.

That's also why her confidence doesn't come as a percentage. Once both ends are owned, the range she states is certain. What's left to doubt is the funds – a Treasury fund pays what its bonds are worth when it ends, not a promised sum – so we didn't take them at face value. Each end is worked out as if cash waiting inside the funds earned no more interest, after the funds' 0.07% yearly fee, less a further 0.5% for other fund costs, rounded down to the nearest $5,000. The five funds of this kind that have already ended showed about 0.27% of those other costs; the top is only missed if they pass about 3% of the funds' value, with cash earning nothing.

Once the move is done, only her flexibility can still change – the half of the shares promised to no one, about $21,300 in January 2033 in our model's central case. Never the range, never a payment. Before the move, the top isn't owned yet. In our model a 40% fall in world shares would drag it down to between about $105,000 and the bottom itself, while the bottom and every payment stay put. That's why Laura names nothing until the move is done.

=== P4 ===
On today's figures, the number Laura takes into the 2031 meetings is $115,000. It's her facility contribution – what she gives – and the figure co-sponsors count, so it's the one she leads with. Her range is two inkings with two dates: the bottom, $95,000, inked in January 2028 when the late-2032 Treasury fund was bought, and the rest of the top inked before the meetings when half her shares moved into Treasuries – so the space between its ends records when each part became hers, not how unsure anyone is.

Wharton also asks how confident she can be, and we'd rather not answer with a percentage. Once both ends are owned, the range she states is certain. Where we did have to be careful was the funds. A Treasury fund pays what its bonds are worth when it ends, not a promised amount, so each end assumes the cash waiting inside the funds never earns another cent of interest, takes off the funds' 0.07% yearly fee, takes off another 0.5% for other fund costs, and rounds down to the nearest $5,000. For scale, the five funds of this kind that have already ended showed about 0.27% of those other costs. The top is only missed if they run past about 3% of the funds' value, with cash earning nothing.

After the move, the one thing that can still shrink is her flexibility – the half of the shares promised to no one, about $21,300 in January 2033 in our model's central case. The range stays put, and so does every payment. Before the move it's a different story. In our model a 40% fall in world shares would pull the top down to somewhere between about $105,000 and the bottom itself, even with the bottom and every payment untouched – which is exactly why Laura doesn't name a figure until the move is done.

=== NOTES ===
P1 and P3 felt closest to the IPS and the Trading Note reflections: "we" doing things, a dash where the team would take a breath, and the stale-price reasoning borrowed from the team's own way of arguing it (too low a yield is overpaying, too high is a bond nobody trades). The two-inkings turn did a real job in P4: it carries the "record, not a margin of error" argument that P3 needed two sentences for, and the rest of P4 could stay plain because of it. The key-plate turn helped less, because P1 already makes the same point in one plain clause ("bought last, so any slack ... was always going to land on it"), and putting the plain reason (the payment dates) inside the image sentence made it the longest sentence in the passage. I left out the four program officers who booked the top, because nothing I was allowed to read told me whether they were real foundation staff, and the report must not present a simulated test as a real event.
