# Voice round 6: blind reading, reader 1

Read: the IPS twice (the second time slowly), then the pack of 13 passages. I opened no other repository file.

What I heard in the IPS: people talking straight to Laura, a little eager, with rough joins left in ("So we hedge it!",
"But worry not", "Yet another Treasury fund"). They make bold claims and like a strong adjective ("immune to markets",
"ever-reliable Treasuries"). They hang asides off spaced dashes, write numbers as numerals, and have one metaphor ("your
career is a large holding of shares"), which they explain in plain words and then leave behind.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It tells the story the way the IPS argues: a plain setup, then "almost everything went to plan – but two
  things nearly didn't", with homely turns ("hard to fudge", "trouble either way round"). It is tidier than the IPS, but
  it still sounds like students who were there at 00:30.
- **Meaning:** The team wrote targets before trading. All ten Treasury holdings hit them and VT ran three units over, as
  the plan allowed. Two scares produced two rules: a 0.25-point price check, and only Ray placing orders. The book is
  the $450,000 plan scaled down to $300,000.
- **Check question (Buying it):** Before trading they set a target number of units for each of the 10 Treasury holdings,
  VT to be bought last with whatever cash was left, and cash never below $1,000. The 0.25-point yield band came later.
  All 10 Treasuries landed exactly on target, every bond sat inside the band, cash never fell below $1,000 and fees were
  $200. The only miss was VT, at 167 units instead of 164.
- **Image:** It is clear in its own sentence ("once an order lands it's done, and there's no rubbing it out"), but it
  bends "ink" to mean any act that can't be undone rather than a figure she owns, so it neither helped nor hurt much.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's own register. One long, slightly overloaded sentence ("which means we can avoid paying the
  price for the vain illusion ... instead buying ...") ends on a breezy "(as per JPMorgan)" and a rhetorical question.
  It sounds enthusiastic and a bit showy, like a confident student.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong, because no payment depends on it. It buys the whole world
  market cheaply for a forecast of about 7.5% a year, and it answers how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question:** none fits.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clipped and economical, almost a set of compressed notes. Each sentence carries one mechanism, and
  there is a neat closing turn ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). The IPS writers ramble a little and
  talk to Laura; this writer is tighter and cooler, though "a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad
  year" is a very human line.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half the shares, which move into Treasuries
  before she names it. Any shortfall from reinvesting the bonds' interest is first covered from her kept shares, and if
  they can't cover it the range comes down.
- **Check question (The 2031 range):** The passage doesn't say which figure a co-sponsor should count. It explains the
  range only as a floor plus half the shares, both moved into Treasuries before she names it, so from this passage alone
  I can't say why she states a range rather than one number.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the trading-note feel of the IPS. It switches tense ("we purchased ... we'll simply ignore"), says
  "The catch is,", strings adjectives together, and gives a slightly stiff "as opposed to a promise of par". The rough
  edges are the IPS's own: a student explaining a practical choice, glad of the trade-off.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS listed no Treasury bond for a year, the team used iBonds Treasury funds. Held to their end,
  these behave like bonds, but they pay out a net asset value rather than par; in return they are easier to sell than
  an odd-lot bond.
- **Check question:** none fits.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is the same story as Passage 1, told smoothly with a colloquial aside ("It wasn't all smooth, mind you").
  It ends on its framing point (89.55% Treasuries "on purpose"). The parallel close of the price paragraph ("a fair
  price never trips it and a stale one can't slip through") is more polished than anything in the IPS, but the voice is
  still plausibly theirs.
- **Meaning:** Judged against targets written before trading, the book did what they said. The ten Treasuries were
  exact, VT ran over as planned, and two incidents led to the price rule and to Ray alone placing orders. The book is
  mostly Treasuries by design and is a scaled version of the full plan.
- **Check question (Buying it):** They set unit targets for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT last from leftover
  cash, and a $1,000 cash floor. All ten Treasuries hit exactly, VT came in at 167 against 164, cash never broke the
  floor, every bond was inside the 0.25-point band, and fees were $200.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The conversational frame is like the team's ("we'd rather not answer with a percentage", "Before the move
  it's a different story"). But the second paragraph is a dense chain of fund-cost caveats, and the first sentence about
  "two inkings with two dates" is overloaded. It reads like the team at its most careful, a little laboured.
- **Meaning:** Laura leads with the $115,000 top, and the range's two ends were bought on two dates. Her confidence is
  "certain" once both ends are owned, after deliberately cautious fund assumptions. After the move only her flexibility
  can shrink; before it, a 40% share fall could pull the top down, which is why she waits.
- **Check question (The 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is her facility contribution, the money she
  actually gives. She states a range because the two ends became hers on two dates (the bottom in January 2028, the rest
  before the meetings), so the gap records timing, not doubt.
- **Image:** "Two inkings with two dates" helped me see the range as two purchases rather than a guess, though the noun
  "inkings" is awkward and the sentence carrying it is too long.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain and well paced, and it talks through the reasoning as a person would ("We worked each end out
  cautiously – almost stubbornly", "isn't us hedging our bets"). A couple of tidy closers ("The timing does all the
  work", "Never the range, and never a payment") are a shade more crafted than the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The figure that matters is the $115,000 top. The range down to $95,000 shows what she owned first and
  what the 2031 move added. Both ends are owned and worked out cautiously, so the range is certain; only flexibility can
  change afterwards, and a top named before the move might not be owned.
- **Check question (The 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is what she hands over and what a co-sponsor plans
  around. She states a range because the bottom ($95,000, held since January 2028) and the rest (added by the 2031 move)
  were owned at different times. The gap shows that order, not uncertainty.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is clean and impersonal, and it reads like a textbook. It opens with how pension funds do things, then
  describes the plan flatly. None of the IPS writers' warmth, eagerness or asides comes through; it could have been
  written by any careful explainer.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the team buys one bond per payment and holds it, so
  no payment depends on shares and nothing needs forecasting or trading.
- **Check question:** none fits.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds exactly like the IPS's authors writing a trade note. It has a big confident claim ("a
  near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that"), a dramatic flourish ("anything the restless markets might
  decide to do"), and a "Notably –" aside. The slightly loose technical wording ("through duration-matching") belongs
  to enthusiastic students rather than a polished writer.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury bond won't create wealth but removes uncertainty for a payment, and because the
  payments are fixed in nominal dollars, plain nominal Treasuries fit them and TIPS aren't needed.
- **Check question:** none fits.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Most of it is the same plain, talked-through explanation as Passages 7 and 13 and could be theirs. The third
  paragraph's image ("Rub out the pencils while the ink is still tacky and it smears") stops the flow, and I had to
  decode it, which the IPS never makes me do with its one metaphor. That sentence pulls the passage down.
- **Meaning:** The top ($115,000) is the figure that counts. The range only records which part she owned first, and the
  ends are pitched low on purpose. Naming the top before the 2031 move risks naming money she doesn't yet own, so she
  waits; after that only her flexibility can move.
- **Check question (The 2031 range):** The top, $115,000, because it is her facility contribution, the number a
  co-sponsor books. She states a range because the bottom was owned from January 2028 and the rest came with the move,
  so the range records which part came first, not a margin for error.
- **Image:** It got in the way. I couldn't map "rubbing out pencil while the ink is tacky" onto "naming the top a year
  early", and the plain clause after the colon did all the work alone.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the most IPS-like movement of the implementation passages: a rhetorical question answered at once
  ("did we buy what we said we'd buy? Yes, though not without two scares"), a "That matters more than it sounds", and
  everyday phrasing ("all up", "no undo button", "stops a stale one cold"). It sounds like a student telling you what
  happened, with numbers as numerals as in the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The team judged itself against targets written before trading and met them, apart from a planned VT
  overshoot. There were two scares (stale prices and a mis-click at a 00:30 open) and a rule from each. The book can't
  show the 2028 deposit, so it is the plan scaled down.
- **Check question (Buying it):** Exact unit targets for each of the 10 Treasury holdings, VT bought last with leftover
  cash, and cash never under $1,000; later, a 0.25-point yield band. All 10 Treasuries hit their targets exactly, every
  bond was inside the band, cash stayed at or above $1,000, fees were $200, and VT ended at 167 against 164.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The account is the same as in Passages 1, 5 and 11, in a similar voice, with a nice "Why 0.25?" turn. It
  opens defensively, though ("not a lack of nerve"), and a printing-press image ("our key plate") is set mid-sentence.
  The image is explained, but it is showier than the IPS, and the stale-price sentence is compressed into a fragment.
- **Meaning:** The book is mostly Treasuries by design. The ten Treasuries were bought first to exact targets and VT
  last from leftover cash, and both bought-in rules (the price check and Ray alone ordering) came from incidents during
  trading.
- **Check question (Buying it):** Unit targets for each of the ten Treasury holdings, VT bought last, and a $1,000 cash
  floor. All ten Treasuries landed exactly, VT came in at 167 against 164, cash stayed above the floor, every bond is
  inside the 0.25 band, and fees were $200.
- **Image:** This passage uses a printing "key plate" image, not pencil and ink. It is clear in its sentence, but it
  pulled my attention from the result to the metaphor.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is conversational and explains as it goes ("which makes it an odd sort of range. Most ranges are a best
  guess ... but hers isn't"), the way the IPS opens by setting up an expectation and then overturning it. The caveats
  are dense in the middle, but the voice stays a person's.
- **Meaning:** Laura leads with the $115,000 top. The range isn't a margin for error but a record of what she owned
  first and what the move added. Her confidence is certainty about owned figures, worked out cautiously for the funds.
  After the move only her flexibility can change, and she names nothing before it.
- **Check question (The 2031 range):** She leads with $115,000, because it is her facility contribution and the figure
  co-sponsors count. She states a range because the two ends were owned at different times, the bottom since January
  2028 and the rest from the 2031 move. The gap is that history, not room to be wrong. This passage explains the "odd
  range" best of the five.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like the same people who wrote "So we hedge it!" and "worry not". They are eager and
a little rough at the joins. They make big claims and use colourful adjectives ("restless markets", "vain illusion",
"near-ironclad"), and they explain one thing at a time in a run of "so" and "which means" before landing on a plain
claim or question. The middle group (the implementation and range accounts) keeps the IPS's talking voice and asides,
but it is smoother, more balanced, and heavier with caveats than anything in the IPS. The lowest are either flat and
impersonal (8), clipped into notes with a neat closing line (3), or interrupted by an image I had to decode (10, and to
a lesser degree the key plate in 12 and the "inkings" in 6). The IPS states its one metaphor in plain words and moves
on.
