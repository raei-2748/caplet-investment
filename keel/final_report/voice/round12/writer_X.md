=== P1 ===
In January 2027 Laura's first deposit buys ten Treasuries, one for each $50,000 payment, each paying out before the 1 January it is for. For 2033 to 2037 they are Treasury funds, each holding only Treasuries that mature in one year, because for most of those years no Treasury bond we could buy matured in time. We buy them with her first money, at least six years early, because the payments are fixed and anything held in shares meanwhile could fall. A Treasury held to its end pays exactly its stated interest and face value on fixed dates, guaranteed by the US government, so no share price decides whether a payment is made.

But $300,000 covers nearly all ten only on one condition: the interest they pay along the way, and any payout that arrives months early, must earn the rates today's Treasury prices imply until each 1 January. Those rates are not a forecast, and rates have often turned out lower: in a replay of US rates since 1981, the ten as bought would have come up short in 80 of 100 starting months. Nothing comes into the portfolio after 2028, so a shortfall found in 2035 could never be closed. We therefore spend $36,774 of the 2028 deposit on more of the same Treasuries, enough to pay all ten even if that waiting cash earns nothing and each fund's costs take 0.5% of its payout, nearly twice what five ended funds of the same kind lost. That is $36,774 the building does not get. If rates turn out as implied it is never needed and returns to Laura by 2042, worth about $46,600 at the start of 2033.
=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura tells co-sponsors a range for her building contribution: we recommend $95,000 to $115,000 on today's figures.

In January 2028 most of her second deposit goes into a Treasury fund that holds only Treasuries maturing in 2032 and pays out in mid-December, just before the residency opens. So that she never states more than the fund delivers, we count its payout as if the interest paid out along the way earned nothing more, take off 0.5% for the fund's costs, and round down to a whole $5,000. That gives the bottom, $95,000, and no later move in rates or shares can lower it.

The rest goes into a world share fund: nothing promised depends on it, and it has three years to grow. In January 2031, before the meetings, half her shares are sold to buy more of the same Treasury fund, and the top is counted the same cautious way on both purchases. How far it reaches depends on shares until then: if they grow 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's assumption, it comes to $115,000, but seen from today it is at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures and at least $115,000 in about 57. So she names no figure before the move; after it, nothing about her figure is uncertain.

She still gives a range, as the case asks, because its ends say different true things. The bottom is what she would have stated had shares fallen to nothing, and what she can tell the residency's planners from 2028; the gap above it is what half her shares bought in January 2031. Both ends are held, so co-sponsors can plan around the top.
=== P4 ===
In January 2031 half the world shares Laura bought in 2028 are sold to buy Treasuries for the figure she will state to co-sponsors, $115,000 on today's figures. The other half stays in shares, promised to no one, and is what we call her flexibility. If shares grow the 7.5% a year J.P. Morgan assumes, it is worth about $21,200 at the start of 2033; in 1 simulated future in 20, $9,600 or less. We leave it in shares because nothing promised rests on it, which makes it the one part of her money free to grow, and to shrink.

It is for three things that happen to building projects. The building is paid for in Taiwan dollars and her money is in US dollars, so if the Taiwan dollar strengthened 10% between the 2031 meetings and the payment, the same building would need about $11,500 more than her $115,000. Her flexibility alone would have covered the currency's real two-year moves since 1983 about 98 times in 100. Since 1970, in Taiwan and five other economies, building costs rose in about four years out of five, including years when shares fell, so an overrun can arrive in a year her flexibility has shrunk. And a co-sponsor may fall a little short.

It cannot cover the worst. Four times since 1991, costs and the currency together made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years: about $32,200 on her $115,000, more than all her flexibility. A gap that size falls to the residency's other funders, a smaller design or a later start, which is why nothing should be built before the money is in hand.
=== NOTES ===
P3 was the hardest. A reader who knows only the case expects a range to exist because the money is uncertain, yet in this plan both ends are held in Treasuries by the time Laura speaks, so the passage had to keep "uncertain seen from today" apart from "settled once stated" and still give a true reason for a range at all. The cautious count behind each end (interest earning nothing more, 0.5% off, rounding down) also had to be explained in plain steps, because without it the reader cannot see why no market can lower the bottom. In P1 and P3 I left out figures that do not add up on the page (the $445,400 the ten would pay against the $36,774 that closes the gap; the fund's expected $102,000 against the $95,000 bottom), because a reader who does the subtraction would stall on them.
