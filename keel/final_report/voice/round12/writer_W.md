=== P1 ===
In January 2027 we use Laura's first deposit to buy ten Treasuries, one for each $50,000 payment, each paying out before the 1 January it pays for. A Treasury is a bond the US government issues and guarantees; held to the end, it pays exactly its stated interest and face value on fixed dates, so a payment fixed in dollars rests on fixed dollars, not a share price. Its price wanders in between, but we never sell early. Five of the ten are Treasury funds, each holding Treasuries that mature in one year and paying out their value when it closes, because for most of those years no Treasury bond open to the plan matures in time. The ten cost a little over $300,000 on today's figures; the 2028 deposit buys the last $2,388.

Even then, each reaches $50,000 only if the money it pays out early, such as its interest, earns interest while it waits, at the rates implied by today's Treasury prices. That depends on fifteen years of rates: bought in any month since 1962 and left to earn the rates that followed, the ten would have come up short in about 1 in 6 starting months up to 1980, and 80 in 100 from 1981. Since nothing comes in after January 2028, a shortfall found in 2033 could never be closed. So about a quarter of the 2028 deposit, $36,774 that could have gone to the building, buys extra Treasuries, enough to meet every payment even if waiting cash earns nothing and every fund pays out 0.5% less than its holdings are worth, to allow for costs. If rates do as today's prices imply, the payments never touch them and they return to Laura by 2042.
=== P3 ===
Laura cannot know in 2031 what her portfolio will be worth in 2033, but she can know what is already paid for, so we arrange for any figure she gives co-sponsors to be paid by Treasuries held for her before she says it. In January 2028, $81,244 of her second deposit buys more of IBTM, a Treasury fund that holds only Treasuries maturing in 2032 and pays out what they are worth that December, just before her contribution is due. We count it as if its interest earned nothing more, take off 0.5% for the fund's costs and round down to the nearest $5,000, so the figure sits below what the fund will pay even if its cash earns nothing: on today's figures, a bottom of $95,000. The rest buys VT, a fund of shares in thousands of companies worldwide. In January 2031, before the meetings, half of those shares are sold to buy more of IBTM, and the top is counted the same way on both purchases: $115,000.

Seen from today, the top is uncertain, because it waits on interest rates until 2028 and share prices until 2031: in about 91 of 100 futures our model simulates it is at least $100,000, and in about 57 at least $115,000. Once the shares are moved nothing about it is uncertain, because the Treasuries that will pay it are held for her, which is why she names no figure before then. She still gives a range, as the case asks, though not out of doubt: the two ends were settled three years apart. Co-sponsors plan around the top, and the bottom shows them how much of it no market could touch after January 2028.
=== P4 ===
In January 2031, before Laura meets co-sponsors, half her holding in a world share fund is sold to buy the Treasury fund that will pay her contribution, so that what she promises is paid for; the other half, promised to no one, is her flexibility. That is about $21,200 on 1 January 2033 if shares earn J.P. Morgan's 7.5% a year, and $9,600 or less in 1 in 20 of our model's futures. Its value is a share price, so naming a figure for it would be the overpromise the case warns against.

We keep it for three things that could happen to a building in Taiwan. The building is paid in Taiwan dollars and her money is in US dollars, so if the Taiwan dollar strengthens 10% between the 2031 meetings and the payment, the $115,000 top of her range needs about $11,500 more; her flexibility would have covered the real two-year moves since 1983 in about 98 stretches in 100. Building costs rose in about four years in five since 1970, in Taiwan and five other economies, in bad years for shares as well as good, so an overrun can arrive when her shares have not grown. And a co-sponsor may give less, perhaps in the same downturn that shrank her shares.

What it cannot carry is the worst. In 1991-93, 2002-05, 2006-08 and 2019-21, building costs and the Taiwan dollar together made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years: about $32,200 on her $115,000, more than all her flexibility. A gap that size, or a lost co-sponsor, falls to the other funders, a smaller design or a later start, so building should wait until the money is in hand.
=== NOTES ===
P1 was the hardest. A reader sees at once why a Treasury held to the end pays its $50,000, but not why ten Treasuries already bought could still fall short; that rests on the interest they pay out early having to earn interest of its own while it waits, an idea that needs its own sentence before the history and the extra Treasuries make sense. P3 was hard in a different way: by the time Laura speaks both ends are already held, so the reason for giving a range at all had to come from the two ends being settled on different dates, not from doubt.
