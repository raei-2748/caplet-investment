# Voice round 2: Keel's main session (no conceit), written after reading the IPS twice and the reflections

=== P1 ===
Laura asked for her ten payments to be met with "a high degree of certainty". We gave that a definition she can check: none of them depends on a share price, or on anybody else's money, when it falls due. Only Treasuries meet it. Hold a Treasury to its date and it pays its interest and its face value, guaranteed – whatever the market thinks of it in between is noise. So in January 2027 her first deposit buys ten of them (T-Bonds where WInS listed a bond for that year, iShares iBonds funds where it didn't), one for each January from 2033 to 2042.

But one Treasury per payment wasn't quite the end of it. A bond pays interest twice a year, years before the payment it funds, and one of ours – the February 2037 bond – matures a full ten and a half months before the January 2038 payment. All of that money sits and waits. If it earns what the market expects, fine. If it doesn't, a plan we'd called certain would turn out to have been quietly betting on interest rates. So the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit goes to the payments before anything else. $2,388 finishes the ten, exactly as the IPS said it would ("we'll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit"). The other $35,894 buys extra Treasuries – enough that every payment is met even if the waiting cash earns nothing at all, not a cent, between a bond's maturity and the day the money goes out.

That sounds expensive, and mostly it isn't. If rates do what the market expects, most of it comes back to Laura (about $45,500 in 2033 dollars) as the payments stop needing it, so it's closer to a deposit than a fee. And from January 2028 the ten payments rest on bonds she owns and on nothing else. By 1 January 2033 the operating reserve holds about $444,500, roughly $1.11 for every dollar the ten need.

So how certain is certain? As certain as the US Treasury – which is about as certain as money gets.

=== P2 ===
Half of Laura's shares are promised to no one, and it's the most deliberate number in the plan.

Here's the trade. Every dollar of shares that moves into Treasuries before 2031 is a dollar she can tell co-sponsors about. Every dollar left in shares is a dollar she can't promise, but can still use – for building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor who falls short. The same dollar can't do both jobs, and the numbers show how tight the swap is: each extra $5,000 she promises at the top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps. Keep 30% back instead of half and she could promise about $10,000 more, but her flexibility would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half of our model futures. Keep 70% back and she promises $10,000 less, for a bigger cushion and a narrower range. Either way, the total she ends up with barely moves (within about $1,100 at the median). The split doesn't decide how much money there is. It decides how much is promised, and how much stays hers.

So why half? Partly because it's the middle of a trade that's nearly one-for-one, and neither end suited Laura. Promise too much and a bad year in Taiwan lands on her credibility, which the case tells us she can't afford to spend. Promise too little and her co-sponsors see a timid number from someone who could clearly have done more. At half, her flexibility comes to about $21,300 by January 2033, centrally, and covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar (about $11,500 more) in about 93 of 100 model futures.

What we won't claim is that this half is a natural hedge, because it isn't one. Building costs have more often risen in the years shares fell, so the cushion can be at its smallest exactly when costs press hardest – about $9,900 in a bad 1-in-20 future. We'd rather Laura knew that now than found out in 2032.

=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura sits down with her co-sponsors and names a range. On today's figures it's $95,000 to $115,000. But the figures matter less than the rule behind them, because the rule is what she can actually defend.

The bottom is what the late-2032 Treasury fund will pay if it earns nothing more from the day it's bought, less 0.5% for the fund's costs, rounded down to the nearest $5,000. The top is the same sum plus the shares she's moved into Treasuries, worked out the same way and rounded down once. That's the whole rule. Every dollar in the range is a dollar she already owns in Treasuries that will pay it in late 2032 – so the range isn't a forecast at all, it's closer to an inventory.

That's also why she says nothing before the move. Until half the shares are in Treasuries, the top is only a guess about the market, and guesses go wrong: a top stated twelve months early isn't actually owned in about 11 of 100 of our model futures. (Before the move it could land anywhere from about $95,000 to $140,000, one time in twenty each way – not something you'd want to discover in front of a funder.) Wait for the move, and the market can't touch either end.

So how sure can she be? Inside the range, certain. At the top, certain unless the funds' running costs beyond their 0.07% fee eat more than the 0.5% we held back, and the five iShares Treasury funds that have already ended point to about 0.27%. If things go better than that, whatever the funds pay above the top doesn't vanish; it joins her flexibility.

Is it a modest range? A little. It's also one she'll never have to walk back, and fourteen of the fifteen investment houses whose long-run forecasts we checked would give her the same $115,000 top. So when a co-sponsor asks how sure she is, Laura gets to give the one answer funders almost never hear: completely.

=== P4 ===
On 5 October, a week into trading, Darren called our world fund a copout. It was a fair hit. We'd spent days on the bonds and then put every share into one fund anyone could buy. Why not pick? A Taiwan fund was on the table (that's where the building is), and so was gold. We took it seriously, because if the world fund was lazy we wanted to know before Laura's money went in.

We kept it, and not because picking is hard. We kept it because of what this particular holding has to do. Laura's range in 2031 has to be checkable: fifteen investment houses publish long-run forecasts for world shares, and run through our rule, fourteen of them give the same $115,000 top. That only works if she owns the world. Own a Taiwan fund instead and there's nobody to check against – and you'd own a fund that's about a fifth one company (TSMC, 21.89%) and three-quarters technology, at 0.59% a year against VT's 0.06%. VT already holds Taiwan anyway, about 3.5% of it.

Then there's the promise in the IPS: only two reasons to change the plan. A fund of picks needs opinions, and opinions want updating. Barber and Odean followed 66,465 households and found that the ones who traded most earned 11.4% a year while the market made 17.9%. The arithmetic of picking is worse still – Bessembinder found most stocks lost to Treasury bills over their lives, and the market's entire gain came from about 4% of companies. Miss those and you've missed everything. Gold, meanwhile, guards against inflation, a risk Laura's fixed-dollar payments simply don't have.

What settled it for us was one comparison. In our model, which shares she holds moves the outcome by about $800 to $1,600; how the market does moves it by $9,881 to $41,258. The choice Darren wanted us to sweat over was worth a rounding error. The one that mattered – how much to put in shares at all – we'd already made.
