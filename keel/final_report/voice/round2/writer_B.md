## Warm-up 1: Franklin's method on "The building is a different matter"

Notes, a few words per sentence:
1. building = different matter
2. most of 2028 -> Treasury fund, matures late 2032; its payout = bare minimum to promise co-sponsors, no doubt
3. rest -> world share fund, thousands of cos, VT spelled out; returns help set the max contribution
4. so markets push top up/down as 2031 nears; bottom constant, Treasuries
5. importantly: half the shares' value promised to no one
6. = flexibility: overruns, stronger TWD, co-sponsor short
7. before 2031 meetings other half -> Treasuries; any figure chosen = money already owned

Rebuilt from the notes alone, IPS put away:

The building is a different matter. Most of your 2028 deposit will go into a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032, and what it pays out is the bare minimum you can promise your co-sponsors without any doubt. The rest goes into VT, a world share fund that owns thousands of companies, and how it does will help set the most you can contribute. So as 2031 gets closer, markets can push the top of your range up or down – but the bottom stays put, because it's built on Treasuries. Importantly, half of the shares' value is promised to no one. That's your flexibility – for building costs that run over, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor who falls short. The other half moves into Treasuries before you meet your co-sponsors in 2031, so whatever figure you name is money you already own.

Where mine drifted: I smoothed their reaches away – "ever-reliable Treasuries", the full "Vanguard Total World Stock ETF", "increase or decrease the top of your planned band", and the slightly overloaded "whose payout is the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt" all came back as tidier, shorter clauses. I also explained ("because it's built on Treasuries") where they decorated and enjoyed it, so mine reads like an explainer rather than people pleased with their own plan.

## Warm-up 2: the next page, after "Yours, Caplet"

The letter above is what Laura got in November: 500 words, no charts, and no number we couldn't defend in a sentence. This report is the rest of it. Nothing in her portfolio has moved since the freeze, and nothing will, so none of what follows is a new plan – it's us going back over the old one and checking that it really does what we told her it would. We'll start with the 10 payments, naturally. If those aren't certain, nothing else here matters very much.

=== P1 ===
Laura asked for her 10 payments to be met with "a high degree of certainty", which we took to mean a near-ironclad guarantee: on the day each falls due, no payment should depend on a share price, an interest rate or anybody else's money.

Laura is a graphic novelist, and her world has a habit that makes this easier to see. Comic pages are drawn twice – first in pencil, which can be rubbed out, then in ink, which is what gets printed. So in this report, ink is a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on its date, and pencil is any figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet: her next advance, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, whatever her shares are worth, every forecast. A recession is one eraser across three pencil lines at once (her advances, share prices and co-sponsors' budgets), which is why a promised payment can't be left in pencil.

Most of each payment is inked in January 2027, when the first deposit buys 10 Treasuries, each maturing before the 1 January it pays. At the 6 November close that cost about $301,500, roughly the whole deposit.

The subtler problem was the time in between. The bonds pay interest along the way, and the February 2037 bond matures ten and a half months before its payment, so cash sits and waits, and a "certain" plan could quietly lean on future interest rates. That was the one pencil line left. The first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit inks it: $2,388 is the top-up that finishes the 10, and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, so the payments are met even if waiting cash earns nothing at all. Publishers do the same with royalties, holding some back against returns until the risk passes – and if rates hold, most of this comes back to Laura too, about $45,500 in 2033 money.

By 1 January 2033 the operating reserve is about $444,500, roughly 111% of what the payments need. Our first plan, about three-quarters shares, missed a payment in 3.2% of model runs, and in 40.8% without the 2028 deposit. Buying the payments first never missed one.

=== P2 ===
Half of what Laura's shares are worth will never be promised to anyone. That half is her flexibility, and the IPS gave it three jobs: building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor falling short. On our central numbers it's about $21,300 in January 2033, and about $9,900 in a bad 1-in-20 future.

Is that enough? The Taiwan dollar is the one job we can test properly. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 would need about $11,500 more, and her flexibility covers that in about 93 of 100 model futures – and in about 98 of 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983.

So why half? Honestly, because we chose it. Keep only 30% back and Laura could promise about $10,000 more, but her flexibility would cover that stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half the futures. Keep 70% back and she promises about $10,000 less, with a bigger cushion and a narrower range. Each $5,000 more of promised top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps, while the total she ends up with barely moves (within about $1,100 either way at the median). In other words, the split doesn't decide how much money there is – only how much of it is promised, and how much stays hers. Half is the middle of that trade, and no model picked it.

And there's a reason from Laura's own drawing desk. You can always add ink to a page, but you can't take it off cleanly – a mistake gets painted over, and the original keeps the record. A figure told to co-sponsors is the same: she can always give more than she said, never quietly less. So half the shares stay pencil on purpose, out of co-sponsors' sight, and anything the funds pay above her stated top goes back to her flexibility.

The catch is, building costs more often rise in the years shares fall, so her flexibility can be at its smallest exactly when costs press hardest. It's the one honest weakness in the design, and a good argument against trimming the cushion to make the promise look bigger.

=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura has to give her co-sponsors two things: a dollar range for her facility contribution, and how confident she is in it. On today's figures that range is $95,000 to $115,000. The figures will move before then; the rule that produces them won't.

The bottom is fixed in January 2028, when the 2028 deposit buys the Treasury fund maturing in late 2032. That fund pays about $103,000 in 2033, and the bottom of her range sits below it. The top is fixed later, when the other half of the shares moves into Treasuries before the meetings. Both ends are rounded down, and 0.5% is held back as well. Printers work in much the same way: they run the art past the line where the page is cut, because the blade wanders, and keep the words well inside it. Laura's stated top is the cut line, the 0.5% and the rounding are the bleed, and the bottom sits safely inside.

Why say nothing before the shares move? Because until then the top is still pencil – it's just whatever the shares happen to be worth that week – and Laura only states a figure once it's inked. A number announced in 2029 could be out of reach by 2031, and the case is blunt that overpromising would damage her credibility. After the move, every dollar of her range is money she already owns, so the confidence part is easy: she can tell co-sponsors, quite truthfully, that she's certain.

We tested the range from both directions. Erase every pencil line – say the shares went to zero – and the page still reads: the 10 payments don't change, the floor still pays about $103,178, and the range becomes one figure, $95,000. Swap our forecast for someone else's and the top holds: of the fifteen investment houses that publish long-run forecasts for world shares, fourteen give the same $115,000 top under the rule. J.P. Morgan's 7.5%, the figure we used, sits in the upper third of them, not at the top.

=== P4 ===
On 5 October Darren argued that the world fund was a copout, and that we should be picking stocks and ETFs ourselves. A Taiwan fund, EWT, came up that day, and so did gold. We kept the one world fund, VT. Still, "copout" is a fair charge – a fund that owns everything looks like the one choice that needs no thought at all.

The first thing that stands out is how little that choice matters to Laura. In the plan's numbers, which shares she holds is worth about $800 to $1,600 to the outcome, while how the market as a whole does is worth $9,881 to $41,258 (1 in 20 each way). Picking would have meant working hardest on the smallest number in the plan.

EWT shows what picking costs in practice. It's about a fifth one company (TSMC, at 21.89%) and about three-quarters technology, and it charges 0.59% a year against VT's 0.06% – while VT already holds Taiwan, at about 3.5% of the fund. Gold answers inflation, which is a real problem, just not Laura's: her 10 payments are fixed in dollars.

The reason we like best is about checking our work. Comic artists used to draw their construction lines in a light blue the printing camera couldn't see, and our models, the 7.5% and every median in this report are like that: the judges see them, Laura's co-sponsors never will. With a whole-world fund, those blue lines aren't only ours. Fifteen investment houses publish long-run forecasts for world shares, and fourteen of the fifteen give the same $115,000 top on our rule. Our own picks would have had nobody else's lines to check against.

And a holding that needs no opinions keeps the IPS's promise that only two things can change the plan. When Barber and Odean (2000) studied 66,465 US households, the ones that traded most earned 11.4% a year, against the market's 17.9%. Picks need opinions, and opinions need revisiting.

So VT stayed. Darren was right that it's the easiest fund to buy; his argument is how we found out it's also the hardest one to argue against.

=== NOTES ===
Keeping the Trading Notes' habits (the "In other words", "The catch is,", the dash before a payoff) made the passages sound more like them than any single sentence I tried to imitate, but in a report for judges Laura is "her" rather than "you", and I can hear the warmth flattening without anyone to address. The conceit sat best in their voice when it came in the way they bring in an idea, with a bit of relish ("her world has a habit that makes this easier to see"), and worst when the turns stacked up; I cut the printed-page aside from P3 because three turns in one part began to feel like a tour of a print shop. The team reaches for one big word now and then ("ever-reliable", "restless markets", "terminal payout") and I mostly didn't, so these are probably still too even. The end of P4 is the one place I let a flourish land the way theirs do, and it may be one turn too neat.
