# Writer E, voice round 2

## Warm-up 1: Franklin's method ("The building is a different matter")

Notes, a few words per sentence:
1. Building = different matter.
2. Most of 2028 deposit -> Treasury fund, matures late 2032; its payout = bare minimum she can promise co-sponsors, no doubt.
3. Rest -> world share fund, thousands of companies, VT; its returns help set the maximum facility contribution.
4. So: markets move the top of the band up or down as 2031 nears; bottom stays, sits on Treasuries.
5. Importantly for her: half the shares' value promised to no one.
6. = her flexibility: overruns, stronger Taiwan dollar, co-sponsor short.
7. Before the 2031 meetings the other half -> Treasuries, so the figure she picks = money she already owns.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:

The building is another story. Most of your 2028 deposit will buy a Treasury fund that matures late in 2032, and what it pays out is the least you could ever promise your co-sponsors – the number nobody can take off you. The rest goes into a world share fund, VT, which owns thousands of companies, and how it does will decide how high your facility contribution can go. So as 2031 gets closer the markets can push the top of your range up or down, but the bottom stays put, sitting on Treasuries. And here's the part that matters most for you: half of what the shares are worth is promised to nobody. That's your flexibility – for building costs that run over, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor who comes up short. The other half moves into Treasuries before you sit down with co-sponsors in 2031, so whatever number you give them is money you already own.

Where it drifted: mine came out plainer and more spoken ("another story", "here's the part that matters most"), and it lost the little reaches the original enjoys ("without doubt", "the ever-reliable Treasuries", "Importantly for you"). I also added a gloss the original didn't need ("the number nobody can take off you"), explaining where they simply trusted the reader.

## Warm-up 2: the next page, for a judge (about 80 words)

That letter was written for Laura, so it kept the numbers out of her way. This report puts them back in. Every figure below comes from our own model, run on the Treasury curve of 6 November 2026 – none of it is a forecast, and none of it leans on what our WInS portfolio happened to earn. We'll take the plan in the order Laura will live it: the ten payments, then the building, then the range she has to stand behind in 2031.

=== P1 ===
Laura asked for her ten payments to be met with a 'high degree of certainty'. The comfortable reading is a high probability in a model. Our first plan, about three-quarters shares, would have passed that test – it missed at least one payment in only 3.2% of our model runs. But that climbed to 13.7% if her 2028 deposit came in at $75,000, and 40.8% if it never arrived. That deposit comes from advances, speaking fees and licences – the same good years that lift shares. A plan that is certain only while her career goes well isn't what she asked for.

So we read it the strict way: no payment should depend on a share price, a forecast or anyone else's money when it falls due. Buying the ten payments first never missed one. At the close on 6 November that cost about $301,500, roughly her whole first deposit, and if yields fell a full point before January it would be nearer $334,000. Which sounds bad, but isn't: the 2028 deposit simply covers the difference.

The harder part was the small print. The bonds pay interest along the way, and the February 2037 bond matures ten and a half months before the 1 January 2038 payment it funds. Cash waiting like that earns whatever rates happen to be then, which quietly turns a locked plan into a bet on future interest rates. That's the job of the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit: $2,388 is the top-up the IPS promised, finishing the ten, and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, enough that the payments are met even if waiting cash earns nothing at all. If rates hold, most of it comes back to her.

The iBonds funds (IBTM to IBTR) pay out what their bonds are worth, not a fixed sum – close, not exact – and the margin covers that too. By 1 January 2033 the operating reserve should be about $444,500, about 111% of what the ten payments need, all of it bought by January 2028: five years before the first payment goes out, and fourteen before the last.

=== P2 ===
The obvious move with the shares would have been to promise all of them. Co-sponsors like a big number, and Laura wants to give as much as she responsibly can – so why hold half back? Because 'responsibly' is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence. The IPS names three things that can go wrong once the building is under way: costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor who falls short. Half the shares, promised to no one, is the money for those – about $21,300 in January 2033 on our central figures.

Is that enough? For the currency, mostly. If the Taiwan dollar is 10% stronger than in 2031, the building needs about $11,500 more. Her flexibility covers that in about 93 of 100 futures we modelled, and in about 98 of 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983 – which we trust a little more, since it actually happened.

Now the cost, because there is one. Every $5,000 more she promises at the top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps. Keep only 30% back and she could promise about $10,000 more, but the cushion would then cover that currency move in only about half the futures. Keep 70% back and she promises about $10,000 less, with a fatter cushion and a narrower range. Yet the total barely moves – at the median, within about $1,100 either way. The split doesn't change how much money there is, only how much of it is promised. Half is the middle of that trade, and we chose it; no model did, and we won't call it optimal.

One honest caveat: this cushion is not a hedge. Building costs more often rise in the years shares fall, and a recession is also when co-sponsors trim their budgets, so the cushion can be thinnest just when it's needed most – about $9,900 in a bad 1-in-20 future. That's why none of it is promised: co-sponsors never hear about it, so in a bad year it can shrink without costing Laura anyone's trust.

=== P3 ===
In 2031 Laura sits down with people about to commit their own money and tells them how much she'll put in, and how sure she is. If she overshoots, the damage isn't a few thousand dollars – it's the credibility the whole residency is built on, and co-sponsors remember. So her range isn't a forecast. It comes out of a rule, and on today's figures the rule gives $95,000 to $115,000, with $115,000 as the central contribution.

The bottom is the easy end. The Treasury fund bought in January 2028 matures in late 2032 and should pay about $103,000 in 2033, and $95,000 sits comfortably beneath that. From the day that fund is bought, the bottom is fixed. Push it to the extreme – every share she owns goes to zero – and the ten payments don't change, the floor still pays out, and her range simply becomes one figure: $95,000.

The top is where VT comes in, and it's why Laura says nothing until half the shares have moved into Treasuries. Until then, markets can push the top up or down as they please, and any figure she quoted would really be a guess about where the world's markets will sit a few months later. If they fell after the meeting, she'd be the one explaining the gap, to people who'd planned around her number. After the move, the top is money she owns, in Treasuries. She isn't predicting anything by then – she's reading her own statement.

So how sure can she be? Of the range she actually announces, completely. Both ends are in Treasuries, both are rounded down, and anything the funds pay above the top goes to her flexibility rather than to anyone she's made a promise to. What stays uncertain is only where the top lands, and that's a question about the next few years of world markets – one Laura never has to answer in public until the answer is already sitting in her account.

=== P4 ===
On 5 October Darren called VT a copout, and it was a fair hit. We were six students in an investment competition, and our answer to 'which shares should Laura own?' was, more or less, 'all of them'. He wanted us to pick stocks and ETFs, and a Taiwan fund (EWT) and gold were both raised. We kept the one world fund, and the reasons still hold.

The first is simply scale. In our model, which shares Laura holds is worth about $800 to $1,600 to her outcome; how the market as a whole does is worth $9,881 to $41,258 (both 1 in 20 each way). Picking would have meant spending our effort, and her fees, on the small number.

EWT is about a fifth one company – TSMC, at 21.89% – and about three-quarters technology, with a fee of 0.59% against VT's 0.06%. So it was largely a technology bet with one chipmaker at its centre, and VT already holds Taiwan, at about 3.5%. Gold answers inflation, a real worry for plenty of people, just not for ten payments fixed in dollars.

Then the plan's own promise: only two things would ever change it, a missed deposit or a Treasury default. A portfolio of picks runs on opinions, and opinions want updating, which means trading – and in Barber and Odean's study of 66,465 US households, the ones that traded most earned 11.4% a year while the market earned 17.9%.

The reason we'd put first now, though, is about Laura. Because VT owns the whole world market, her 2031 range can be checked against the long-run forecasts that fifteen investment houses publish for world shares, and fourteen of the fifteen give the same $115,000 top on our rule. J.P. Morgan's 7.5%, the figure in our trading note, sits in their upper third, not at the top. Had we picked, her top would have rested on what six students happened to think in October 2026. So the copout does the one thing a pick never could: it lets a co-sponsor check Laura's top without taking our word for anything.

=== NOTES ===
These came out closer to the IPS's register than to the Trading Notes': spoken, a little cheeky, with dashes doing the turning, but I think they still run more orderly than the team does – each passage walks from question to answer to caveat, where the team's own writing tends to blurt the conclusion first and justify it after. The asides ("which we trust a little more, since it actually happened", "and co-sponsors remember") felt most like them; the closing lines of P2 and P4 felt most like me reaching for an ending. Ahaan's habit of naming the objection and then meeting it head-on helped most in P1 and P4, where there was a real opposing reading to answer. I kept the team's love of a finance term light (little beyond 'margin' and 'median'), and I suspect they would have reached for one or two more, the way the Trading Notes reach for 'duration-matching' and 'growth premium'.
