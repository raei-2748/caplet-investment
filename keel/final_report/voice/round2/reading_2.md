# Reading 2: voice round 2 (blind reader 2)

What I heard in the IPS (read twice): a letter from young people talking straight to their client, warm and a little
cheeky ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not", "Fair enough; that's your job"), earnest and sometimes slightly over-eager
in its word choice ("ever-reliable Treasuries", "definitely not a contributor", "take that one step further"), with asides
tacked on after dashes, few figures, one big reframing idea (her career is a stock she can't sell) rather than
decorative imagery, and one carefully turned closing line. It is confident but not polished smooth; you can hear the
seams.

### Passage 1
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves like a team talking honestly about an argument it had, with quick, lively turns ("It was a fair hit", "Why not pick?", "Miss those and you've missed everything") and a plain landing on the comparison that settled it; it is more organised and evidence-heavy than the IPS, but the casual, slightly scrappy confidence sounds like the same people grown more careful.
- **Meaning:** Darren's charge that VT was a copout was taken seriously, and VT was kept because the 2031 range can then be checked against outside forecasts, because picks need opinions that invite trading, and because which shares she holds matters far less than how much is in shares at all.

### Passage 2
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The first half is clear, plain analysis with a nice honest beat ("Honestly, because we chose it"), but then it swerves into a drawing-desk image that is hard to follow and closes on a careful, balanced caveat; it reads like a skilled writer assembling a case rather than the IPS's chatty voice.
- **Meaning:** Keeping half the shares unpromised gives Laura about $21,300 of flexibility (about $9,900 in a bad case), which covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar most of the time; half is a chosen middle of a near one-for-one trade, and its weakness is that building costs tend to rise when shares fall.
- **Image:** The ink-can't-come-off picture got in the way: "the original keeps the record" is obscure, and it then flips to "pencil" for the kept half without having set that up.

### Passage 3
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The argument is well built and the last line has some wit, but it is laid out like an essay ("The first thing that stands out", "The reason we like best") and the comic-artist digression arrives out of nowhere, in a register the IPS writers never reach for.
- **Meaning:** VT was kept against Darren's copout charge because share selection barely moves the outcome, EWT and gold are poor fits, a whole-world fund lets outsiders' forecasts check the $115,000 top, and a no-opinion holding keeps the "only two reasons" promise.
- **Image:** The non-photo-blue lines got in the way more than they helped; the point (judges see the models, co-sponsors never will) is clear without it, and the image needs its own explanation first.

### Passage 4
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is clean and confident, steps through the rule in plain sentences, and ends with a crisp, quotable answer ("completely") much like the IPS's closing move; it is more exact and controlled than the IPS, but the directness and the slight swagger fit.
- **Meaning:** Laura's 2031 range comes from a fixed rule rather than a forecast, so every dollar in it is already owned in Treasuries; she says nothing before half the shares move, and once they have, she can tell co-sponsors she is completely sure.
- **Range rule (my words):** The bottom is what the late-2032 Treasury fund will pay assuming it earns nothing more, minus 0.5% for costs, rounded down to the nearest $5,000; the top adds the shares already moved into Treasuries, worked out the same way and rounded down; she names nothing until that move.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. The move into Treasuries has not happened, so the top is still a guess about markets (the passage says a top stated even twelve months early is not owned in about 11 of 100 futures).

### Passage 5
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Plain, direct, and built around one clear trade ("Here's the trade."), with the client's situation kept in view and a frank, human close ("We'd rather Laura knew that now than found out in 2032"); it lacks the IPS's bounce but not its plain-spokenness.
- **Meaning:** Every share dollar is either promisable (moved to Treasuries) or kept as flexibility, never both; half is the deliberate middle of a near one-for-one trade, it covers a stronger Taiwan dollar most of the time, and it is not a natural hedge.

### Passage 6
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It reads like a neutral textbook summary: it opens with what pension funds do rather than with Laura, and moves in flat, correct, compressed sentences with no personality; the IPS writers always start from her and never sound this impersonal.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways to cover fixed payments, the plan buys one Treasury per payment and holds it, so in January 2027 the first $300,000 buys a ladder that pays each January without relying on shares.

### Passage 7
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens strongly and argumentatively ("We'd argue that reading is a trap"), but then piles on an art-world definition, a piece of comics-industry trivia in brackets and a publishing analogy; that layering of clever references is a different kind of writer from the IPS's.
- **Meaning:** "High degree of certainty" should mean no payment rests on anything not yet bought, because a mostly-shares plan fails exactly when Laura's own deposit is weak; most of each payment is bought in January 2027, and the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit covers the risk that waiting cash earns nothing.
- **Image:** The pencil-and-ink definition helped a little, but the Image Comics aside and the royalties comparison on top of it got in the way.

### Passage 8
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It speaks plainly and puts the stakes in human terms (credibility, "co-sponsors remember", "reading her own statement"), moving end by end in a steady, confident way; smoother than the IPS, but the same straight talk to a non-expert.
- **Meaning:** Laura's range comes from a rule: the bottom is fixed from January 2028 below what the late-2032 fund pays, the top only becomes real once half the shares are in Treasuries, so she is completely sure of whatever she announces.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom: $95,000, set comfortably under the roughly $103,000 the late-2032 fund pays, fixed once that fund is bought; top: the floor plus the half of the shares moved into Treasuries, both rounded down; nothing is said until the move. (Oddly, it calls $115,000 both the top and the "central contribution".)
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. Before the move the top is a guess about markets, and she would be the one explaining the gap if they fell.

### Passage 9
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds like the IPS's own hand: an earnest, slightly showy line that reaches for big words ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners", "growth premium"), a clause bolted on with "as per JPMorgan", and one compact sentence explaining what the holding is for; it has the same mix of conviction and teenage gloss.
- **Meaning:** VT is where the plan can afford to be wrong, because the Treasuries already cover the payments; it buys the whole market cheaply instead of picking winners, and its job is to decide how generous the facility contribution can be.

### Passage 10
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Dense and literary: it defines a working metaphor, extends it to an eraser, adds a publishing comparison, and stacks figures in almost every sentence; it is controlled and clever in a way the IPS's looser, chattier voice is not.
- **Meaning:** Certainty means no payment depends on markets or others' money when due; the first deposit buys most of each payment in 2027, the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit covers the waiting-cash gap, and by 2033 the reserve is about 111% of what is needed.
- **Image:** Mostly helped: pencil versus ink is defined clearly, and "one eraser across three pencil lines" makes the correlated-risk point vividly, though the royalties comparison at the end is one image too many.

### Passage 11
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Conversational and a bit wry ("'responsibly' is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence", "which we trust a little more, since it actually happened"), it argues with itself in an easy, natural way; it is longer and more measured than the IPS, but the dry humour is close to "Fair enough; that's your job".
- **Meaning:** Half the shares are held back unpromised as money for overruns, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a short co-sponsor; that covers the currency risk most of the time, half is a chosen middle of a near one-for-one trade, and it is not a hedge, which is why none of it is promised.

### Passage 12
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Careful and polished, it explains the rule and then dresses it in a printers' bleed analogy plus erase-the-pencil imagery and a forecast check; it feels like an accomplished writer building set pieces rather than students writing to their client.
- **Meaning:** The range comes from a stable rule: the bottom is fixed when the late-2032 fund is bought in January 2028, the top when the other half of the shares moves into Treasuries before the meetings, and after that move Laura can truthfully say she is certain.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom set below the roughly $103,000 the late-2032 fund pays, fixed in January 2028; top fixed when the second half of the shares is moved into Treasuries just before the 2031 meetings; both rounded down with 0.5% held back; she states a figure only once it is owned.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. The top is still "pencil" until the shares move, and the passage itself warns that a number announced early could be out of reach by 2031.
- **Image:** Got in the way: I had to stop and map cut line, bleed and safe area onto the top, the 0.5% and the bottom, and the mapping does not quite fit (the margin sits below the top, not beyond it).

### Passage 13
- **Verdict:** Surely not
- **Why:** It is the most writerly passage in the pack, layering three separate trade images (bleed and cut line, pencil and eraser, non-photo blue) into one short explanation with a polished, almost lyrical finish; nothing in the IPS suggests its writers would stack imagery like this.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range is set by a short rule, the bottom is certain from January 2028, Laura names nothing until half the shares are in Treasuries, and the forecasts behind today's top are scaffolding that co-sponsors will never need to see.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom from the late-2032 fund's roughly $103,000 payout, fixed when it is bought in January 2028; top adds what half the shares will pay once moved into Treasuries; both rounded down with a little held back; nothing named until the move.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. The passage says so directly: a co-sponsor told $115,000 in 2030 could be holding a number the market rubs out a month later.
- **Image:** Got in the way: each image is clever alone, but three in a row turn a simple rule into a puzzle.

### Passage 14
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Clipped and procedural, like a planner's summary: every sentence carries a mechanism and none carries warmth or humour, and the one turn of phrase ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does") sounds composed rather than spoken.
- **Meaning:** The range is the floor plus half the shares moved into Treasuries before she names it, and before naming it the reinvestment shortfall is priced and covered from her kept shares, cutting the range if they cannot cover it.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom: the floor rounded down; top: the floor plus half the shares once moved into Treasuries; before naming it, set aside from her kept shares enough to cover the ladder's reinvestment shortfall plus a margin; if that cannot be covered, lower the whole range.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. The passage has her name the range only after the shares move and the shortfall is priced, and it gives no $115,000 to quote anyway.

### Passage 15
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It reasons in a plain, client-facing way and has a little of the IPS's reassuring bounce ("Which sounds bad, but isn't: the 2028 deposit simply covers the difference" is close to "But worry not ... we'll simply complete it"); it is more thorough and measured than the IPS but sounds like the same people explaining more.
- **Meaning:** A model probability is the wrong reading of "high degree of certainty", because a mostly-shares plan fails when Laura's career does; buying the payments first never misses, and the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit removes the waiting-cash risk, leaving a reserve of about 111% by 2033.

### Passage 16
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Direct, sure of itself and willing to sound blunt ("If it earns what the market expects, fine", "not a cent"), quoting its own IPS and closing on a short, punchy question-and-answer that echoes the IPS's closing line; the shape is more careful than the IPS but the attitude matches.
- **Meaning:** Certainty is defined as no payment depending on a share price or anyone else's money, which only Treasuries meet; the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit finishes the ladder and covers the waiting-cash risk, most of it returns to Laura, and the reserve is about $1.11 per dollar needed.

### Passage 17
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** Same voice as the IPS: earnest, eager, a little grand ("a near-ironclad guarantee", "the restless markets"), with asides strung on dashes ("Notably – ...") and a run-on final sentence that piles reasons together; it sounds like a student proud of having understood duration and TIPS.
- **Meaning:** The Treasury bonds remove uncertainty rather than create wealth, matching Laura's fixed nominal payments so closely that inflation-protected bonds would be unnecessary.

### Passage 18
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Lucid and well argued, with one good self-aware joke ("five stocks a team of Sydney students happens to like"), but the art-book simile and the mannered, balanced last sentence ("a good deal better defended for his having made it") sound like a practised adult essayist rather than the IPS's writers.
- **Meaning:** Darren's copout argument belongs in the report because it forced the team to say what VT is for: share choice barely matters, EWT and gold fit badly, picking bets on the rare big winners, and only a whole-world, no-opinion fund can be checked against others' forecasts and keep the "two reasons" promise.
- **Image:** Neutral to slightly in the way: the art-book comparison is decoration; it tells me nothing the plain sentence after it doesn't.

### Passage 19
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the IPS's self-aware humour and plain directness ("our answer to 'which shares should Laura own?' was, more or less, 'all of them'"), argues in a natural order and ends with a clean, turned line about what the "copout" does; it is the most IPS-like of the long passages.
- **Meaning:** VT was kept against Darren's challenge because share selection is a small number next to market performance, EWT and gold fit badly, picks would break the "two reasons" promise, and above all a world fund lets co-sponsors check Laura's top against fifteen houses' forecasts.

### Passage 20
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Analytic and tidy, but it leans on pencil, ink and eraser images it never sets up, and the voice is more composed and gently literary ("sadly", "the original keeps the record") than the IPS's.
- **Meaning:** The split of the shares decides how much is promised, not how much there is; half is a chosen middle that still lets her flexibility cover a stronger Taiwan dollar most of the time, and its weakness is that a recession shrinks the cushion and co-sponsors' budgets together.
- **Image:** Got in the way: "ink only goes one way" makes sense, but "pencil lines" and the eraser arrive undefined, so I had to guess what they stood for.

### Passage 21
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has the IPS's earnest, slightly jargon-proud energy (a run of adjectives, "so we'll simply ignore price noise", "The catch is", "as opposed to") and a human unevenness in tense ("we purchased ... we'll simply ignore"); more technical than the letter, but it sounds like the same students writing a note on a trade.
- **Meaning:** Where no Treasury bond matured in a given year, iBonds ETFs were bought instead; they act like bonds held to the end, so each payment rests on its own instrument, at the cost of paying out net asset value rather than par in exchange for liquidity.

## What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like the IPS's writers on an ordinary day: earnest, a little eager to show what
they know, comfortable sounding slightly naive or overstated, with asides tacked on after dashes and no interest in
decorating the point. The strong middle (19, 1, 16, 15, 11) keeps the letter's plain talk and self-aware humour but is
far more organised and evidence-dense than the IPS, so I believe it as these people writing carefully rather than
hear it as them. The lowest passages either stack art-world and printing analogies (bleed, non-photo blue, erasers,
royalties, comics trivia) into an explanation, which is a polished, writerly habit the IPS never shows, or go the other
way into clipped, impersonal summary (6, 14) with none of the letter's warmth. Across the long passages, the more often
a paragraph followed the same tidy rhetorical question, answer and honest-caveat pattern, the less it sounded like the
students and the more like a skilled outside writer.
