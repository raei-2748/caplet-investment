# Reading 1 (voice round 2)

How I heard the IPS, after two readings: a warm, slightly showy letter by people who are pleased with their idea and
want Laura to be pleased too. Plain claims get dressed up a little ("the ever-reliable Treasuries", "worry not",
"definitely not a contributor", "the actual nature of a writer's income"). Dashes hinge a claim to what it means.
There are few numbers, one image (her career as a stock she can't sell), and one closing turn ("You take risks on the
page ... ours is to take none"). It sounds confident and earnest, and a bit pleased with itself, the way a bright
sixteen-year-old's good essay does. It does not sound like a polished adult writer.

### Passage 1
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It starts plainly and some of it is casual in an IPS way ("it's just whatever the shares happen to be worth that week"). Then it piles a printing analogy on top of a pencil-and-ink image it never defines, and the passage turns into someone working a conceit rather than explaining a plan. The balanced line "The figures will move before then; the rule that produces them won't" is more crafted than anything in the IPS.
- **Meaning:** Laura's 2031 range ($95,000-$115,000 today) comes from a fixed rule: a floor bought in 2028, a top fixed once half the shares are in Treasuries, both rounded down. It survives the shares going to zero and survives other houses' forecasts.
- **Range rule, in my words:** The bottom is set in January 2028 by the Treasury fund maturing in late 2032 (about $103,000), and her stated bottom sits below it. The top is set only when the other half of the shares has moved into Treasuries, just before the 2031 meetings. Both are rounded down with 0.5% held back, and she says no figure until it is owned.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. Before the move the top is "still pencil", and the passage warns that a number announced early "could be out of reach by 2031".
- **Image:** It got in the way. The bleed mapping ("the 0.5% and the rounding are the bleed") confused me, because bleed is what lies past the cut, and I had to reread it. The "erase every pencil line" test did help.

### Passage 2
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is very compressed and cool, like a spec or a word-limited draft. It has the IPS's brevity but none of its warmth, dashes or enthusiasm. The two neat aphorisms ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does") sound engineered rather than spoken.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 the range runs from the floor to the floor plus half the shares, once they are moved into Treasuries; any reinvestment shortfall is priced first and taken from her kept shares, and if those cannot cover it the range comes down.
- **Range rule, in my words:** The bottom is the floor, rounded down. The top adds half the shares once they have moved into Treasuries. Before she names it, the cost of the ladder's reinvestment gap at that day's rates, plus a margin, moves from her kept shares into Treasuries, and if that is not enough the whole range is lowered.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No, as far as I can tell. She names the range only after half the shares are in Treasuries and the shortfall has been priced. The passage puts that just before she names it in 2031, not in 2030.
- **Image:** None.

### Passage 3
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is a clear, patient, conversational explanation that a careful student could write. It has a comma splice ("isn't a forecast at all, it's closer to an inventory") and casual asides ("not something you'd want to discover in front of a funder"). The repeated question-and-answer rhythm and the polished last line ("the one answer funders almost never hear: completely") are smoother than the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The range is a rule about money she already owns, not a forecast. She waits for the move because an early top is often not owned, and she can then be certain apart from fund costs beyond the 0.5% held back.
- **Range rule, in my words:** The bottom is what the late-2032 fund pays if it earns nothing more, less 0.5%, rounded down to the nearest $5,000. The top is that sum plus the shares already moved into Treasuries, worked out the same way and rounded down once. She names nothing before the move.
- **January 2030, may she tell a co-sponsor $115,000?** No. Before half the shares are in Treasuries the top is "only a guess about the market". A top stated twelve months early is not owned in about 11 of 100 model futures, and it could land anywhere from about $95,000 to $140,000.
- **Image:** Only "closer to an inventory", which is brief and helped.

## What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest sound like the IPS's own people. They are earnest and a little showy, plain claims are dressed in one grand word ("restless markets", "vain illusion", "near-ironclad"), and dashes hinge each point to what it means. They carry few figures, borrow no imagery, and are occasionally clunky. The middle group keeps the IPS's frank first-person "we" and its habit of setting up the obvious plan and then turning it, while carrying more numbers. The lowest-ranked passages are smoother and more engineered than the IPS ever is. They stack images from printing and comics (bleed, cut lines, non-photo blue, erasers, Image Comics, royalties), signpost themselves ("Here's the trade", "One honest caveat"), and end on polished turns. They sound like a skilled adult writer working a conceit rather than students explaining a plan they are proud of.
