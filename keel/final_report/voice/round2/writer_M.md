# Writer M, voice round 2

## Warm-up 1: Franklin's method ("The building is a different matter")

Notes, a few words per sentence:
1. building = different matter
2. most of 2028 deposit -> Treasury fund, matures late 2032; payout = bare minimum she can promise co-sponsors, no doubt
3. rest -> world share fund, thousands of companies, VT; returns help set her maximum contribution
4. so markets move the top up/down as 2031 nears; bottom stays put, Treasuries
5. importantly: half the shares promised to no one
6. = her flexibility: overruns, stronger Taiwan dollar, co-sponsor short
7. other half -> Treasuries before 2031 meetings; any figure = money she already owns

Rebuilt from the notes alone:

The building is a different matter. Most of the 2028 deposit will go into a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032, and what it pays out is the very least you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors – with no doubt at all. The rest goes into a world share fund holding thousands of companies, VT (Vanguard Total World Stock ETF), and how it does will help decide the most you can give. So as 2031 gets closer, markets can push the top of your range up or down, but the bottom won't move, because it rests on Treasuries. And, importantly for you, half of the shares will be promised to no one. That half is your flexibility – for building costs that run over, a stronger Taiwan dollar, or a co-sponsor who falls short. The other half moves into Treasuries before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, so whatever figure you name is money you already own.

Where mine drifted: I tidied it. I dropped "the ever-reliable Treasuries", the little flourish that reaches further than it strictly needs to, swapped their "That means" for a cleaner "So", and explained a link ("because it rests on Treasuries") where they simply set the two things side by side and trusted Laura to see it.

## Warm-up 2: the next page, after "Yours, Caplet"

The letter told Laura what we'll do. This report has the harder job of showing why it works, and where it could still go wrong – because there are a couple of places, and we'd much rather point them out ourselves than leave a judge to find them. Nearly everything that follows comes back to one question we didn't expect to spend so long on: how do you promise money six years before you hand it over, and actually mean it?

=== P1 ===
Laura asked for her 10 payments to be met with 'a high degree of certainty', and the easy reading of that is a percentage. We'd argue that reading is a trap. Our first plan, about three-quarters shares, missed at least one payment in only 3.2% of our model runs. But look at which runs. With a 2028 deposit of $75,000 instead of $150,000, the misses rose to 13.7%; if it never came, to 40.8%. And a writer's deposit is most likely to arrive late and small in exactly the years shares are falling. So we read the phrase differently: no payment should rest on anything that hasn't already been bought.

Laura draws for a living, so she'll know this distinction better than we do. A comic page is pencilled first, and pencil rubs out; only the ink gets printed. Here, ink is a dollar figure Laura already owns in Treasuries that will pay it on its date – set in stone. Pencil is any figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet: her next advance, the 2028 deposit before it lands, her shares, every forecast we make. Most of each payment is inked in January 2027, when the first deposit buys the 10 Treasuries – six years before the first payment goes out. (Image Comics, in 2015, wanted 'three completed issues' before offering a series to shops – much the same instinct.)

The catch is that one line stayed in pencil. The bonds pay interest along the way, and that cash has to wait until the next 1 January; the February 2037 bond matures ten and a half months before the payment it pays for. If the waiting cash earned nothing, a 'certain' plan would have been leaning on future interest rates all along. On today's figures, the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit inks that line: $2,388 is the top-up the IPS promised, and $35,894 buys extra Treasuries, enough to pay all 10 even if the waiting cash earns nothing at all. It's rather like the part of an author's royalties a publisher holds back against returns. If rates simply hold, most of it comes back to Laura – about $45,500 by 2033.

=== P2 ===
You'd expect the split of Laura's shares to be a question of risk. Partly it is. But try other splits and the total she ends up with barely moves. Keep only 30% of the shares back and she could promise co-sponsors about $10,000 more; keep 70% back and she'd promise about $10,000 less. Either way, at the median, her total lands within about $1,100 of where it is now. So the split doesn't decide how much there is – it decides how much of it is promised, and how much stays hers.

That matters because ink only goes one way. An artist can always add a line, but can't take one off cleanly; a mistake gets painted over, and the original keeps the record. A figure Laura has told a co-sponsor works the same way. Each $5,000 more of stated top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps. Half is the middle of that trade. No model chose it. We did, because at half, her flexibility can still do the job it's there for.

And the job is a specific one – building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor who falls short. Centrally, the half kept back is worth about $21,300 in January 2033. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger than in 2031 would need about $11,500 more. Her flexibility covers that in about 93 of 100 model futures, and in about 98 of every 100 two-year stretches of the real Taiwan dollar since 1983. Keep only 30% back, and that falls to about half.

One thing it isn't, sadly, is a hedge. Building costs more often rise in the years shares fall, and a recession is one eraser that takes out several pencil lines at once – share prices, and with them her cushion, and co-sponsors' budgets, the very thing that cushion is meant to cover. In a bad 1-in-20 future her flexibility is about $9,900, less than we'd like. Still, every dollar of that loss lands on money nobody was ever shown – not a payment, and not a figure in anyone's fundraising pitch.

=== P3 ===
Laura's 2031 range comes from a rule, not a guess, and the rule is short. The bottom comes from the Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, which pays about $103,000. It's bought in January 2028, and from then on the bottom is set in stone. The top adds what half the shares will pay once they've moved into Treasuries. Both ends are rounded down, with a little held back, which on today's figures gives $95,000 to $115,000. It's the logic of every page Laura has sent to print: printers run the art past the line where the page is cut, because the blade wanders, and keep the words well inside it. $115,000 is the cut line. Whatever the funds pay beyond it bleeds off into her flexibility, and $95,000 sits safely inside the roughly $103,000 the floor will actually pay.

Until the move, though, the top is pencil. It's worth whatever the market feels like that week, and a co-sponsor told '$115,000' in 2030 could be holding a number the market rubs out a month later. So Laura names nothing until half the shares are in Treasuries – after which a fall in shares can only touch her flexibility, never a figure she has said out loud.

How sure can she be? About the bottom, completely. Erase every pencil line on the page – send the shares to zero – and the 10 payments don't change, the floor still pays about $103,178, and the range simply becomes one figure: $95,000. About the top, today, we lean on forecasts, and they agree remarkably well. Of fifteen investment houses' long-run forecasts for world shares, fourteen give the same $115,000 top on the plan's rule (J.P. Morgan's 7.5% sits in the upper third of them). All of that, though – models, medians, forecasts – is non-photo blue, the pale blue artists used for construction lines because the printing camera couldn't see it. Judges reading this report can see it; co-sponsors in 2031 won't, and won't need to, because by then both of Laura's figures will be money she owns.

=== P4 ===
On 5 October Darren argued that VT was a copout, and that we should be picking stocks and ETFs instead; a Taiwan fund (EWT) came up, and so did gold. It's easy to see the appeal: this is an investing competition, and one fund that buys everything can look like a team declining to invest. We kept VT. The argument still belongs in this report, the way some art books print an artist's pencils beside the finished page: it made us answer the question every holding should have to answer, which is what it's actually for.

First, the size of what we were arguing about. In the plan's numbers, which shares Laura holds is worth about $800 to $1,600 to the outcome; how the market as a whole does is worth $9,881 to $41,258 (1 in 20 each way). Stock-picking would have been an argument about the rounding.

The particular ideas fared worse. EWT is about a fifth one company – TSMC, at 21.89% – and about three-quarters technology, for a fee of 0.59% against VT's 0.06%, and VT already holds Taiwan (about 3.5%). Gold answers inflation, and Laura's payments are fixed in dollars. And picking at all means betting we'd catch the few companies that carry everything: Bessembinder (2018) found that most individual stocks lost to one-month Treasury bills over their lifetimes, and that the market's whole gain came from about 4% of listed companies.

The two reasons that matter most, though, are about Laura rather than markets. Only a fund that owns the whole world can be checked against anyone else's forecasts. Fifteen investment houses publish long-run forecasts for world shares, and fourteen of them give the same $115,000 top on our rule; nobody publishes a forecast for five stocks a team of Sydney students happens to like. And a holding that needs no opinions keeps the IPS's promise of only two reasons to change the plan. Opinions invite trading, and trading is expensive: in Barber and Odean's (2000) study of 66,465 US households, those who traded most earned 11.4% a year against the market's 17.9%. Darren lost the argument, though VT is a good deal better defended for his having made it.

=== NOTES ===
Hearing the team think aloud in the Bible helped more than the IPS alone: the concession-then-turn ("Fair enough", "Then again", "partly it is") and the dry jokes are where I found their pulse, and I tried to carry that rather than any phrase. The passages still feel more even-tempered than the IPS, which gets excited and reassures in a way I only managed in a few places ("sadly", "whatever the market feels like that week"). The conceit worked best where a turn carried a number that needed explaining anyway (the one line that waited, trim and bleed); the art-book line in P4 is the one I'd cut first if it reads as staged. I kept every claim about the team's own past to what the record says, which meant giving up a few warmer details I'd otherwise have reached for.
