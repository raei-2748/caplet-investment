# Reading 3 (voice round 2, 21 passages)

How the IPS sounds to me after two readings: earnest students who are pleased with an idea and say so. It opens by setting up what you'd expect and then turning against it ("until we stopped and considered"). There are bursts of enthusiasm ("So we hedge it!", "But worry not –") and grand adjectives used without irony ("immune to markets", "ever-reliable Treasuries", "no price fluctuations for us to fear"). Spaced dashes are everywhere, small numbers go in digits ("10", "6", "2"), and a few sentences are slightly clumsy ("the bare minimum you'll be able to promise your co-sponsors without doubt"). There is almost no data, and it ends on one crafted line. It is warm and direct, not polished.

### Passage 1
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It sounds like the IPS's writers in a trading note: grand words used earnestly ("restless markets", "near-ironclad guarantee"), a stray "Notably –" dash, and a slightly over-eager claim ("matches the liability perfectly"). The confidence is a little untidy and not quite expert ("duration-matching"), the way a keen student writes, not an editor.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury is there to remove uncertainty rather than make money, and because Laura's payments are fixed in dollars, ordinary Treasuries match them and TIPS aren't needed.

### Passage 2
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is a lively, honest story told as a team ("It was a fair hit", "Why not pick?") that moves quickly from the argument to reasons and then to "what settled it". It is more research-heavy and more tightly built than the IPS, and the "not because X, because Y" turn feels a bit worked, but a team could plausibly sound like this in a longer report.
- **Meaning:** When a teammate called the world fund a copout, the team kept VT because it can be checked against outside forecasts, it needs no opinions, and which shares she holds barely changes the outcome compared with how the market does.

### Passage 3
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The opening echoes the IPS's "You'd think" turn ("You'd expect ... Partly it is"), but then the passage packs in model figures and switches to an ink-and-eraser image that arrives without being set up. It reads like a skilled writer imitating the team's openings, not the team's own loose enthusiasm.
- **Meaning:** Changing how much of the shares is held back hardly changes Laura's total, only how much of it is promised; half is a chosen middle, it covers a stronger Taiwan dollar most of the time, and it is not a hedge.
- **Image:** It got in the way more than it helped. "Ink only goes one way" makes the point that a stated figure can't be taken back, but the painted-over mistake and the eraser across "pencil lines" come out of nowhere here, and I had to translate them.

### Passage 4
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Measured and essay-like, it moves in tidy steps ("First, the size...", "The particular ideas fared worse", "The two reasons that matter most, though...") with adult phrasing ("a good deal better defended for his having made it"). It sounds like a polished older writer, not the bouncy "we" of the IPS.
- **Meaning:** Picking stocks would have argued over a tiny part of the outcome, the specific alternatives were worse, and a whole-world fund lets Laura's top be checked and keeps the plan free of opinions, so VT stayed.
- **Image:** The art-book pencils simile got in the way slightly; it is decoration that adds nothing to the reasoning.

### Passage 5
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves like the IPS: an obvious move, then "why hold half back? Because...". It has conversational asides with some personality ("'responsibly' is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence", "which we trust a little more, since it actually happened"). A couple of defensive phrases ("One honest caveat", "we won't call it optimal") feel written against a checklist, which lowers it a notch.
- **Meaning:** Half the shares are kept unpromised as Laura's cushion for overruns, the currency and short co-sponsors; that half covers a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar most of the time, it trades almost one-for-one against a higher stated top, and it is not a hedge.

### Passage 6
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is efficient and explanatory, and it builds a full analogy system (pencil, ink, eraser, royalties) in a voice that is more report-writer than student. Paragraphs are crammed with exact figures, and the last paragraph jumps to model miss rates without a turn.
- **Meaning:** "High certainty" means no payment depends on markets or other people's money; the first deposit buys the 10 Treasuries in 2027, the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit covers the gap while cash waits between bond and payment, and buying the payments first never missed one.
- **Image:** It helped. The pencil/ink definition gives a clear test (owned in Treasuries or not), and the eraser across three lines explains why a recession is the danger. The royalties comparison is one image too many.

### Passage 7
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain, warm and concrete about the human stakes ("co-sponsors remember", "she's reading her own statement"), and it builds from the easy end to the hard end the way the IPS builds its logic. A few "isn't X – it's Y" turns feel crafted, and calling $115,000 "the central contribution" is confusing.
- **Meaning:** Laura's 2031 range comes from a rule: the bottom is already owned in the 2028 Treasury fund, and the top becomes owned only after half the shares move into Treasuries, so she says nothing until then and is completely sure of what she announces.
- **Range rule (my words):** The bottom is set below what the late-2032 Treasury fund will pay (about $103,000), at $95,000. The top is the bottom plus what half the shares are worth once they have been moved into Treasuries before the 2031 meetings. Both ends are rounded down, and anything paid above the top goes to her flexibility.
- **January 2030, may she say $115,000?** No. The shares haven't moved into Treasuries yet, so any top would be a guess about markets; the passage says she says nothing until half the shares have moved. (Calling $115,000 "the central contribution" muddied this for a moment.)

### Passage 8
- **Verdict:** Surely not
- **Why:** It is clipped and note-like: definitions in a few words, a colon-packed summary, and an aphoristic flip ("Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). Nobody talks to anyone; there is none of the IPS's warmth, asides or "we". It reads like a compressed brief by a different hand.
- **Meaning:** The range's bottom is the owned floor and its top adds half the shares moved into Treasuries; before naming it, any reinvestment shortfall in the ladder is priced and covered from the kept half, and if that isn't enough the range comes down.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom = the floor fund rounded down. Top = the floor plus half the shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it. Before naming, a priced reinvestment shortfall plus a margin is moved from her kept shares into Treasuries; if the kept half can't cover it, the whole range is lowered.
- **January 2030, may she say $115,000?** No. The range is named in 2031 after the shares move, and a number said aloud can't be cut back after a bad year.

### Passage 9
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It has the IPS's particular rhythm: a long run of adjectives ("Defined-maturity, investment-grade, liquid, cheap and transparent about yields"), "we'll simply ignore price noise", "The catch is," and a confident aside about odd-lot bonds. The sentences are a bit stiff and a bit proud, the way students write when they've just learned the terms.
- **Meaning:** Where WInS had no Treasury for a year, the team bought iShares iBonds funds, which act like bonds when held to the end; the trade-off is a payout based on net asset value instead of par, in return for liquidity.

### Passage 10
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is conversational and confident, with human emphasis ("fine.", "not a cent"), it quotes the IPS back to itself, and it argues in plain steps a teenager could defend aloud. The closing Q&A ("So how certain is certain?") is a bit of a set piece, but the IPS also ends on a crafted line.
- **Meaning:** The ten payments rest only on Treasuries bought in 2027, and the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit finishes the ladder and buys extra Treasuries so the payments are met even if waiting cash earns nothing; most of it comes back to Laura if rates hold.

### Passage 11
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is clear and patient, with a "that's the whole rule" plainness and a nice image ("closer to an inventory"), and it ends on a confident line about funders. In places it gets technical and dense (fees beyond 0.07%, 0.27% from ended funds), which is a stretch from the IPS's lightness, but it still sounds like the same "we".
- **Meaning:** The range comes from a rule that counts only money already held in Treasuries, so Laura says nothing until half the shares have moved, and she can then tell co-sponsors she is completely sure.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom = what the late-2032 Treasury fund will pay if it earns nothing more after purchase, minus 0.5% for costs, rounded down to the nearest $5,000. Top = that same sum plus the shares she has moved into Treasuries, worked out the same way and rounded down once.
- **January 2030, may she say $115,000?** No. Before the move the top is only a market guess (it could land anywhere from about $95,000 to $140,000), and a top stated twelve months early isn't actually owned in about 11 of 100 futures.
- **Image:** "Closer to an inventory" helped; it captures in one phrase why the range isn't a forecast.

### Passage 12
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It argues the way the IPS does: it names the comfortable reading, shows why it fails because a writer's income moves with shares, then takes the strict reading. The tone is plain and slightly playful ("Which sounds bad, but isn't"), close to the IPS's "But worry not". It is denser with figures, but the movement is theirs.
- **Meaning:** A model probability isn't enough, because the 2028 deposit fails in the same years shares do, so the plan buys all ten payments in Treasuries by January 2028, with extra Treasuries so waiting cash can earn nothing.

### Passage 13
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It starts like a debater ("We'd argue that reading is a trap") and has some energy. Then it stacks three outside references on one point (pencil and ink, Image Comics in 2015, royalties held back), which feels like someone showing they've done the reading rather than students explaining their plan.
- **Meaning:** "High certainty" should mean nothing rests on anything not yet bought; the Treasuries "ink" most of each payment in 2027, and the first $38,282 of the 2028 deposit inks the waiting-cash gap.
- **Image:** The core pencil/ink image helped, but the Image Comics aside and the royalties comparison got in the way; by the third image I was reading the references, not the plan.

### Passage 14
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It opens like a textbook ("Pension funds cover fixed payments either by...") and stays flat and generic. It says what the IPS's third paragraph says, but with none of its personality ("no price fluctuations for us to fear").
- **Meaning:** The plan uses the buy-one-bond-per-payment method, so the first $300,000 buys a Treasury ladder that pays each January payment without forecasts or trading.

### Passage 15
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is the most writerly passage in the pack. It lays three images from the print shop on top of each other (pencil, bleed and cut line, non-photo blue) and turns to talk to "Judges reading this report". It sounds like a talented adult performing the team's world, not the plain, eager team of the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The range comes from a short rule, the bottom is fixed once the 2028 fund is bought, the top becomes owned only after half the shares move, and only the top leans on forecasts, which mostly agree.
- **Range rule (my words):** The bottom is what the late-2032 Treasury fund pays (about $103,000), rounded down with a little held back, giving $95,000. The top adds what half the shares will pay once moved into Treasuries, rounded down the same way. She names nothing until the move.
- **January 2030, may she say $115,000?** No. The passage says it outright: a co-sponsor told "$115,000" in 2030 holds a pencil number the market could rub out a month later.
- **Image:** Mostly in the way. The cut line gave me a quick picture of why the stated top sits inside what she owns, but three images in one passage, plus non-photo blue, made me decode metaphors instead of following the rule.

### Passage 16
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The core is plain and honest ("Honestly, because we chose it"), with Q&A steps, and is close to passages 5 and 21. Then it brings in "a reason from Laura's own drawing desk", which feels bolted on, and the ending sounds like a writer tying a bow.
- **Meaning:** Half the shares stay unpromised as Laura's flexibility (about $21,300 centrally); half is a chosen middle of a near one-for-one trade, it can't be taken back once stated, and it is weakest when costs rise as shares fall.
- **Image:** It helped a little. "You can always add ink, never take it off cleanly" made the one-way nature of a stated figure easy to see, but its introduction felt forced.

### Passage 17
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is careful and balanced and ends on a polished reversal ("the easiest fund to buy ... the hardest one to argue against"). The non-photo-blue comparison is strained. It sounds like a capable editor, not a team of six with the IPS's bounce.
- **Meaning:** The team kept VT over a Taiwan fund or gold because which shares she holds barely matters, the alternatives are worse, a world fund can be checked against outside forecasts, and it needs no opinions.
- **Image:** It got in the way. Mapping "light blue construction lines" onto "forecasts other houses also publish" took me two reads, and the point is simpler without it.

### Passage 18
- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It is pure IPS voice: one long, breathless sentence that piles clause on clause, a grand phrase used sincerely ("the vain illusion that we can pick winners"), "just 0.06%", "as per JPMorgan", and an opening line that's pleased with itself ("where we allow ourselves to be wrong").
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong because no payment depends on it; it buys the whole market cheaply for growth and answers how generous the facility contribution can be.

### Passage 19
- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It is methodical and clear and moves in report steps ("We tested the range from both directions"), with a measured, adult tone. It combines pencil/ink, erasing and the printer's cut line. It's competent, but the voice is calmer and more managed than the IPS's.
- **Meaning:** The range comes from a fixed rule: the bottom is set when the 2028 Treasury fund is bought and the top when half the shares move into Treasuries; she states it only once both are owned, and it holds even if shares go to zero or other forecasts are used.
- **Range rule (my words):** Bottom = a figure below the roughly $103,000 the late-2032 fund pays, fixed in January 2028. Top = fixed when the other half of the shares moves into Treasuries before the meetings. Both are rounded down, with 0.5% held back, and she states the range only once it is owned.
- **January 2030, may she say $115,000?** No. Until the shares move, the top is just what they're worth that week, and a number announced early could be out of reach by 2031.
- **Image:** It helped somewhat. Here the bleed is mapped clearly (the 0.5% and the rounding are the bleed, the top is the cut line), which is easier to follow than in 15, though "the page still reads" stretches it.

### Passage 20
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is humble, a bit funny and very human ("our answer ... was, more or less, 'all of them'"), with plain reasons in order and a closing line that lands without strain. It sounds like students looking back honestly, close to the IPS's warmth.
- **Meaning:** The team kept VT despite the copout charge because which shares she holds matters little, EWT and gold were poor fits, picks would need trading, and above all a world fund lets co-sponsors check Laura's top against fifteen houses' forecasts.

### Passage 21
- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is plain and logical ("Here's the trade.", "The same dollar can't do both jobs"), with no ornament, and it explains the trade from Laura's side with a candid ending ("We'd rather Laura knew that now"). It is a little more controlled than the IPS, but it shares its directness and its "promised to no one".
- **Meaning:** Half the shares are kept unpromised because each dollar can either be promised or kept as flexibility; half balances a credible promise against a useful cushion, and it isn't a hedge.

## What separates the top from the bottom

The passages at the top sound like the IPS's own students. They use grand words earnestly ("restless markets", "vain illusion", "ever-reliable"), their long sentences pile up clauses, and they are a little untidy and pleased with themselves. They explain an idea rather than polish it. The next tier (10, 12, 20, 2) keeps that talky, honest "we", lets the argument turn against what you'd expect as the IPS's opening does, and allows itself human asides without decoration. The bottom passages are either clipped, note-like summaries built on aphoristic flips (8, 14), or polished, essay-like pieces that stack images from Laura's craft (bleed lines, non-photo blue, Image Comics, art-book pencils) and even address the judges. Those sound like a skilled adult performing the team's voice, and they carry far more precise model statistics than the IPS's writers ever showed.
