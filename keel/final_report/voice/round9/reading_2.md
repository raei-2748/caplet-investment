# Voice round 9, blind reader 2

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly, before the pack. What I hear in it: students explaining a decision to a
person they like, half talking and half arguing. Sentences run long with lists, dashes carry the turns, small
informal words show up ("So, naturally", "worry not", "simply", "In effect", "definitely not"), there is the
occasional exclamation, and a neat line now and then ("a giant stock she can't sell", "ours is to take none"). It is a
little uneven in a way that reads as human.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The informal markers are there ("in other words", "just", contractions), but the passage is mostly
  conditions packed into clauses. The first paragraph's list breaks its grammar in the middle with "since a downturn
  delays a writer's income first", so it reads like someone turning a checklist into sentences, not someone
  explaining to a reader.
- **Meaning:** Only a US default, a missing or much smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs beyond the fee outrunning the
  margin can make a payment short or late, and falling shares cannot. A stated figure breaks only if its Treasuries
  pay less, and her flexibility is a limit, not a promise.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment falls short or arrives late only through a US default, a 2028
  deposit that never comes or comes far smaller, or fund costs beyond the fee outgrowing the margin while waiting cash
  earns too little. No fall in shares, however steep, can do it. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay
  less or late: apart from a default, the funds' other costs would have to pass about 2% of what the two Treasury funds
  behind the top pay, even with waiting cash earning nothing.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Short, plain and natural, with a person's turns of phrase ("so I'm not asking anyone for those", "nothing
  else of mine is on offer", "My broker can send you a statement"). It sounds like a real person writing a quick note,
  and the IPS writers could have drafted it for her. "Though mine still comes first" is the one loose spot: I was not
  sure whether it means she pays anyway.
- **Meaning:** Laura's ten payments are already bought; she will give $115,000 for the building in December 2032, all
  of it already in Treasuries; and she asks co-sponsors for a pledge to the building total and yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** Write $115,000. Of that, $95,000 has been held in Treasuries since January 2028
  and the rest since January 2031. If the pledges haven't reached the total by December 2032, no co-sponsor has to
  pay. Hers "still comes first", which I take to mean she pays regardless, though the wording leaves it open.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Clear and complete, but a little stiff in places: it switches between contractions and "I am asking", "it
  will not rise", "does not depend", and lays things out formally ("The first is ... The second is ..."). It sounds like
  a careful drafter writing for her rather than her speaking. Still plausible from the IPS writers, but further from
  their looseness.
- **Meaning:** The ten payments are guaranteed and already bought. Laura will give $115,000 for the building in
  December 2032, held in Treasuries in two stages, and asks for pledges to the building total plus yearly programme
  money, all to the residency.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** Write $115,000. $95,000 of it has been in Treasuries since January 2028 and the
  rest since January 2031, when shares moved into Treasuries. If pledges don't reach the total by December 2032, none of
  the co-sponsors pays, and her own $115,000 comes first and does not depend on anyone else.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This is the IPS's way of moving: a practical fact, then a dash and "so", then a cheerful "we'll simply",
  then "The catch is" with the trade-off owned. The adjectives are a slightly stacked list ("Defined-maturity,
  investment-grade, liquid, cheap and transparent"), the kind of thing students do. It reads like a trading note more
  than a report section, but the hands sound the same.
- **Meaning:** Where no Treasury bond matures in a needed year, they used iBond Treasury funds, which act like bonds held
  to the end. The cost is a payout at net asset value instead of par, and the gain is liquidity.
- **Check question:** None fits.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It opens with a strong, slightly playful claim ("where we allow ourselves to be wrong"), runs one long
  breathless sentence with a borrowed phrase and "as per JPMorgan", and ends on a dash and a question, very close to
  the IPS's energy and its "world share fund of thousands of companies". It sounds like a student who believes the
  argument and enjoys making it.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding that no payment depends on, bought because it is the whole market at low cost
  rather than a bet on picking winners, and it decides how generous the building gift can be.
- **Check question:** None fits.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** A clear signpost ("Three things could do that:") and an even pace, with one picture set apart in its own
  paragraph. Overall it reads like a careful third-person summary, closer to a report than to the IPS's talk. It is
  tidy but low on the IPS writers' personality, and "the range down to one figure, the bottom" is compressed.
- **Meaning:** A payment fails only through a default, a much smaller or missing 2028 deposit, or extra fund costs
  beyond the margin. A stated figure would need extra costs above about 2%, and shares at zero still leave every
  payment and the bottom of the range standing.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A US default, a 2028 deposit that doesn't come or comes much smaller, or
  fund costs beyond the fee eating through the small margin could make a payment short or late. Shares cannot, since
  no promise rests on a share price. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or late: a default, or
  costs beyond the fee passing about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay.
- **Image:** It helped: erasing the pencil and seeing the page still read is a quick way to see that a share crash
  leaves the promises alone, though "the range down to one figure, the bottom" needed a second read.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Clean, calm and impersonal, like a textbook or consultant's explainer: "Pension funds cover fixed payments
  either by ... or by ...", then "We do the second". The last two sentences echo the IPS's "each maturing just before
  it's needed ... these pay what they say", but without the asides, dashes or warmth that make the IPS sound like
  these particular people.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds meet fixed payments, they chose one bond per payment held to maturity, so
  Laura's first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasuries and no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question:** None fits.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This one sounds most like a person talking at a first meeting: it starts with "Thank you for seeing me",
  uses short full sentences, contractions and "either way", and nothing sounds technical. It is plain, as Laura's own
  words should be, and the IPS writers could well have drafted it for her.
- **Meaning:** Laura's running-cost payments are already paid for. She will give $115,000 for the building, already held
  in Treasuries, and asks for building pledges and yearly programme money, all going to the residency.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** Write $115,000. $95,000 has been in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest
  since January 2031. If the pledges aren't all made by December 2032, none of the co-sponsors has to pay, and her own
  $115,000 comes first either way.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It starts with the picture before it has said what could go wrong, then gives the list of risks with a
  "because" clause dropped into the middle. "And her word to co-sponsors with it" is awkward. The pieces are all
  there, but the order and joins feel assembled rather than written through by one person explaining.
- **Meaning:** With shares at zero the ten payments and the range's bottom still stand. Only a default, a missing or
  smaller 2028 deposit, or excess fund costs could hurt them, and her flexibility is a limit whose running out breaks
  nothing.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A US default, a missing or far smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs beyond
  the fee outrunning the margin while waiting cash earns too little could make a payment short or late. Shares could
  not. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or later, which, leaving default aside, takes costs
  beyond the fees passing about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay.
- **Image:** It helped a little: "rub out every pencil line" pictures shares at zero well, but placed before the risks
  it interrupted the argument, and the plain clause "since both stand on Treasuries she already owns" did the real
  work.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Short, confident sentences that each carry one idea, with well-made turns ("a number said aloud can't
  quietly be halved after a bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). The IPS writers do write
  lines like that, but here they come one after another, so it sounds more polished and more edited than the IPS.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the floor, rounded down, to the floor plus half her shares moved into Treasuries
  before she names it. Any shortfall from reinvesting interest at low rates is priced and covered from her kept shares
  first, and only if that fails does the range come down.
- **Check question:** None fits exactly. It tells what lowers the range before it is stated, not what breaks a figure
  already stated.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** The IPS's habits are all here: "In other words", dashes doing the work of commas and colons, a touch of
  personality ("anything the restless markets might decide to do"), an odd "Notably –", and a student's loose use of a
  technical term ("duration-matching" for what is really bond-per-payment matching). It sounds like the same eager,
  slightly unpolished team.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury bond exists to remove uncertainty, not to grow wealth. They read "high degree of
  certainty" as a near-guarantee, and because the payments are fixed in nominal dollars, nominal Treasuries fit and TIPS
  are not needed.
- **Check question:** None fits.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The smoothest of the limits passages. The risks come one per sentence, ending on a short line ("No fall in
  share prices can.") that echoes the IPS's "A fall in the market is not one of them.", and the dashes carry turns the
  way the IPS's do. It is more controlled than the IPS but sounds like the same people writing more carefully.
- **Meaning:** Only a default, a missing or smaller 2028 deposit, or excess fund costs can make a payment short or
  late. A stated figure needs a default or costs above about 2% to fail, and falling shares only lower what she can
  state.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A US default, a 2028 deposit that never comes or comes far smaller, or
  fund costs beyond the fee outgrowing the small margin could make a payment short or late. No fall in share prices
  can. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or late: short of a default, costs beyond the fees
  would have to pass about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay. The margin is only part of that room,
  and rounding down made the rest.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** A real letter, framed like the IPS ("Dear ..." through to a sign-off), and it opens by telling the reader
  what the residency is, which a stranger would need. It is clear and courteous but long and careful, with every point
  closed off; it sounds like a well-drafted letter for her more than her own easy voice.
- **Meaning:** Laura introduces the 2033 residency, says her running-cost payments are already bought and guaranteed,
  commits $115,000 to the building from Treasuries, and asks for building pledges and yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** Write $115,000. $95,000 has been held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest
  since January 2031, when her shares moved into Treasuries. If pledges aren't all made by December 2032, none of the
  co-sponsors has to pay, and her own $115,000 comes first and doesn't depend on anyone else.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest have a person in them. Each takes a position and enjoys it, and its turns come through
dashes, a "so", a "the catch is" or a closing question, with small loose words ("simply", "as per", "restless
markets") that sound like students arguing a case out loud, as the IPS does. The lowest pack several conditions into
each sentence. Their lists lose their grammar halfway through, and they read like a checklist made into prose with no
one being spoken to. Passage 7 is the other kind of low: perfectly clean but impersonal, a textbook voice. Laura's
notes sit in the middle: plain and natural, which is right for her, but by design they carry little of the team's own
spark, so the spoken one (8) ranks above the more formal ones (3, 13).
