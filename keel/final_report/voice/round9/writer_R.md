## Warm-up (IPS paragraph "In January 2027 we’ll use your first deposit")

Notes, one line per sentence:

2. Held to the end – pay what they say – price swings don’t scare us.
3. Today’s yields – about $300k – i.e. the whole first deposit.
4. Don’t worry – plan isn’t betting on yields staying put!
5. Yields fall, cost over $300k – 2028 deposit completes it.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 your first deposit buys 10 Treasuries – a T-Bond or an iShares iBonds fund for each year, whichever fits best – each one maturing just before its payment. Held to the end, they pay what they say, so their prices along the way don’t worry us. At today’s yields that costs about $300,000, which is your whole first deposit. Don’t worry, though – the plan isn’t a bet on yields holding still! If they fall and the ten cost more than $300,000, we’ll simply finish the job with the 2028 deposit.

Where mine drifted: it came out plainer and a little tidier, losing “no price fluctuations for us to fear” and “in other words”, which is where the original’s voice actually sits. I also swapped “complete it” for “finish the job”, an idiom the team didn’t reach for.

=== P1 ===
Laura’s ten payments fail only if one is short, or late, on its 1 January. A US default could do that. So could a 2028 deposit that never comes, or comes far smaller (Section 2), or fund costs beyond the fee outgrowing the small margin we held back. No fall in share prices can.

A figure she has stated fails if the Treasuries behind it pay less, or late – in other words, what breaks is her word to co-sponsors. Short of a default, that would take fund costs beyond the fees passing about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay; the margin is only part of that room, and rounding down made the rest. Falling shares before the move only lower what she can state.

Nobody is promised her flexibility, so if it runs out, nothing breaks – it’s a limit. On today’s rates, and counting whatever the funds pay above the top, it covers about a 25% rise in the US-dollar cost of the part of the building she pays for, though the worst two-year spans since 1991 rose by more.

The case’s five areas fold into these: our assumed return barely moves the top, timing is the 2028 deposit, no outside money sits under the payments, and inflation changes what they buy, not whether they’re paid.

The IPS’s two reasons to change the plan – a missed deposit, a Treasury default – are a separate list: exits for when it can’t be carried out.

=== P3 ===
Dear [name],

I’m opening an artists’ residency in Taiwan in 2033, and my part of its running costs is already paid for: $50,000 each January from 2033 to 2042, each from a US Treasury held for me that pays out just before that January. I bought them in January 2027 and completed them in January 2028. They’re guaranteed, and I’m not asking anyone for them.

I will give $115,000 towards the building, paid in December 2032, before building starts in 2033. Of that, $95,000 has been held for me in Treasuries since January 2028, and the rest since January 2031, when my shares moved into Treasuries. It’s fixed in US dollars, so it won’t rise if the building costs more.

I’m asking for two things. The first is money for the building, pledged towards the one total the residency has set in its budget. If pledges towards that total aren’t all made by December 2032, none of the co-sponsors has to pay; my own $115,000 comes first and doesn’t depend on anyone else. The second is yearly money for the residency’s programme from 2033, one year at a time, which is how Taiwan’s funders give.

Both go to the residency, its own organisation in Taiwan, which owns the site and runs the building. I’m not asking anyone to match my figure, and neither the running-cost payments nor the rest of my money is part of the offer.

The firm that holds my Treasuries will send a statement on request.

Warm regards,
Laura

=== NOTES ===
P1 and P2 felt more compressed than the team’s writing: the part has to carry three promises, two figures, five areas and the two reasons in under 250 words, so the quick asides the IPS enjoys (“worry not”, “Fair enough”) had no room, and the dashes and one “in other words” are as close as it gets. The turn helped rather than got in the way: one sentence replaced the two separate “shares can’t touch this” lines in P1 and covered the payments and the range together, though “before the meetings”, which it needs to stay true, slows it a little. P3 and P4 are plainer than the IPS on purpose, in Laura’s first person and full sentences; to fit P4’s length I left out the 2027 and 2028 purchase dates and the line about the rest of her money, so the spoken version is a slightly shorter set of the same facts in the same order. P2 (255 words) and P4 (179) run a little over their ranges.
