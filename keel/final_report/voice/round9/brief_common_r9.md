## Topic one (P1 plain; P2 one turn): assumptions and limits, failure first
Wharton asks the report to "clearly identify important assumptions, uncertainties, and limitations", and the case names
five areas: investment performance, the timing of cash flows, outside funding, inflation, and flexibility. Round 7 and
8's readers lost this part when it carried twenty ideas in two hundred words. This time the part is built around three
things Laura's plan promises or keeps, and for each says: what failing would mean, what could make it fail, and (for
two of them) one figure. Then the five areas in one sentence or two, and then, as a separate point, the IPS's two
reasons to change the plan. At most two figures in the whole passage: the two given below.

1. **The ten payments** ($50,000 each January, 2033 to 2042, for the residency's running costs). Most of each is owned
   in Treasuries from January 2027; all ten from January 2028, when the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them.
   The standard they meet: paid even if cash waiting between a bond's end and its January earns nothing, with a small
   margin held back for fund costs beyond the funds' fee. They fail only if a payment is short of $50,000, or late, on
   its 1 January. What could do that: a US default (the IPS's own condition, named here once; watched as any Treasury
   payment more than five business days late); no 2028 deposit, or one far smaller than planned (Section 2 gives the
   size; say "(Section 2)" and give no figure here), because the deposit comes from income paid on contract dates
   (advances, speaking fees, licences) which a downturn delays first; fund costs beyond the fee bigger than that margin
   while cash waiting earns too little; and, late rather than short, a broker failing while the account holds more than
   the broker-failure insurance covers. What cannot: any fall in share prices.
2. **A figure Laura has stated to co-sponsors** (the bottom of her range, owned from January 2028; the top, owned once
   half her shares move into Treasuries in January 2031, before the meetings). It fails if the Treasuries she owns for
   it pay less than the figure, or pay late: what would break is her word to co-sponsors. What could do that: the same
   default, and fund costs beyond the two fees bigger than the room left under the figure (the margin of 0.5% held back,
   plus the rounding down to a round figure). **Figure:** those costs would have to pass about 2% of what the two
   Treasury funds behind the top pay (the 2032 fund bought in January 2028 and the one the moved shares buy), even if
   cash waiting earns nothing: the 0.5% held back is only part of it, because rounding down to $115,000 leaves the rest.
   What cannot: a missed deposit (then no range is stated, since nothing is stated before it is owned); a fall in yields
   before January 2028, which lowers the bottom she will be able to state, never one she has stated; a fall in share
   prices, which before the move lowers the figure she can state and breaks nothing.
3. **Her flexibility** (half the shares, promised to no one): a limit, not a promise. It runs out if its uses together
   outrun it, and that breaks no promise. Its uses: building costs that overrun and a stronger Taiwan dollar (tested,
   not forecast); a co-sponsor falling short (not sized), whose budget can fall in the same downturn as shares. It
   shrinks if shares fall before 2033; the honest limit is a share slump before the meetings followed by a jump in costs.
   **Figure:** on today's market rates, her flexibility plus anything the funds pay above the top, about $28,700, covers
   a rise of about 25% in the US-dollar cost of the part of a building budget her $115,000 pays for; the worst two-year
   spans since 1991 rose by more. That is a limit she meets, never a change of plan.
4. **The five areas, briefly:** investment performance: the return assumed for world shares (J.P. Morgan's, given with
   the range's table) barely moves the top she states; what shares do before the meetings sets it. Timing: the 2028
   deposit in full at the start of 2028 (point 1). Outside funding: none for the payments or the bottom; co-sponsors'
   money is point 3's. Inflation: the payments are fixed in dollars, so inflation decides what they buy, never whether
   they are paid; building costs are point 3's. Flexibility: point 3.
5. **The IPS's two reasons to change the plan, a separate list** (a missed deposit, a Treasury default): they are the
   exits for when the plan cannot be carried out, not the ways a promise can fail, and a fall in the market is not one
   of them. Never write "only two things can break the plan".

**P2's turn, the same for both writers: erase every pencil line.** Use it once, where it does its job, glossed in the
same sentence: put the shares at zero (rub out every pencil line) and the page still reads: all ten payments made, and
the range shrunk to one figure, the bottom. Then carry on plainly. Pencil stays a figure (the shares, the top before
the move), never a whole plan; never call the January 2027 purchase, the IPS or the Trading Notes pencil.

## Topic two (P3 written; P4 spoken): Laura's words to co-sponsors in 2031
Who reads it: someone at a foundation or a company's giving office, in Taiwan or the US, deciding whether to take a
meeting and what figure to put in a paper for their board. They read for thirty seconds. Checked facts, in the order
the words should follow:
1. **Her ten operating payments are already owned.** $50,000 each January, 2033 to 2042, for the residency's running
   costs, each paid by a US Treasury held for her that matures just before its January. Bought in January 2027 and
   completed in January 2028. Say "held for me", never "in my name". She is not asking anyone for these. She may call
   her Treasuries guaranteed, with nothing attached.
2. **Her facility contribution, one figure, then what it rests on.** One sentence that promises: she will give $115,000
   towards the building, paid in December 2032, before building starts in 2033 (amount, purpose and when, in that one
   sentence). No "about" before it, and never "intend", "expect", "hope", "aim", "at least" or "up to" on it. Then one
   sentence of fact under it: $95,000 of it has been held for her in Treasuries since January 2028, and the rest since
   January 2031, when her shares moved into Treasuries. Name the year every time ("since January 2031", never "since
   January"). Never "secured". At most, if it fits, "with a margin kept for the funds' running costs"; no rates, no
   percentages.
3. **It is fixed in US dollars.** It does not rise if the building costs more. One dated size in Taiwan dollars may sit
   beside it, labelled as a size on that date and not a promise: about NT$3.77 million at 32.768 Taiwan dollars to the
   US dollar on 6 November 2026.
4. **The ask, in two parts.** (a) Money for the building, pledged towards one total that the residency sets in its
   budget before these meetings. Say the condition as what happens, and whose it is: if pledges towards that total are
   not all made by December 2032, none of the co-sponsors has to pay. Her own $115,000 comes first and does not depend on
   anyone else. Do not invent the total. (b) Yearly money for the residency's programme from 2033, which is how Taiwan's
   funders give: one year at a time.
5. **Who receives it.** The residency, its own organisation in Taiwan, which owns the site and runs the building.
6. **What she never asks for or offers.** Her ten payments are not on the table; she does not ask anyone to match her
   figure; the rest of her money is not part of the offer (do not name or size it).
7. **Proof.** A statement from the firm that holds her Treasuries, on request.
Words to leave out, because round 8's readers heard them as an older fundraiser's, not the team's: "draw", "close date",
"conditional pledge", "release", "custodian", "tranche", "ladder", "maturity", "net asset value", "duration". The spoken
version is in full sentences: no fragments such as "Bought in 2027, finished in 2028." or "Then two asks." No
exclamation marks, no slogans, no closing aphorism. Never mention the team, Wharton, models, probabilities or the
report.
