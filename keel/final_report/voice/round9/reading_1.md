# Voice round 9, blind reader 1

Read the IPS twice (the second time slowly), then the pack of 13 passages.

What I heard in the IPS: a warm, quick "we" writing to Laura, with momentum and small flourishes ("So, naturally",
"worry not", "Fair enough; that's your job"), spaced dashes for asides and turns, a habit of explaining why before
how, some cheerful overreach ("immune to markets", "ever-reliable Treasuries"), and short closing lines that land a
point ("A fall in the market is not one of them.").

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** It moves exactly like the IPS: it opens with a lively claim, turns on a dash, runs one long enthusiastic
  sentence with a slightly casual citation ("as per JPMorgan"), and ends on a question that puts the holding in its
  place. It sounds like a confident student who enjoys the idea.
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to be wrong because no payment rests on it; it buys the whole world market
  cheaply rather than picking winners, and its job is to decide how generous the building gift can be.
- **Check question:** none fits.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Plain, calm first-person speech that sounds like a real person talking ("topped them up", "My broker can send
  you a statement"); it is short and sensible, though it has none of the team's own spark, and one line ("nothing else
  of mine is on offer") is a little stiff.
- **Meaning:** Laura's running-cost payments are already bought, she will give $115,000 to the building in December 2032,
  and she asks co-sponsors for a pledge to the building total and yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** A board paper should carry $115,000. $95,000 of it has been held in Treasuries
  since January 2028 and the rest since January 2031. If pledges have not reached the total by December 2032, no
  co-sponsor has to pay; her own gift "still comes first", which I read as still being paid, though the wording leaves
  that slightly open.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It sounds spoken, as at a first meeting ("Thank you for seeing me"), and is laid out the way a careful
  student would draft for someone else ("I'm asking for two things"); clear and natural, warmer than 2, but still
  plainer than the IPS's own voice.
- **Meaning:** The ten payments are already paid for in Treasuries, her building gift is $115,000 in December 2032, and
  she asks for a building pledge and yearly programme money, without asking anyone to match her.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000; $95,000 held in Treasuries since January 2028, the remaining $20,000
  since January 2031; if the pledges are not all made by December 2032, none of the co-sponsors has to pay, and her own
  $115,000 is paid first either way.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** A clear, teacherly paragraph that sets two methods side by side and picks one with a reason; it echoes the IPS
  ("Held to the end, these pay what they say") in a cooler, more textbook register, like the same writers on a
  careful day.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan uses one bond per payment held to maturity,
  which needs no forecast or trading, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question:** none fits.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** The student "we" again, practical and a little breezy ("we'll simply ignore price noise", "The catch is,"),
  with a pile-up of adjectives and a dash turn that admits a cost and names what is gained; it reads like the IPS
  writers explaining a trade they made.
- **Meaning:** Where no Treasury bond matures in a needed year, the team used iBond funds, which act like bonds held to
  the end but pay out a net asset value rather than par, in exchange for better liquidity.
- **Check question:** none fits.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** A complete, courteous letter that reads like something a careful team would draft for a client to sign; it
  echoes the IPS's "complete it with the 2028 deposit" and is easy to follow, but it is long and evenly formal, with
  little of the team's own lift.
- **Meaning:** Laura tells a co-sponsor her running-cost payments are already bought, commits $115,000 to the building,
  and asks for a building pledge and yearly programme money, with nothing else of hers on offer.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000; $95,000 held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest since January
  2031, when her shares moved into Treasuries; if pledges are not all made by December 2032, no co-sponsor has to pay,
  and her own $115,000 comes first and does not depend on anyone else.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Dense and compressed, as if every clause had been squeezed to carry a condition; one sentence loses its
  thread ("a missing or far smaller 2028 deposit (Section 2), because a downturn delays a writer's income first, or fund
  costs..."), and the tone is a cautious analyst's, not the IPS's warm and quick "we".
- **Meaning:** The payments can fail only through a US default, a missing or much smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs
  outrunning the margin; a stated figure fails only if its Treasuries pay less, and her flexibility is a limit whose
  running out breaks nothing.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or late through a US default, a 2028 deposit
  that never comes or comes far smaller, or fund costs beyond the fee outrunning the margin while waiting cash earns too
  little; shares, even at zero, could not. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or later: short
  of a default, costs beyond the funds' fees would have to pass about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top
  pay.
- **Image:** It helped a little, because it glosses itself ("put her shares at zero") and gives a picture of the worst
  case; but "the page still reads ... the range down to one figure" took me two reads.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** Crisp, short declarative sentences with a memorable turn ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a
  bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"), much like the IPS's habit of landing a point at the
  end; it is tighter and more polished than the IPS, and its "one weakness" sits a little oddly beside the IPS's "no
  price fluctuations for us to fear".
- **Meaning:** In 2031 the range runs from the owned floor to that plus half the shares (moved into Treasuries first),
  and any reinvestment shortfall is priced and covered from her kept shares before she names it.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits, the nearest fit):** A payment could fall short if rates fall and stay low and
  the ladder's reinvested interest comes up short, but that gap is priced and moved into Treasuries before she names
  the range; a stated figure would fall only if her kept money could not cover the gap, in which case the range comes
  down before it is stated rather than after.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Same careful, legalistic build as 7, though a little more readable thanks to a few plain lines ("No fall in
  shares, however steep, can do that", "in other words"); it still sounds like a cautious analyst listing conditions,
  and the "since a downturn delays a writer's income first" clause is wedged in awkwardly.
- **Meaning:** Only a default, a missing or smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs outgrowing the margin can break a
  payment; a stated figure breaks only if its Treasuries pay less; falling shares shrink what she can state, never what
  she has.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or late through a US default, a 2028 deposit
  that never comes or is far smaller, or fund costs beyond the fee outgrowing the margin while waiting cash earns too
  little; no fall in shares can. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or late, which short of a
  default needs the funds' other costs to pass about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** Clearer than 7 and 9 (it counts "Three things" and names them), but it is still a compressed, qualified list
  of failure conditions in an even, cautious tone, with a cryptic explanation ("because rounding down left room beyond
  the margin") and the image dropped in mid-way as a recap.
- **Meaning:** Three things can break a payment; a stated figure needs costs above about 2% of the funds' payout to
  fail; with shares at zero everything promised still stands; flexibility is a limit that can run out without breaking
  anything.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or late through a US default, a 2028 deposit
  that does not come or comes much smaller, or fund costs beyond the fee eating through the margin; shares at zero could
  not. A stated figure falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or late, which besides a default needs extra costs
  above about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay.
- **Image:** It helped as a summary of the worst case, since "put the shares at zero" says what it means, but coming
  after the failure list it felt like a repeat rather than a way in.

### Passage 11

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The most readable of the four limits passages: short sentences that build ("A US default could do that. So
  could ... No fall in share prices can.") in the same rhythm as the IPS's "A fall in the market is not one of them";
  the middle paragraph is still dense and careful, so it sounds like the team at its most disciplined rather than its
  most natural.
- **Meaning:** A payment fails only through a default, a missing or smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs outgrowing the
  margin; a stated figure would need costs above about 2% of the funds' payout; flexibility is a limit, not a promise.
- **Check question (assumptions and limits):** A payment could fall short or late through a US default, a missing or far
  smaller 2028 deposit, or fund costs beyond the fee outgrowing the margin; no fall in share prices can. A stated figure
  falls short only if its Treasuries pay less or late, which short of a default needs fund costs beyond the fees above
  about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay (the margin plus the room left by rounding down).
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 12

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** The same content as 6 but stiffer: few contractions ("I will not ask", "I am asking"), a one-sentence paragraph
  standing alone, and an even, formal register that reads more like a template statement than like people who wrote
  "worry not" and "Fair enough"; the team could have drafted it, but nothing in it sounds like them.
- **Meaning:** Laura's ten payments are bought and guaranteed, she will give $115,000 to the building in December 2032,
  and she asks for a building pledge and yearly programme money, with no matching asked.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000; $95,000 held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest since January
  2031; if pledges do not reach the total by December 2032, no co-sponsor has to pay, and her own $115,000 comes first
  and does not depend on anyone else.
- **Image:** not used.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** Unmistakably the IPS's student voice: a concession that turns ("won't create wealth, but they will remove
  uncertainty"), "In other words", "Notably –", a vivid phrase ("anything the restless markets might decide to do"), and
  a slightly over-eager claim ("near-ironclad guarantee", "matches the liability perfectly").
- **Meaning:** The Treasury bonds are there to remove uncertainty rather than to grow money, and because the payments
  are fixed in dollars, ordinary nominal Treasuries match them and TIPS are not needed.
- **Check question:** none fits.
- **Image:** not used.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest talk as "we", explain why a holding exists, and move with the IPS's energy: a lively
opening, a dash that turns the thought, a casual aside ("as per JPMorgan", "The catch is,", "restless markets"), even a
bit of cheerful overreach. The lowest are careful lists of conditions in which every clause carries a qualifier
("costs beyond the funds' fees would need to pass about 2% of what the two Treasury funds behind the top pay"), with
cross-references and no warmth, so they sound like an analyst or a lawyer rather than six students writing to a client.
Laura's words sit in the middle: plain and believable as a team's careful draft, and the closer they come to spoken
speech with contractions, the more they sound like the IPS writers. In the limits passages the pencil image helped only
where it explained itself at once, and it did not make the dense writing around it sound any more like the team.
