# The facts of the case and the plan

This sheet gives the writers of Team Caplet's Final Report the facts to write from: what happens, who does it, when, with what money, and what it pays.
It was rebuilt on Thursday 19 November 2026, after the comprehension study.

## The client and the case

- Laura Gao is a Wharton graduate who left a job in tech to write and illustrate books, among them *The Wuhan I Know* and the graphic memoir *Messy Roots*.
- She is planning a creative residency in Taiwan for artists, writers and designers. It opens in 2033.
- Laura puts $300,000 into her portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028. The second sum comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, so it depends on her career going well. No other money goes in or comes out before 2033.
- From 2033 to 2042 Laura pays $50,000 at the start of each year to run the residency: ten payments in all. The case says the ten must be met "with a high degree of certainty", from the portfolio alone.
- Beyond the ten payments, Laura wants to give as much as she responsibly can towards the residency's building, while keeping flexibility as the project develops. The case calls this money her facility contribution.
- Other funders, the co-sponsors, also back the residency. In 2031 Laura meets them (the 2031 meetings) and tells them, in dollars, the range her facility contribution will fall in and how confident she is. Promising too much would damage her credibility. The case asks the team to draft part of what she says to them.
- Laura has said in public: "As an author and illustrator, I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves" (*Climbing* magazine, 4 March 2025).
- Team Caplet is a team of students in Sydney. It set out its plan for Laura in an Investment Policy Statement (the IPS), a short letter to her, submitted on 6 November 2026, US time. The Final Report must stay consistent with it.
- Laura's plan may use only stocks, ETFs (funds traded like shares) and Treasury bonds listed on WInS, the competition's trading simulator, where the team placed its orders. TIPS and STRIPS, two other kinds of Treasury security, are not allowed this year.
- The plan acts on three dates: January 2027, January 2028 and January 2031.

## 2027: the ten payments are bought

- In January 2027 Laura's first deposit buys ten Treasuries, one for each payment, each maturing before the 1 January it pays. January 2027 is six years before the first payment goes out.
- The US government guarantees every Treasury. A Treasury bond held to the end pays exactly its stated interest and its face value (the sum it repays at the end), on fixed dates.
- The payments of 2033 to 2037 are funded by iShares iBonds Treasury funds (IBTM, IBTO, IBTP, IBTQ and IBTR), and those of 2038 to 2042 by Treasury bonds. The IPS chose "whichever works best for each year". WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036.
- An iBonds fund holds Treasuries that mature in one year. It closes in mid-December of that year and pays its holders what its holdings are then worth. An iBonds fund's payout is close to a fixed amount but not exactly one, because in its last months the fund holds cash, and the payout depends on what that cash earns. Each fund also charges a small yearly fee.
- A Treasury's yield is the interest rate it earns if held to the end. When yields fall, the same payments cost more to buy.
- Today's rates are the Treasury yields at the close on 6 November 2026, the day trading in WInS froze, with later rates following what the market then expected.
- Each Treasury pays interest along the way. The ten reach $50,000 each only if that interest is reinvested at today's rates until each payment falls due.
- One bond, maturing in February 2037, pays out ten and a half months before the 1 January 2038 payment it funds. Its money waits as cash until then. Money that a bond or fund has paid out, and that is waiting for the 1 January payment it belongs to, is called cash waiting.
- At today's rates the ten payments cost about $301,500, slightly more than Laura's whole first deposit of $300,000.
- The bonds and funds the plan actually holds cost a little more than that, because of the funds' fee. At those prices, the part of the ten that $300,000 cannot buy costs $2,388 when it is bought in January 2028. This $2,388 is the top-up.
- The IPS, which calls the ten Treasuries a ladder, planned for this: "Even if yields fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up being above $300,000, we’ll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit."
- If yields fell 1 point from their 6 November level before January 2027, the same ten would cost about $334,000, and the 2028 deposit would complete them a year later.

## 2028: the second deposit

- The 2028 deposit arrives in January 2028. At today's rates its first $39,162 goes to the ten payments, in this order:
  - $2,388 buys the top-up, which completes the ten.
  - $36,774 buys the extra Treasuries: more of the bonds and funds that pay the later payments. They are sized so that all ten payments are met even if cash waiting earns nothing at all. They are also sized for every iBonds fund paying out 0.5% less than its holdings are worth, an allowance for the funds' costs beyond their fee.
- The operating reserve is all the Treasury money set aside for the ten payments: the ten Treasuries plus the extra Treasuries. The case asks the team to recommend its size. The extra Treasuries are the part the team added above what the ten need when their interest earns today's rates.
- At today's rates, most of the rest of the deposit buys more of the late-2032 fund: IBTM, the iBonds fund that closes in mid-December 2032 and already holds the money for the 2033 payment. This January 2028 purchase is called the floor, because its payout sets the lowest figure Laura can state in 2031.
- At today's rates the floor pays about $102,000 in mid-December 2032. The team's Trading Notes, its written notes on its WInS trades, said of this fund: "It holds 2032 Treasuries, so the timing fits, although the final payout is not fixed."
- The rest of the deposit buys VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF, one fund of shares in thousands of companies around the world.

## Before the 2031 meetings

- At the 2031 meetings Laura tells co-sponsors a range for her facility contribution: the 2031 range. She states it in $5,000 steps, and each end is rounded down. Its lower end is called the bottom, and its upper end the top.
- Today's figures are what the team's model gives at today's rates, with world shares growing 7.5% a year, J.P. Morgan's long-term forecast for world shares. This is the central case. On today's figures the 2031 range is $95,000 to $115,000.
- The same model also simulates many possible futures for interest rates and share prices. "In 11 of 100 model futures" means in about 11% of them.
- The bottom is what the floor will pay, counted cautiously: as if none of the interest the late-2032 fund pays out after the floor is bought earns anything more. That amount is then cut by 0.5% for the late-2032 fund's costs beyond its fee, and rounded down. Counting cautiously, cutting 0.5% and rounding down turn the floor's expected $102,000 into a bottom of $95,000.
- The bottom is fixed when the floor is bought in January 2028, and it does not change after that. Before then, the bottom Laura will be able to state depends on Treasury yields on the day of purchase: higher yields, a higher bottom.
- In January 2031, before the 2031 meetings, half the value of Laura's shares is sold, and the money buys more of the late-2032 fund. This step is the 2031 move.
- The other half of the shares stays in VT and is promised to no one. The IPS calls it her flexibility.
- The top is worked out the same way on both purchases together: what the floor and the moved half will pay, counted cautiously, cut by 0.5%, and rounded down once. On today's figures the top is $115,000. Share prices up to the 2031 move decide how far above the bottom the top reaches.
- After the 2031 move, Treasuries Laura owns in the late-2032 fund will pay both the bottom and the top, so the 2031 range she states is certain. In every model future the late-2032 fund pays at least the top, so her facility contribution is the top: $115,000 on today's figures. Co-sponsors plan around the top.
- The bottom is the part of that $115,000 held in Treasuries since January 2028. The 2031 move added the rest.
- The 0.5% cut and the rounding down together leave the top about 2% below what the late-2032 fund will pay on both purchases, even if its cash earns nothing. The 2% is a total over the fund's life, not a yearly figure. The late-2032 fund would pay less than the top only if its costs beyond its fee took more than that.
- Laura states no figure before the 2031 move. If she stated a top a year before the 2031 move, the move would buy less than that top in about 11 of 100 model futures.
- The IPS chose to keep half the shares back. The model shows what other splits would do, on today's figures:
  - Keep 30% of the shares back instead of half, and Laura could state a top about $5,000 higher. But her flexibility alone would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half of model futures, against about 93 of 100 with half.
  - Keep 70% back, and she would state a top about $10,000 lower, with more flexibility and a narrower 2031 range.
  - Each extra $5,000 of top costs her about $5,300 of what she keeps.
  - What she gives plus what she keeps changes by about $1,100 or less at the median, whether she keeps 30%, half or 70%. The split decides how her money divides between what is promised and what stays hers, not how much there is.
  - No model chose half. Half sits in the middle of the trade.
- The team watches four things: whether the 2028 deposit arrives, whether any Treasury payment arrives late, whether any iBonds fund announces that it will close early, and the Taiwan dollar.

## 2033 onward

- In mid-December 2032 the late-2032 fund pays out. Laura's facility contribution, the top, is paid from that money. Anything the late-2032 fund pays above the top is also hers, promised to no one.
- From 2033 to 2042 each Treasury pays out before its payment falls due, and each payment of $50,000 is made on 1 January.
- On 1 January 2033 the operating reserve is worth about $445,700 at today's rates. The reserve is worth less than the $500,000 still to be paid, because the later bonds and funds keep earning interest until their payments fall due.
- If later rates turn out as the market expected on 6 November 2026, the ten payments never need the extra Treasuries. Their money and its interest come back to Laura, all of it by 2042: about $46,600, valued at 1 January 2033. If cash waiting earns nothing, the extra Treasuries are spent on the payments, and at most the 0.5% allowance for fund costs comes back.
- In the central case Laura's flexibility is worth about $21,200 on 1 January 2033. Adding what the late-2032 fund pays above the top, she keeps about $28,700. In 1 model future in 20, her flexibility is about $9,600 or less.
- Her flexibility is for the three things the IPS names: "building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short".
- Across Taiwan and the other places the team checked, building costs rise in about four years out of five, whether world shares rise or fall. So her shares do not tend to rise when building costs do.

## What can go wrong

- A recession can shrink three things in the same downturn: Laura's income from books, share prices and co-sponsors' budgets. They need not fall in the same months, and not every co-sponsor cuts.
- The 2028 deposit could arrive smaller, late or not at all, because it comes from her career. With no deposit at all, and cash waiting earning nothing, the ten payments would together fall $57,455 short, and nothing would buy the floor or the shares. The $57,455 is larger than the $39,162 the deposit would have spent on the payments, because those dollars would have bought Treasuries that pay back more, with their interest, by 2042.
- Yields could fall before January 2027, making the ten dearer. The 2028 deposit would complete them a year later.
- Rates could fall later, or cash waiting could earn nothing. From January 2028 the extra Treasuries cover that, and every payment is still met.
- The US government could default, and its Treasuries would then pay late. The team watches for any Treasury payment arriving late.
- An iBonds fund could cost more than its fee and the 0.5% allowance, or close early. The late-2032 fund would pay less than the top only if its extra costs took more than about 2% of what it pays.
- Shares could fall before the 2031 move. The top Laura can state would then be lower. If shares fell to nothing before the 2031 move, the ten payments would not change, and the 2031 range would be one figure: the bottom, $95,000 on today's figures. After the 2031 move, a fall in shares shrinks only her flexibility.
- The Taiwan dollar could strengthen between the 2031 meetings and the contribution, so the same building would cost more US dollars. A Taiwan dollar 10% stronger would need about $11,500 more. Her flexibility alone covers that in about 93 of 100 model futures. On the real Taiwan dollar since 1983, her flexibility would have covered the actual two-year move in about 98 of 100 two-year stretches.
- Building costs could overrun, and a share slump followed by a building-cost burst can leave her flexibility short. World shares bought at the start of 2000, as hers are bought at the start of 2028, were about 41% down by January 2003, the point that matches her 2031 meetings. Taiwan's building costs then rose about 18% in Taiwan dollars before the point that matches her contribution.
- A co-sponsor could fall short. Covering a co-sponsor's shortfall is the third use of her flexibility.

## The history and the evidence the team gathered

What the ten payments cost:
- On 16 October 2026 yields fell, and the room under $300,000 for the ten payments shrank to about $1,900.
- At the close on 6 November 2026 the ten cost about $301,500, slightly more than the first deposit.

The team's first plan, and why it changed:
- The team's first plan held mostly shares and would have bought all ten payments' Treasuries on 1 January 2033 with whatever the money had become by then. A shortfall found on that date could not be closed, because no money comes in after January 2028. A miss needed no crash: several poor years for shares before 2033 were enough.
- On 27 September 2026, before any trade, the team dropped that plan because of how a writer is paid. In the IPS's words, the team "stopped and considered the actual nature of a writer’s income".
- A model run that day measured what the change bought. In that model the first plan missed at least one payment in about 1 run in 31 (3.2%). With a 2028 deposit of only $75,000 it missed in 13.7% of runs, and with no deposit in 40.8%.
- The 27 September model did not test the Treasury plan: it assumed that plan never missed. The evidence that the Treasury plan misses no payment is the test it is now built to pass: from January 2028, every payment is met even if cash waiting earns nothing. The test needs the 2028 deposit's first $39,162. With no deposit, the Treasury plan falls short too.
- The first plan paid all ten in 96.8 of 100 runs. A 96.8 in 100 chance would already pass the standards of certainty that judges and the US Securities and Exchange Commission put into numbers. The IPS went "one step further". On the same two deposits, that step took the chance of a missed payment from about 1 in 31 to none from January 2028.

## Pencil and ink

- Laura is a graphic novelist and illustrator. Comic pages are drawn in pencil first, which can be rubbed out, and then inked. Only the ink is printed.
- In the report, a dollar figure is in ink when Treasuries Laura already owns will pay it on the date it is due.
- A dollar figure is in pencil when no Treasury she owns will pay it yet: her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the value of her shares, interest that cash waiting might earn, and every forecast. She owns her shares, but no Treasury pays their value, so their value is pencil.
- Laura states a figure only once it is in ink. The IPS says so: "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries, so whatever figure you choose is money you already own."
- When each figure is inked:
  - January 2027: the ten Treasuries are bought, and most of each payment is in ink. Two parts stay in pencil for a year: the part still unbought (the top-up), and the interest cash waiting must earn for the ten to reach $50,000 each.
  - January 2028: the 2028 deposit arrives. The top-up and the extra Treasuries put the rest of every payment in ink: from then, Treasuries Laura owns will pay all ten in full even if cash waiting earns nothing. The floor is bought, and the bottom is in ink.
  - January 2031: the 2031 move puts the top in ink, before the 2031 meetings.
  - Never: her flexibility, the half of the shares kept back, stays in pencil. It is promised to no one.
- Caplet chose pencil and ink for the report on 14 November 2026, and on 15 November described it in these words: "It is intricate. It involves strikingly dissimilar parallels. It offers a startling new perspective."
- Facts from comics and printing:
  - Image Comics' publisher said in 2015 that new series must launch with "three completed issues" already finished.
  - Publishers hold back part of an author's royalties against returns (unsold books that shops send back) and pay it out once that risk has passed.
  - Ink can be added but not cleanly taken off: a mistake is painted over, and the original art keeps the record.
  - Printers run the art past the line where the page will be cut, because the blade wanders, and keep the words well inside it.
  - Artists drew construction lines in a light "non-photo" blue that the printing camera could not see.
  - A press prints a little darker than the file, so printers lighten the file first and the print lands where they want it.
  - Printers put registration marks outside the page, check them on every pass, and cut them off.
