## The client and the case

- Laura Gao is the client in the case written for the 2026-27 Wharton Global High School Investment Competition. Each team plays young analysts at an asset management firm and recommends her an investment strategy. Team Caplet, six students in Sydney, is one of those teams. Darren, Eric and Ray are three of its six students; Ray is the team leader.
- Laura is a Wharton graduate who left a job in tech to write and illustrate books, among them *The Wuhan I Know* and the graphic memoir *Messy Roots*.
- She is planning a creative residency in Taiwan for artists, writers and designers. It opens in 2033.
- Laura puts $300,000 into her portfolio at the start of 2027 and $150,000 at the start of 2028. The second sum comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, so it depends on her career going well. No other money goes in or comes out before 2033.
- From 2033 to 2042 Laura pays $50,000 at the start of each year to run the residency: ten payments in all, $500,000. They are fixed in US dollars and are not raised for inflation. The case says the ten must be met "with a high degree of certainty", from the portfolio alone, with no outside money.
- Beyond the ten payments, Laura wants to give as much as she responsibly can towards the residency's building, while keeping flexibility as the project develops. The case calls this money her facility contribution.
- Laura plans to ask other funders, the co-sponsors, to back the residency, and begins approaching them in 2031 (the 2031 meetings). She will tell them, in dollars, the range her facility contribution will fall in. The case asks for a range "rather than promising one exact amount", because what her portfolio will be worth in 2033 is uncertain, and it warns that promising too much would damage her credibility. It asks the team to recommend the range, to say how confident the team is that her contribution will fall inside it, and to draft part of her fundraising materials for co-sponsors.
- Taxes, Taiwan law and estimates of what the building will cost are outside the case.
- Laura has said in public: "As an author and illustrator, I don't know how my book will do until the day it hits the shelves" (*Climbing* magazine, 4 March 2025).
- Team Caplet set out its plan for Laura in an Investment Policy Statement (the IPS), a short letter to her, submitted on 6 November 2026, US time. The Final Report must stay consistent with it.
- Laura's plan may use only stocks, ETFs (funds traded like shares) and Treasury bonds listed on WInS, the competition's trading simulator. TIPS and STRIPS, two other kinds of Treasury security, are not allowed this year.
- The plan acts on three dates: January 2027, January 2028 and January 2031. Its mix of holdings changes only on those three dates.

## 2027: the ten payments are bought

- In January 2027 Laura's first deposit buys ten Treasuries, one for each payment, each paying out before the 1 January it pays for. January 2027 is six years before the first payment goes out.
- A Treasury is a bond issued by the US government, which guarantees it. A Treasury bond held to the end pays exactly its stated interest and its face value (the sum it repays at the end), on fixed dates.
- The payments of 2033 to 2037 are funded by five iShares iBonds Treasury funds (IBTM, IBTO, IBTP, IBTQ and IBTR), and those of 2038 to 2042 by five Treasury bonds. For the four payments of 2033 to 2036 a fund was the only choice, because WInS listed no Treasury bond maturing between February 2031 and February 2036. For 2037, WInS listed a 4.5% bond maturing in February 2036, ten and a half months before that payment; the team chose the fund (IBTR) instead, which pays out about two weeks before the payment and, in the team's trading record, cost a little less. The bond's payout was more fixed. It was a choice, not a forced one. The IPS chose "whichever works best for each year".
- Each iBonds fund holds Treasuries that all mature in one calendar year, the year in its name: IBTM's mature during 2032. It closes on or about 15 December of that year and pays its holders what its holdings are then worth. That payout is not a fixed sum. Laura owns shares of the fund, and the fund owns the Treasuries. The team's Trading Notes, its written notes on its WInS trades, said of IBTM: "It holds 2032 Treasuries, so the timing fits, although the final payout is not fixed."
- Each fund charges a fee of 0.07% a year. It may also bear other costs the fee does not cover, such as the cost of trading. Five iBonds Treasury funds have already ended, and each paid out within about 0.07% of its last price. The plan allows up to about 0.27% for such costs over a fund's life, inside the 0.5% the plan takes off what each fund pays.
- A Treasury's yield is the interest rate it earns if held to the end. When yields fall, the same payments cost more to buy.
- For the years after 6 November 2026 the plan uses the interest rates implied by that day's Treasury prices for each later year. These are not a forecast of what rates will be: they include extra pay that lenders ask for lending longer.
- Each Treasury pays interest along the way. The ten reach $50,000 each only if that interest is reinvested, at those implied rates, until each payment falls due. Why: part of what each Treasury earns is paid out as interest years before its payment date, and that interest has to earn interest of its own to reach $50,000 by the date. Counted without that, the ten as bought pay about $445,400 of the $500,000.
- Two bonds pay out early: the one maturing on 15 February 2037, ten and a half months before the 1 January 2038 payment it funds, and the one maturing on 15 May 2038, seven and a half months before the 1 January 2039 payment. Money that a bond or fund has paid out, and that is waiting for the 1 January payment it belongs to, waits in a short-term Treasury fund; the team's model charges it 0.15% a year. This money is called cash waiting.
- The ten bonds and funds the plan holds, with the funds' fee, cost about $302,300 at 6 November rates. That is $2,287 more than Laura's whole first deposit of $300,000. The part that $300,000 cannot buy is bought a year later, in January 2028, when it costs $2,388. This $2,388 is the top-up. It is the same gap as the $2,287, a year on: a Treasury costs a little more as its payment date comes nearer, by about a year's interest, so a writer gives one of the two figures with its date, never both side by side. The IPS planned for it: "Even if yields fall and the total cost of the ladder ends up being above $300,000, we’ll simply complete it with the 2028 deposit." (The IPS calls the ten Treasuries a ladder.)
- The longest-dated payments are bought first, so the part still unbought in 2027 is part of the 2033 payment. Why: the 2033 payment's piece is the shortest-dated, so its price is the least moved by a change in rates during the year it waits, and it is finished from the 2028 deposit, which arrives five years before that payment is due.
- How much the top-up is depends on yields until January 2027. If yields fell 1 point from their 6 November level before then, the same ten would cost about $334,000, and the 2028 deposit would complete them a year later. If yields rose about 0.1 point, there would be no top-up.

## 2028: the second deposit

- The 2028 deposit arrives in January 2028. At today's rates its first $39,162 goes to the ten payments, in this order:
  - $2,388 buys the top-up, which completes the ten.
  - $36,774 buys the extra Treasuries: more of the same bonds and funds that pay the later payments.
- Why the extra Treasuries are needed: the ten reach $50,000 each only if cash waiting earns interest. If it earned nothing, the ten as bought in 2027 would pay about $445,400 of the $500,000, about $54,600 short. Replaying US interest rates since 1962, the ten as bought would have come up short in about 80 of 100 starting months from 1981, and in about 17 of 100 from 1962 to 1980. Why the difference: rates mostly fell from the early 1980s to 2020, so interest paid out and reinvested later earned less than the rates of the starting month implied; from 1962 to 1980 rates mostly rose. Why the gap could not be closed later from other money: nothing comes in after January 2028, and in 2033 the rest of her money is IBTM's payout, promised to the building, and her flexibility, whose value is a share price; closing the gap then would take money promised to the building or rest a fixed payment on a share price. Why about $36,800 covers a gap of about $54,600: the extra Treasuries pay their own interest for years before they are needed, and their interest and principal, counted without reinvesting any of it, add up to more than they cost. The extra Treasuries are sized so that every payment is met even if cash waiting earns nothing at all, and even if every iBonds fund pays out 0.5% less than its holdings are worth. That 0.5% is the margin for the funds' costs beyond their fee.
- From the start of 2033 the operating reserve is all the Treasury money set aside for the ten payments: the ten Treasuries plus the extra Treasuries (the IPS: "From 2033 the Treasuries are the operating reserve"). The case asks the team to recommend its size. The extra Treasuries are what the team added above what the ten need when their interest earns the implied rates.
- At today's rates the 2028 deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller and all ten payments would still be met even if cash waiting earns nothing.
- Next, $81,244 of the deposit (about 54% of it) buys more of IBTM, the fund that closes in mid-December 2032. Laura has held IBTM since January 2027, as the Treasury for the 2033 payment, so this is a second purchase of a fund she already owns. This purchase is called the floor, because what it pays sets the lowest figure Laura can state in 2031. Why this size: the IPS says "most" of the deposit goes to this fund, and $81,244 is about 73 of every 100 dollars left once the payments are complete, the same split between IBTM and VT as the portfolio the team bought and then could no longer change in the competition (3,361 shares of IBTM against 167 of VT); it also keeps more than half the whole deposit in the fund. At today's rates the floor pays about $102,000 in mid-December 2032.
- The interest the floor pays out before December 2032, about $15,400, waits in the short-term Treasury fund until her contribution is paid.
- The rest of the deposit, $29,594, buys VT, the Vanguard Total World Stock ETF: one fund of shares in thousands of companies around the world. Why shares: the IPS, "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares, where it can grow". Shares are expected to earn more than Treasuries and can fall, so they decide how far above the bottom the top reaches, and the half never moved is her flexibility.
- Why each part is bought in January 2028 and in this order: nothing else comes in after the 2028 deposit, so every promise must be paid for from it; the payments come first because they are fixed; the floor is bought next so that the lowest figure Laura can state is held in Treasuries three years before she states it; shares come last because no promise depends on them, and they have three years to grow before 2031.

## The WInS portfolio

- Wharton gave each team $300,000 in WInS. The team's WInS portfolio is Laura's plan as it would stand after January 2028, scaled down to $300,000: the ten Treasuries, IBTM for the floor, and VT. All eleven orders filled on 7-8 October 2026 (Sydney time); trading froze on 6 November 2026.
- The team's WInS trade notes, written when each order was placed, said each Treasury bond's "fixed coupons and principal cover most of it; reinvested coupons fund the rest" (the note on the November 2039 bond). That reliance on future interest rates is what the extra Treasuries, bought in January 2028, take out.
- The WInS portfolio was built in October, before the team sized the extra Treasuries. So in the plan the floor and VT are smaller than in the WInS portfolio scaled up to $450,000, because the extra Treasuries come first from the 2028 deposit.

## The portfolio as built

- If every share Laura owned became worthless, all ten payments would still be paid on their dates, and the building would still get the bottom of her range, $95,000 at 6 November 2026 rates. Only the top and her flexibility would go. Why: in January 2028 the ten payments and the bottom of her range are bought in Treasuries before any money goes into shares, and the top is bought in Treasuries, from half of the shares, in January 2031, before she states it in the 2031 meetings. Every figure is in Treasuries before it is promised; until then the top is money in shares, promised to no one. The bottom stopped depending on rates and on the 2028 deposit in January 2028, when it was bought.
- Finance's name for buying one bond for each payment and holding each to its date is dedication. Martin Leibowitz (1986a) called matching each payment with a bond's cash "the simplest form of dedication". Laura's plan applies the same rule to figures she has not yet promised: the bottom and the top of her range are each bought in Treasuries before she states them.
- Her holdings change on only three dated steps: January 2027 (the ten), January 2028 (the top-up, the extra Treasuries, the floor and VT) and January 2031 (half of VT moves into IBTM, bought for the second time). Each step buys a promise before it is stated. Nothing is rebalanced in between: each Treasury and Treasury fund is held to the date it pays, and the shares have no target weight to be brought back to, because the order of spending, not a target mix, decides what is held.
- How the money is spread. The shares are spread across thousands of companies: VT held 10,185 of them at 30 September 2026 (Vanguard's fact sheet), for a fee of 0.06% a year. The promised money has one issuer, the US government, and is spread across dates instead. Five of the funds come from one sponsor (iShares). Every holding sits at one broker. The funds' Treasuries are held by two custodian banks: JPMorgan for IBTM, IBTO, IBTQ and IBTR, and State Street for IBTP and VT. IBTM carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top, about a quarter of all she holds in late 2032.
- Where her career reaches the plan. In a recession a writer's advances, fees and licences come later and smaller (the IPS). Only one of the plan's inputs depends on her career: the 2028 deposit. The plan buys no shares until that deposit has arrived and paid for the ten first, and the deposit could arrive about three-quarters smaller with every payment still met. In 2008 world shares fell; in 2009, while they rose, the receipts of US independent artists, writers and performers working for themselves fell 7.8% (US Census Bureau), and did not pass their 2008 level again until 2011.
- How the portfolio was bought. Before ordering, the team checked that every bond's yield was within 0.25 points of the official Treasury yields; every bond passed that check on 5 October (from 10 basis points under to 6 over) and again at its fill (2.6 to 8.8 basis points; a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point; NUMBERS 8m, a model). The check is there to catch a stale price, like the two WInS bond prices about 1 point away found on 29-30 September; passing it rules out a gross error, and the fills' small gaps say the prices paid were close to the market's. All ten Treasury holdings were bought at exactly the units planned, and VT last, with the cash then left. Eleven trades cost $200 in commissions (six fund trades at $25 and five bond trades at $10), about 0.07% of $300,000; $3,514 was left in cash.
- How rare a year it was. $300,000 bought all ten payments' Treasuries on about 11 of every 100 trading days since 2000, and on one day from August 2007 to August 2026, Thursday 19 October 2023. On 4 August 2020 the same ten would have cost $470,217, more than both of Laura's deposits together. Monday 5 October 2026, two trading days before the team's orders filled, was the cheapest day for the ten since May 2002. The plan does not need such a day: whatever the ten cost in January 2027, the first part of the 2028 deposit completes them.

## Before the 2031 meetings

- The team's model simulates 200,000 futures for interest rates and share prices. In them, world shares grow 7.5% a year in the middle case, with a yearly spread of about 18%, and Treasury yields move about 0.9 point a year; the two are drawn separately. "In 11 of 100 simulated futures" means in about 11% of them.
- The 7.5% is J.P. Morgan's 2027 assumption for world shares, above the middle of published forecasts. Today's figures are what the model gives at today's rates with shares growing 7.5% a year: the central case. Built up from what shares pay out, how fast companies grow and what investors pay for them, world shares come to about 5% a year; the top would still be $115,000. At 3.1% a year it would be one step lower, $110,000.
- Why the top can be $115,000 at a 5% average yet reached in only about 57 of 100 futures: the stated figure is rounded down to the nearest $5,000, so a smaller average return still rounds to $115,000 (at 7.5% the unrounded top is about $117,100; at 5% about $115,800); but around any average, luck spreads the outcomes, and a little under half the futures land below the central case.
- The 2031 range has two ends, the bottom and the top. Laura states each as a whole number of $5,000s, rounding down.
- The bottom is worked out from what the floor will pay, in three steps. First, count as if none of the interest IBTM pays out after January 2028 earns anything more. Second, take off 0.5% for the fund's costs beyond its fee. Third, round down to a whole number of $5,000s. The floor's expected $102,000 becomes a bottom of $95,000. Why count so cautiously: so that the figure Laura states is never more than the fund will pay, even if every cent of interest waiting earned nothing and the fund cost more to run than its fee; a co-sponsor will plan a building on it.
- The bottom is worked out once, when the floor is bought in January 2028, and is not measured again. Until then, the bottom Laura will be able to state depends on Treasury yields up to that day, including before January 2027: if yields were 1 point lower by January 2027, the bottom would be $85,000. A tenth of a point can move either end by one $5,000 step. The stated figures belong to the rates of 6 November 2026.
- In January 2031, before the 2031 meetings, half the value of Laura's shares is sold, and the money buys more of IBTM. This is done before the meetings so that the Treasuries behind any figure she names are already held for her. The IPS: "Before you meet co-sponsors in 2031, the other half will move into Treasuries, so whatever figure you choose is money you already own."
- The other half of the shares stays in VT and is promised to no one. The IPS calls it her flexibility.
- Why Laura signs for the top rather than the bottom: the half of her shares moved in January 2031 was the building's half from the start (the IPS: the shares' returns "will help determine your maximum facility contribution", and the other half is "promised to no one"). Once moved, it is held for her in IBTM, so she can give the top without touching her flexibility. "Whatever figure you choose is money you already own" says why she can sign any figure; this is why she signs the top. The strong words ("guaranteed", "no market can change what they pay") belong to the Treasuries; the top rests on IBTM, a fund that holds Treasuries maturing in 2032, whose payout is not fixed to the dollar.
- The top is worked out the same way on both purchases of IBTM together, the floor and the shares moved in January 2031: count as if the interest earns nothing more, take off 0.5%, and round the total down once. On today's figures the top is $115,000. Share prices up to January 2031 decide how far above the bottom the top reaches.
- The 0.5% and the rounding down together leave the top about 2% below what IBTM will pay on both purchases, even if cash waiting earns nothing. The 2% is a total over the fund's life, not a yearly figure.
- If cash waiting earns nothing, IBTM pays less than the top in three cases. Its costs beyond its fee take more than about 2% of what it pays. Or the fund ends early, merges or moves its end during 2031: then it pays what its Treasuries are worth that day, without the interest they still had to earn, and the top is up to about $4,600 short (the bottom up to about $2,900 and the 2033 payment up to about $1,800). What she kept back could cover that, but her letter does not promise it, because the IPS promises that money to no one. A fund can also pay a little less than its Treasuries when it swaps them for others maturing the same year, which it does every month to follow its index. Or the broker holding her funds fails: the government-backed protection for brokers' customers tops up only to $500,000, and the risk above it is a delay. At today's market rates, an early end leaves nothing short, because cash waiting earns enough. The model contains none of these events. The five ended iBonds Treasury funds all paid out in their final December.
- Every holding is at one broker. The funds' Treasuries are kept by custodian banks: JPMorgan for IBTM, IBTO, IBTQ and IBTR, State Street for IBTP and VT. One fund, IBTM, carries the 2033 payment, the bottom and the top.
- Laura names no figure for co-sponsors before January 2031. If she named a top in January 2030, a year before the shares are moved, the move would buy less than that top in about 11 of 100 simulated futures.
- Seen from today, the figure Laura will be able to state is uncertain: a top of at least $100,000 in about 91 of 100 simulated futures, and of at least $115,000 in about 57 of 100. After January 2031 no share price and no interest rate can change the figure she states: the Treasuries that will pay it are held for her, through IBTM. What could still leave it short is the fund itself, in the three cases listed above (its costs beyond its fee, an early end or merger, the broker), and a US default.
- Why a range rather than one figure: the case asks Laura for a range "rather than promising one exact amount", because what her portfolio will be worth in 2033 is uncertain when she first plans. Her range says which part has been held since January 2028 (the bottom, which the residency's own planners can count from then) and how much more her shares added before the move (up to the top). Co-sponsors plan around the top, which is held for her before she names it, and she signs a pledge letter for it.
- The IPS chose to keep half the shares back. Half is the team's choice in its IPS, not the result of a calculation: it divides the shares equally between the building's figure and her flexibility, for the three uses the IPS lists (building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar, a co-sponsor falling short). The model shows what other splits would do, on today's figures. Keep 30% back instead, and Laura could state a top about $5,000 higher, but her flexibility alone would cover a 10% stronger Taiwan dollar in only about half of simulated futures, against about 93 in 100 with half kept. Keep 70% back, and she would state a top about $10,000 lower, with more flexibility and a narrower range. Each extra $5,000 of top made certain gives up about $270 of the share growth she would otherwise expect, at the median, because shares moved into Treasuries before 2031 stop growing at the share rate. What she gives plus her flexibility changes by about $1,100 or less at the median, whichever split she chooses: the split decides how her money divides between what is promised and what stays hers, not how much there is.

## What the team recommends (decided before writing)

- In her words to co-sponsors Laura states her range first, because the case asks for a range: the bottom, $95,000, held for her in US Treasuries since January 2028, and the top, $115,000, held since January 2031, when half her shares were moved. Each end goes in its own sentence (voice round 16). Voice round 17 tested the range first against the top first: all six readers booked $115,000 either way, so the range stays first.
- What the range is, once both ends are held: two dates, not two outcomes. Since January 2031 all $115,000 has been held for her; the $95,000 is the part held since January 2028. The range does not mean the gift could land anywhere between the two (voice round 18: a reader playing a co-sponsor still asked "in what case would we ever see only the $95,000?").
- What the bottom is, once the top is held: the part that never depended on her shares. It would have been there if every share she owned had become worthless. Since January 2031 neither end depends on her shares, and no share price or interest rate can change either. Her words say this before she says why she signs the top; without it, every reader in round 17 asked why a range is stated at all and what could make her gift $95,000.
- She signs for the top, $115,000. The letter states the sum and what holds it: Treasuries maturing in 2032, held for her through one fund. It does not promise to make up a shortfall from what she kept back: the IPS promises that half of the shares to no one, and names a co-sponsor falling short among its uses, so pledging it to a co-sponsor would go against the IPS. In every simulated future and every history test the fund pays at least the top (centrally about $122,500). The team lists, for the judges, what could leave the fund short (above); Laura's words list none of it.
- Why the letter names the fund beside the sum when no market can change either end: her Treasuries are held through a fund, and a fund can pay out a little less than the Treasuries it holds (its own costs, or an early end while cash waits earning nothing). That is the one cause, said in plain words in one clause. What the building's Treasuries will pay by December 2032 is more than $115,000 (never under about $117,700 on today's figures), and that gap is the room for what the fund itself might take. The letter binds her estate. What she kept back is never sized for co-sponsors, and never promised to them.
- Why the top, in numbers: the $20,000 between the two ends came from the shares she moved in January 2031, and those shares were the building's half from the start.
- She states it in US dollars, the currency her Treasuries pay, with its Taiwan-dollar value on a named day.
- She offers a signed pledge letter (amount, purpose, date). Owning the Treasuries shows the money exists; a signed pledge says it will be given.
- Co-sponsors can check it. The building's Treasury fund shares sit in a second account at the same broker, holding nothing else, and the broker sends co-sponsors its statement (at least quarterly), at the 2031 meetings and again before the December 2032 payout. On the day of the January 2031 move that statement would show about $111,700 (its market value, at 6 November 2026 rates), a little under the $115,000 she signs for: a Treasury fund is priced below what its Treasuries will pay until they pay it. What those Treasuries will pay by December 2032 is about $117,700 even if cash waiting earns nothing. The team says this beside the statement, in its own words, not Laura's (NUMBERS 8y).
- Her words state her whole commitment in two parts: $50,000 a year for the running costs, 2033 to 2042 ($500,000), bought from January 2027 and complete since January 2028; and $115,000 for the building. If a total is given, it is $615,000 in all, never "about": ten times $50,000 plus $115,000 is exact, and five of six readers in round 17 asked which part "about" made uncertain. The total is a sum of promises at face, as pledges are stated; it is never set against what comes back to her. They are a base, not the residency's whole budget.
- From January 2028 she may tell the residency's own planners "$95,000, held for me since January 2028", and that any more is named in 2031. Not "at least $95,000": a funder reading the notes said planners' "at least" and co-sponsors' "$115,000" would put two figures about.
- The pledge is in US dollars, the currency her Treasuries pay. Its Taiwan-dollar value is given on a named day (on 6 November 2026, $115,000 was about NT$3.77 million at 32.768 to the dollar), and the letter says plainly that the Taiwan-dollar amount received depends on the rate on the day it is paid. Whether Laura adds anything from her flexibility if the Taiwan dollar strengthens is her choice, not a promise in the letter.
- In her words Laura says the money will be given "without waiting for anyone to match it". She pays in January 2033, which may be after co-sponsors who give earlier; "first" is true only of her commitment in writing.
- A larger building overrun, or a lost co-sponsor, is met by the residency's co-sponsors, by phasing or redesign, and by not building before the money is in hand. Why wait for the money: a smaller design or a later start can absorb a gap only before building begins; once it is under way, neither is open. Her flexibility closes a small gap.

## 2033 onward

- In mid-December 2032 IBTM pays out. Laura's facility contribution, the top, is paid from that money. Anything IBTM pays above the top is also hers, promised to no one.
- From 2033 to 2042 each Treasury pays out before its payment falls due, and each payment of $50,000 is made on 1 January.
- On 1 January 2033 the operating reserve is worth about $445,700 at today's rates. That is less than the $500,000 still to be paid, because the later bonds and funds keep earning interest until their payments fall due.
- If later rates turn out as the 6 November prices implied, the ten payments never need the extra Treasuries. Their money and its interest, about $46,600, come back to Laura by 2042. If cash waiting earns nothing, the extra Treasuries are spent on the payments.
- In the central case Laura's flexibility is worth about $21,200 on 1 January 2033, if shares earn the 7.5% assumed. Treasury rates bear on that figure because they set how much of the 2028 deposit the payments and the floor take, and so how much is left to buy shares. With what IBTM pays above the top, about $28,700. In 1 simulated future in 20, her flexibility is about $9,600 or less.
- Her flexibility plus anything IBTM pays above the top, about $28,700 in the central case, would cover a rise of about 25% in the cost of her share of the building, if shares earn the 7.5% a year assumed. A larger rise, or a lost co-sponsor, is met by the residency's other funders, by a smaller design or a later start, and by not building before the money is in hand.
- Her flexibility is for the three things the IPS names: "building costs that overrun, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a co-sponsor falling short".
- Since 1970, across Taiwan, the United States, Hong Kong, the United Kingdom, Singapore and Japan, building costs rose in about four years out of five, in years when world shares rose and in years when they fell. Over two years the link between the two is weak and unstable.

## What can go wrong

- A recession can bring Laura's income later and smaller (advances are paid in instalments and can be delayed), and in the same downturn share prices can fall and co-sponsors can cut their budgets. They need not fall in the same months, and not every co-sponsor cuts.
- The 2028 deposit could arrive smaller, late or not at all. With no deposit at all, and cash waiting earning nothing, the ten payments would together fall $57,455 short, and nothing would buy the floor or the shares. The $57,455 is larger than the $39,162 the deposit would have spent on the payments, because those dollars would have bought Treasuries that pay back more, with their interest, by 2042.
- Yields could fall before January 2027, making the ten dearer. The 2028 deposit would complete them a year later.
- Rates could fall later, or cash waiting could earn nothing. From January 2028 the extra Treasuries cover that, and every payment is still met while each fund runs to its stated end.
- The US government could default. A Treasury payment more than five business days late is one of the IPS's two reasons to change the plan; a missed 2028 deposit (nothing received by 31 March 2028) is the other. The five business days are S&P's test of a default where no grace period is written, so a payment delayed for a day or two by a holiday or a settlement problem is watched, not treated as a default. What a default would change: check what was actually received, hold, and restate; never a forced sale.
- The IPS's two reasons are both money that fails to arrive. Its word "only" rules out a rise in share prices as a reason to change, as firmly as a fall. From January 2028 the ten payments ask one thing of anyone, that no one sells what is already bought; they ask no one to judge a market or keep their nerve in one. Rules written in advance are often broken: in one experiment, executives who had agreed in advance to a rule for when to stop backing a failing project broke it about half the time (8 of 17; Boulding, Morgan and Staelin, 1997).
- An iBonds fund could end early, merge or move its end. One already has: IBTA ended 15 days before its scheduled date, so the plan's watch list looks for each fund's end notice in September and October, not only on the day. The 0.5% margin pays the trading costs of moving to another fund; the interest its Treasuries still had to earn is made up only by what cash waiting earns (at today's market rates, in full).
- Shares could fall before January 2031. The top Laura can state would then be lower. If shares fell to nothing, the ten payments would not change, and the 2031 range would be one figure: the bottom, $95,000 on today's figures. After January 2031 a fall in shares shrinks only her flexibility.
- The building is paid for in Taiwan dollars, and her money is in US dollars. If the Taiwan dollar strengthened between the 2031 meetings and the contribution, the same building would cost more US dollars: 10% stronger needs about $11,500 more on her $115,000. Her flexibility alone covers that in about 93 of 100 simulated futures. On the Taiwan dollar's actual history since October 1986 (not adjusted for inflation), the half of the shares alone, without what IBTM pays above the top, would have covered the actual two-year move in about 98 of 100 two-year stretches. The ten payments are fixed in US dollars, so their Taiwan-dollar value moves too.
- Four times since 1991, in four separate episodes (1991-93, 2002-05, 2006-08 and 2019-21), building costs and the Taiwan dollar together made the same building 28% or more dearer in US dollars within two years: about $32,200 more on her $115,000, more than all her flexibility. Beyond that, the residency's other funders, a smaller design or a later start carry the rest.
- A share slump followed by a building-cost rise can leave her flexibility short. World shares (the MSCI ACWI index) bought at the start of 2000 were more than 40% down three years later, at the start of 2003, as 2031 is three years after her shares are bought. They then rose again but were still about 17% below what they cost at the end of 2004, five years after buying, as December 2032 is for her. Over those last two years (January 2003 to December 2004) Taiwan's building costs rose about 18% in Taiwan dollars, about 27% in US dollars. Most of the rise came in one quarter, December 2003 to March 2004, when world steel prices jumped and steel bar in Taiwan rose 38%; costs had begun rising before shares hit bottom. Both world-share falls of 30% or more since 2000 were followed by a Taiwan building about a quarter dearer in US dollars: once through steel (2003-04), once through a stronger Taiwan dollar (after 2008-09). Replaying Taiwan's own building-cost and currency moves since 1991 against share prices of the same years, one start each January, her flexibility with anything above the top covered the rise in 29 of 31 starts; the two it did not cover began in January 1988 and January 2000, the second of them this episode.
- A co-sponsor could fall short. Covering a small shortfall is the third use of her flexibility.

## Four cases from outside Laura's plan, and where each differs

- **The California State Lottery's rule (for the commitment and the reserve).** The Lottery pays big prizes over many years. Its Commission's investment policy (approved for 2025 on 25 September 2025) says its investments must mature in amounts "sufficient to meet required prize payouts on dates immediately prior, or as near prior as practicable, to prize payment dates". That is Laura's ten payments in other words: each bought to end just before it is paid. The difference: the Lottery's Treasuries are zero-coupon bonds, which pay nothing until they mature, so they leave it no interest to reinvest. Laura's plan may not hold them (the competition bans STRIPS), so her bonds pay interest years early, and the same rule makes her hold her extra Treasuries ($36,774). Most of that comes back to her (about $46,600 by 2042 if rates follow today's prices), so what it costs her is holding it, not losing it.
- **The Reserve Bank of Australia's guidance (for the range and its confidence).** On 2 February 2021 Australia's central bank said it did not expect the conditions for raising interest rates to be met "until 2024 at the earliest". It raised them in May 2022. Its own review of that guidance (15 November 2022) found that "many people interpreted the forward guidance as 'a promise'". A forecast said aloud is heard as a promise; that is why Laura states only what is already held.
- **Lehman Brothers and Spelman College (for the words to co-sponsors).** In October 2007 Lehman Brothers promised Spelman College $10 million over five years. Eleven months later the bank filed for bankruptcy, and the college received $3 million. Laura's money is held for her in Treasuries before she signs.
- **The 2022 gilt crisis (for the assumptions and limits).** In September and October 2022 British government bond (gilt) prices fell fast. The funds forced to sell were those that had borrowed against their gilts or used derivatives, pooled funds above all: long gilt yields jumped more than a percentage point in a few days, and the banks on the other side asked for more collateral faster than the funds' pension-scheme investors could send cash. In November 2022 the pensions' insurer told Parliament that the crisis "was one of short-term liquidity, not fundamentally the solvency" of the schemes, and that it had seen no claims from it. Laura borrows nothing, so no fall in prices can make her sell; her one planned sale, the 2031 move, waits for a price near the funds' value.

## Pencil and ink

- Laura is a graphic novelist and illustrator. Comic pages are drawn in pencil first, which can be rubbed out, and then inked. Only the ink is printed.
- Caplet's image for the report: a dollar figure is in ink once Treasuries held for Laura, directly or through the Treasury funds, will pay it on its date, counted as if their cash earned nothing more and after the 0.5% margin. A figure is in pencil while no Treasury held for her will pay it yet: her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the value of her shares, interest that cash waiting might earn, and every forecast. She owns her shares, but no Treasury pays their value, so their value is pencil.
- What she owns is the Treasuries and the fund shares, never the figure: the figure is what they will pay.
- Laura states a figure only once it is in ink.
- When each figure is inked:
  - January 2027: the ten Treasuries are bought, and most of each payment is in ink. Two parts stay in pencil for a year: the part still unbought (the top-up), and the interest cash waiting must earn for the ten to reach $50,000 each.
  - January 2028: the 2028 deposit arrives. The top-up and the extra Treasuries put the rest of every payment in ink: from then, Treasuries held for Laura will pay all ten in full even if cash waiting earns nothing. The floor is bought, and the bottom is in ink.
  - January 2031: the shares moved into IBTM put the top in ink, before the 2031 meetings.
  - Never: her flexibility, the half of the shares kept back, stays in pencil. It is promised to no one.
- Facts from comics and printing:
  - Image Comics' publisher said in 2015 that new series must launch with "three completed issues" already finished.
  - Publishers hold back part of an author's royalties against returns (unsold books that shops send back) and pay it out once that risk has passed.
  - Ink can be added but not cleanly taken off: a mistake is painted over, and the original art keeps the record.
  - Printers run the art past the line where the page will be cut, because the blade wanders, and keep the words well inside it.
  - Artists drew construction lines in a light "non-photo" blue. The printing camera's film saw light blue as white, so the lines dropped out of the print.
  - A press prints a little darker than the file, so printers lighten the file first and the print lands where they want it.
  - Printers put registration marks outside the page, check them on every pass, and cut them off.
