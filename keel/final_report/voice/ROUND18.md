# Voice round 18: Laura's words to co-sponsors, repaired (Tue 24 Nov 2026, evening)

Round 17 (`ROUND17.md`) found one fault in both orders of Laura's words: with the top held and signed, every reader
asked why a range is stated at all and what could make her gift $95,000. The morning repaired the words in notes
(`research/COSPONSOR_NOTES.md` section 8, C1-C12; `FACTS_PLAIN.md` version 8). This round reads the repaired words on
three readers in round 17's roles. Keel's prediction and the deciding rule were written before anyone read
(`round18/PREDICTION.md`, 19:20 Sydney).

## Set-up

- **Writer.** One fresh writer who read only Caplet's writing (`CORPUS.md`, the IPS), the facts (`FACTS_PLAIN.md`
  version 8) and the case (brief `round18/brief_r18_Y.md`). Warm-up carrying the IPS on ("we" 1.6 per 100 words), then
  the team's lead-in (L, 118 words: the broker's statement, the signed letter, Lehman and Spelman) and Laura's words (N,
  253 words: the range first, what the bottom is now, why the top, the letter's fixed sum, the currency, her whole
  commitment in two parts, "without waiting for anyone to match me"). Texts in `round18/writer_Y.md`.
- **Readers.** Three blind readers: the head of giving at a Taiwanese family company that has sponsored arts venues, a
  programme officer at a US foundation that funds artists' residencies, a grants officer at Taiwan's Ministry of
  Culture. Each read version 61 (calibration), Caplet's IPS paragraph C3 (control), then L and N, marking Follow and
  Sense per sentence, then round 17's five questions from memory, unchanged. Scores in `round18/SCORES_R18.md`.
  Readers are simulated, never people; three against three is a signal, not a measurement.

## Results

**1. The morning's repairs worked where they were aimed.** No reader stopped at the total ("$615,000 in all"; five of
six stopped at "about $615,000" in round 17) or at the lead-in's first sentence (four of six misread round 17's
"offer ... as well as the money held"). All three booked $115,000 in US dollars, paid January 2033; all three named the
$95,000 as the part held since January 2028 that never depended on her shares; no reader asked for a probability.
Followed first time: 57 of 66 sentence readings (86%), against 85% for round 17's shorter range-first text.

**2. The question moved again, and this time it names the real gap.** Round 16's readers asked why the top; round 17's
asked why a bottom; round 18's asked what stands behind the promise to make up a shortfall. All three marked "no market
can change either one" with a "?" because two sentences later the fund "pays out less", and all three asked, in
question 5, how much money she kept back and where it is. The reader from the Ministry put the missing fact in one line:
"I take it a fund is not the same as a Treasury, but the page does not say so." The words claimed the Treasuries'
strength for the figures, then took part of it back without saying why.

**3. The range still needs one sentence saying what it is not.** Reader 1, the co-sponsor, still asked "in what case
would we ever see only the $95,000 she still quotes?", and reader 3 stopped at the top's sentence for the same reason.
Two of three also needed a second reading of why she signs the top: "the reason only works once you see the top is the
bottom plus the moved shares".

**4. A dating phrase cost every reader a stop.** The lead-in's "a pledge letter ... for $115,000 on today's figures"
was read by all three as a figure that could still move, against "fixes the sum" in her words.

**5. The currency sentence cost two of three a second reading,** and taught them the right thing: no reader booked a
Taiwan-dollar figure, and the grants officer would plan the Taiwan-dollar budget at a cautious rate.

## Keel's prediction, scored

Five of six held (the undertaking draws a new question; followed at least as well; the total heard as exact; the
currency costs a stop; all book $115,000). One failed: the bottom's question did not go away for the co-sponsor. Not
predicted: the dating phrase.

## What changes (the same evening; `research/COSPONSOR_NOTES.md` section 8, C13-C17; `FACTS_PLAIN.md` version 9)

By the rule written before reading: the bottom's sentence is rewritten from reader 1's words, and because the
undertaking drew a question from all three, one fact answering it goes in the lead-in, not in her words.

- **The range says what it is not (C13).** After the two ends, one plain sentence: the two figures are two dates, not
  two outcomes; since January 2031 all $115,000 has been held for her. Then the bottom's sentence as before (the part
  that never depended on her shares).
- **The fund is named for what it is (C14).** "No market can change either one" stays only with the reason the promise
  is still needed beside it: the Treasuries are held through a fund, and a fund can pay out a little less than the
  Treasuries it holds, so her letter fixes the sum at $115,000 and she makes up any difference herself. One clause, the
  one cause in plain words; never a list.
- **Why the top, with its arithmetic (C15).** The $20,000 between the two ends came from the shares she moved in
  January 2031, and those shares were always meant for the building.
- **The lead-in answers "how much did she keep back?" without showing her flexibility (C16).** Beside the broker's
  statement, what the building's Treasuries will pay by December 2032 is more than $115,000 (NUMBERS 8y: never under
  $117,731 on today's figures), so her undertaking covers only what the fund itself might take, and the letter binds
  her estate (C10). The size of what she kept back stays off every page co-sponsors see.
- **No dating phrase beside the letter's sum (C17).** "On today's figures" is said once, in 3c, where the range is
  dated to the 6 November 2026 curve; the lead-in about the letter names no figure.
