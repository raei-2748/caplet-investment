### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It's a plain, orderly letter: what's already paid, what she gives, what she asks for, what's off the table. The bold, unhedged "They're guaranteed" fits a team that called Treasuries "immune to markets" and "ever-reliable". It's more controlled than the IPS, but that's what you'd expect when the team writes in Laura's voice, and it still sounds like a person.
- **Meaning:** Laura tells a co-sponsor that the ten running payments are already covered by Treasuries, that she'll give $115,000 to the building in December 2032, and that she wants conditional building pledges plus yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words to a co-sponsor):** The board paper should carry $115,000. $95,000 of it has been held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest since her shares moved into Treasuries in January 2031, so all of it is held by the time she writes. She doesn't ask them for the ten $50,000 running payments, doesn't ask anyone to match her figure, and the rest of her money isn't part of the offer.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 3

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** It's tight and even, built from short explanatory sentences and two crafted turns ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). Every sentence is shaped, and none has the IPS's looseness, warmth or slightly wobbly enthusiasm. It reads like a skilled editor summarising the plan, not the students talking.
- **Meaning:** In 2031 Laura's range runs from the owned floor to the floor plus half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it; any interest-reinvestment shortfall is priced and covered from her kept shares first, and the range comes down only if those can't cover it.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has a real team's texture: dated notes, quoted words ("very tight", "lumpy", "copout"), named teammates, Eric asleep at 00:30, "luck, not skill". It moves through events in order. The abrupt first line about the advisor and the tidy closing tally ("Two recommendations changed and one held") make it feel assembled for a rubric, but the people are present.
- **Meaning:** The team reflects on three moments: the first deposit barely buying the ten payments (so the 2028 deposit got a first job), dropping a share-heavy first plan because of how Laura is paid, and keeping VT after a challenge, now for reasons about Laura.
- **Check question (the team's reflection):** They dropped the share-heavy first plan because a writer's income is "lumpy", arriving later and smaller in the same recessions that knock shares down, so shares would fall just when her income did. The first model only priced that change: the first plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs, the Treasury plan none once the 2028 deposit arrives, and it showed the danger sat on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (the world fund):** They kept VT. Before, it was a sensible default, cheap and spread wide. After, it was the one holding allowed to be wrong, wrong only with the whole market (most single stocks trail Treasury bills), needing no opinions so never a third reason to change the plan, and a Taiwan fund would only add more Taiwan.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It follows the IPS's own story: thoughtful risk and six years to grow, then a writer's income in a bad year, and a portfolio that "would have fallen with her career instead of balancing it" (close to the IPS's "counterbalance, not a contributor"). It's smoother and more even than the IPS, with no exclamations and no showing off, but the order of thought feels like theirs.
- **Meaning:** The team dropped its share-heavy first plan because Laura's income falls with shares in recessions; the model that day only priced it (1 in 31 against none) and showed the danger was the single date when all ten payments would have been bought.
- **Check question (the team's reflection):** Because a writer's income ("lumpy": advances, fees, licences) arrives later and smaller in the same recessions that pull shares down, so shares would have fallen with her career. The first model just priced the change: at least one missed payment in about 1 in 31 runs against none for the Treasury plan once the 2028 deposit arrives, with the danger on 1 January 2033.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest (1, 3, 11) have the IPS's own habits. They're slightly showy and enthusiastic ("near-ironclad", "restless markets", "vain illusion", "we'll simply"), they stack clauses with dashes and asides, and they make claims a bit bolder than a careful editor would allow, so you hear students explaining a choice they're pleased with. The reflections in the middle keep the team's facts, names and the IPS's turn of thought ("then we thought about how a writer is paid"), but their sentences are smoother and more evenly weighted than the IPS ever is. The letters are plausible drafts for Laura, and I ranked them lower the more they leaned on professional jargon (draw, custodian) or clipped speechwriter fragments. The bottom two (5, 8) are compressed, even-toned explanation with no person in them, all shape and no roughness or excitement, which is the opposite of how the IPS sounds.
