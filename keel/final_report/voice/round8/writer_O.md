# Writer O, voice round 8

## Warm-up

Notes on the IPS paragraph that starts "In January 2027 we'll use your first deposit", one line per sentence:
1. Jan 2027, first deposit buys 10 Treasuries; bonds or iBonds, whichever suits each year; each matures just before it's needed.
2. Held to the end, they pay what they say; dash; no price swings to fear.
3. Today's yields, about $300k; dash, "in other words": the whole first deposit.
4. Reassure her; dash; plan doesn't depend on yields staying put; exclamation.
5. If yields fall and cost goes over $300k, the 2028 deposit "simply" completes it.

Rebuilt from the notes alone:

In January 2027 your first deposit buys 10 Treasuries – a T-Bond or an iShares iBonds fund for each year, whichever
fits better – and each one matures just before its payment is due. Held to the end, they pay what they say, so a price
moving around in the meantime is nothing for us to fear. At today's yields that costs about $300,000, which is all of
your first deposit. Worry not – the plan doesn't need yields to stay where they are! If they fall and the ten end up
costing more than $300,000, the 2028 deposit simply finishes the job.

Where mine drifted: I pulled the brackets into the sentence and explained more than the IPS bothers to ("a price moving
around in the meantime"), which made it longer and a little flatter. I also lost "in other words", "But" and "the
ladder", the small talking connectors that make the original sound like people thinking while they write.

=== P1 ===
I'm opening a residency for artists, writers and designers in Taiwan in 2033, and before I ask for anything, here is
where it stands.

Its running costs, $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, are already paid for. Each payment comes from a US Treasury
held for me that matures just before its January, bought in January 2027 and completed in January 2028. I'm not asking
anyone for this money.

I will give $115,000 to build the residency's facility, paid in December 2032, before the building's first draw in 2033.
$95,000 of it has been held for me in Treasuries since January 2028, and the rest since my shares moved into Treasuries
in January 2031. It is fixed in US dollars and won't rise if the building costs more.

I'm asking for two things. First, capital for the facility, pledged on one condition: that the total set in the
residency's budget is reached by a single close date, no later than December 2032, so every conditional pledge releases
together. Mine comes first and carries no condition. Second, yearly programme money from 2033, one year at a time, the
way Taiwan's funders give.

Both go to the residency itself, its own body in Taiwan, which owns the site and runs the building. I won't ask you to
match my figure, and neither the ten payments nor the rest of my money is part of this offer.

The custodian holding the Treasuries can send you a statement on request.

Laura

=== P2 ===
Can I start with what's already done? The residency's running costs – $50,000 every January, 2033 to 2042 – are paid
for, each one by a US Treasury held for me that matures just before its January. Bought in 2027, finished in 2028: I'm

For the building, I'm giving $115,000, paid in December 2032. $95,000 of it has been in Treasuries for me since 2028,
the rest since my shares moved over in January. It's in US dollars, so it won't rise with building costs.

My ask is two things. Capital for the facility, conditional on the residency's budgeted total being reached by one close
date, so every pledge releases together – mine goes in first, unconditionally. Then yearly programme money from 2033, a
year at a time.

It all goes to the residency, which owns the site and runs the building. I won't ask you to match me, and the ten
payments and the rest of my money aren't on the table. The custodian can send you a statement.

=== P3 ===
Our advisor handled administration only; we made the decisions and wrote our own IPS.

Our first Big Idea was that Laura's first $300,000 could buy Treasuries for all ten payments on its own. Our notes
called it "very tight", and it was. Our orders filled on 7 and 8 October, within two trading days of the year's cheapest
close, and that was luck, not skill – the market opened at 00:30 Sydney time on the 7th, and Eric was asleep before it
did. Priced at every close of 2026, the ten cost less than $300,000 only from September into early November, and on 16
October the room left was about $1,900. So the 2028 deposit got a first job: if the ten ever cost more than $300,000, it
finishes them before it does anything else.

The case pulls Laura two ways: she is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and her payments must be met "with a high
degree of certainty". On 27 September our first plan followed the first phrase – about three-quarters shares. We dropped
it before any trade, and the reason was Laura's work, not the market. Our notes that day already called her income
"lumpy": advances, fees and licences come later and smaller in the same recessions that knock shares down, so shares
would have been falling just when her income was. Our first model only priced the change. The first plan missed at least
one payment in about 1 in 31 runs; the Treasury plan misses none once the 2028 deposit's first dollars arrive. Most
people would call 30 in 31 "high" certainty – we didn't keep it, because of how Laura is paid. The model also showed the
danger wasn't a crash: the first plan needed share prices in the right place on one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten
payments had to be bought with whatever the shares had become.

The shares had their own argument. On 30 September, before anyone objected, we wrote down the bar an alternative had to
clear to replace the one world fund, VT, and only then ran the numbers. We tested four, from American shares alone to VT
with some gold and property trusts beside it. None cleared the bar; gold and property came closest, mostly by cutting
off the good outcomes. Then on 5 October Darren called one world fund a "copout" and wanted us to pick stocks and ETFs
like a normal team; a Taiwan fund and gold came up too. We decided, in the end, to keep the one world fund. Before, VT
had been a sensible default, cheap and spread wide; after, we could say why it suits Laura in particular. It's the one
holding allowed to be wrong, and it can only be wrong along with the whole market – and most single US stocks have done
worse than Treasury bills over their lives (Bessembinder, 2018). It needs no opinions, so it never becomes a third
reason to change the plan. And a Taiwan fund would only add more Taiwan to a plan whose building is already there.

Two recommendations changed and one held: the payments left shares for Treasuries because of how Laura is paid, the 2028
deposit got its first job because the room under $300,000 nearly ran out, and the world fund stayed, now for reasons
that are about Laura.

=== P4 ===
The case pulls Laura two ways. She is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and her ten payments must be met "with a high
degree of certainty". On 27 September our first plan leaned on the first phrase – about three-quarters shares, with six
years for them to grow. We dropped it before a single trade, because of what a writer's income does in a bad year. Our
notes that day already called it "lumpy": advances, fees and licences arrive later and smaller in the same recessions
that pull shares down, so a portfolio built on shares would have fallen with her career instead of balancing it. Our
first model just priced the change. That plan's payments were in pencil, because it held no Treasury that would pay them
– all ten would have been bought on 1 January 2033 with whatever the shares had become, so the danger was not a crash
but share prices in the wrong place on that one date. The first plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs,
while the Treasury plan misses none once the 2028 deposit's first dollars arrive. Most people would have called 30 in 31
"high" certainty, and we still didn't keep it, because of how Laura is paid.

=== NOTES ===
Laura's two passages were the easiest to hear in the team's voice once I stopped trying to make her sound like a bank:
the IPS's habit of saying the reassuring thing first ("what's already done") carried over naturally, and the spoken
version wanted fewer dates than the facts allowed. The Team Reflection felt closest to the team's writing where Eric and
Darren were in it and furthest where a moment had to carry both a date and a figure in one sentence. In P4 the turn did
one useful job, putting "the payments weren't owned yet" and "one date carried the risk" into a single clause, but it
sat between two plain explanations and I'm not sure it said anything the plain version of the same moment couldn't.
