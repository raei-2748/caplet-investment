Rebuilt from the notes alone:
In January 2027 your first deposit buys ten Treasuries – a T-Bond where there's one for that year, an iShares iBonds fund where there isn't – each one maturing just before the payment it's there for. Hold them to the end and they pay what they say, so a bad week for bond prices along the way is nothing to lose sleep over. Right now that costs about $300,000, which is the whole of your first deposit. That's tight, but the plan doesn't need yields to sit still: if they fall and the ten cost more than $300,000, the 2028 deposit finishes the job.

Where mine drifted: I lost the IPS's cheer ("But worry not", the exclamation mark) and added a judgement it left unsaid ("That's tight"), so mine sounds more careful and less pleased with itself. I also explained why a year gets a fund instead of a bond, which the IPS skips because Laura doesn't need to know.

=== P1 ===
I'm opening an artists' residency in Taiwan in 2033. Before I ask for anything, here is where my own money stands.

Its running costs are already paid for: $50,000 each January from 2033 to 2042, each covered by a US Treasury held for me that matures just before its January. I bought them in January 2027 and completed the set in January 2028. They're guaranteed, and I'm not asking anyone for them.

I will give $115,000 to the residency's building, paid in December 2032, before construction needs its first payment in 2033. Of that, $95,000 has been held for me in Treasuries since January 2028, and the rest since my shares moved into Treasuries in January 2031. It is fixed in US dollars and won't rise if the building costs more.

I'm asking for two things. First, capital for the building, pledged on one condition: that the total in the residency's budget is reached by a single closing date. Every pledge closes on that date, no later than December 2032, so they all release together. My figure comes first, with no condition. Second, programme money from 2033, one year at a time.

Everything goes to the residency, its own organisation in Taiwan, which owns the site and runs the building. The ten running payments aren't on the table, I won't ask anyone to match my figure, and the rest of my money isn't part of the offer.

The firm holding my Treasuries can send your board a statement.

Laura

=== P2 ===
First, what I'm not asking for. The running costs – fifty thousand dollars every January, 2033 to 2042 – are already paid, each by a US Treasury held for me that matures just before it. Bought in 2027, finished in 2028.

For the building, I'm giving a hundred and fifteen thousand dollars, paid in December 2032, before the builders need a cent. Ninety-five thousand has been in Treasuries since 2028, the rest since my shares moved over in January. It's in US dollars; if the building costs more, it doesn't move.

Then two asks. Building capital, pledged on condition we reach the residency's budgeted total by one closing date – December 2032 at the latest – so every pledge releases together. Mine has no condition. And yearly programme money from 2033.

It all goes to the residency, which owns the site. I won't ask anyone to match me, and the rest of my money isn't part of this. The firm holding my Treasuries will send your board a statement.

=== P3 ===
Our first Big Idea was that Laura's first deposit, on its own, buys Treasuries for all ten payments. Our notes that week called it "very tight". Priced on every closing price of 2026, the ten fit inside that deposit only between 10 September and 3 November, and on 16 October, when rates fell, the room left was about $1,900. We didn't want the payments to depend on which week we bought in, so we gave the 2028 deposit a first job before the building sees any of it: finishing the ten if they cost more than the first deposit. Our orders filled on 7 and 8 October, Sydney time, within two trading days of the year's cheapest close. That was timing – luck, honestly – not skill. The market opened at 00:30 our time, and Eric fell asleep before it did.

Laura's case pulls two ways: she is "willing to take thoughtful risks", and her payments must be met "with a high degree of certainty". On 27 September we listened mostly to the first half, and built a plan on shares, the usual answer to a six-year wait. We dropped it before a single trade, and the reason was her income, not a number. A writer's income is lumpy – our word that day – and the lumps come later and smaller in exactly the recessions that pull shares down, so shares would have leaned her savings the same way as her work. The model we ran that day only priced the change: the first plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs, the Treasury plan none once the 2028 deposit's first dollars arrive. Most people would have called 30 in 31 "high" certainty. We didn't keep it, because of how Laura is paid. What the check did show was where the danger sat – not in a crash, but on one date, 1 January 2033, when all ten payments would have had to be bought with whatever the shares had become.

On 30 September, before anyone had objected to the world fund, VT, we wrote down what an alternative would have to beat for us to leave it, and only then ran the numbers on four. None cleared the bar; gold and property trusts came closest, mostly by trimming the good outcomes. Five days later Darren called one world fund "a copout" and argued we should pick stocks and ETFs like a normal team. A Taiwan fund came up, and gold. In the end we kept VT. Before that week it was a sensible default – cheap, and spread wide. After it, we could say why it suits Laura in particular. It is the one holding allowed to be wrong, and it can only be wrong together with the whole market, while most single US stocks have done worse than Treasury bills over their lives (Bessembinder, 2018). It needs no opinions, so it never hands us a third reason to change the plan. And a Taiwan fund would only add to what the building in Taiwan already carries.

The tight fit of the first deposit is why the 2028 deposit finishes the payments before it touches the building; Laura's lumpy income is why every payment sits in Treasuries; and Darren's challenge left VT where it was, now for reasons that are about Laura.

=== NOTES ===
Laura's words were the hardest to hear as the IPS's writers rather than as a bank: the facts are all promises and conditions, and the team's quickness lives in asides and dashes that a program officer reading for thirty seconds doesn't want, so P1 came out plainer than anything Caplet has written. The Reflection felt closest to the team when it let a person or a confession in (Eric asleep, "luck, honestly", Darren's word) and furthest when it had to carry the 1-in-31 figure and its "none". In P4 the turn did one real job, putting the one-date danger and its reason in a single sentence, which let me drop the separate "what the check showed" sentence P3 needed; the cost is that it sits right after "we dropped it", so it can read as the reason when the reason is the income two sentences earlier.
