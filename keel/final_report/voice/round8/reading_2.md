# Reading 2, voice round 8 (blind reader 2)

I read the IPS twice, the second time slowly. What I hear in it: a lively, slightly showy team writing a letter, plain
words with a little flourish ("immune to markets", "ever-reliable Treasuries", "But worry not"), conversational
connectors, dashes for asides, digits for small numbers, an explanation for a non-expert at each step, and a punchy
close. It sometimes overreaches and is not perfectly smooth, and that roughness is part of why it sounds like people.

### Passage 1

- **Verdict:** Probably not
- **Why:** This is a confident speaker running through a term sheet, and the sentences are clipped very short ("Bought in 2027, finished in 2028: I'm not asking anyone for that."). The middle sentence about the condition is packed with fundraising jargon ("one close date", "every pledge releases together", "the custodian") and none of it is explained. The IPS writers explain as they go. This sounds like a professional fundraiser, though the team could have drafted something close to it.
- **Meaning:** Laura tells a co-sponsor that the ten running payments are already paid for by Treasuries. She will give $115,000 to the building, already held in Treasuries, and she asks for conditional building capital and yearly programme money, but not for a match.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** A board paper should carry $115,000. All of it is held: $95,000 has been in Treasuries since 2028 and the rest since "January", which presumably means January 2031, though the passage never names the year. She does not ask them to match her, and the ten payments and the rest of her money are not on the table.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 2

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** The reasoning is the IPS's own (a writer's income falls in the same recessions as shares), and "lumpy" and "we still didn't keep it" sound like the team. But the passage is dense. One long middle sentence carries a definition, a colon and a dash, so it reads as worked over rather than spoken.
- **Meaning:** The team dropped its share-heavy first plan because a writer's income falls together with shares, and the model only priced that choice: the old plan missed a payment in about 1 in 31 runs, all of its risk sat on one date, and the Treasury plan never misses.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** The team dropped the plan because Laura's income (advances, fees, licences) arrives later and smaller in the same recessions that pull shares down, so shares would have fallen along with her career. The first model only priced the change: the first plan missed at least one payment in about 1 in 31 runs, the Treasury plan none once the 2028 deposit arrives, and the danger was share prices on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Image:** It gets slightly in the way. It arrives in the middle of the argument and the same sentence has to spell out what "pencil" means, so it adds a step. "In pencil" is a neat way of saying "not secured yet", though.

### Passage 3

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is a calm, orderly note in plain words, in the right order: what's done, what I give, what I ask, where it goes, how to check. "They're guaranteed" has the same blunt confidence as the IPS's "immune to markets". The phrase "every pledge closes on that date ... so they all release together" is more fundraising language than the team usually uses, but on the whole it reads like a careful student draft for Laura.
- **Meaning:** Laura says the running costs are already covered by Treasuries she owns, she gives a fixed $115,000 to the building (held in Treasuries since 2028 and 2031), and she asks for capital pledged to one closing date plus yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000, paid in December 2032. $95,000 has been held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest since January 2031, so all of it is held. She does not ask them for the ten running payments, she won't ask anyone to match her figure, and the rest of her money isn't part of the offer.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 4

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This has the IPS's energy and its small overreaches: "where we allow ourselves to be wrong", "the vain illusion that we can pick winners", "as per JPMorgan", and a rhetorical question to finish. One sentence runs on a bit, the way a student's would when making a point with gusto. It sounds like the same people who wrote "So we hedge it!"
- **Meaning:** VT is the one holding allowed to take risk, because the bonds already cover the payments; it is a cheap, diversified fund whose job is to set how generous the facility contribution can be.
- **Check question:** None fits strictly, because this is not the reflection. On the world fund it keeps VT as the one holding allowed to be wrong, but it describes no change in the reasons.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 5

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is almost the same note as Passage 3, a little fuller and a little more polished: "artists, writers and designers", "the way Taiwan's funders give", "on request". Terms like "the building's first draw", "close date" and "conditional pledge releases" sound more like a fundraising professional than a student. It is still plain, orderly and believable as a team draft.
- **Meaning:** Laura says the running costs are already funded, she gives a fixed $115,000 held in Treasuries, and she asks for capital pledged to one close date plus yearly programme money, all paid to the residency's own body.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000, paid December 2032. $95,000 has been held in Treasuries since January 2028 and the rest since January 2031, so all of it is held. She does not ask them to match her, or for the ten payments or the rest of her money.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 6

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It has real human touches: "that was luck, not skill", Eric asleep before the 00:30 open, Darren's "copout", a "Big Idea" in capitals. The reasoning also follows the IPS. But the opening sentence about the advisor reads like a declaration bolted on, and the last sentence ties everything up very neatly, so parts of it feel arranged rather than told.
- **Meaning:** The reflection gives three outcomes. The tight first deposit gave the 2028 deposit the first job of finishing the payments, Laura's lumpy income moved the payments from shares to Treasuries, and a challenge to the world fund ended with VT kept for reasons specific to Laura.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** The team dropped the plan because Laura's income comes later and smaller in the same recessions that knock shares down. The first model only priced the change (about 1 in 31 runs missed a payment, against none for the Treasury plan) and showed the danger was one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (reflection, world fund):** The team kept VT. Before, it was a sensible default, cheap and spread wide. After, the team could say why it suits Laura: it is the one holding allowed to be wrong and can only be wrong along with the whole market, it needs no opinions (so it never becomes a third reason to change the plan), and a Taiwan fund would only add more Taiwan.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 7

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It is short, plain and confident, and it ends sentences on small aphorisms ("a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year", "Her spare money shrinks before her promise does"). The IPS does the same with "A fall in the market is not one of them". But it is tighter and cooler than the IPS, more like an editor's summary than the team talking.
- **Meaning:** The 2031 range runs from the floor up to the floor plus half the shares, which move into Treasuries before Laura names it. Any shortfall from reinvesting the ladder's interest is priced and covered from her kept shares first, and if that isn't enough the range comes down.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 8

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** It moves like a person telling what happened: they answered the first phrase, then thought about how a writer is paid, then dropped the plan. Phrases like "put her savings on the same side as her work" and "for most clients it would be" sound fair-minded and human. It is smoother and more even than the IPS, with fewer of its quirks.
- **Meaning:** The team first answered "willing to take thoughtful risks" with a share-heavy plan, then dropped it because a writer's income falls with shares; the model that day only priced the change.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** The team dropped the plan because Laura's lumpy income arrives later and smaller in the same recessions that pull shares down, which would put her savings on the same side as her work. The model that day priced the change and nothing more: about 1 in 31 runs missed at least one payment against none for the Treasury plan, and the danger was one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Image:** It helps a little. The gloss after the dash makes "pencil" clear at once and leads straight into the one-date danger, though the passage would work without it.

### Passage 9

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is written to be spoken, with the numbers spelled out and short turns. "Before the builders need a cent" and "if the building costs more, it doesn't move" are the kind of plain, slightly playful phrases the IPS writers use. The condition sentence is still jargon-heavy, and like Passage 1 it leaves "January" without a year.
- **Meaning:** Laura opens with what she's not asking for (the running costs, already paid by Treasuries), states her $115,000 building gift held in Treasuries, and asks for conditional building capital and yearly programme money.
- **Check question (Laura's words):** $115,000, paid in December 2032. Ninety-five thousand has been in Treasuries since 2028 and the rest since "January", presumably 2031, so all of it is held. She doesn't ask for the running costs or a match, and the rest of her money isn't part of it.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 10

- **Verdict:** Probably
- **Why:** This is the most natural of the long reflections. It tells things in order and gives a reason before each decision ("We didn't want the payments to depend on which week we bought in"). It admits things in a human way ("luck, honestly", "Eric fell asleep before it did"), and the asides set off by dashes ("our word that day") have the IPS's conversational feel. The closing summary is neat but earned.
- **Meaning:** Three things changed or held: the tight first deposit gave the 2028 deposit the job of finishing the payments, Laura's lumpy income put every payment in Treasuries, and Darren's challenge left VT in place for reasons specific to Laura.
- **Check question (reflection, first plan):** The team dropped the plan because a writer's income is lumpy and shrinks in the same recessions that pull shares down, so shares would lean her savings the same way as her work. The model only priced the change (about 1 in 31 runs missed a payment, against none for the Treasury plan) and showed the danger sat on one date, 1 January 2033.
- **Check question (reflection, world fund):** The team kept VT. Before that week it was a sensible default, cheap and spread wide. After, it was kept for Laura: it is the one holding allowed to be wrong, wrong only with the whole market, it needs no opinions and so gives no third reason to change the plan, and a Taiwan fund would only add to the building's Taiwan exposure.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 11

- **Why:** This is the IPS's voice almost exactly. It uses the same connectors ("In other words", "Notably –"), the same move of taking "high degree of certainty" further ("a near-ironclad guarantee"), and the same small flourish ("the restless markets"). Its slightly clumsy, earnest logic ("a nonexistent inflation risk") sounds like a student showing they understand the point.
- **Meaning:** The 2037 Treasury gives up growth to remove uncertainty by matching a fixed nominal payment, so TIPS aren't needed.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 12

- **Why:** It is correct and clear but flat, like a textbook or an analyst's memo: two methods, we pick the second, here's how it works. No person comes through, it has none of the IPS's warmth or flourish, and "Pension funds cover fixed payments ..." is an opening the IPS writers never reach for.
- **Meaning:** Of the two ways pension funds cover fixed payments, the plan uses one bond per payment held to maturity, so no payment depends on shares.
- **Check question:** None fits.
- **Image:** Not used.

### Passage 13

- **Verdict:** Surely
- **Why:** This sounds like a trading note from the same hand as the IPS: "so we'll simply ignore price noise", "The catch is,", a stack of adjectives, and a bracket to make sure the reader keeps up. It is eager, a little over-packed, and honest about the trade-off, the same mix as the IPS's "Held to the end, these pay what they say".
- **Meaning:** Where WInS lists no Treasury for a given year, iBond ETFs stand in. They act like bonds when held to the end, but they pay their net asset value rather than par, in exchange for liquidity.

### What separates the top from the bottom

The passages I ranked highest have the IPS's own habits. They talk to a non-expert with connectors like "In other
words", "Notably", "The catch is" and "simply", they reach a little too far for a phrase ("vain illusion", "restless
markets", "near-ironclad"), and they are slightly uneven in a way that sounds like eager students. The middle group,
the long reflections, keep real people and admissions (Eric asleep at the 00:30 open, Darren's "copout", "luck, not
skill"), but they are more controlled and tidier than the IPS, and they often close on a neat summary. At the bottom
are passages with no person behind them. They read as a textbook explanation (12) or a compressed, jargon-heavy term
sheet (1), correct and polished but without the explaining, the warmth or the small flourishes that run through the
IPS.
