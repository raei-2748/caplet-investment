## 1. What Caplet decided, and why it fits

- **A conceit, not a motif.** Caplet's definition: "a specialised, intricate type of extended metaphor. It involves
  strikingly dissimilar, surprising and seemingly far-fetched parallels. It displays wit, intellectual ingenuity, and
  offers a startling new perspective." The IPS had none because "that was a letter; this is not a letter".
- **What it overrides.** For the Final Report only, it replaces Keel's reading of 8 Nov (no long-running image; the
  IPS's idea as the only thread; `../EXPECTATIONS.md` Part 6, T4, and lesson 4.9's items 4 and 10). The IPS is frozen
  and stays as it was. Ray's warning of 5 Nov survives as the conceit's guard, not its ban: "metaphors alone does not
  count as creativity". Every image sentence has to do an investing job (section 7).
- **Why it is the right image.** It comes from Laura's own trade (the case: "a bestselling graphic novelist,
  illustrator"; her first success was "a comic"). It continues the IPS's last line, "You take risks on the page." And
  it is the IPS's own governing rule made visible: "only money you haven't promised yet goes into shares" (l.11) and
  "whatever figure you choose is money you already own" (l.15). In the image, a figure is shown only once it is inked.

## 2. The rule, in the image

- **Ink** is a dollar figure already owned in Treasuries that will pay it on its date, barring a US default. It is
  defined once, in 3.0, as an explicit comparison that uses the word "owned": in everyday English "inked" means
  "signed" or "committed", and a reader who takes it that way hears "Laura states a figure only once she has committed
  to it", which is circular (card Q42). In comics only the inked line prints: the pencils are rubbed out once the ink
  is dry, and a pale "non-photo blue" pencil is dropped out before printing. Gary Martin: "The inked line is what's
  reproduced in the finished comic book, since pencil lines are erased after inking" (*The Art of Comic Book Inking*,
  3rd ed., Dark Horse Books, 2019, p. 6; verified in print, card Q44).
- **Pencil** is a figure no Treasury she owns will pay yet: her future income, the 2028 deposit before it arrives, the
  world share fund (VT) before half of it moves, the interest that coupons and payouts earn while they wait (cash
  waiting), and every model result. Never "not yet owned" for the shares: she owns them, and Table 1 shows it; no
  Treasury pays their value (coherence reader 4, Sat 14 Nov). Never "interest not yet earned" for a Treasury's own
  coupons, which are fixed and owned.
- **The rule:** Laura states a figure only once it is inked. Pencil can be rubbed out and redrawn; ink can only be
  painted over or patched, and the correction stays on the original. New ink can always be added; taking it away "is a
  lot more complicated (and messy)" (cartoonist Tom Richmond, 2006; card Q44), so the report says "never taken off
  cleanly", not "cannot be removed".
- **Ink holds because each Treasury is held to its date.** A Treasury's price moves every day and it can be sold; a
  figure is ink only because the plan holds each Treasury to the date it pays. Dollars on dates, never values (card
  Q42; bond reader 2).
- **Ink carries its exceptions with it.** "Inked" never means guaranteed. An iBonds fund pays "a net asset value, as
  opposed to a promise of par" (Trading Note 2, TNA l.15). So each payment is counted at what its holdings pay if cash
  waiting earns nothing, at each fund's stated fee and the bill fund's 0.15%; each stated end of the range is counted
  the same way, less 0.5% held back once for fund costs beyond the 0.07% fee (for either end before the move, for the
  top after it), and rounded down to $5,000 (`NUMBERS.md` 4). Any fund event (an early end, a merger, an end moved) is
  swapped as Table 3 row 4 says. Zero interest is a margin, not the worst case: Treasury-bill yields have been below
  zero for days (2008, 2015, March 2020; bond reader 2).

## 7. The guards (Caplet's five, kept; Keel's additions, with the audit's, the readers' and card Q42's)

1. **Every image sentence carries a number or a rule,** and the plain name of the thing (`WORDS.md`). The image is a
   verb or an adjective inside a sentence that already says what it means: never "the inked floor" as a new name,
   never "the pencil money". Deletion test: strike the image word and the sentence must still state its number and
   rule (card Q42). No new figure enters through an image: it uses its part's own headline number, inside SKELETON's
   density cut.
2. **Never in tables, figures, exhibit notes or the co-sponsor words.**
3. **At most one image sentence in a part; never a part's last line, and never a part's first line.** "Never the
   last line" has evidence behind it (a metaphor only in the conclusion did worse than plain wording; card Q42);
   "never the first line" is a voice rule from round 1's blind readers, who heard set-piece openings as Keel, not
   Caplet (`voice/ROUND1.md`), untested at this scale. Inside its paragraph the image comes before or in the same
   sentence as the number it explains (it may open its paragraph), never after it as a summary.
4. **Two words carry the conceit: pencil and ink.** Any other craft fact is used only if it extends "owned or not
   yet owned", and at most one in the whole report, explained in plain words in the same sentence (card Q42: one
   use that switches mapping cancels the gain). No colour for VT. No comics jargon beyond that: no panels, gutters,
   splash pages, origin stories or sound effects, and no puns on "issue", "draw" or "drawdown".
5. **"Inked" never overclaims.** One definition, stated once in 3.0, as an explicit comparison: a dollar figure on a
   date, owned in Treasuries (directly or through iBonds funds) and held to that date, paid even if cash waiting earns
   nothing (each fund at its stated fee, the bill fund at 0.15%), barring a US default; for the range only, 0.5% of
   value held back once for fund costs beyond the 0.07% fee (either end before the move, the top after it); any fund
   event swapped as Table 3 row 4 says; an iBonds fund pays its net asset value, not a promise of par (TNA l.15).
   "Owned", not "promised" or "signed" (everyday English reads "inked" as signed; card Q42). It always travels with
   "barring a US default" and with its date; never "guaranteed", "locked", "set in stone" or "risk-free". Dollars on
   dates only, never values. Zero interest is a margin, never "the worst case". Never for her flexibility, the 7.5%,
   any median, or anything before its date. Ink never means "bought", "held" or "in WInS", and the January 2027
   purchase is never pencil.
6. **The IPS is never called pencil, draft or thumbnail,** nor are the Trading Notes or the WInS notes. They are frozen
   ink, and a judge reads "pencil" as provisional. The report inks what the IPS left open (how the ten are sized, the
   range rule, which bonds are bought first); it never redraws the IPS. A method note for the writers, not report text:
   inking is not tracing (Martin 2019, p. 7: the inker decides line weight and fixes what the pencils got wrong, inside
   the penciller's intent), which is Book 17's rule that the report evaluates and does not repeat the IPS.
7. **The report's own modelled figures are pencil, and say so** ("we modelled", "on today's market rates"). Never
   "only inked numbers are shown" about the report: it shows today's figures as the rule's value, never as a promise.
8. **The image never carries the thesis and never explains finance.** Pencil carries timing (no Treasury pays it yet),
   not the IPS's correlation (her income falls when shares fall and co-sponsors cut). The judges are experts; the image
   carries only the team's own rule and its dates, never what a Treasury or a default is (card Q42). Never say
   inflation, the Taiwan dollar or the yield curve fade the ink after its date: they change what the dollars buy, not
   whether they are paid.
9. **`WORDS.md`'s banned names survive inside images:** no "buffer", "cushion", "backstop", "kept half", "moved half",
   "IBTM floor", "gift" or "excess"; "reasons", never "triggers". "All ten owned", never "each stands alone". Never
   "full funding" or "fully funded" (OMB's term for a whole asset; Laura funds her share; card Q43), "prudent
   accounting" or "a conservative estimate".
10. **The conceit never chooses.** It describes figures, decisions and events chosen on their merits; it never selects
    one because it fits the image. No craft fact cards Q40 and Q44 have not checked, and nothing about Laura's own
    drawing process.
11. **Johnson's test, judged by a fresh reader.** Samuel Johnson defined wit as "a combination of dissimilar images, or
    discovery of occult resemblances in things apparently unlike", praised wit "which he that never found it, wonders
    how he missed", and warned that with forced conceits "the reader commonly thinks his improvement dearly bought,
    and, though he sometimes admires, is seldom pleased" (*Life of Cowley*, 1779). Each image must be true to the craft
    and true to the plan, and must cost the reader less than it teaches. Drop it where a fresh reader's repeat-back
    misses the rule or adds a wrong inference (the writer cannot judge this; card Q42), and say it plainly ("one
    sealed envelope per payment" is the fallback).
12. **No outside words in Laura's mouth beyond what she has said publicly.** Her Climbing interview may be quoted once,
    exactly and cited, about how a book sells, never about the case's money; nothing she has not said is attributed to
    her ("clip before you climb" is in no source, and her climbing book is a novel), and the case remains the only
    source on her plan, money and dates.

## 8. Where the image is honest, and where it is not (for the writers; the report says the limit where it uses the image)

- **Ink can be corrected, visibly.** The plan has two reasons to change; a change is a patch on the original, recorded,
  never a quiet erasure.
- **An iBonds fund is ink that is not quite dry until mid-December of its year.** Hence the cut and the rounding.
- **Ink holds because each Treasury is held to its date.** A bond reader knows a Treasury can be sold any day and its
  price moves; the plan holds each one to the date it pays, which is what makes the figure ink.
- **A US default is not a smudge; it is the paper.** The image has nothing to say about it, and the report names it
  plainly every time.
- **Inflation and the Taiwan dollar do not fade the ink.** The payments are fixed in dollars, so the ink holds; what
  changes is what the dollars buy in Taiwan (3e).
- **Her flexibility is not a bleed:** its size varies (about $9,900 to $41,300, 1 in 20 each way), it is about a third
  smaller in the years shares fall, it is not a natural hedge, it stays hers, and on its own it covers a 10% stronger
  Taiwan dollar in about 93 of 100 simulated futures, so it can show a white edge. If the bleed is used at all, it is
  the 0.5% the rule holds back plus the rounding down (section 5b, rank 3); what the funds pay above the top joins her
  flexibility.
- **The two-thirds image is not used:** it holds for the ratio only. VT is 8.84% of the portfolio but 6.63% of the
  plan, and the portfolio holds no top-up, no extra Treasuries and no Treasuries for the moved half, because the plan
  buys them in January 2028 and 2031 (coherence reader 4; card Q42).
- **"Redraw" overclaims a little:** a missed deposit changes sizes in the IPS's order, and the ten bought in 2027 stay.
- **What can still fade the ink after its date:** a US default; fund running costs above the 0.5% cut (a few hundred
  dollars); a fund's board ending, merging or extending a fund (Table 3 row 4); a fee on waiting money above the
  modelled 0.15% (its fund unverified). Table 3 watches each (row 4 watches the bill fund's fee since Sat 14 Nov).
