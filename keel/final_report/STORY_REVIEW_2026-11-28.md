# Story review of Big W's Final Report (Sat 28 Nov 2026)

What this is: a reading of the "big w's big w" tab of the Final Report Google Doc (about 3,550 words with tables,
read in full on Sat 28 Nov, 21:00 Sydney), through one lens only: storytelling, originality, emotional resonance and
memorability, as a financially literate Wharton judge who has already read dozens of competent reports would read it.
The doc was not edited. Every recommendation is keyed to a quoted passage and says what to do, not what to write.

Not covered here, because other threads have them: the audit of the numbers, the reorder of the middle section around
six questions, and the cold-reader test. Where a note below touches one of those, it says so.

Protected throughout, as the team and the ChatGPT review asked: the opening, pencil and ink, the electricity bill
passage, the words for the co-sponsors and most of the reflection. Nothing below asks for any of them to be rewritten.

## The verdict

This report has something most competent reports do not: a plot. It opens on a question, makes a promise (we bought
what Laura owes), confesses a flaw in that promise at the end of Section 2 ("Whether we bought enough of it is another
matter"), pays for the repair in Section 3, prices what the repair cost the building ($40,000) and gives that price a
human face (the electricity bill). Then the reflection reveals that the opening question was a teammate's, asked over
McFlurries, and answers it twice: once for Laura and once for the team. Those two bookends are why this report would
be remembered. The voice is dry, British in its understatement, confident without strutting, and it says "we" the way
Caplet's own IPS does.

Where it loses a judge is the middle of Section 3. For about 900 words after the confession, and again around Table 3,
the prose turns into a committee's minutes: the same caveat in a dozen forms, two shortfalls met in the wrong order,
and the plan's most surprising rule given without its reason. None of this is a failure of voice. It is a failure of
pacing: the report stops telling the reader why each step matters at exactly the places where the numbers get dense.

## What a judge would remember the next morning

In rough order of how firmly each sticks, and why:

1. "Nobody will hold a ceremony when the electricity bill is paid in January 2041." The single most memorable sentence.
   It turns an abstract commitment (ten payments) into one dull, real, unglamorous Tuesday, and it makes the cost of
   certainty ($40,000 less for the building) feel like a choice made for someone.
2. "A $300,000 simulator invites a team to show what it can pick; we used ours to show what Laura owes." The report's
   thesis said as a contrast with every other team, without naming them. A judge who has just read ten stock-pickers
   will feel this one.
3. "Laura needed to participate in the growth of the world economy, not necessarily in six students' opinions about its
   future." Wit that carries a real investing idea (why an index fund), told inside a real argument with Darren.
4. "If the shares do well, Laura can afford to be more ambitious. If they do poorly, she can be less ambitious. What she
   cannot afford to be is less reliable." The whole plan in three sentences.
5. "On the whole, we bought what we said we would. Whether we bought enough of it is another matter." The hinge of the
   report. Judges rarely see a team test its own submitted strategy and find it $57,070 short; this is where the report
   earns trust.
6. The McFlurries, the "suitably un-Christmassy" weather, and the reveal that the opening question was Ahaan's. Human,
   specific, and it reframes the first page after the fact.
7. "A perfectly sound bond maturing in 2041 is of little comfort to someone with a bill to pay in 2038." Why a ladder,
   in one image a non-specialist keeps.
8. "Sitting opposite Laura in January 2031, you might reasonably ask how much of her own money she has set aside before
   offering yours." Putting the judge in the co-sponsor's chair is the boldest move in the report.
9. "By the fifty-fifth version of our Investment Policy Statement, we were still finding things to change, though fewer
   things to learn." The best sentence in the reflection.

Nothing in the middle of Section 3 (from "To find out, we examined" to "whatever remains after the ten payments is
Laura's", and from Table 3 to "verify her projected receipts") is on this list. That is the map of where attention
goes.

## The narrative architecture

The spine, as a judge experiences it:

Thematic continuity is the report's quiet strength. The opening's worry (a bad year for Laura is a bad year for an
artist) returns as "especially if her co-sponsors were making the same discovery", then as "Credibility matters more
than expected size", then as "without asking those who depended on her to take them too". A judge who notices this will
credit the team with a single idea held for twelve pages, which is rarer than any single insight.

## Where attention wanders

Three stretches, in order of severity.

1. From "To find out, we examined each holding's coupons" to "whatever remains after the ten payments is Laura's".
   The confession has just landed and the reader wants the repair. Instead they get an assumptions paragraph, a second
   and smaller shortfall ($2,388), a second rhetorical question that repeats the first, the definition of the test that
   was already run, and only then the fix. Then a paragraph on a 12% surplus that is not counted, and an operational
   recommendation about a Treasury-bill ETF "subject to settlement checks".
2. From "We compared both approaches through Monte Carlo simulations" to "Laura should therefore verify her projected
   receipts before announcing either figure." Table 3 is the right evidence, but the prose around it restates the table
   ("reduces the median outcome by $7,435 but improves the fifth-percentile outcome by $23,921") and then stacks four
   caveats. The one sentence with life in it ("Were Laura investing solely for herself ...") is in the middle and gets
   no room.
3. From "A stronger Taiwan dollar" to "reduces her indicative facility range to $95,000-$105,000": four figures in four
   sentences ($96,700, $28,700, $12,500, $95,000-$105,000), each a different scenario. Each is fine; together they read
   as a printout. The paragraph is rescued by its last two sentences.

In each stretch, the Big W voice is still there in single lines ("We would rather find the extra money in 2028 than
leave a future director to find it"); what is missing is the reason for each step, which is exactly what Book 22 asks
for and what would bring the voice back without any decoration.

## Recommendations, most important first

**R1. Name Laura's craft before the image.** "Taking risks has served Laura rather well, bringing her from a career in
technology to the pages of bestselling books." The next paragraph says "A graphic novelist can leave much of a drawing
in pencil", but the reader has not been told Laura is one. At present the image reads as an analogy the team chose;
with her craft named in the first paragraph it becomes her own way of working, which is what makes the image feel
inevitable rather than clever. A word or two in the existing sentence is enough.

**R2. Take the citation out of the hook.** "(Wharton Global Youth Program, 2026a)" sits inside the opening paragraph's
second sentence, between the stakes and the punchline. Cite the case at the first factual sentence instead (the "ten
annual payments of $50,000" sentence, or the assumptions paragraph, which already cites it). APA is satisfied either way;
the opening keeps its rhythm.

**R3. Check the "Hence".** "Hence the 89.55% allocation to US Treasuries. Rather unenterprising for an entrepreneur,
perhaps." The joke is good and should stay. But the 89.55% is what the ten payments cost, not a figure that follows from
the argument about her income; the income argument explains why the rest is not all in shares. A sophisticated judge
will catch the jump, and it is the one place in Section 2 where a sentence fails Book 21's second test (it reads well and
does not quite follow). Re-point the "hence" to what it truly explains, keep the punchline after it.

**R4. Once the confession lands, get to the repair.** This is pacing, and it overlaps the reorder thread, so treat it as
a constraint for that work rather than a separate edit:
- Keep the cliffhanger ("But what if it earns nothing?") through any reorder. It is the report's best piece of
  architecture.
- The test's definition ("We took her request for a 'high degree of certainty' to mean meeting all ten payments without
  equity gains, outside money, forced sales or interest on early receipts") is the most original reading of the case in
  the report: the team turned the client's vague phrase into a test it could fail. At present it arrives after the test
  has been run and failed. Rules of a test belong before its result, so the reader feels the $57,070 as a verdict.
- "Reinvestment income makes the ladder cheaper to assemble. What would it cost to do without it?" asks again what
  "But what if it earns nothing?" already asked. One question, answered once. Keep the first.
- The reader meets "$57,070 short" first and "$2,388 short" second, on different bases. Order them so the small,
  expected shortfall (the one the IPS foresaw) comes first and the surprising one second; otherwise the $2,388 reads as an
  anticlimax or, worse, a contradiction.

**R5. Say the caveat once and trust it.** "Under our stress assumptions", "under these assumptions", "under our stated
assumptions", "under our assumptions" and "our stated assumptions" appear about ten times, three times in one paragraph
(the "$39,162 goes to operating commitments" paragraph). Each repetition is true; together they make a confident report
sound defensive, and by the fifth the judge stops hearing it. Name the test once, where it is defined (R4), and then
refer to it by that name. The honesty survives; the hedging chorus goes.

**R6. Collect the IBTM plant.** "It was convenient, but it also meant that nearly a third of our portfolio depended on
what one fund would eventually pay." This is a good, self-critical plant, and a judge will wait for it to come back. It
never does by name: "Early fund termination, settlement failures and US Treasury default introduce further
uncertainties" is generic. Name IBTM where those risks are listed so the earlier admission is paid off.

**R7. Make the inflation echo audible.** "We know how many dollars Laura has promised; we cannot know how much a dollar
will buy by then" is planted in Section 2 and paid off in "Laura's holding" ("would have the purchasing power of
approximately $96,700 in 2026 dollars"). The payoff uses different words, so few readers will connect them. Let the
later sentence echo the earlier one's phrasing so the reader feels the report keeping its own threads.

**R8. Close the co-sponsor's chair.** "Sitting opposite Laura in January 2031, you might reasonably ask ... We thought
it unreasonable to give you a figure ..." The judge is put in the chair for two sentences, then the frame is dropped
for three paragraphs of Laura-in-the-third-person and Table 3. When Laura's words arrive ("In the central case, she
might put it to them like this"), "them" pulls the judge back out of the chair just as the payoff arrives. One clause
returning to "you" in the lead-in to her words would let the judge hear those words as spoken to them. This is the
cheapest change in this list with the largest effect on how her words land.

**R9. Give the January 2031 move its reason where it first appears.** "Before the meeting, Laura sells half of her
shares and invests the proceeds in IBTM", and later "Move half of VT into IBTM, whatever the market has done." This is
the plan's most counterintuitive rule: a sophisticated reader's first question is "even after a crash?" The calendar's
"whatever the market has done" answers the what but never the why. One sentence at first mention (a rule decided now
is a rule nobody has to argue with in a falling market; the co-sponsors need a figure, not a forecast; or whatever the
team's own reason is, in the IPS's terms) turns the most questionable-looking step into one of the most persuasive.

**R10. Give "Making a promise is different from keeping it" room.** The paragraph carries the report's sharpest idea:
before 2031, $115,000 is a figure Laura can offer in about 57% of futures; once the half is bought, none falls short.
Confidence changes meaning at the moment money is bought. At present this is three compressed sentences between a table
and a stack of caveats, and the reason the number jumps (the money now exists) is left for the reader to infer. This is
also the one place in the middle where the opening image would explain rather than decorate: it is the moment a pencil
line is inked. Caplet's direction keeps pencil and ink to the opening and the close, so the team should decide whether
to name it here; if not named, the why still needs saying in plain words. Put the paragraph's point first and the
caveats after, in their own paragraph.

**R11. Let the deletion test stand alone.** "Even if her shares were worth nothing, both deposits and our stated
assumptions would still support all ten operating payments and a $95,000 facility contribution." This is the cleanest
proof in the report, and it is used as a hinge into Laura's words ("In the central case, she might put it to them like
this"). A judge reads it on the way to something else. Give it the end of its own paragraph, so it lands as a finding,
and let the lead-in to Laura's words be its own short step.

**R12. One number per paragraph carries the point.** In "Laura's holding" and around Table 3, each paragraph has two to
four figures from different scenarios. A judge keeps one. Decide which figure carries each paragraph's point and lead
with it; let the rest live in the tables or their notes. (Which figures are right is the numbers thread's call; this is
only about how many a sentence can carry.)

**R13. Where the register turns to minutes, the missing why is the cure.** "We do not treat this difference as money
available for the building." and "We recommend keeping the facility assets separate and holding early receipts in a
Treasury-bill ETF, subject to settlement checks." are the two flattest sentences in the report, and both are flat for
the same reason: they state a decision without its reason. The first one's reason is the one the report already gave
for the stress test (the surplus exists only if waiting money earns interest); a back-reference of a clause restores the
logic and the voice together. Do not add wit to these stretches to compensate; the reason is enough.

**R14. One seam in the reflection.** The reflection asks "what was the point of sitting around a table with our papers
and calculations when we could simply enter a prompt into Claude or ChatGPT?" It answers once ("There was something
valuable in having to explain an idea to five other people, particularly when one of them thought it was rubbish"),
then asks again ("So what was the point of those afternoons at McDonald's?") and answers with a different idea ("As
students, we had the luxury of being wrong"). Both answers are good. The seam is that the second answer is about Laura,
not about the table. The join is one link the reflection already contains but never states: the table was where being
wrong was cheap, and that is what the pencil echo then names. If the second answer is shown to grow out of the first,
the ending closes both questions at once and "And we are rather glad it was" lands harder.

**R15. Two vague sentences in a concrete account.** "It was exciting to find ourselves reading about things we had not
even known existed a fortnight earlier" and "Different modelling engines gave us different numbers" are the only places
where the reflection generalises. One true, named instance each, from the team's own memory, would make them as vivid
as the copout and the fifty-fifth version (for the first, something the report itself cites would tie the reflection to
the analysis). Only the team knows which instances are true; do not borrow one.

**R16. The bond-quote beat.** "We learnt something similar when two bond quotations in WInS did not agree with the
Treasury curve. From then on, we checked yields against the curve before placing orders." "Similar" to what is left for
the reader to supply, and the beat is the weakest in the reflection. Either say in a clause what was similar (both were
moments of testing the team's own confidence against an outside standard, which is also what the next paragraph admits
they failed to do with their own tools) or cut it; the reflection does not need it to work.

**R17. Watch the "rather".** It appears eleven times: five as "rather than", and six as understatement or preference,
two of those in the opening paragraph ("rather well", "a rather different sort of disappointment") and the last as the
report's final word but one ("rather glad"). It is part of Big W's voice and should stay. Thinning one of the two in the
opening paragraph would keep the device from being noticed early, so the last one lands as the understatement it is.

**R18. The title page has no title.** Optional. A judge files a report under a phrase. If the team wants one, the
report's own words supply candidates (the opening question is one); nothing new needs inventing.

## What not to touch

- The opening paragraph and the pencil-and-ink paragraph, apart from R1 and R2.
- "A $300,000 simulator invites a team to show what it can pick; we used ours to show what Laura owes."
- "diversified across time rather than across borrowers" and the 2041-for-2038 sentence.
- The three-sentence "ambitious ... reliable" passage and the "Whether we bought enough of it" turn.
- The electricity bill passage, from "It is easy enough to picture Laura opening the residency in 2033" to "a more
  impressive one struggling to keep its doors open". Oman is the only outside story in the report and it works because
  it comes from Laura's world. Resist adding more outside parallels anywhere: one that belongs beats three that impress.
- "But a recession would be an inconvenient time to discover she had less than expected, especially if her co-sponsors
  were making the same discovery."
- Laura's words. They sound like a person, they open on the artists, and they end by inviting a conversation. If
  anything sounds like a term sheet it is "independently of your contributions"; that is the team's call.
- "Laura can choose to promise more when she knows what the residency needs. Promising less afterwards would be
  considerably more difficult." This is pencil and ink at work without the words, and it is better unnamed.
- The reflection's McFlurries opening, the copout, "Somewhat embarrassingly", the fifty-fifth version, "the luxury of
  being wrong" and the last two lines.

## Why this reads as human, and how to keep it that way

The report never announces its own cleverness. Its wit comes from understatement about real stakes ("Rather
unenterprising for an entrepreneur, perhaps"; "an inconvenient time to discover"), not from images laid on top. Its
emotion is carried by objects and dates (an electricity bill in January 2041, a modest residency still in use in 2042),
never by adjectives. Every recommendation above keeps to that: none adds an image, a quotation or a flourish. Most
remove a repetition or restore a reason. If a later round adds anything, the test is whether the new sentence does work
the report needs done (a reason, a link, a payoff); if it only adds polish, it is the first thing a judge will hear as
generic.
