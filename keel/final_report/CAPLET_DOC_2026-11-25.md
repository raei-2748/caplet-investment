## The opening question

Caplet's test: the question must provoke curiosity about an intellectual direction, not restate the client's problem.
Keel's reading tests bear on it: in voice round 13 the strongest payoff came from a question the facts themselves raise,
which a reader can hold without finance, and a line that announces a point was heard as a signpost.

**"How should we invest when the risks we take are no longer ours alone?"** The direction is right (who bears the risk
once she has promised). Three things weaken it: a judge can answer it before reading on ("carefully"), since every
adviser invests risk that is not only theirs; "we" and "ours" make a cold reader work out whose risks these are; and the
IPS ended "With these promises, ours is to take none", so the team taking risks others bear pulls against the line the
report continues.

Options, each with the answer the next paragraph gives:

- **A. "When does Laura's money stop being hers to risk?"** Not when she spends it, but when she promises it. The
  building's money is spent in January 2033 yet leaves shares in January 2031, because 2031 is when she names a figure.
  The ten payments are bought with the first deposit (finished with the second), the building's bottom with the second
  and its top in January 2031, before she names either; only money promised to no one stays in shares. The deletion test is the proof; pencil and ink names it (her career and her
  unpromised shares in pencil; a promise in ink only once Treasuries held for her will pay it). Keel's recommendation:
  it keeps Caplet's thesis, continues the IPS's last line, and its answer is dated and checkable. The surprise against
  ordinary practice: a target-date fund cuts risk as the spending date nears; her plan cuts it as the promise date nears
  (GOLDMINE 27; the spine of card Q163-Q174: a promise breaks in the gap between the day it is made and the day the money
  for it exists).
- **B. "What would Laura lose if every share she owns were worthless before 2031?"** None of her promises: all ten
  payments and $95,000 for the building are still paid; she loses only the top of the range and money she never
  promised. The most concrete; it may read as narrow, so it serves best as A's answer.
- **C. "How do $450,000 of deposits keep $615,000 of promises?"** Time: interest on Treasuries bought years before each
  date, and, for the top alone, three years of share growth moved into Treasuries before she names it. Round 13's strongest payoff came from this question;
  it is arithmetic rather than a direction, so it fits the opening of Section 3.
