## What tonight's research found

Tonight twelve researchers read well outside finance: Shakespeare and Austen on debts, philosophers on what makes a
promise legitimate, the history of public reserves from Athens to Walpole, the anthropology of the gift, what economists
have meant by "certain", why buildings overrun, and how accountants decide a debt is already settled. They kept
arriving at the same place. A promise breaks in the gap between the day it is made and the day the money for it exists.
Antonio expected his ships home a month before his bond fell due, carrying nine times its value; they did come home,
but only after the bond was forfeit. In 5 CE Rome voted its soldiers fixed discharge payments a year before it set up a
treasury to pay them, and when the money ran short it kept the sums and moved the date. In 1960 the New South Wales
Parliament wrote into law that the Opera House's estimated cost "shall not, under any circumstances, be exceeded by
more than ten per centum"; it cost about nine and a half times that first limit. In Osaka the lords sold rice bills that by 1749
claimed almost four times the rice in the warehouses. Carnegie's libraries were built on towns' promises of running
money that a third of Ohio's towns had broken by 1916.

Laura's plan closes that gap from the other side. We buy the Treasuries first, and Laura states a figure only once she
owns what will pay it. That is the idea under the whole report, and pencil and ink is its picture. The research for the
next seven days sets out to test that idea from every direction, find where it breaks, and give each part of the
report one idea that explains a real decision in the plan rather than decorating it.

Tonight's 388 new ideas were all rated the same evening (239 kept, 69 kept with a correction, 76 merged into stronger
ones, 4 refused as colour only), so the store now holds 8,742. The copout already has its own researcher, working
from theory and wide reading.

## What the week is for

Three jobs, in this order:

1. **Research and analysis that change what the report can say.** Every run sends at least twelve researchers at once,
   each on one question, each reading at the source. A find counts only if it explains, tests or changes a decision in
   Laura's plan, and says where the parallel breaks.
2. **Numbers and exhibits ready to drop into the report.** Every figure re-derived a second way, every table built at
   its printed size, and a page budget proven on the template before Friday.
3. **A voice that is understood on one reading and sounds like us.** Caplet's messages of Thursday set two tests: every sentence
   is understood by an intelligent reader the first time and makes sense once understood; and the writing keeps "we",
   explains the why, has some dry wit, and is neither plain-generic nor mannered.

One rule from tonight's analysis of version 61 runs through all three. Version 61 failed partly because Keel's checks
turned into sentences: a correction became a defending clause, and the clause used names the reader had never been
given. So from now on research goes to the plan, checks go to the checkers, and the writers see only the facts and
Caplet's own writing (`voice/WHY_V61_FAILED.md`).

## Voice: what each round finds out

Two categories, from Caplet's message of Thursday afternoon. The first and far more urgent is that every sentence is understood by an intelligent
reader on one reading and makes sense once understood. The second is a distinctive voice, learned from Caplet's essay,
IPS and Trading Notes reflections, with the dry wit Caplet asked for on Thursday (and never Caplet's own Adam Smith
example). Caplet's notes of Thursday evening add three that apply to both: keep "we" at the rate Caplet's own writing uses it, explain the
why rather than compress, and fall into neither the plain-generic style nor the mannered one.

How every round works:
- Before any reader sees a passage, Keel writes down which sentences it expects to fail, so its own judgement is
  tested too.
- Writers read only Caplet's own writing and the facts. They never see a rule list or Keel's checks; the thirteen
  "don'ts" from tonight's analysis are for the checkers and readers only (`voice/WHY_V61_FAILED.md`).
- Readers first read Caplet's seventeen version 61 lines. A reader who follows them all is not used, because those are
  the lines Caplet could not follow.
- Readers use only what the page says. For each sentence they paraphrase it, say whether they had to reread it, and
  whether it makes sense. A reread counts as a failure.
- Caplet's real passages are mixed in, unmarked, as controls.

## Friday 27 November: the writing session

One 24-hour session of at least twelve rounds. In each round fresh writers write each part from Caplet's writing, the
fact sheet and the part's section notes; the main session checks every draft against Wharton's instructions, the submitted IPS
and Trading Notes, and the numbers; a literal reader and a logician then go sentence by sentence, and any sentence they
had to reread, or whose "because" does not produce its claim, is rewritten whole rather than squeezed. Blind readers,
with Caplet's real passages mixed in, judge the voice. The final draft goes to Caplet on Saturday 28 November.
