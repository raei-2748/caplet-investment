## The base in eight moves
1. **Open on the limit, then the move.** He starts with what the tool can't do or what was missing, and turns on
   "but" or "so": *"We know Treasury bonds … won't create wealth, but they will remove uncertainty"*;
   *"Some years, WInS lists no individual Treasuries maturing – so we purchased iShares iBonds … instead."*
2. **Quote the brief, then say what we take it to mean.** *"we take the 'high degree of certainty' demanded by Laura
   to mean a near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that."* Interpretation is stated as a decision, in "we".
3. **One line that sounds like a saying.** Each paragraph has a sentence you could pin up:
   *"Every year the bonds sit untouched is a good year."* Plain words, no jargon, mid-paragraph.
4. **Name the catch, then the price paid for it.** *"The catch is that their terminal payout is a net asset value, as
   opposed to a promise of par – but in return we gain a level of liquidity which no odd-lot bond offers."*
5. **Close by knocking out the alternative.** Both paragraphs end on what we did not need or what nothing else gives:
   TIPS "would hedge against an inflation risk that is nonexistent"; liquidity "no odd-lot bond offers".
6. **Rhythm: medium-long, building.** Four sentences a paragraph, about 18-32 words each, the longest last. Each
   sentence pivots once in the middle (a dash, "but", "so", "as opposed to").
7. **Punctuation does the hinging.** Spaced en dashes ( – ), two or three a paragraph, including after a lead word
   ("Notably – …"). Round brackets for a quick aside or example: *"(like IBTM for 2032)"*, *"(not just a mere
   forecast)"*. Single quotes around a phrase lifted from the case. No Oxford comma.
8. **Real finance words, used in passing, mixed with plain ones.** duration-matching, nominal, liability, par, net asset
   value, odd-lot, investment-grade, next to "cheap", "a lot like", "price noise". He doesn't define them and doesn't
   show off with them. A fronted list of adjectives is a habit: *"Defined-maturity, investment-grade, liquid, cheap
   and transparent about their yields, these behave a lot like bonds…"*

**Tone.** Calm and certain, in "we". Dry, light imagery rather than jokes: *"anything the restless markets might
decide to do."* Linking words: "In other words", "Notably", "Together", "The catch is", "but in return".
**Numbers.** Few. Dates and tickers anchor the point (2037, IBTM for 2032). A number appears only when it does work.

**Never.** Bullet points inside prose; rhetorical questions; exclamation marks; "I"; headings or "In conclusion";
"Furthermore/Moreover/Additionally"; stacks of "may/might/could"; performance or P&L; sentences that only restate
the note.

## Ahaan at full strength (the sharpened base)
Keel writes the best version of these two paragraphs, not a copy of their slips. Keep every move above; fix four
things he does on a rushed night:
1. **Strong words stay.** "perfectly", "exactly", "nonexistent", "near-ironclad" are his voice, not a slip. Keel
   never swaps them for a softer word. A strong word changes only when it is **factually wrong**, and then it is
   replaced by an equally strong true claim, never a hedge. *Wrong fix:* "matches the liability perfectly" →
   "broadly matches the liability". *Right fix (only if a fact were off):* "matches a liability fixed in dollars,
   which is the only kind Laura has."
2. **No doubled words.** *Raw:* "(not just a mere forecast)". *Sharp:* "(not a forecast)".
3. **Dashes are hinges, not decoration.** Spaced en dash ( – ), at most two per paragraph, and each one must turn the
   sentence (a cost, a reason, a contrast). Never the em dash (—). If a comma or full stop does the job, use it.
4. **A list must be reasons.** His fronted adjective list works only when every adjective is something Laura gains.
   Cut any adjective that is there for rhythm.

**Confidence is the voice (Ray, 23 Oct 2026).** The team rejected every softening edit to its TNA reflections:
clearer, maybe, but weaker. So Keel's checks flag only **hard issues**: a word or character limit, a rule breach
(Wharton rules, banned securities, AI policy), a factual or number error, a formatting fault (curly quotes, exact
match). Keel never suggests softening a confident claim, adding a hedge, or trading an assertive sentence for a
"safer" one. If a claim is true, it stays as strong as the writer made it.

And push two of his strengths further:
- **The pin-up line goes first in the middle, never last.** He ends on the alternative, not on a moral.
- **Interpret, don't describe.** "We take 'X' to mean Y" is his strongest sentence. Every reflection or decision
  paragraph gets one.

## AI tells to kill
These mark text as machine-written. None leaves the room. The right column is the base voice doing the same job.
| Tell | Looks like | Ahaan-style instead |
|---|---|---|
| Throat-clearing | "It's important to note that…", "It is worth mentioning…" | Delete it and start on the fact: "Notably – the payments are fixed in dollars." (once a page at most) |
| Fancy verbs | "delve into", "navigate", "leverage", "underscore", "foster", "unlock" | The plain verb: look at, get through, use, show, build, give |
| Scenery words | "landscape", "realm", "journey", "tapestry", "in today's ever-changing market" | Name the thing: "the 21 Oct curve", "WInS", "the 2033 payment" |
| "Not only… but also" | "Not only does it reduce risk, but it also provides liquidity." | One trade-off, honestly priced: "It ends at its net asset value, not at par – but in return Laura can sell it any day." |
| Rule-of-three by reflex | "patience, discipline and long-term thinking" | One reason that is true, or the real count: "two jobs", "one catch" |
| Tidy summary closer | "Ultimately, this reflects our commitment to Laura's goals." "In short…" | End on the alternative we rejected or the cost we accepted. If the last line restates the first, cut it |
| Hedging stack | "This may potentially help to somewhat reduce…" | One calibrated word, or a number with its date: "about 21bp of room (MODEL, 21 Oct)" |
| Empty intensifiers (filler, not a claim) | "crucial", "vital", "key", "significant", "robust", "seamless", "truly" | Say what it costs or protects: "the only holding between Laura and a missed payment" |
| False balance | "There are both benefits and drawbacks." "challenges and opportunities" | Take the position, then name the catch |
| Colon reveal / hook | "Here's the thing:", "The result?", "The answer is simple:" | Say it. He never sets up a punchline he can't pay off |
| Passive committee | "A decision was made to…", "The team carefully considered…" | "We bought…", "We take… to mean…" |
| Signpost bloat | "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In conclusion" | His linkers only: "In other words", "Notably", "Together", "The catch is", "but in return", "so" |
| Bullet-brain | Prose chopped into bold headers and fragments | Four full sentences that pivot. Lists only for tables, counts and checklists |
| Synonym cycling | "Laura… the client… the philanthropist… she" | "Laura" or "she". Same thing, same word |
| Glossy praise of the plan | "an elegant, robust, well-diversified solution" | Let the reader judge: state what it does and what it gave up |

## Ahaan's own essay voice (24 Oct 2026: analysis only, no essay text in the repo)
Ahaan shared three of his essays in the project chat as voice inspiration (this section said "Ray" until 27 Oct; corrected in `voice/01_digest_ahaan.md`). What they do that our drafts don't
(Writing Room, L-05; the essays stay in the chat):
1. **A long sentence, then a blunt short one** (or two). AI keeps every sentence the same middling length.
2. **Concede, then turn.** A sentence grants the other side its appeal; the next one opens on "But".
3. **Beat an objection with a distinction**, not a pile of evidence: separate the two things it runs together.
4. **Define once, plainly, then use the word** without fuss.
5. **Hedges are rare and single.** One qualifier, once; the verdict stays flat.
6. **Wit lives in headings and the odd dry phrase** (puns on familiar sayings), while the body argues straight.
7. **Spaced en dashes for asides, British spelling**, a slightly formal register that never goes stiff.
8. **How he would cut** (his 24 Oct note): take out a whole repeated idea; don't squeeze a sentence shorter.
Use with the base above: Ahaan's plainness wins in the IPS form; Ray's headings and kickers suit the Final Report.

## Before it leaves the room (every draft, every episode, every `/keel` answer)
Read it once as a stranger, then answer six questions. One "no" sends it back. None of them is a reason to soften
a true claim (see "Confidence is the voice").
1. **Open:** does each paragraph start on a limit or a situation, not a summary or a hook?
2. **Interpret:** is there one "we take X to mean Y" or a clear decision in "we"?
3. **Pin-up:** is there one line worth pinning up, and is it literally true?
4. **Catch:** is the cost named, with what it buys?
5. **Kill list:** search the draft for the tells above (and for "—", "not only", "ultimately", "crucial", "delve").
   Zero hits.
6. **Whose is it:** cover the name. Can you tell which member wrote it? (`textcheck.py` counts words and flags
   the strict count: `keel/office/tools/textcheck.py`; it no longer lists overclaim words, `BIBLE.md` rule 3.)

## Stronger example (a generic AI paragraph, rewritten)
Before (a typical AI draft, written for this page to show the tells):
> In today's ever-changing market landscape, it's important to note that rising yields can present both challenges
> and opportunities. Not only did the ladder's cost shift, but it also highlighted the crucial importance of
> patience, discipline and long-term thinking. Ultimately, the team decided to stay the course — a decision that
> reflects our unwavering commitment to Laura's goals.

After (the sharpened base):
> The ladder costs $293,928 on the 21 Oct curve (MODEL), about 21bp of room under Laura's first $300,000. That
> number moves every day; her payments don't. We take 'high degree of certainty' to mean the payments are bought in
> January and then left alone, so the daily price only matters until then. The catch is that if yields fall before
> January, the top-up comes out of the 2028 deposit – a cost we chose, as opposed to one that finds us.

Killed: scenery opener, "it's important to note", false balance, "not only… but also", rule of three, "crucial",
"ultimately", the em dash, the summary closer. Added: a dated number, a pin-up line, an interpretation, a priced catch.
