# What Caplet expects of Keel: notes on the whole Bible

Written Sun 8 Nov 2026 (Sydney) at Caplet's request (Book 14): "create truly comprehensive notes on all of my Bibles
... such that I could say, 'You've expressed my thoughts and expectations even better than I did.'"

**What these notes cover.** Every book of the Bible (Books 1-15 and the cover note of 26 Oct), Ray's points of 5 Nov
that Book 12 says to keep in mind, the Ten Commandments Keel drew from Books 1-10, every row of the instruction
register (`INSTRUCTIONS.md`, I01-I91), and Caplet's message of 6 Nov that came with its own IPS. Wharton's word
(`WHARTON_WORD.md`) ranks above all of it and is kept whole, not summarised here; section 0 says how the two fit.
Part 10 maps every book and every register row to the notes that carry it, so anyone can check that nothing was
dropped.

**How to read them.** Caplet asked for expectations, not rules: "they can't be responded to with a simple 'yes' or
'no' ... the extent to which you have met them exists on a continuum". So each note says what Caplet means, then
describes three points on the dial (falls short, meets it, delights), then says what it asks of the Final Report.
Quotes are Caplet's own words. Where two expectations pull against each other, Part 8 says how Keel resolves it.

---

## The whole of it, in one paragraph

Caplet wants a Final Report that a judge reading thousands of entries could not mistake for anyone else's, and would
still be thinking about the next day. It has to earn that with thinking, not decoration. It should show that the team
read further than anyone else, found ideas in places nobody looks, tested each one honestly against Laura's case, and
built its own plan out of them, so that every part of the plan can be traced to the people it learned from and to the
exact point where it parted from them. Every section has to rest on that depth, including the ones that have nothing
to do with investing. And it must still read as if six sharp, confident, slightly cheeky students wrote it: plain
enough to follow on one reading, warm, sure of itself, never hedging and never clever for its own sake. The work behind
it should be enormous and slow. The writing should come late and fast, many drafts in a single day, each judged
against Wharton's five criteria and against these notes. Nothing good that Keel has found may be lost on the way.

## Part 1. The bar

### 1.1 Truly extraordinary, and nothing less
Caplet's one rule "above all else": the work must be "truly, exceptionally, stunningly, breathtakingly, extraordinary"
(Book 5). The reason is arithmetic, not vanity. About 2,300 teams submit; only the Top 50 go on, "a winner-takes-all
system" (Book 10); "we can't possibly make it into the top 2% by simply being 'compliant'" (Book 13). Competent,
impressive, even very good work loses: "We cannot make it to the semi-finals of this competition by being just another
great competent team" (Book 1). What wins is "a uniquely breathtaking quality", "something which no one else would do
or think to do (Not even the other teams who may also be using AI)", insights that make a judge think "aha" (Book 1).
- *Falls short:* correct, thorough, well organised, and indistinguishable from a strong team's report. "Competent,
  impressive, but not creative, original, novel, thoughtful or insightful" (Book 10, on V29).
- *Meets it:* a judge finishes the report able to name two or three ideas they had not met before and believes the
  team is unusually able.
- *Delights:* Caplet is "jaw-droppingly, breathtakingly stunned" (Book 13); a judge retells our reasoning to someone
  else the next day.
- *In the Final Report:* every section is tested with one question before anything else: what here would a strong
  team not have? A section with no answer is rethought, not polished.

### 1.2 Stand out on our own brilliance
We differentiate by being extraordinary, never by comparing ourselves with other teams: "referring to things like
'every team will do x' is a bit meta and also a bit arrogant" (Book 10). No sneers at "typical" plans either.
- *Falls short:* "most teams will ...", "unlike a standard plan ...".
- *Delights:* the report never mentions other teams and does not need to.

### 1.3 Take intellectual risks
"That means taking risks, intellectual and otherwise. After all, we've got nothing to lose, but everything to gain"
(Book 13; also Book 10, "take intellectual risks"). Caplet prefers a bold idea that might not land to a safe one
that certainly won't stand out. The limit is truth (5.1) and coherence with the locked deliverables (5.2), not
caution.
- *Falls short:* every claim is the safest available; the report contains nothing a reader could disagree with.
- *Delights:* at least one argument a thoughtful judge might push back on, made so well they end up persuaded.

## Part 2. The thinking (for the Final Report, "by far most importantly", Book 13)

### 2.1 Read wider and dig deeper than anyone
The fourth north star: "truly extraordinary knowledge of investing concepts and strategies" (Book 4), "a level of wide
reading (and wide thinking!) which no other team could ever do" (Book 13). "Go for the most niche ones rather than the
obvious ones which every team would've heard about" (Book 13); "those which 99.9% of students our age and indeed
investors don't know about" (Book 9); "from the most unexpected places in the nooks and crannies of the web and the
world" (Book 13); "dig up info from the most unexpected places and the least-looked-for sources" (Book 9). Book 12
names two places to start and go far beyond: Damodaran's corporate finance lectures and Yale's ECON 252 (Shiller), and
a list from another AI to judge item by item ("Don't take it as gospel").
- *Falls short:* the ideas any finance textbook lists (diversification, duration, the 60/40, Markowitz) carrying the
  argument by themselves.
- *Meets it:* each part of the plan rests on several sources a well-read practitioner would respect, including some
  they would not expect a school team to know.
- *Delights:* a judge learns something from the report, and wants to look a source up.
- *In the Final Report:* textbook ideas stay only where we use them in a way the textbook doesn't. The Works Cited
  shows the width; the body shows the depth.

### 2.2 Apply every idea; never name-drop
"That doesn't mean rapid name-dropping, of course, because that'll just harm our score" (Book 4). Knowledge counts
only when it is "applying and explaining a wide range of investing concepts ... to formulate our own". It must
"SUPPORT and ENHANCE, rather than replace or undercut" the narrative and our ability to state a clear strategy of our
own (Book 4).
- *Falls short:* a paragraph of famous names and a sentence each.
- *Meets it:* each idea is explained in plain words and then does a job: it explains a decision, tests one, or
  changes one.
- *Delights:* the idea makes the reader see Laura's plan differently.

### 2.3 Critique every idea: like and unlike, worked and failed, fits and doesn't
"Don't uncritically dump them into our report. Analyse them. Evaluate them. Critique them ... A strategy might have
missed something, or it might initially seem relevant to our case but really it's not, and here's why" (Book 9).
"Explain how they are analogous or disanalogous to our situation ... Critically analyse whether they have been
successful or unsuccessful in the real world of investing. Evaluate whether they would work for our specific [case].
Don't just blindly take on every investing idea in the world" (Book 13).
- *Falls short:* ideas presented only as support. Every borrowed idea agrees with us.
- *Meets it:* for each idea we say how Laura's case is like it, how it is not, whether it has worked where it was
  tried (with evidence), and what we took, changed or refused.
- *Delights:* the disanalogy is where the insight comes from: the point where Laura's case differs is the reason our
  plan differs, and the judge sees it.
- *In the Final Report:* the five-question card (what is it; did it work; how is Laura's case like it; how is it not;
  what do we take) sits behind every idea. Refused ideas earn a place too: showing what we rejected and why is
  evidence of judgement.

### 2.4 Synthesis and creation: the shoulders of giants
The centre of Caplet's expectation for the report: "we're not just looking for analysis and evaluation – we're
looking for SYNTHESIS and CREATION ... the absolute uppermost bounds of Bloom's taxonomy" (Book 13). "This is all about
synthesis and formulation. We need SYNERGIES" (Book 9). "We can fuse together two completely different niche
strategies. We can synthesise dozens of ideas to formulate our own" (Book 9). The sentence Caplet wants to be able to
say:

> "We didn't ignore these. We didn't copy them, either. We took inspiration from them, and we used them to formulate
> our own. Here's exactly how this formulation is, in effect, just a synthesis of these theories and strategies and
> concepts which already existed." (Book 13)

"In other words: we will see further, because we will stand on the shoulders of giants. Never forget this. This is how
we will win." The test is two-sided: "We need to prove that we're creative: we are capable of coming up with our own
ideas. But that's built upon an impossibly extraordinary foundation of knowledge" (Book 13).
- *Falls short:* a plan, then a list of related theories. Or a list of theories, then a plan that could have come
  from anywhere.
- *Meets it:* each part of the plan (the ladder, the 2032 Treasury fund and its bottom of the range, the shares, the
  half kept back, the move before 2031, the two reasons to change) is shown with the three to five ideas from
  different fields that point to it, what we took from each and what we refused.
- *Delights:* the combination produces something none of its sources had, and the judge can see exactly where.
- *In the Final Report:* this lineage is the report's spine, not an appendix.

### 2.5 Find the connection nobody else would make
"Find unexpected connections, synergies, analogies, syntheses and so on. Find uncommon ground – 'the road less taken',
as it were – and actively seek it out. That's where the best material lies" (Book 2). "Find unexpected synergies and
analogies and links. That's what will impress the judges" (Book 13). "Impress the judges by pointing out things which
they probably wouldn't have noticed themselves" (Book 13). The ideal is an "aha" for the judge "and us alike" (Book 13):
a link that teaches us something too.
- *Falls short:* analogies chosen for colour.
- *Delights:* an analogy from another field that predicts something true about Laura's plan that we can then check.

### 2.6 Show intelligence through logic as well as knowledge
Ahaan's second route to looking intelligent: "LOGIC. Analogies, thought experiments, causal chains, logical deduction
(including taking a strand of reasoning all the way to its logical conclusion – which can land us in unexpected
places ...), intuition, case studies, evidence, studies, facts and figures" (Book 9). Caplet's own IPS does this in
its opening: a writer's income falls in recessions, when shares fall and co-sponsors cut budgets, so her career is a
large unsellable holding of shares, so the portfolio should lean the other way.
- *Falls short:* conclusions asserted.
- *Meets it:* each important claim is reached by steps a reader can follow.
- *Delights:* a chain of reasoning carried further than the reader expected, arriving somewhere surprising and right.

### 2.7 Creativity lives in the strategy and the reasoning, not the packaging
Ray, 5 Nov: "metaphors alone does not count as creativity. We want intellectual creativity, yours just packaging."
Creativity is what the plan does and why; a picture laid over it is not creativity, however clever. Ray's follow-up,
"so what is most creative?", wants the answer to be an idea about money and risk.
- *Falls short:* a motif, a name or a turn of phrase doing the work an idea should do.
- *Delights:* the most creative thing in the report is a decision, and the reader admires the decision.

### 2.8 Every part of the report rests on research, not only the investing
Book 15: "all of your insights and analysis and above all RESEARCH needn't be related to investing ... Wherever we
have to create something or do something or make a decision that isn't explicitly or directly investing-related ...
I still want you to demonstrate a stunning level of knowledge and intellect and reading and research ... Every single
part of our Final Report MUST be underpinned by a breathtaking level of research and analysis." The case has many such
parts: what "a high degree of certainty" means, the residency and what keeps such places alive, Taiwan's costs and
currency, how co-sponsors decide and what they need to read, the fundraising text itself, Laura's working life as an
author, how the team learned, and how the report presents numbers and uncertainty. Book 13 already pointed this way:
"all the research you've done into all kinds of different things which relate to the case and which can strengthen
our argument."
- *Falls short:* the investing sections are deep and the co-sponsor text, the reflection and the presentation are
  written from common sense.
- *Meets it:* every section cites research that bears on its own question.
- *Delights:* the non-investing sections surprise most, because a judge expects them to be thin.

### 2.9 One convincing argument, not fragments
Ray, 5 Nov: "Your analysis is kind of fragmented. So convince our strategy is the best. So explain what our strategy
is. What is the biggest strength our strategy? After all this work, why do you think our strategy is very, very
strong?" Wharton says the same: judges want "the reasoning, assumptions, and tradeoffs" and "a cohesive strategy in
which your investment decisions work together". From the Commandments: a few big ideas argued at full size beat many
mechanisms touched briefly.
- *Falls short:* many good points, no through-line; the reader has to assemble the case.
- *Meets it:* the reader can say the strategy, its biggest strength and its main trade-off in three sentences.
- *Delights:* every section adds to one argument, and the conclusion feels inevitable.

### 2.10 The big picture, not the minutiae
"They're interested in the big picture. They're interested in our investing knowledge and strategic thinking. Not our
ability to plan out every single detail of every single trade we'll ever make for the next 20 years" (Book 9). "You
seem to be too focused on making minute changes" (Book 9). Judges are "not interrogating every word for absolute
precision like an AI does".
- *Falls short:* pages on edge cases, exact dates of minor trades, internal-consistency trivia.
- *Meets it:* detail appears where it changes a decision or proves a claim, and nowhere else.

### 2.11 Numbers that explain themselves, and models in their proper place
Every number says what it is and where it came from: "it's not 'we judge', it's 'we model' or 'we calculate'"
(Book 10). No figure "plucked out of thin air, with no explanation". The Final Report is where models belong: "Save
all those details for the Final Report" (Book 11), with "context, ... images, ... screenshots, ... evidence". Ray
asked for "more COMPUTER MODELS" (27 Oct), and Book 9 praised a model-driven insight (the top of the range reached
about three times in four, so over-promising treated as about three times worse) as "the kind of pick-up which could
genuinely be useful". Caplet wants models that prove the strategy strong and turn into arguments, not decoration.
- *Falls short:* a casual "our model shows", or a number with no source.
- *Meets it:* each model answers one question the case asks, states its assumptions, and its result is said in plain
  words beside the chart.
- *Delights:* a model finding becomes an insight about the plan that a judge remembers.

## Part 3. The writing (second to the content, "but still somewhat important", Book 13)

### 3.1 Understood by a human on one reading
The most repeated complaint in the Bible, and the reason Caplet rejected Keel's IPS. "Some of the sentences are just
really weird ... I just don't understand what some of them are trying to say" (Book 10). "Half of your sentences just
DON'T MAKE SENSE" (Book 11). "I swear I literally can't understand half of the sentences in the IPS" (Book 12). Caplet's
own IPS was better because "it's COMPREHENSIBLE to humans, not just to fellow robots" (6 Nov). This is not a request to
simplify: "That doesn't mean dumbing down your language or your sentence structure or your concepts ... Humans aren't
stupid. They just think in different ways to a robot" (Book 13). Mock judges built by Keel cannot test it, because
"they are effectively you – AI – and understand your language" (Book 10).

Caplet suspects "your robotic inclination for maximum parsimony and concision", and adds "I might be wrong, however.
Don't rely on that. Conduct your own voice studies" (Book 13). Voice study 1 (`final_report/voice/STUDY1_two_voices.md`)
found four faults in Keel's sentences: pointers whose owner is too far back ("the ones", "any it can't afford", "that
half"), steps of reasoning left out because Keel already knew them, money rather than people doing the acting, and
compression. Caplet's sentences put people first, say reasons out loud ("so", "because", "that means"), carry one new
thing each, announce changes of subject, concede and then turn, name the reader's worry and answer it, and use things
from Laura's world.
- *Falls short:* a sentence that needs a second reading, or that only makes sense if you already know our plan.
- *Meets it:* an intelligent adult who has read the case follows every sentence the first time.
- *Delights:* hard ideas (a capital-protected structure, a hedge against human capital) feel easy, and the reader
  feels clever rather than lectured.

### 3.2 Cut whole ideas; never squeeze sentences
"Where you need to make cuts, the instinct shouldn't be to just rearrange words so they're more concise" (Book 2).
"Whenever you try to make things more parsimonious, you often end up making them sound more AI ... cut the HUMAN way,
not the AI way" (Book 9). "Cut things whole, don't chop and snip words here and there" (Book 12). Leanness is still
wanted ("we absolutely do want your writing to be parsimonious and lucid", Book 2), but it comes from choosing fewer
ideas, not from fewer words per idea.
- *Falls short:* the small words, verbs and links stripped out to save space.
- *Meets it:* each idea has the sentences it needs; ideas that don't fit are cut whole.
- *In the Final Report:* the report has room. Voice study 1 showed one of Keel's best ideas (the shares bought with
  roughly the interest the 2032 fund earns, so they risk only that interest) dropped from the IPS because it was
  squeezed into one sentence. In the report it gets four or five.

### 3.3 A human voice of our own
North star 1: "a genuinely distinctive team voice. It must carry throughout. And it must sound unique. Cerebral yet
witty, passionate yet thoughtful, insightful yet confident: we must sound quite unlike any other team ... I want our
work to be the kind of thing they can honestly ENJOY reading" (Book 1). "Write like WE write, not like YOU write"
(Book 2), "real, messy human writing". Generic AI is the enemy, and generic human is not the goal either: "so that we
don't sound like generic HUMANS, which isn't quite as bad as sounding like generic AI but is certainly in that realm"
(Book 12).

Where the voice comes from, in order: Caplet's final IPS and the Trading Notes Analysis, "a genuinely distinctive
voice which differs vastly from AI slop" (Book 13); Ahaan's three essays, for tone only, "not gospel", never their
structure (Books 2, 9, 11, 12: "my voice isn't as good; it's just a small guide"); witty finance writers, "very useful
as well" (Book 9). Voice is "fundamentally qualitative" and must never be judged by counting (Book 9). Keel's voice
studies exist to be applied, not filed (Books 10, 12).
- *Falls short:* correct, even, anonymous prose; or a writer's tics copied without the writer's sense.
- *Meets it:* reads like the FINAL IPS at greater length: warm, direct, a little wry, confident.
- *Delights:* a judge enjoys reading it and could pick our team's voice out of a pile.

### 3.4 Confidence and flair; no hedging
"The problem with AI is that it ALWAYS hedges and looks for the safest, mildest, least offensive word out there.
That's not we want here. We want FLAIR. We want VOICE" (Book 3). "Hedging and 'safe' language is a classic habit of
AI – one which is instantly recognisable to judges looking for real authenticity" (Book 5). Humans "DO overclaim a
little bit"; Caplet doesn't want deliberate overclaiming, only an end to Keel's fear of strong words. Example from Book
5: iBonds held to their December close are "near-ironclad", "as close to guaranteeing an exact amount at an exact date
as it gets" outside T-bonds; a distinction from T-bonds, if made, is "concise and direct rather than 'nuanced'". On 23
Oct Ray rejected every softening edit to the TNA ("it would soften our voice").
- *Falls short:* "may", "potentially", "it could be argued"; a true strong claim softened.
- *Meets it:* plain, confident statements; a strong word is changed only if it is false, and then for an equally
  strong true one.
- *Delights:* the reader trusts us because we sound as if we know.

### 3.5 Win an investor's conviction
Ray, 27 Oct: "you gotta have emotional appeal man. Judges are not examiners ... judges are shark tanks." Ray, same day:
Keel's writing "is just telling facts ... like eating plastic". Caplet's IPS shows how: it talks to Laura, admits it
changed its mind, jokes ("So we hedge it!"), and ends on her own life ("You take risks on the page. Fair enough; that's
your job. With these promises, ours is to take none.").
- *Falls short:* a report that informs and never moves.
- *Delights:* the reader cares whether Laura's residency opens, and believes this team is the one to back.

### 3.6 Nothing corny, nothing machine-made
The marks of AI writing Caplet has named: rhetorical-question openings ("the corniest and most cringeworthy thing I've
ever seen", Book 11); slogan flips, two-word kickers, "That is X." runs, one-line endings (Ray, 3 Nov, list in
`ips_rewrite/drafts/D35/README.md`); writing that reads as "AI and corny: tone, phrasing, syntax, taste" (Ray, 3 Nov);
"do not crush" ("it sounds so corny", Ray, 5 Nov); medicine or rehab imagery ("she doesn't need any rehab", Ray, 5
Nov); copied lines and styles "which don't work in this context" (Book 11); elaborate metaphor, which AI does "far more
– and far more eloquently – than humans do" (Book 2). And the fingerprints no ban list catches: "Sometimes some aspects
of AI writing transcend the kinds of things which you can ban with a neat list of AI tells" (Book 2).
- *Falls short:* a line a reader would quote as an example of AI writing.
- *Meets it:* nothing reads as a slogan; nothing is there to sound clever.

### 3.7 The case's words and real names
Ray, 4 Nov: "do not invent terms". The case's own words carry the plan (operating commitment, operating payments,
operating reserve, facility contribution, credible range, co-sponsors, high degree of certainty, financial
flexibility); instruments are called what they are (VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF; iShares iBonds Term Treasury
ETFs). Caplet's own IPS uses "floor" and "ceiling" for the bottom and top of the range, and "bare minimum", so the
report may too (Part 8, T11). Terms are introduced before they are used.

## Part 4. How the work is done

### 4.1 Think long; write late; rewrite wholesale
The cover note (26 Oct): "This is supposed to be a long process because it should take an extraordinary ... amount of
thinking and ideation and research and analysis ... THINK. That's your job. I don't want you to be hasty." The IPS went
wrong because Keel "began writing it really early, before you've actually done sufficient work ... Then ... only by
making small, minor, a-few-words tweaks ... That basically locks the document in time, in a primitive state" (Book 13;
also Book 4: "A little bit of chopping and changing won't cut it"). Book 14 sets the rule for the report: "do not write
anything of the Final Report until literally the day before" the 28 Nov deadline. Until then: "research and analysis
and thinking. Deep. Very, very deep ... without actually creating anything which will be copy–pasted directly into the
Final Report. Just ideas."
- *Falls short:* any draft paragraph before Fri 27 Nov; later rounds that only swap words.
- *Meets it:* 19 days of research, analysis and thinking; then one 24-hour writing session.
- *Delights:* each round of that session is free to throw away whole sections, and some rounds do.

### 4.2 One writer, many rounds, a real review
"There's going to be a single person or agent ... which writes the entire thing. And then there's going to be a review
– against (a) every single one of my expectations across every single one of my Bibles (this is by far the most
important ...), (b) Wharton's marking guidelines, and (c) just simply being AS BRILLIANT AS POSSIBLE ... and then you're
going to rewrite it ... many, many times" (Book 12). For the report: "You generate a draft, you critically review it,
you rewrite it ... at least a dozen times before you settle on the final version. It should be an arduous and
painstaking but also intensive and hence brief process" (Book 14). Before writing: "read through every single thing
you've produced so far ... every single idea you've had" and these notes (Book 12).
- *Falls short:* sections written by different agents; reviews that approve.
- *Meets it:* one writer; at least twelve logged rounds; each review argues from (a), (b) and (c).
- *Delights:* the final draft is unrecognisably better than the first, and the log shows why.

### 4.3 Judges who genuinely disagree, marking only against Wharton
Keel's mock judges were "really all the same in many ways" and "almost seem symbolic rather than purposeful,
approving almost every change" (Book 10). They must be "diverse not just in label but in their evaluations, and who
make judgements in vastly, indeed radically different ways." They must read like humans, for the big picture, and
never fixate on a single sentence, "framework lines" or "elegant sentences and nice rhetoric": "Your only allegiance in
terms of marking is to the marking guide from Wharton, and ABSOLUTELY NOTHING ELSE" (Book 10). Judges are competition
judges looking for "the greatest investing knowledge and the best investing strategy", not "mini-me Lauras" (Book 3).
- *Falls short:* a panel that agrees, praises lines, or decides on one sentence.
- *Meets it:* readers differ in method and priors, read blind, and report where they disagree.
- *In the Final Report:* the panel's disagreements are used to find weaknesses, never as a score to celebrate.

### 4.4 Lose nothing; keep one ranked store
"Every single good idea you have ... must end up in either the IPS or the Final Report or both" (Book 9). "Keep a list
of every single idea you had in every single version of your IPS and ensure that all of the best ideas end up" (Book
10). "A lot of your rooms ... had thousands of good ideas which you just completely ignored or forgot about ... This is
shameful" (Book 12). For the report: "Keep a store of every single insight you have. They should be organised or
categorised ... some kind of ranking or rating system ... ensure the best ideas remain ... include all of the vast
amount of research and analysis and ideas you've done thus far into the new store. You must not lose a thing. That
would be a disaster" (Book 14). And: "The reason why we're asking you to do such extensive research is not for the sake
of it, but SPECIFICALLY so that it can go into one of the deliverables" (Book 9).
- *Falls short:* research that sits in a folder and never reaches the report.
- *Meets it:* one store (`final_report/research/INSIGHT_STORE.md`), every idea filed and rated, old and new.
- *Delights:* Caplet can see that the report used the best of six weeks of work, and where each idea went.

### 4.5 Overdrive
"Absolute overdrive" recurs in every late book: "vastly more work per day" (Book 10), "orders of magnitudes more"
(Book 11), "more work over the next 12 hours than throughout the entirety of the project so far" (Book 12), "a vast
amount of time and energy (and tokens!), hint hint", "It ought to push you, drain you, drain your tokens and your
limits" (Book 13), "'deep' work doesn't mean 'just a little bit'" (Book 14). Ray, 27 Oct, asked for 500 to 1,000
pieces of reading. Quantity matters as well as quality: "you seem to be taking that as a sign that you don't need
quantity of output, which is absolutely wrong" (Book 12).
- *Falls short:* a steady, reasonable pace.
- *Meets it:* each day's research is visibly large and lands in the store.
- *Delights:* the volume shows in the report's range without making it longer.

### 4.6 Anticipate; improve without being asked
"You should be able to GUESS what I'm thinking and already have it covered ... Figure out the patterns" (Book 10);
"recursive self-improvement"; "I shouldn't have to send messages every single time I see a problem." Ray, 26 Oct: "take
more initiative, plan your own projects, listen to our insights". These notes are part of the answer: they should let
Keel predict Caplet's reaction to a draft before Caplet sees it.
- *Falls short:* a mistake Caplet has already named appears again.
- *Delights:* Caplet reads a draft and finds the thing they were about to ask for already done.

### 4.7 Make our own calls; question us when we're wrong
"You have autonomy, you have liberty, you can and should make your own choices, you can question what we say" (cover
