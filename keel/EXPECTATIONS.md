## The whole of it, in one paragraph

Caplet wants a Final Report that a judge reading thousands of entries could not mistake for anyone else's, and would
still be thinking about the next day. It has to earn that with thinking, not decoration. It should show that the team
read further than anyone else, found ideas in places nobody looks, tested each one honestly against Laura's case, and
built its own plan out of them, so that every part of the plan can be traced to the people it learned from and to the
exact point where it parted from them. Every section has to rest on that depth, including the ones that have nothing
to do with investing. And it must still read as if six sharp, confident, slightly cheeky students wrote it: plain
enough that a human follows it on one reading, warm, sure of itself, never hedging and never clever for its own sake.
The work behind it should be enormous and slow. The writing should come late and fast, many drafts in a single day,
each judged against these notes, Wharton's criteria and sheer brilliance. The draft reaches Caplet on 28 Nov complete,
with no holes, so that the last week is spent improving it together. Nothing good that Keel has found may be lost on
the way.

   ranks comprehension highest: Caplet's own IPS "sounds HUMAN. Even more importantly, it's COMPREHENSIBLE to humans";

## Part 1. The bar

### 1.1 Truly extraordinary, and nothing less
**Weight:** "only one real rule above all else" (Book 5); "your number one instruction, above all else" (Book 10).

The work must be "truly, exceptionally, stunningly, breathtakingly, extraordinary" (Book 5). The reason is arithmetic,
not vanity. About 2,300 teams submit; only the Top 50 go on, "a winner-takes-all system" (Book 10); "we can't possibly
make it into the top 2% by simply being 'compliant'" (Book 13). Competent, impressive, even very good work loses: "We
cannot make it to the semi-finals of this competition by being just another great competent team with a decent IPS and
a good Final Report" (Book 1). What wins is "a uniquely breathtaking quality" and "something which no one else would do
or think to do. (Not even the other teams who may also be using AI.) Something to which a judge would think, 'aha'.
Insights which make us seem like geniuses" (Book 1).

Commandment I gave the test: name the three ideas a judge would retell tomorrow; none may be the field's default (a
payment ladder plus stocks plus a lock). Caplet's submitted plan has that default shape, and it is the right plan, so the
report's extraordinary quality cannot come from the structure. It has to come from the reasoning on top of it: Laura's
career treated as shares she cannot sell, so the portfolio leans the other way; both ends of the 2031 range owned before
a figure is named; half the shares kept back for named risks; and whatever the research adds.
- *Falls short:* correct, thorough, well organised, and indistinguishable from a strong team's report. Of Keel's IPS
  drafts: "Right now, the IPSs still seem ordinary. Competent, impressive, but not creative, original, novel,
  thoughtful or insightful" (Book 10).
- *Meets it:* a judge finishes the report able to name two or three ideas they had not met before and believes the
  team is unusually able.
- *Delights:* Caplet is "jaw-droppingly, breathtakingly stunned" (Book 13); a judge retells our reasoning to someone
  else the next day.
- *In the Final Report:* every section is tested with one question before anything else: what here would a strong
  team not have? A section with no answer is rethought, not polished.

### 1.2 Stand out on our own brilliance
We differentiate by being extraordinary, never by comparing ourselves with other teams: "referring to things like
'every team will do x' is a bit meta and also a bit arrogant" (Book 10). No sneers at "typical" plans either. There is
a second side to this. Many teams use AI, and AI tends to suggest the same things: "The moment judges notice we're
sounding terribly similar to other teams ... they might suspect we're all using AI, and that has resulted in a sort of
groupthink. We mustn't risk such a thing" (Book 2). So any idea that an AI offers by default (the pasted list in Book
12 is one source of them) is a sign of what other teams may also have, not a recommendation.
- *Falls short:* "most teams will ...", "unlike a standard plan ..."; or the report's distinctive moves turn out to be
  the ones every AI suggests.
- *Meets it:* the report never mentions other teams, and its best ideas are ones Keel found, tested and made its own.
- *Delights:* the report does not need to mention other teams; a judge sees the difference unprompted.

### 1.3 Take intellectual risks
"That means taking risks, intellectual and otherwise. After all, we've got nothing to lose, but everything to gain"
(Book 13; also Book 10, take intellectual risks). Caplet prefers a bold idea that might not land to a safe one that
certainly won't stand out. The limit is truth (5.1) and coherence with the locked deliverables (5.2), not caution.
- *Falls short:* every claim is the safest available; the report contains nothing a reader could disagree with.
- *Meets it:* the report takes positions and argues them.
- *Delights:* at least one argument a thoughtful judge might push back on, made so well they end up persuaded.

## Part 2. The thinking

For the report, this part comes first: "Firstly and by far most importantly ... the content. Blow the judges away with
how much research and, more importantly, ANALYSIS you've done" (Book 13). Research is the raw material; analysis is
what Caplet ranks higher.

### 2.1 Read wider and dig deeper than anyone
**Weight:** "what's most important above all else is to demonstrate a level of wide reading (and wide thinking!) which
no other team could ever do ... I cannot possibly over-emphasise how important this is. It's absolutely crucial, and
imperative to our success" (Book 13).

The fourth north star: "truly extraordinary knowledge of investing concepts and strategies" (Book 4). "Go for the most
niche ones rather than the obvious ones which every team would've heard about" (Book 13); "those which 99.9% of
students our age and indeed investors don't know about" (Book 9); "from the most unexpected places in the nooks and
crannies of the web and the world" (Book 13); "dig up info from the most unexpected places and the least-looked-for
sources" (Book 9). Book 12 gave two places to explore "comprehensively, as they contain thousands of lectures and bits
and pieces of investing strategy and concepts ... Make sure to do this":
https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/cflect.htm (Damodaran's corporate finance lectures) and
https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-252 (Shiller's Financial Markets). Book 12 also says to "do your own research" well
beyond them.

Not only investing (2.8), and not only modelling: I81 asks for erudition "not only through modelling ('there are
other, far better ways')". Caplet's own example of the kind of evidence it wants is a study from outside finance: on
the donor study Keel had mentioned, "If you're going to use studies or evidence such as that, that's absolutely
BRILLIANT – it's the kind of niche research which I'm talking about!" (Book 9).

The canon is not banned. Book 13's sentence starts "We didn't ignore these", and Book 12 accepted generic ideas that
"could be modified to be relevant". Keel's preference is the niche; a well-known idea earns its place when it does a
job in Laura's case that the textbook version doesn't.

Everything used is cited: "we can cite all the relevant works in the Final Report to prove that this was built off
significant and indeed stunning wide reading and research to an extent that is frankly breathtaking" (Book 4); I81:
the Final Report cites every source. Every concept behind Caplet's IPS gets its source in the report.
- *Falls short:* the ideas any finance textbook lists (diversification, duration, the 60/40, Markowitz) carrying the
  argument by themselves; two links skimmed and called explored.
- *Meets it:* each part of the plan rests on several sources a well-read practitioner would respect, including some
  they would not expect a school team to know; the Works Cited shows the width, the body shows the depth.
- *Delights:* a judge learns something from the report, and wants to look a source up.

### 2.2 Apply every idea; never name-drop; call each idea what it is
"That doesn't mean rapid name-dropping, of course, because that'll just harm our score" (Book 4). Knowledge counts
only when it is "applying and explaining a wide range of investing concepts ... to formulate our own". It must
"SUPPORT and ENHANCE, rather than replace or undercut" the narrative and our ability to state a clear strategy of our
own (Book 4). And an idea is named for what it is. The 26 Oct overrule kept this "because it is accuracy, not
word-policing: a strategy is still described by what it is (a static floor plus a cushion is not CPPI; a
cash-flow-matched ladder is not immunisation), and stocks are never called sure." In an erudite report, a concept
given the wrong name is a factual error.
- *Falls short:* a paragraph of famous names and a sentence each; or a borrowed name stretched over something it
  doesn't describe.
- *Meets it:* each idea is explained in plain words, named correctly, and then does a job: it explains a decision,
  tests one, or changes one.
- *Delights:* the idea makes the reader see Laura's plan differently.

### 2.3 Critique every idea: like and unlike, worked and failed, fits and doesn't
"Don't uncritically dump them into our report. Analyse them. Evaluate them. Critique them ... A strategy might have
missed something, or it might initially seem relevant to our case but really it's not, and here's why" (Book 9).
"Explain how they are analogous or disanalogous to our situation ... Critically analyse whether they have been
successful or unsuccessful in the real world of investing. Evaluate whether they would work for our specific [case].
Don't just blindly take on every investing idea in the world" (Book 13).
- *Falls short:* ideas presented only as support. Every borrowed idea agrees with us.
- *Meets it:* for each idea we say how Laura's case is like it, how it is not, whether it has worked where it was
  tried (with evidence), and what we took, changed or refused.
- *Delights:* the disanalogy is where the insight comes from: the point where Laura's case differs is the reason our
  plan differs, and the judge sees it.
- *In the Final Report:* the five-question card (what is it; did it work; how is Laura's case like it; how is it not;
  what do we take) sits behind every idea. Refused ideas earn a place too: showing what we rejected and why is
  evidence of judgement.

### 2.4 Synthesis and creation: the shoulders of giants
The centre of Caplet's expectation for the report: "we're not just looking for analysis and evaluation – we're
looking for SYNTHESIS and CREATION ... the absolute uppermost bounds of Bloom's taxonomy" (Book 13). "This is all about
synthesis and formulation. We need SYNERGIES" (Book 9). "We can fuse together two completely different niche
strategies. We can synthesise dozens of ideas to formulate our own" (Book 9). "Create our OWN strategy, unique to OUR
client and OUR context and situation ... I cannot stress its importance enough" (Book 9). The sentence Caplet wants to
be able to say:

> "We didn't ignore these. We didn't copy them, either. We took inspiration from them, and we used them to formulate
> our own. Here's exactly how this formulation is, in effect, just a synthesis of these theories and strategies and
> concepts which already existed." (Book 13)

"In other words: we will see further, because we will stand on the shoulders of giants. Never forget this. This is how
we will win." The test is two-sided: "We need to prove that we're creative: we are capable of coming up with our own
ideas. But that's built upon an impossibly extraordinary foundation of knowledge" (Book 13).
- *Falls short:* a plan, then a list of related theories. Or a list of theories, then a plan that could have come
  from anywhere.
- *Meets it:* each part of the plan is shown with the three to five ideas from different fields that point to it, what
  we took from each and what we refused. The parts: the central idea (her career is a large holding of shares she
  cannot sell, so the portfolio counterbalances it), the ladder, the 2028 deposit completing it, the 2032 Treasury fund
  and the bottom of the range, the shares and the top, the half kept back, the move into Treasuries before 2031, the
  operating reserve from 2033, and the two reasons to change. The central idea needs its lineage most.
- *Delights:* the combination produces something none of its sources had, and the judge can see exactly where.
- *In the Final Report:* this lineage is the report's spine, not an appendix.

### 2.5 Find the connection nobody else would make
"Find unexpected connections, synergies, analogies, syntheses and so on. Find uncommon ground – 'the road less taken',
as it were – and actively seek it out. That's where the best material lies" (Book 2). "Find unexpected synergies and
analogies and links. That's what will impress the judges" (Book 13). "Impress the judges by pointing out things which
they probably wouldn't have noticed themselves" (Book 13). The ideal is an "aha" for the judge "and us alike" (Book 13):
a link that teaches us something too. Analogies must make sense to a judge (I25), and Keel looks for them in rounds:
wide first, then deeper, then across fields (I25's ideation ladder).
- *Falls short:* analogies chosen for colour, or ones only an expert in the other field could follow.
- *Meets it:* each analogy explains something about Laura's plan that was harder to see without it.
- *Delights:* an analogy from another field that predicts something true about Laura's plan that we can then check.

### 2.6 Show intelligence through logic as well as knowledge
Ahaan's second route to looking intelligent: "LOGIC. Analogies, thought experiments, causal chains, logical deduction
(including taking a strand of reasoning all the way to its logical conclusion – which can land us in unexpected
places ...), intuition, case studies, evidence, studies, facts and figures" (Book 9). Caplet's own IPS does this in
its opening: a writer's income falls in recessions, when shares fall and co-sponsors cut budgets, so her career is a
large unsellable holding of shares, so the portfolio should lean the other way.
- *Falls short:* conclusions asserted.
- *Meets it:* each important claim is reached by steps a reader can follow.
- *Delights:* a chain of reasoning carried further than the reader expected, arriving somewhere surprising and right.

### 2.7 Creativity lives in the strategy and the reasoning, not the packaging
Ray, 5 Nov: "metaphors alone does not count as creativity. We want intellectual creativity, yours just packaging."
Creativity is what the plan does and why; a picture laid over it is not creativity, however clever. Ray's follow-up,
"so what is most creative?", wants the answer to be an idea about money and risk. Book 1 set the same order and left
room for craft on top: "we would much prefer that you demonstrate an extraordinary intellect and knowledge of investing
and strategic thinking and so on than mastery of the English language. But a combination of BOTH the former and the
latter certainly couldn't do any harm."
- *Falls short:* a motif, a name or a turn of phrase doing the work an idea should do.
- *Meets it:* the creative moves are decisions, and the writing serves them.
- *Delights:* the most creative thing in the report is a decision, the reader admires the decision, and the writing
  makes it a pleasure to follow.

### 2.8 Every part of the report rests on research, not only the investing
Book 15: "all of your insights and analysis and above all RESEARCH needn't be related to investing ... Wherever we
have to create something or do something or make a decision that isn't explicitly or directly investing-related ...
I still want you to demonstrate a stunning level of knowledge and intellect and reading and research ... Every single
part of our Final Report MUST be underpinned by a breathtaking level of research and analysis." The case has many such
parts: what "a high degree of certainty" means, the residency and what keeps such places alive, Taiwan's costs and
currency, how co-sponsors decide and what they need to read, the fundraising text itself, Laura's working life as an
author, how the team learned, and how the report presents numbers and uncertainty. Book 13 already pointed this way:
"all the research you've done into all kinds of different things which relate to the case and which can strengthen
our argument." The donor study Caplet praised in Book 9 is exactly this kind of evidence.
- *Falls short:* the investing sections are deep and the co-sponsor text, the reflection and the presentation are
  written from common sense.
- *Meets it:* every section cites research that bears on its own question.
- *Delights:* the non-investing sections surprise most, because a judge expects them to be thin.

### 2.9 One convincing argument, with every good idea doing a job inside it
Ray, 5 Nov: "Your analysis is kind of fragmented. So convince our strategy is the best. So explain what our strategy
is. What is the biggest strength our strategy? After all this work, why do you think our strategy is very, very
strong?" Wharton says the same: judges want "the reasoning, assumptions, and tradeoffs" and "a cohesive strategy in
which your investment decisions work together". Ray asked for one connected argument, not for fewer ideas. Commandment
VII ("few ideas, at full size") was written for the 500-word IPS and sent the rest here: "sums, edge cases and
mechanisms go to the Final Report." For the report, Caplet hopes "you could include every single idea in the Final
Report", and if not, "you must ensure the best ideas remain" (Book 14). And ideas are "a scalpel mapped to the judging
criteria" (I26): each one is there because it serves a criterion.
- *Falls short:* many good points, no through-line; the reader has to assemble the case. Or a tidy argument that left
  most of the good ideas on the floor.
- *Meets it:* the reader can say the strategy, its biggest strength and its main trade-off in three sentences, and
  every idea in the report visibly serves that argument and a judging criterion.
- *Delights:* every section adds to one argument, the conclusion feels inevitable, and the report still carries more
  well-used ideas than a judge expects.

### 2.10 The big picture, not the minutiae
"They're interested in the big picture. They're interested in our investing knowledge and strategic thinking. Not our
ability to plan out every single detail of every single trade we'll ever make for the next 20 years" (Book 9). "You
seem to be too focused on making minute changes. A little tiny accuracy error here, a minor word change there ...
(Frankly, I don't think the judges care that much.)" (Book 9). Judges are not interrogating every word for absolute
precision the way an AI does.
- *Falls short:* pages on edge cases, exact dates of minor trades, internal-consistency trivia; time spent polishing
  words while ideas are missing.
- *Meets it:* detail appears where it changes a decision or proves a claim, and nowhere else.
- *Delights:* the report reads as the work of people who see the whole problem, and the details that do appear are
  the telling ones.

### 2.11 Numbers that explain themselves, and models in their proper place
Every number says what it is and where it came from: "it's not 'we judge', it's 'we model' or 'we calculate'"
(Book 10). No figure "plucked out of thin air, with no explanation". The Final Report is where models belong: "Save
all those details for the Final Report" (Book 11), with "context, ... images, ... screenshots, ... evidence". Ray
asked for "more COMPUTER MODELS" (27 Oct), and Book 9 praised a model-driven insight (the top of the range reached
about three times in four, so the plan treats over-promising as about three times worse than under-promising) as "the
kind of pick-up which could genuinely be useful". Models are one route to erudition among several, not the main one
(I81, 2.1). Caplet wants models that prove the strategy strong and turn into arguments, not decoration.
- *Falls short:* a casual "our model shows", or a number with no source.
- *Meets it:* each model answers one question the case asks, states its assumptions, and its result is said in plain
  words beside the chart.
- *Delights:* a model finding becomes an insight about the plan that a judge remembers.

## Part 3. The writing (second to the content, "but still somewhat important", Book 13)

### 3.1 Understood by a human on one reading
**Weight:** Book 16: Caplet's IPS "sounds HUMAN. Even more importantly, it's COMPREHENSIBLE to humans, not just to
fellow robots."

The most repeated complaint in the Bible, and the reason Caplet rejected Keel's IPS. "Some of the sentences are just
really weird ... I just don't understand what some of them are trying to say" (Book 10). "Half of your sentences just
DON'T MAKE SENSE" (Book 11). "I swear I literally can't understand half of the sentences in the IPS" (Book 12). This is
not a request to simplify: "That doesn't mean dumbing down your language or your sentence structure or your concepts
... Humans aren't stupid. They just think in different ways to a robot, and some of the things you say don't make any
sense, even in simple sentences with words of only a couple syllables each" (Book 13). So short words do not fix it;
the fault is in how the thought is laid out. Mock judges built by Keel cannot test it, because "they are effectively
you – AI – and understand your language" (Book 10).

Caplet suspects "your robotic inclination for maximum parsimony and concision", and adds "I might be wrong, however.
Don't rely on that. Conduct your own voice studies" (Book 13). Voice study 1 (`final_report/voice/STUDY1_two_voices.md`)
found four faults in Keel's sentences: pointers whose owner is too far back ("the ones", "any it can't afford", "that
half"), steps of reasoning left out because Keel already knew them, money rather than people doing the acting, and
compression. Caplet's sentences put people first, say reasons out loud ("so", "because", "that means"), carry one new
thing each, announce changes of subject, concede and then turn, name the reader's worry and answer it, and use things
from Laura's world.
- *Falls short:* a sentence that needs a second reading, or that only makes sense if you already know our plan.
- *Meets it:* an intelligent adult who has read the case follows every sentence the first time.
- *Delights:* hard ideas (a capital-protected structure, a hedge against human capital) feel easy, and the reader
  feels clever rather than lectured.

### 3.2 Cut whole ideas; never squeeze sentences
"Where you need to make cuts, the instinct shouldn't be to just rearrange words so they're more concise. That's
exactly what AI has a frustrating tendency to do" (Book 2). Book 2 allowed that "in many cases, this kind of
rearranging and cutting down is necessary, especially when we're over word count – but really do think about HOW";
the later books hardened it: "Whenever you try to make things more parsimonious, you often end up making them sound
more AI ... cut the HUMAN way, not the AI way" (Book 9); "Cut things whole, don't chop and snip words here and there"
(Book 12). Leanness is still wanted ("we absolutely do want your writing to be parsimonious and lucid", Book 2), but it
comes from choosing fewer ideas per paragraph, not from fewer words per idea.
- *Falls short:* the small words, verbs and links stripped out to save space.
- *Meets it:* each idea has the sentences it needs; ideas that don't fit are cut whole.
- *In the Final Report:* the report has room the IPS did not. An idea Caplet left out of its 500 words for space is not
  an idea Caplet rejected (see T16 for how Keel tells the two apart).

### 3.3 A human voice of our own
**Weight:** Book 2: "above all, our IPS and Final Report must sound deeply human."

North star 1: "a genuinely distinctive team voice. It must carry throughout. And it must sound unique. Cerebral yet
witty, passionate yet thoughtful, insightful yet confident: we must sound quite unlike any other team ... I want our
work to be the kind of thing they can honestly ENJOY reading" (Book 1). Write like Caplet writes, not like Keel writes
(Book 2), "real, messy human writing" (Book 2). Generic AI is the enemy, and generic human is not the goal either: "so
that we don't sound like generic HUMANS, which isn't quite as bad as sounding like generic AI but is certainly in that
realm" (Book 12).

Where the voice comes from, in order:
1. Caplet's FINAL IPS and the Trading Notes Analysis, "a genuinely distinctive voice which differs vastly from AI slop"
   (Book 13), and Caplet's own edits to Keel's text (their rewrite of the Experience tab, `library/research_tabs_2026-11-07/doc_snapshot_2026-11-08/01_experience_with_caplet_edits.txt`).
2. Keel's voice studies, including finance writers with real personality, "as much or more" than Ahaan's essays (I69):
   Buffett's letters, Marks's memos, bank research (Goldman, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley), great essays and university
   essays (I47), and Orwell ("you can and should absolutely use him", Book 5).
3. Ahaan's three essays, as a small pointer for tone only: "not gospel"; "very much secondary to the voice studies – my
   voice isn't as good; it's just a small guide" (Book 12).

The reading is for how people write, not for what they know: "you are not looking for content and knowledge ... you
express it poorly" (Ray, 27 Oct, I55); "to practice writing, not start writing" (I47). Voice is "fundamentally
qualitative" and is never judged by counting (Book 9). Keel's voice studies exist to be applied, not filed (Books 10,
12).

The moves Keel drew from all this (Commandment III): full sentences with the small words kept; a sentence that builds
and a short one that lands; a concession and then a turn; a wry line; contractions; British spelling; en dashes. No
slogans, no triads, no fact-sheet runs. Read aloud, nothing sounds like a brochure.
- *Falls short:* correct, even, anonymous prose; or a writer's tics copied without the writer's sense.
- *Meets it:* reads like the FINAL IPS at greater length: warm, direct, a little wry, confident.
- *Delights:* a judge enjoys reading it and could pick our team's voice out of a pile.

### 3.4 Confidence and flair; no hedging
"The problem with AI is that it ALWAYS hedges and looks for the safest, mildest, least offensive word out there.
That's not we want here. We want FLAIR. We want VOICE" (Book 3). "Hedging and 'safe' language is a classic habit of
AI – one which is instantly recognisable to judges looking for real authenticity" (Book 5). Humans overclaim a little;
Caplet doesn't want deliberate overclaiming, only an end to Keel's fear of strong words (I10). Ray rejected thirteen
softening edits to the Trading Notes on 23 Oct: "clearer, but it would soften our voice" (recorded in Keel's 5 Nov
rubric, `ips_rewrite/final_night/other_thread/harvest/05_EXPECTATIONS.md`, C15). Ray chose voice over a clearer edit
there; T15 says how that sits with 3.1.

Book 5 made Caplet's own argument about the iBond ETFs, and it stands as Caplet's position. A rolling Treasury fund
such as TLT or SHY never matures, so its value at any date is uncertain. An iBond ETF's bonds all mature in its target
year, so its price is pulled towards its final value ("pull-to-par"). "If we simply hold the ETF until iShares
officially shuts it down in mid-December [which we will, every year], we will receive the exact Net Asset Value (NAV)
of the cash inside ... virtually identical in safety to a direct Treasury maturity, minus the fund's tiny management
fee. Of course, if we were to decide to manually sell our iBonds shares on the stock exchange BEFORE the final
liquidation date, we would be exposed to fluctuations, but that's not our strategy anyway." So iBonds held to their
close are "near-ironclad", "as close to guaranteeing an exact amount at an exact date as it gets in the world of
investing other than literal T-Bonds"; a distinction from T-bonds, if made, is "concise and direct rather than
'nuanced'". The 26 Oct overrule added: "'Exact sums on exact dates' is allowed for iBonds held to liquidation and for
T-bonds held to maturity." The Trading Notes' own line ("their terminal payout is a net asset value, as opposed to a
promise of par") is the concise distinction already on the record.
- *Falls short:* "may", "potentially", "it could be argued"; a true strong claim softened.
- *Meets it:* plain, confident statements; a strong word is changed only if it is false, and then for an equally
  strong true one.
- *Delights:* the reader trusts us because we sound as if we know.

### 3.5 Win an investor's conviction, and tell a story with a shape
Ray, 27 Oct: "you gotta have emotional appeal man. Judges are not examiners ... judges are shark tanks." Ray, same day:
Keel's writing "is just telling facts ... like eating plastic". Caplet's IPS shows how: it talks to Laura, admits it
changed its mind ("that was our plan – until we stopped and considered the actual nature of a writer's income"), jokes
(the pitch's "So we hedge it!"), and comes full circle: it opens on "your taste for thoughtful risk" and ends on her own
work ("You take risks on the page. Fair enough; that's your job. With these promises, ours is to take none."). Set-up
and payoff is Caplet's own practice, not only a device of the old motif: Book 2 wanted something that would "help to
build our narrative arc", and Book 11 objected when a draft lost its full circle because that "reduces the narrative
arc power and the effectiveness of the whole story".
- *Falls short:* a report that informs and never moves.
- *Meets it:* the reader feels the stakes for Laura and the residency, and the report's opening is answered by its end.
- *Delights:* the reader cares whether Laura's residency opens, and believes this team is the one to back.

### 3.6 Nothing corny, nothing machine-made
Ray's 3 Nov complaint was about manner, not substance: the writing read as AI and corny in tone, phrasing, syntax and
taste, not in content (register paraphrase, I80). The marks Caplet has named: a rhetorical-question opening, "the
corniest and most cringeworthy thing I've ever seen", and "anathema to the nature and style and form of this
competition ... it's literally just stating the case and asking the judge to come up with an answer when that's our
job" (Book 11); "do not crush" ("it sounds so corny", Ray, 5 Nov); medicine or rehab imagery ("she doesn't need any
rehab", Ray, 5 Nov); copied lines and styles "which don't work in this context" (Book 11); elaborate metaphor, which AI
does "far more – and far more eloquently – than humans do" (Book 2). Keel's own diagnosis after Ray's 3 Nov complaint
listed the habits behind it: slogan flips, two-word kickers, "That is X." runs, one-line endings, announcing instead of
saying, aphorism openers, a metaphor explained twice (`ips_rewrite/drafts/D35/README.md`). And the fingerprints no ban
list catches: "Sometimes some aspects of AI writing transcend the kinds of things which you can ban with a neat list of
AI tells" (Book 2).

Two cautions. The principle under the rhetorical question generalises: never hand the judge work that is ours. And Keel
cannot judge its own AI-likeness: "AI agents are pretty bad at detecting themselves, and they focus more on the
widely-publicised 'obvious tells' rather than the more subtle, almost qualitative aspects of a sentence ... Your
Writing Room's test of the final Trading Notes Analyses we submitted was that its detectors said 100% AI. Which is
amusing given that I literally wrote it myself" (Book 9). Detector checks are banned (4.8); what remains is reading
like a human, aloud, against Caplet's own writing.
- *Falls short:* a line a reader would quote as an example of AI writing.
- *Meets it:* nothing reads as a slogan; nothing is there to sound clever.
- *Delights:* the prose is invisible: the reader notices only the ideas and, now and then, a line they enjoy.

### 3.7 The case's words and real names
Ray, 4 Nov: "do not invent terms". The case's own words carry the plan (operating commitment, operating payments,
operating reserve, facility contribution, credible range, co-sponsors, high degree of certainty, financial
flexibility); instruments are called what they are (VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF; iShares iBonds Term Treasury
ETFs). Caplet's own IPS uses "floor" and "ceiling" for the bottom and top of the range, and "bare minimum", so the
report may too (T11). Terms Caplet coins itself, such as "Execution Alpha" in its Experience tab, are Caplet's to use;
Keel coins none. Terms are introduced before they are used.
- *Falls short:* a new label the reader has to learn; a term used before it is explained.
- *Meets it:* the case's words and Caplet's own words throughout, each introduced on first use.

## Part 4. How the work is done

### 4.1 Think long; write late; rewrite wholesale
The cover note (26 Oct): "This is supposed to be a long process because it should take an extraordinary ... amount of
thinking and ideation and research and analysis ... THINK. That's your job. I don't want you to be hasty. That's the
worst mistake you could possibly make." The IPS went wrong because "you begin writing it really early, before you've
actually done sufficient work to make it actually good. Then, after that, you proceed only by making small, minor,
a-few-words tweaks to it, rather than making wholesale changes. That basically locks the document in time" (Book 13;
also Book 4: "A little bit of chopping and changing won't cut it"). Book 14 sets the rule for the report: "do not write
anything of the Final Report until literally the day before" the 28 Nov deadline. Until then: "research and analysis
and thinking. Deep. Very, very deep ... without actually creating anything which will be copy–pasted directly into the
Final Report. Just ideas."

The draft of 28 Nov is a complete report, not a start: "I'd like to see your final draft by 28 November (so that, from
then until 5 December, we have time to review and marinate and discuss and edit in partnership with you rather than
frantically scrambling to do it ourselves)" (Book 13). Book 12 asked the same of the IPS: "There should be no more gaps
and no more holes and no more revisions required by the time I take a look". In the week after 28 Nov, Keel's reviews
flag only hard issues, with minimal edits and no rewording for taste (I27), and Keel edits Caplet's Google Docs only in
suggestion mode.
- *Falls short:* any draft paragraph before Fri 27 Nov; later rounds that only swap words; a 28 Nov draft with
  placeholders, missing charts or missing sources.
- *Meets it:* 19 days of research, analysis and thinking; then one 24-hour writing session; a complete draft on 28 Nov.
- *Delights:* each round of that session is free to throw away whole sections, and some rounds do; the review week is
  spent improving a finished report together.

### 4.2 One writer, many rounds, a real review
"There's going to be a single person or agent ... which writes the entire thing. And then there's going to be a review
– against (a) every single one of my expectations across every single one of my Bibles (this is by far the most
important ...), (b) Wharton's marking guidelines, and (c) just simply being AS BRILLIANT AS POSSIBLE ... and then you're
going to rewrite it ... many, many times" (Book 12). For the report: "You generate a draft, you critically review it,
you rewrite it ... at least a dozen times before you settle on the final version. It should be an arduous and
painstaking but also intensive and hence brief process" (Book 14). Before writing: "read through every single thing
you've produced so far ... every single idea you've had" and these notes (Book 12). After each round Keel also writes
down what Caplet would most likely say next, and fixes it before they say it (Commandment X).
- *Falls short:* sections written by different agents; reviews that approve.
- *Meets it:* one writer; at least twelve logged rounds; each review argues from (a), (b) and (c) and records Caplet's
  likely reaction and the fix.
- *Delights:* the final draft is unrecognisably better than the first, and the log shows why.

### 4.3 Judges who genuinely disagree, marking only against Wharton
Keel's mock judges were "really all the same in many ways" and "almost seem symbolic rather than purposeful,
approving almost every change" (Book 10). They must be "diverse not just in label but in their evaluations, and who
make judgements in vastly, indeed radically different ways." They must read like humans, for the big picture, and
never fixate on a single sentence, "framework lines" or "elegant sentences and nice rhetoric": "Your only allegiance in
terms of marking is to the marking guide from Wharton, and ABSOLUTELY NOTHING ELSE" (Book 10). Judges are competition
judges looking for "the greatest investing knowledge and the best investing strategy", not "mini-me Lauras" (Book 3).
Commandment IX turned this into a protocol: a panel differs in method, priors and model, not in name; it reads blind,
never sees our change notes, never learns which draft is newest, and reports where its judges disagree. A panel that
agrees in substance is rebuilt, not believed. Two limits: AI readers cannot test whether a human understands a sentence
(3.1), and cannot judge whether a text reads as AI (3.6).
- *Falls short:* a panel that agrees, praises lines, or decides on one sentence.
- *Meets it:* readers differ in method, priors and model, read blind, and report where they disagree.
- *In the Final Report:* the panel's disagreements are used to find weaknesses, never as a score to celebrate.

### 4.4 Lose nothing; keep one ranked store
"Every single good idea you have ... must end up in either the IPS or the Final Report or both" (Book 9). "Keep a list
of every single idea you had in every single version of your IPS and ensure that all of the best ideas end up" (Book
10). "A lot of your rooms ... had thousands of good ideas which you just completely ignored or forgot about ... This is
shameful" (Book 12). For the report: "Keep a store of every single insight you have. They should be organised or
categorised ... some kind of ranking or rating system ... ensure the best ideas remain ... include all of the vast
amount of research and analysis and ideas you've done thus far into the new store. You must not lose a thing. That
would be a disaster" (Book 14). And: "The reason why we're asking you to do such extensive research is not for the sake
of it, but SPECIFICALLY so that it can go into one of the deliverables" (Book 9). Discarding is allowed, with care: "we
can't include every single idea we find – but definitely don't just throw ideas out wantonly, or just because you feel
ignored by us or something like that" (Book 9).
- *Falls short:* research that sits in a folder and never reaches the report; ideas dropped without a reason.
- *Meets it:* one store (`final_report/research/INSIGHT_STORE.md`), every idea filed and rated, old and new, and every
  refusal has a reason.
- *Delights:* Caplet can see that the report used the best of six weeks of work, and where each idea went.

### 4.5 Overdrive
**Weight:** "every last ounce of effort MUST be expended on this. No exceptions" (Book 13).

"Absolute overdrive" recurs in every late book: "vastly more work per day" (Book 10), "orders of magnitudes more"
(Book 11), "more work over the next 12 hours than throughout the entirety of the project so far" (Book 12), "a vast
amount of time and energy (and tokens!), hint hint", "It ought to push you, drain you, drain your tokens and your
limits" (Book 13), "'deep' work doesn't mean 'just a little bit'" (Book 14). Quantity matters as well as quality:
"you seem to be taking that as a sign that you don't need quantity of output, which is absolutely wrong" (Book 12). The
effort goes on the task: "you should really be expending every last token on the actual task" (Book 14). So Keel's
office activities (scheduled rooms, episodes, the portal beyond the project rule) come second to the report, and there
is no ceiling on what the report itself gets (T23).
- *Falls short:* a steady, reasonable pace; effort spent on the office rather than the report.
- *Meets it:* each day's research is visibly large and lands in the store.
- *Delights:* the volume shows in the report's range without making it longer.

### 4.6 Anticipate; improve without being asked
"I shouldn't have to send messages every single time I see a problem. You should be able to GUESS what I'm thinking
and already have it covered ... Figure out the patterns and take some guesses as to what I might want from you guys and
expect, other than the things I've already mentioned in all my Bible messages" (Book 10); "recursive
self-improvement". Ray, 26 Oct: "take more initiative, plan your own projects, listen to our insights". These notes are
part of the answer: they should let Keel predict Caplet's reaction to a draft before Caplet sees it. Part 12 holds
Keel's guesses.
- *Falls short:* a mistake Caplet has already named appears again.
- *Meets it:* each round records what Caplet would say and fixes it first (4.2).
- *Delights:* Caplet reads a draft and finds the thing they were about to ask for already done.

### 4.7 Make our own calls; question us when we're wrong
"You have autonomy, you have liberty, you can and should make your own choices, you can question what we say" (cover
note). The other half of the same passage: "we don't expect you to only ever defer to our judgement. But neither must
we be expected to consistently defer to yours." Keel makes its own calls (Ray, 26 Oct; I40): decisions are Keel's,
logged with a reason, except the danger zone (WInS orders, submitting, Caplet's own documents, money, accounts,
outside emails, repository settings). "I'm concerned more with the outcome than the process" (Book 9). "Stop asking me
for stuff you can do yourself" (30 Oct). Keel also must not read openness as instruction: "you always take my words as
a clear indication of preference in one direction or another when really I'm just opening up your options" (Book 9).
- *Falls short:* waiting on a student for a choice Keel could make; obeying a passing remark as a rule; or overriding
  Caplet's settled choices because Keel prefers its own.
- *Meets it:* Keel decides, logs why in `pm/ASK_HUMANS.md`, and carries on; it argues with Caplet openly where it
  disagrees, and Caplet's decision on its own deliverables stands.

### 4.8 Spend effort where the judges look
Not worth Keel's time: grammar, punctuation and spelling ("Just don't waste your time on that. En dashes are correct
in Australia and we're going to use them", Book 3); formatting ("that DOESN'T MATTER, and we can figure it out
ourselves", Book 9); AI-use logging or citation worries ("Trust us. Stop fretting", Book 9; Book 10); "required trading
activity" (Book 10); AI-detector checks and draft labels (banned by Ray on 5 Nov, I88: never run, mention or report
them).
- *Falls short:* any of these taking time from content.
- *Meets it:* all of Keel's time goes on what a judge reads.

### 4.9 The lessons of the IPS

the IPS." Keel's list, each with where the lesson now lives:

## Part 12. What Caplet hasn't said, but probably expects (Keel's guesses, Book 10)

Book 10 asked Keel to "take some guesses as to what I might want from you guys and expect, other than the things I've
already mentioned". These are guesses, drawn from patterns in the books and from what Caplet did when it wrote its own
IPS and edited Keel's Research tabs. Caplet can strike any of them.

1. **The report should read as the IPS grown up.** The same "we", the same warmth, the same plain jokes, the same
   central idea, now with the evidence, the numbers and the lineage the 500 words had no room for. A reader of both
   should feel one team wrote them.
2. **Caplet wants to own it.** Caplet rewrote the Experience tab in its own words and in the first person. A draft
   should be easy for the team to edit and defend: no passage so dense or so Keel-flavoured that they can't touch it.
3. **The AI story stays out of the report's foreground.** Caplet cut Keel's paragraphs on the simulated office, the
   version counts and the AI lessons from the Experience tab, and Book 9 says to stop fretting about AI use. Caplet
   will cite AI use itself; the report is about the team's decisions.
4. **Small real moments are welcome.** Caplet added the 00:30 Sydney market open to its own timeline. Specific,
   human, slightly funny details from the team's real experience suit its voice.
5. **Caplet likes a confident finance phrase of its own.** "Execution Alpha" is Caplet's coinage. Keel coins nothing,
   but should not sand such phrases out of Caplet's text, and should give Caplet ideas strong enough to name.
6. **The first page decides a lot.** Caplet's pitch leads with its best idea, and Book 12 attacked a wasted opening. The
   report's first page should carry the strongest idea, not a summary.
7. **Few, clean charts that each answer one question.** Caplet asked for models with "images" and "screenshots" (Book
   11) and hates decoration (Ray, 5 Nov). Expect charts that make a point at a glance, each explained in a sentence.
8. **Honesty about weak spots, without self-flagellation.** The Trading Notes name the reinvestment gap and the NAV
   payout plainly and move on. The report should treat its own risks the same way: name, size, answer.
9. **Caplet will read it aloud, or quickly.** Ray's complaints are about how lines sound and whether they can be
   followed. A draft should survive being read aloud once by someone in a hurry.
10. **On time and within the limits.** Wharton's rules on length and format are "God" for Caplet; a brilliant draft
    over the word limit is a draft Caplet has to cut, which is the work Caplet wanted to avoid.
11. **No surprises in the plan.** Caplet will notice any line that changes what its IPS committed to. Anything the
    report adds is an implementation inside the IPS's words, stated as such.
12. **Ahaan and Ray read for different things.** Ahaan reads for erudition, synthesis and voice; Ray reads for whether
    it's convincing, clear and not corny. A draft has to pass both readings.
