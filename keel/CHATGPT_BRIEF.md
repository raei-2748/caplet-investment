## 3. What the IPS must do (Wharton's updated IPS instructions, verbatim where quoted)

**Two fields in an online form (SurveyMonkey Apply).** Plain text only. No title page, charts, images, links,
footnotes or citations ("sources do not need to be cited in the IPS"; they go in the Final Report).

- **Pitch, max 50 words:** "Quickly summarize your team's investment strategy. Clearly communicate the central idea
  behind your approach and how it is designed to support your client's financial goals and future funding needs."
- **IPS, max 500 words:** "Clearly explain your team's investment strategy and the reasoning behind it ... Explain how
  your team will approach risk and return, liquidity, portfolio construction and diversification, and planned changes
  in the portfolio as future funding needs approach." It should "establish your strategic approach to preparing for
  the operating commitment, managing the operating reserve's asset composition over time, determining a responsible
  facility contribution, and preserving appropriate financial flexibility."

**The questions it should answer:**
- What is the central idea behind your investment strategy?
- What principles will guide your investment decisions?
- How does your strategy balance growth, risk, liquidity, funding reliability, and financial flexibility?
- How will the portfolio's asset allocation and composition change as future funding needs approach and payments are
  made?
- How will your strategy protect the operating commitment while preserving flexibility for a responsible facility
  contribution?

"Focus on the strategy and decision-making framework that guide your portfolio rather than describing individual
investments or presenting detailed financial calculations." "You do not need to address these points separately.
Your goal is to present a clear, cohesive, and well-supported investment strategy."

**Not expected in the IPS** (Final Report material): "final operating-reserve calculations, detailed portfolio
projections, a final facility-contribution range, or the draft communication to co-sponsors."

## 5. The client: Laura Gao (official case)

Laura is a Wharton graduate (2018, Statistics & Information Decisions Management), former tech product manager, and

**Money in.** $300,000 at the start of 2027. $150,000 more at the start of 2028, "using earnings from publishing

Her living costs are covered outside the portfolio. All flows happen at the start of the year (2026 = Year 0).

**The goal: a creative residency in Taiwan from 2033**, "a small, community-oriented space where artists, writers,
designers, entrepreneurs, and educators can temporarily live, work, teach, and collaborate."

1. **Operating commitment (the promise).** Ten fixed, nominal $50,000 payments, one at the start of each year

   of certainty." No outside money may be relied on. At the start of 2033 she sets aside an **operating reserve** for
   them; teams recommend its size, initial composition and how it changes, and must **define "a high degree of
   funding certainty"**, explain how they evaluated it, and state their assumptions.
2. **Facility contribution (the hope).** After setting the reserve aside, "how much of the remaining portfolio she can
   responsibly contribute" to building the facility. "Committing all remaining assets could limit her financial
   flexibility as the project develops." No set amount; teams recommend and justify it. No contingency fund or
   endowment sizing is expected.
3. **Co-sponsors in 2031.** Two years ahead she approaches co-sponsors and must say how much she expects to give in
   2033. "A meaningful personal contribution may signal that the residency is financially viable ... and make

   she could damage her credibility and lose the confidence or participation of co-sponsors." So she wants "a credible
   range", not one number. Teams recommend the **dollar range and their confidence** that the 2033 gift falls inside
   it, explain how good and bad markets move it, and the range "must also protect the portfolio's ability to fund the
   ten-year operating commitment". The Final Report also drafts part of her fundraising text for co-sponsors.

Teams must state assumptions about investment returns, cash-flow timing, outside funding, inflation's effect on the
projections **and on facility costs**, and the flexibility she should keep. Projections start from $300,000 in 2027
plus $150,000 in 2028 with "reasonable return assumptions"; WInS results are never added.

## 7. Our strategy: Caplet XR

**The name.** "XR" is the pharmacy label for an **extended-release** capsule: one dose, released on a schedule, which
only works if swallowed whole ("Do not crush"). Our spin: **X = 10** (ten payments) and **R = redemption** (each bond or
iBond redeems before its January cheque): ten redemptions, one a year. "Caplet" is our team name and also a kind of
pill, which is why the name fits only us. R must never be "release" ("ten releases" sounds odd). The motif is used
**subtly**: a handful of lines where it explains a real mechanism, never in every sentence.

**The central idea: don't invest the promise; buy it.** Keep the promise (ten cheques) and the hope (the facility
gift) apart, and pay for each with different money.

1. **January 2027: buy the promise.** Laura's first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasuries and iBond Treasury ETFs, one
   rung maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. Held to maturity, each rung turns into a known sum on a known
   date; no cheque depends on a return forecast. On the latest curve we have (22 Oct) the ladder costs about $298,900
   (MODEL), so $300,000 buys all ten cheques "or nearly".
2. **"High degree of certainty", defined as a list of ways to fail, not an adjective.** (a) The US government defaults.
   (b) A recession arrives before we buy: yields fall, the ladder costs more, *and* the same recession shrinks the
   advances, speaking and licensing income behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap (Laura's career is
   already a bet on the economy, so her deposit never funds the cheques). (c) One leak remains: the coupon and
   early-maturing cash waiting for later cheques earns less if yields fall (reinvestment risk). In 2031 we price that
   shortfall and set it aside from the money she keeps, **before** she names any gift.
3. **January 2028: build the floor.** First, any gap in the ladder is topped up. Most of the rest of the 2028 deposit
   buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, sized to grow back to about the whole deposit: **her floor** (about
   $150,000 in 2033). What is left over, roughly the interest those bonds should earn (about $30,000), buys **one world
   index fund** (VT). This is how a bank builds a capital-protected note, except we swap the bank's option (and its
   fee) for shares, which can't expire worthless.
4. **2031: the range for co-sponsors.** **Bottom = the floor** she has owned since 2028 (we quote a median bottom of
   about $145,000 because the iBond payout is a NAV, not a fixed par). **Top = floor + half her stocks.** The other
   half stays Laura's, liquid any day: her financial flexibility, for example if a building priced in Taiwan dollars
   comes in dearer.
5. **The lock.** A capped range has a catch: once named, every fall in stocks comes off the gift while every rise past
   the top goes to Laura. So on the day she names the range, the half she promised moves into Treasuries. In our
   model the gift then reaches its top about **97 times in 100** (MODEL).
6. **2033 onwards: the reserve runs itself down.** The ten holdings *are* the operating reserve; each turns to cash
   before its cheque. No selling, no rebalancing, no forecasts. If anything goes wrong, the order is fixed: **the
   cheques first, then the floor, then the hope.**

**Why not more stocks? (our confession and the strongest rival).** Our own first plan (27 Sep) was about
three-quarters stocks; our model sank it (about 3% of paths missed a cheque, and about 40% if the 2028 deposit
never came). The strongest rival keeps our ladder but puts the 2028 deposit mostly in stocks and buys its floor in
January 2031 with whatever the stocks fetch. Matched to name the same $145,000 median bottom, it usually wins: about
$15,000 more for Laura on average by 2042 (MODEL). But in the twentieth of modelled paths where 2028-30 is worst for
stocks, it can name only about $90,000 to co-sponsors against our $140,000 (MODEL). Our line: "We'd pay the $15,000."

**Principles in one breath.** Owed beats hoped-for whenever there is a date. "Safe" belongs to a date, not an asset
(a Treasury is safe for the January it matures before). Inflation can make $50,000 buy less but cannot make $50,000
cost more, so nominal Treasuries match a nominal promise exactly. A promise you might break is worth less than a
smaller one you won't. Complexity must earn its place (last year's team lesson: over-packaged strategy, and trades
outside the rules).

## 10. What the team expects (our standing rules, in our own words where quoted)

**Priority order:** Wharton's documents first ("in some senses 'God'"). Then the team's "Bible" of instructions, all
equal. Then everything else.

**The four north stars (all equal):**
1. **A genuinely distinctive team voice**, throughout: "Cerebral yet witty, passionate yet thoughtful, insightful yet

2. **One unifying motif** (Caplet XR), but "this isn't a literature competition; it's an investment competition":

   mechanism. Nothing roots- or tree-themed (other AIs keep reaching for Laura's *Messy Roots*; it reads as AI
   groupthink).
3. **Be extraordinary.** "We cannot make it to the semi-finals of this competition by being just another great
   competent team ... Something to which a judge would think, 'aha'." Ray, 30 Oct: the drafts still seem "Competent,
   impressive, but not creative, original, novel, thoughtful or insightful, let alone EXTRAORDINARY."
4. **Extraordinary investing knowledge.** Apply and explain a wide range of investing concepts, niche ones included,
   fused into our own strategy for this case. Never name-dropping. The IPS uses ideas uncited; the Final Report cites
   everything.

**The rules that come from those (the team's "Ten Commandments", condensed):**
- **Stand out on our own brilliance.** Never "every team will ...", never put other teams down.
- **Judge only against Wharton's five criteria**, the way a human reads: big picture first. No single "framework
  line" or pet phrase makes or breaks a draft.
- **Write like a human, specifically like us** (section 11). No slogans, no triads, no fact-sheet runs.
- **Clear on one reading.** A smart adult who knows the case understands every sentence the first time. No term
  (ladder, floor, rung) before it is explained.
- **Cut the human way.** When over the limit, drop a whole idea; never squeeze sentences into code by deleting small
  words, stacking nouns or adding colons. Ahaan: "Whenever you try to make things more parsimonious, you often end up
  making them sound more AI ... cut the HUMAN way, not the AI way." The 50-word pitch is where this goes wrong most.
- **Every number says where it came from**: "we modelled", "we calculate", "the case says". Never "we judge" for a
  model output; no bare multipliers ("three times worse") plucked from nowhere.
- **Few ideas, at full size.** After one read a judge can say the strategy in three sentences; mechanisms and sums go
  to the Final Report.
- **Lose no good idea; critique every borrowed one.** Every good idea lands in the IPS or the Final Report. Each
  concept is explained plainly, fused into our plan and critiqued (what it misses, what it costs us). Ahaan: "We need
  SYNERGIES ... search far and wide ... And don't uncritically dump them into our report. Analyse them. Evaluate them.
  Critique them."
- **Logic is intelligence too:** analogies, thought experiments, causal chains, deduction taken to its conclusion,
  case studies, evidence.
- **No hedging.** "Hedging and 'safe' language is a classic habit of AI." Humans overclaim a little; don't actively
  overclaim, but don't soften true claims. iBonds held to their December liquidation are "near-ironclad"; "exact sums
  on exact dates" is fine. If iBonds are distinguished from T-bonds, do it in one direct sentence.
- **Judges are humans, and are not mini-Lauras.** They are competition judges (think Shark Tank investors, not
  examiners) looking for the best investing knowledge and strategy. Don't grade drafts by "would Laura like it".
- **Real facts and real team events only.** No invented teamwork stories or experiences (Wharton ethics).
- **Don't spend time on** grammar, punctuation, spelling, formatting, AI-use citation, or what "required trading
  activity" means. En dashes and British spelling are correct for us.
- **Detector check:** before the team sees a draft it should score above 50% "human-written only" on GPTZero; but
  having our own voice matters more than the score.

## 11. Our voice (how to sound like us)

**The base is Ahaan's writing**, for *tone*, not as a template (his essays were written for a different contest).
He shared three essays as "my personal voice": *"A clockwork grey – determinism on trial"*, *"Laissez-faire, or
laissez-unfair?"* and *"One small step for man…"*. What they do:
- **Punny section headings** ("Where there's no will, there's no way").
- **A long sentence that builds clause on clause, then a run of blunt short ones** that land like a door closing
  (he voices the opposing view in three curt sentences of its own before answering it).
- **Real concession, then "But".** He states the opposing view fairly, even sympathetically, before turning on it.
- **Homely images inside formal sentences** (retribution as "revenge with a wig on"); one odd, specific detail as
  proof a person wrote it.
- Concrete stats said flat; rhetorical turns; a wry closing kicker; spaced en dashes; British spelling; contractions;
  the small words kept.

**Equally a source: Wall Street writers with personality** (Howard Marks's memos, Buffett's letters, Cliff Asness,
Matt Levine, Michael Cembalest, Aswath Damodaran). From them: every piece is an argument about one idea, with an
opponent, ending on what we will do (Marks); explain the mechanism like a toy, whose money goes where, before any
jargon (Levine); doubt the forecast once, then state the plan flat, rather than a little doubt on every line.

**Who is speaking:** "a sharp seventeen-year-old who argues like a debater and has read too much Howard Marks". Sure of
a few things and says so; honest about one weakness and says that too; funny about once a page, never at Laura's
expense.

**What lands with a reader:** a hook in the first line that someone wants to argue with; stakes you can feel (a
residency where artists and educators live and teach, and Laura's word to the people she asked to join her); one
real, dated confession (our three-quarters-stocks first plan; a plan built on STRIPS that the rules killed on 6 Oct);
one cost accepted in public ("We'd pay the $15,000"); each payment as a cheque with a name on it, not a "liability";
at most about three quotable kickers in 500 words; end on a decision, not a summary.

**What reads as AI to us:** slogans and mirrored "X, not Y" pairs with no argument behind them; triads; colons as
connectors; noun stacks ("growth-protected certainty framework"); every sentence the same middling length; hedging
on every line; over-compressed sentences that only another AI could decode; elegant metaphor in every paragraph.
