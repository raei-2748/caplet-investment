Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them.

Caplet XR in one rule: each promise is made safe no later than the day it becomes one.

The cheques are promises from page one: $50,000 every January, 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars. So her first $300,000 buys them, or nearly, in January 2027: Treasury bonds and funds that turn to cash before each January. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush. To us, X is ten and R is redemption: ten holdings, each redeemed in time for one cheque.

Our first plan, three-quarters stocks, missed cheques in the model. A fixed-percentage withdrawal can ride out a crash; a fixed-dollar withdrawal is a forced sale at the bottom. We spread the stocks across the world, but not the cheques: Ten independent bets, each 99% safe, would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one.

And one reason only Laura has: her 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – income that can shrink in the same slump that makes bonds dearer. So money she has buys certainty; money she expects insures it, then carries risk.

That deposit fills any gap in the ladder, then buys the floor – more of the first cheque's fund – sized to regrow what's left by 2033 and backing the cheques until she names a range. The floor's expected interest, about one dollar in fifteen, buys a world index fund. Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque is worse off – only the gift.

In 2031 she names a range. Its bottom is the floor she owns, rounded down; the top adds half the stocks, and the other half stays hers.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once named, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into the first cheque's fund.

Our one soft spot is coupons reinvested as rates fall, so it is settled in 2031 too: the gap today's yields imply, plus a cushion, moves from spare cash and her half into the first cheque's fund, and anything they can't cover comes off the quoted bottom. In 2033 the ten holdings become the operating reserve, topped up once; after that it only shrinks.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less; it cannot make $50,000 cost more. It and the Taiwan dollar can hurt the building – that is what the rest of Laura's half, sellable any day, is for. Every rule here fires in a year the case already set, never on a hunch; if needs change, the gift takes it before it is named, her half after, a cheque last.

No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush. The residency gets ten Januaries it can count on, and a building promised only from Treasuries she owns.
