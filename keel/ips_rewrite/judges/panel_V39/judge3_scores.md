# Judge J3 scores

Judge: Priya (pension actuary, asset-liability matching). Method: I traced each entry's cash flows ($300,000 in 2027, $150,000 in 2028, the 2031 range, the 2033 set-aside and gift, ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042) and looked for the place where it leaks or the reasoning is hand-waved.

The hole I look for first: ten $50,000 payments worth about $292,000 cannot be bought with coupon Treasuries without coupons arriving from 2027 to 2032, years before the first payment. Individual STRIPS are banned, so that early cash has to be reinvested at whatever yields turn out to be. A ladder that skips this is not "rate-proof", however it is described.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: the only entries that find and price the ladder's reinvestment leak, and that link a shortfall in the 2028 deposit to falling yields; this one states its return assumption and the clearest failure conditions.
2. Entry 2: same strategy and the same rare insight as Entry 3; it states no return assumption and words the failure conditions less clearly.
3. Entry 6: the most rigorous rule for the growth money (CPPI, gap risk sized, cash-lock accepted, explicit odds), but it claims the payments depend on no market price, which misses the reinvestment leak.
4. Entry 4: sound structure with a shortfall waterfall and a floor rounded down for expected shortfall, so it half-sees the leak; no return assumptions and no odds for the top of the range.
5. Entry 1: clean and conventional; it holds early coupons in short Treasuries and then claims rate swings never touch the payments, which is the unpriced hole.
6. Entry 5: the payments are handled, but the gift is staged past 2033 against the case's framing, the gates are made conditional on co-sponsors, and the tech tilt adds risk that moves with Laura's own income.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The architecture is right: a matched Treasury ladder first, with the 2028 deposit filling any gap, then growth on top. It moves the floor of the 2031 range into Treasuries before Laura speaks, and the gift rule (floor plus at most half of the growth, capped at the announced top) leaves her real flexibility. It also states assumptions for returns and inflation, which several entries do not. What holds it back is the liability side. The team sees that coupons arrive early and parks them in short Treasuries, but then claims rate moves can never touch the payments. Those two statements contradict each other, and the shortfall that follows was never sized. The stress test is named but no result is given, and no confidence is attached to the range or to the definition of certainty beyond "only default". The client section is fairly generic: it says little about her 2028 income risk or about Taiwan costs beyond "stocks defend".
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: how a ladder "sized so principal plus coupons covers every payment" deals with the six years of coupons before 2033, since parking them in short Treasuries is itself a bet on rates.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the kind of liability thinking I rarely see from students. They treat the commitment as ten one-year liabilities, not one ten-year pot, and define certainty by its failure conditions (default, or a big rate fall together with a short 2028 deposit). They name the coupon-reinvestment leak and set money aside for it in 2031, before the range is named. They also see that Laura's 2028 income is likely to shrink in the same recessions that push bond prices up and widen the ladder gap. That is a correlation most professionals miss. The gift side is a bond floor plus stocks bought with the interest, and the promised half is locked into Treasuries when the range is named, so the range carries a stated hit rate. It shows learning, and the team rejected its first plan on evidence. What holds it back: no return or inflation assumption is stated, and diversification is one index fund. Flexibility is thin. Because the floor absorbs the whole 2028 remainder, Laura keeps only half of a small stock sleeve, and I would want the team to defend that against the case's warning about committing everything.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: the threshold of about $125,000 for the 2028 deposit after a two-point fall in yields. My rough check puts the ladder gap after such a fall nearer $70,000-$90,000 including the leak, so I could not reconcile it and am unsure what was modelled.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strategy and insight are the same as Entry 2's, with the analysis tightened in the places I care about. The failure conditions for a payment are set out as two separate events. The return assumption behind the comparison with a balanced portfolio is stated. The half Laura keeps is described as liquid for a costlier building, which speaks to flexibility and liquidity. The cash flows trace cleanly. 2027 buys the ladder (bonds and target-maturity Treasury funds, both permitted). 2028 fills the gap first, then buys a floor that grows back to the remainder by 2032, with the interest in world stocks. In 2031 the leak is priced, the range is named and the promised half is locked. In 2033 the ladder becomes a reserve that runs itself down, with a stated order of priority if anything changes. I found no unpriced hole in the payments. What holds it back is the same as Entry 2: flexibility is modest, there is no inflation assumption or Taiwan-dollar view, the equity side is a single fund, and the growth is small enough that an entrepreneur open to "thoughtful risks" might find the gift disappointing. The team does argue that she has already taken her big risk.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: as in Entry 2, the deposit threshold of about $125,000 after a two-point fall looks too high on my rough numbers. I could not tell whether it includes the leak, the floor, or something else.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure is close to Entries 2 and 3: ladder, then a 2032 Treasury floor out of the 2028 remainder, with the rest in a global fund. Buying the longest payments first, because they carry the most rate risk, is a good actuarial instinct. So are the order for meeting any 2033 shortfall and the floor quoted after an expected shortfall, so the team has noticed that the ladder can come up short. Its client reading is the best of the six on risk capacity versus willingness, and it names inflation and the Taiwan dollar as threats to the facility cost. What holds it back is that the quantitative side is mostly absent. No return assumptions are stated and there is no stress result. The shortfall it allows for is never traced to its cause: coupons reinvested in the holding that paid them are exposed to rates. The range runs from the floor to the floor plus half the equity at its 2031 value, with no odds attached to the top. The design guarantees the gift lands in the range, but that makes the range wide and less useful to co-sponsors.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: what the "expected shortfall" in 2033 comes from. The entry plans for it but never says what causes it.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The payment ladder is standard and adequate, apart from the same unpriced coupon reinvestment. The idea of treating the gift as staged real options is original. On my trace, though, it breaks the case in two places. The case asks for a 2033 contribution and a range co-sponsors can rely on in 2031, yet Gate 3 moves money into 2034-2035. Gate 2 makes Laura's contribution depend on co-sponsors committing first, while the case says her commitment is what brings them in, so the logic is circular. The growth sleeve adds QQQ on top of a world fund that already holds those companies, and justifies it by Laura's career in technology and creative work. That is backwards: her 2028 deposit and income already depend on that sector, so the portfolio should not double the exposure. There is one return assumption, no confidence for the range, no stress result and no account of inflation or currency.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: how the top of the 2031 range is set and with what confidence, when parts of it depend on events after 2033.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most disciplined risk machine for the money above the payments. The rule is CPPI: the floor accrues, then becomes the present value of the announced minimum in 2031, and the stock holding is a fixed multiple of the cushion above the floor. The multiplier is chosen against a gap-risk event that is sized, and both cash-lock and gap risk are accepted in writing. Assumptions are explicit (returns, volatility, flow timing, nominal payments), the minimum carries a target confidence, and liquidity, currency and the 2028 deposit are each named as risks. That is real portfolio analysis. What holds it back is the liability side, where the claim is weakest. It says the payments depend on no market price, but a coupon ladder costing about $300,000 depends on reinvesting coupons for six years before the first payment, and that is not priced. The top of the range is set at even odds and the gift is not clearly capped, so the stated chance of landing inside the range is unclear and may be near one half. Diversification is one world ETF plus short Treasuries.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: whether the gift is capped at the top of the range. If it is not, it lands above the range about half the time, and I could not work out what "confidence within the range" the team would report.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reach the same sound skeleton: match the ten payments with Treasuries, let the 2028 deposit fill any gap, and put the growth money above a floor. So the skeleton did not separate them. Whether the team understood where its own skeleton leaks did. A coupon ladder bought in 2027 throws off six years of cash before the first payment, and the 2028 deposit that fills any gap is most likely to fall short in exactly the falling-rate recessions that widen the gap. Only Entries 2 and 3 priced both, and that is why they lead for me. Entry 6 is the strongest on the growth side and would have challenged for first place if it had applied the same rigour to the ladder. To win, Entry 3 needs three things. Its deposit-threshold figure should reconcile on a back-of-envelope check. It should state an inflation and Taiwan-dollar assumption. And it should defend, in a sentence, why keeping only half of a small stock sleeve is "appropriate flexibility" when the case warns against committing nearly everything.
