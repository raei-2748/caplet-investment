## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Clear thesis: pay the ten payments with a matched Treasury ladder, then put the surplus in 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries. This is the allocation I would want for a client who takes thoughtful risks, with her living costs covered elsewhere and five years to the facility date. It handles the time horizons well: a 2031 step moves the announced floor into Treasuries, the ladder becomes the reserve in 2033, and the gift is the floor plus at most half the growth money, capped at the top of the range. It ties the stock exposure to Taiwan construction-cost inflation, which fits the client. What holds it back is analysis. It says the range will carry "a stated, modelled confidence level" but never gives one. It gives no sense of how large the growth portfolio is, how much of the total is exposed to stocks, or how much upside the 2031 de-risking surrenders. The 6-7% and 4-5% return assumptions are plain but unsupported. Honest in direction, light on evidence. Its weakness is that it never counts the price of its own safety.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: not sure how the floor in 2031 is sourced (is it sold from the stock portfolio at that date?) and whether "the remaining stocks" top-of-range figure is a statistic or a hope.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The payment ladder is sensible and the certainty standard (no return assumption behind any payment) is clearly stated. The growth side is where I part company. A 75% world / 25% QQQ split is not "appropriate diversification": a quarter of the growth money is in one concentrated technology index, and the stated reason (tech has led, Laura worked in tech) is a story about the past and about Laura's resume, not about what the portfolio needs. The three-gate staging is a creative frame, but it wanders away from the case: the case asks what Laura contributes in 2033 and what range she names in 2031, and this plan pushes a third tranche into 2034-35 and ties payment to co-sponsor behaviour and project performance, which the portfolio cannot know. There is no dollar range, no confidence level, no share of money in each bucket, and no testing of bad outcomes beyond "deep drawdowns possible". I like the idea of keeping options open, but the entry does not show how much growth it actually exposes or what it expects to give her.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "gate" in 2033 is decided by the portfolio being "above its target" when no target is given; and what happens to the 2031 announced top if gates 2 and 3 do not open.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most complete answer to what the case asks. Payments come from a held-to-maturity ladder, and the 2028 money tops it up first. The growth sleeve runs a stated rule that stays in stocks while there is a cushion above a floor tied to the announced minimum. The 2031 range is tied to that floor, with a 95% target for the low end and roughly even odds for the top. Its analysis uses assumptions (7% and 16% volatility for stocks, 4.5% for Treasuries, 10,000 simulated paths), and it covers liquidity, the 2028-deposit risk, currency and inflation. As an equity person I would normally resist a rule that sells stocks into falls and can sit in Treasuries until 2033, but the entry names that exact cost ("cash-lock", missing a recovery) and accepts it on purpose, and it explains the multiplier of three over five. That is the honest accounting of what safety costs which I rarely see. It is also the only entry that explains how the gift is shared with the flexibility fund: minimum plus at most half the gain above it. It could be clearer in plain English and a lot of terminology sits in a short space; the rule is hard for a non-specialist co-sponsor to follow, and the sleeve is small next to the whole pot, so the headline upside is modest. The "even odds" top end is also a conservative place to point judges.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor "starts near 60% of the sleeve" and then becomes the present value of the announced minimum in 2031 (what happens to the rule at that handover); the monthly mechanics.

### Entry 4

- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The client reading is the strongest part: it separates Laura's ability to bear risk (high) from her willingness (low for a missed payment, above average for the gift above the floor), and it puts the payment and WInS link in plain words. The strategy is a Treasury ladder, a 2032 Treasury ETF that backs the facility floor, and a global index fund as the "branch". But this is exactly the timidity I mark down: it says outright that moving floor money into equities "would raise the expected contribution, but we do not", and then does not say by how much the gift is reduced. That is honest in direction, but the cost is never counted. After the floor and the ladder there is very little left in stocks to grow. Mechanically it is hard to follow: how big the branch is, and why a range of "floor to floor plus half the fund" can be one that "she cannot miss", when the cap is what stops her from reaching more. A 2031 range exists but no dollar figures or odds are given, and the pitch leans on an unsupported claim about what co-sponsors usually pay for.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the sentence about buying a 2032 ETF "sized to return that remainder" - does the whole 2028 remainder go to bonds, and what is left for the index fund?

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A carefully reasoned, numerate entry. It tests a three-quarters-stocks plan, shows it would miss a payment about one future in thirty, and uses that to justify separating the promise from the hope. It prices the ladder's leak, sets money aside in 2031, and states that the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100 with a typical miss of about $1,000. It also admits that a 60/40 portfolio in its model usually leaves Laura richer but fails about one time in three. That is a real accounting of what the safety costs, which is what I look for. But look at what it chooses: most of the 2028 deposit goes into bonds as a floor, and only the interest (about a fifteenth of everything) rides on world stocks. For a client who takes thoughtful risks with nine years of runway on the gift and outside living income, that is a note that mostly pays her own money back. The upside given away is stated in odds but not in dollars. The comparison with the three-quarters-stocks plan feels like a strawman, since that plan put the payments themselves at risk. The XR-pill extended-release image carries a lot of the writing but the strategy is clear.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the two-part certainty sentence about the 2028 deposit needing "$125,000 of its $150,000" after a two-point yield fall; and how the 60/40 figure of "one time in three" compares like-for-like with the plan.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Substantively the same plan as Entry 5: ladder first, a bond floor for the gift sized to grow back to the whole 2028 remainder, and only the interest in world stocks, with the half-promised portion moved to Treasuries on the day the range is named. My view of the strategy is the same: sound and well tested, but timid for this client, with the cost of the safety shown in odds and not dollars. In its favour, this version defines "high degree of certainty" more cleanly (a cheque fails only on a US default or a yield drop plus a 2028 deposit well below $150,000), and its 60/40 comparison is framed as breaking "a promise, a cheque or the gift it named" about one time in four, which is easier to follow. Same limit as the other: it protects the downside far more than it pursues the upside that the case invites.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "balanced 60/40" is not defined (60 stocks and 40 bonds of what?), so I cannot tell what the one-in-four comparison measures.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the entry actually counted something. Entry 3 stated its confidence levels, used a simulation, and wrote down the cost of its own rule (the cash-lock). Entries 5 and 6 counted too, and their honesty about a 60/40 portfolio usually doing better is to their credit, but they then choose a plan that parks most of the gift money in bonds and calls that discipline. Entry 4 does the same without the numbers. Entry 1 has the allocation I like (80/20 growth on a protected promise), but it asks the judge to trust that a confidence level exists. Entry 2 is the least tied to the case. For Entry 3 to win it should say in one plain sentence how much of Laura's money is in stocks at the start and how much of the gift range the 2031 de-risking gives up, and make the rule readable to a co-sponsor. For Entry 1 to catch it, it needs numbers: the confidence, the odds and the dollars.
