## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The team tells a story of how it got here. It started with three-quarters in stocks, tested that, watched it miss a payment about one future in thirty, and then redesigned around ten one-year bills instead of one ten-year bill. That is real reasoning and it reads like people who did the work. The strategy separates the promise (ladder) from the hope (bond floor plus interest in a world fund), and ties Laura's own income risk (advances, speaking and licensing falling in recessions) to why the payments must come from money she already has. It also deals with the 2031 range problem honestly, including the catch that a capped range hands upside to Laura and downside to the gift. What holds it back is an internal tension: it says only two things can break a cheque, then says about one time in three the 60/40 portfolio "misses a cheque or lands below its own range". A judge will ask which it is, and whether that fits "a high degree of certainty". The brand story (the pill, X for ten, R for redemption) is more decoration than argument and I would trade it for another sentence of explanation. Several numbers appear without much indication of where they came from beyond "our model".
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why "the half she promised moves into Treasuries" on the day the range is named, and how that is different from the floor she already owns. The "about a fifteenth of everything she owns" interest figure took a second read. I could not tell whether the one-in-three miss includes cheques or only the gift.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The Treasury ladder for the ten payments is sound and well explained, and the startup "runway" idea is a clear way to frame two kinds of money for an entrepreneur client. But the rest is thin. Everything above the ladder goes into 75% world stocks and 25% QQQ, justified by "innovation-driven companies have led global returns", which is a return-chasing argument and concentrates risk in one sector right when Laura's income is already tied to creative and tech industries. The "stage gates" depend on co-sponsor commitments and project progress, which the team cannot know or control, and one gate falls in 2034-35 when the case puts the facility decision in 2033. There is no dollar range, no confidence level, and no stress test beyond "about 7% with deep drawdowns possible". The "real option" language sounds clever but isn't backed by any analysis. It reads as a metaphor in search of a portfolio.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a tranche "de-risked into Treasuries" in 2031 is meant to be the bottom of the range, and what size that is. Also who decides whether the portfolio is "above its target" at Gate 2.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the same core strategy as Entry 1, tightened in the places where Entry 1 wobbles. It defines a failed cheque in one place (US default, or a 2028 deposit coming in under about $125,000 after yields drop two points) and then says plainly that the balanced portfolio breaks a promise, a cheque or the gift it named, about one time in four. That is a team being straight about its own trade-off, which is exactly what I want to see from students. It shows the full planning arc: ladder in 2027, gap filled from the 2028 deposit, floor and fund, range set in 2031, reserve running down from 2033, and a priority order if things go wrong. Client fit is strong: the residency as her boldest bet, income that shrinks in the same recessions that make bonds dear, and the 2031 credibility problem. Weaknesses: the model-derived figures (97 in 100, one in four, the $1,000 miss) are given without anything about assumptions beyond 7.5% for world stocks in the earlier version, the XR pill branding is stagey, and the document is dense for a 500-word read.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "yields drop two points before we buy" while the money is bought in January 2027 and the deposit arrives in 2028, so the sequence was unclear for a moment. Also what "balanced 60/40" is made of.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, readable, textbook answer: liability-matched Treasury ladder, growth sleeve above it, floor locked into Treasuries before the 2031 conversation, floor plus at most half of the growth sleeve as the contribution. It addresses time horizons, rebalancing and review, and it names assumptions (6-7% stocks, 4-5% Treasuries, 2.5% inflation). It is mostly competent and mostly generic. It does not tell me how likely any outcome is, gives no dollar range or confidence, and treats "good, average and severe markets" as a sentence rather than a result. The Laura link ("keeping her word to collaborators") is reasonable, but the portfolio would look identical for any client with ten fixed payments. The claim that stocks are her best defence against Taiwan construction costs is asserted, not shown. It reads like a good student who followed the instructions and stopped there.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the "locked floor" in 2031 is bought with money from the growth sleeve and how big it is. "Two-thirds of Laura's total contributions" for the ladder is good, but I could not see what is left for the floor.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The central idea, that the minimum told to co-sponsors should be the number the portfolio is mechanically built to protect, is clear and creative, and the team is open about what could go wrong (gap risk, cash-lock, the uncertain 2028 deposit, currency). That honesty impresses me. But the machinery is heavy: a monthly CPPI rule with a multiplier of three, a floor that accrues, 10,000 simulated paths. It is hard to believe the students could defend every part of it under questioning, and "we will not rewrite the rule because markets moved" echoes Wharton while the rule itself trades every month. The cash-lock risk is serious: after a bad stretch the sleeve sits in Treasuries to 2033 and Laura's facility contribution may sit at the bottom of the range. The paragraph structure (bold headings, bullets) feels assembled. Client tailoring is mostly "takes thoughtful risks but needs ten payments", which is thinner than it should be.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can be "near 60% of the sleeve" at the start and later equal the present value of the announced minimum, and what happens in the years between. I was also unsure what "the contribution that about half of 10,000 simulated paths would beat" works out to in dollars.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A grown-up IPS with real thinking in it. It separates Laura's ability to bear risk (high, since no withdrawals and outside income) from her willingness (low for a missed payment, higher above the floor), which is a distinction a judge will credit. It buys the longest payments first because they are the most rate-sensitive, it says what happens if rates fall and the ladder costs more than $300,000, and it names the one-issuer concentration as a weakness it accepts. The strategy then follows through to the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution, and it refuses to move floor money into equities because that could break a number Laura has quoted. That restraint shows judgment. It is held back by the lack of any probabilities or ranges in dollars, so I cannot judge how the plan behaves in bad markets, and by a stiff, formal voice that sounds more like an adult's policy document than a team's argument. The line that she "cannot miss the range" sits uneasily with the case's talk of uncertainty.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how "quoted rounded down after any expected shortfall" works and how big that shortfall is. The pitch's opening claim that co-sponsors who fund buildings rarely pay to run them is an assumption I could not trace to the case.

## My overall view
What separated the top from the rest was whether the team showed me how it thinks, not just what it chose. Entries 3 and 1 tell me they tried a stock-heavy plan, watched it fail, and rebuilt around the real shape of Laura's obligation, then put numbers on how often the new plan still disappoints. Most other entries describe a sensible portfolio without telling me how likely it is to work, and Entry 2 relies on a metaphor and an under-examined stock mix. Entry 3 wins because it states its failure rate next to its promise. To win outright it needs to cut the branding and the cute lines, say where its probabilities come from, and make its definition of "high certainty" agree with its own admission that some paths miss a cheque. Entry 6 would climb if it put real outcome numbers into its careful logic.
