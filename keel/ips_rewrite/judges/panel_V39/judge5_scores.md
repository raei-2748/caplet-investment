# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid (decision science). Method: for each entry I wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a full pairwise tournament (15 pairs) on Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions. Wins: Entry 6 five, Entry 1 four, Entry 5 three, Entry 3 two, Entry 2 one, Entry 4 none. Note for the panel chair: Entries 1 and 6 are, apart from a handful of sentences, the same submission; I scored each on its own reading and the tournament separated them only on clarity.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 — the self-built capital-protected note, the sharpest reading of Laura's risk capacity (her income falls when bonds get dear), and every trade-off priced against an alternative it tested; marginally clearer than its twin.
2. Entry 1 — the same strategy and the same reasoning, two or three sentences less clear on the conditions under which a payment fails and on what the "leak" is.
3. Entry 5 — a pre-committed rule (CPPI) whose floor becomes the number told to co-sponsors; honest about gap risk and cash-lock; the best explicit statement of confidence.
4. Entry 3 — complete, formal and sound, with one real insight about who funds operations; the protective structure is present but not argued.
5. Entry 2 — a genuinely new framing (staged tranches as real options) carried by thin portfolio analysis and one circular gate.
6. Entry 4 — the table-stakes answer, done competently, with nothing I did not already expect.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that Laura's 2028 income (advances, speaking, licensing) is procyclical and therefore likely to be weakest exactly when a Treasury ladder is dearest, so the deposit she already owns should carry the promise and the uncertain deposit should carry the hope. That is a sharper way of seeing her problem than "she can bear risk because she has outside income", and it drives the whole design. The second idea, building the gift as a Treasury floor that grows back to the full remainder with only the expected interest in a world index fund, is a known structure (a capital-protected note) applied cleanly, and the entry argues it against two alternatives it says it modelled (three-quarters stocks; 60/40) with stated failure rates, which is exactly what Wharton means by reasoning, assumptions and trade-offs. The treatment of the 2031 range is the most thoughtful in the set: it notices that a capped range is asymmetric once named and acts on that by de-risking the promised half at announcement. What holds it back: the strategy is very conservative for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks, and the entry never tells me roughly how big the gift is relative to the $150,000 or what Laura gives up in expected contribution for her 97-in-100 certainty; the balance of growth against protection is asserted by probabilities rather than weighed as a cost. The pill branding adds nothing to the strategy and costs words.
- Not understood on first reading: the 60/40 comparison. If the ten payments are bought first with the 2027 deposit, I could not see how a 60/40 portfolio "misses a cheque", so I was unsure whether the alternative was 60/40 on the whole $450,000 or on the growth money. Also "about a fifteenth of everything she owns" told me the stock weight but not the size of the floor, so I could not picture the gift.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution can be treated as a sequence of real options, with each tranche released on evidence about the project as well as the market. That is the one idea in this panel that attacks the case's words "flexibility as the project develops" head-on, and it speaks in a language co-sponsors and an entrepreneur both recognise. The ladder for the payments is standard and correctly described. What holds it back is everything a decision scientist would ask next. The second gate depends on co-sponsors having committed their share, while co-sponsors decide on the strength of Laura's promise, so the gate is circular; a 2034-35 tranche sits after the 2033 contribution the case asks about; the size of the seed tranche, the meaning of "above its target", and the confidence attached to the range are never stated, so funding reliability and the contribution are not actually evaluated under varying outcomes, just narrated. The 25% QQQ tilt is justified by Laura's own career being in technology, which is the opposite of good client reasoning: it concentrates her portfolio in the sector her outside income already depends on. The entry has the best idea and the weakest analysis in the set.
- Not understood on first reading: how the seed tranche is sized and what fixes the top of the range; what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio; whether the 2034-35 gate is part of the 2033 facility contribution Laura must describe in 2031.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that in Laura's world the money that funds a building is easier to raise than the money that runs it, which is a client-world reason (not just a risk reason) to secure the operating payments first. I also liked buying the longest payments first because they are the most rate-sensitive, and the honest acceptance that relying on one issuer is a concentration the team chooses with its eyes open. The entry is the most complete as a policy document: ability and willingness to bear risk separated by objective, a buy-and-hold rule, an annual review trigger, a substitution rule, a shortfall waterfall, inflation and currency named, and a range built so that the contribution lands inside it by construction. What holds it back: the central protective structure (a 2032 Treasury sized to return the whole 2028 remainder, the discount going to equities) appears as a decision without its reasoning, so I cannot tell why this split and not another, or what it costs in expected contribution; funding reliability and the range are described qualitatively, with no statement of how confident the team is or how it evaluated that. It reads as a sound plan rather than an argued one.
- Not understood on first reading: why the floor Treasury is sized to return the whole remainder (and so how much actually goes into the branch); what the "2027 leftover" in the shortfall waterfall is, given the 2027 deposit is almost entirely spent on the ladder.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: nothing I did not already expect from a competent team: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments, an 80/20 growth sleeve with a rebalancing band, the bottom of the range moved into Treasuries in 2031, floor plus at most half the remainder in 2033, a self-liquidating reserve. All of it is correctly reasoned, clearly organised and consistent, and the assumptions are stated. What holds it back is that the decisions that would distinguish it are the ones left open: how the bottom of the announced range is chosen before it is locked, what confidence attaches to the top, and why 80/20 rather than any other split, which is justified only by a five-year horizon. The claim that stocks are the best defence against rising Taiwan construction costs is asserted, not argued. Client knowledge is generic (an entrepreneur who keeps her word); nothing here could only have been written about Laura. This is the median strong entry, and the median does not reach the top 50.
- Not understood on first reading: how the bottom of the range is set; the entry promises a "stated, modelled confidence level" and does not state it.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the number Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 can be made the floor of a pre-committed allocation rule, so that protecting her word and running the growth money are the same mechanism rather than two policies that might disagree. The CPPI design is explained properly (multiplier, cushion, cap at 100% because margin is banned, monthly rebalancing), and the entry does what few teams do: it names the two ways the rule fails (gap risk, cash-lock), says why it chose a multiplier of three, and accepts the cash-lock consequence explicitly. It also gives the clearest confidence statement in the panel (at least 95% on the minimum, roughly even odds on the top) and lists its assumptions, the 2028-deposit variability and the currency exposure. What holds it back: the floor starts "near 60% of the sleeve" with no reason given and no account of what it protects before 2031, so the rule's most important parameter is unargued; a cash-lock early in the period would freeze the gift at a fraction of the deposit for five years, and the entry accepts that without weighing it against a simpler static floor; and Laura herself is thin here, a short paragraph of "she takes thoughtful risks" rather than a reading of her circumstances.
- Not understood on first reading: what the 60% floor protects between 2028 and 2031 and why 60%; whether "half the gain above it" means half of the excess over the minimum or half of the whole sleeve above the minimum.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same two things as Entry 1, since this is the same submission with small changes: that Laura's uncertain 2028 income is correlated with the price of the safety she needs, and that the gift can be built as a home-made capital-protected note so that the promise to co-sponsors is backed before any stock is bought. The reasoning is the best in the panel on Wharton's own terms: the team shows the alternative it started with and why its model rejected it, defines "high degree of certainty" as a short list of named conditions rather than an adjective, prices the ladder's reinvestment leak and sets it aside before the range is named, and explains why the promised half of the stock fund is de-risked on the day the range is announced. The reserve's composition over time and the order of priorities if anything changes are both answered in a sentence each. The differences from Entry 1 are in its favour: the failure conditions for a payment are spelled out as a joint event (a yield fall before purchase and a 2028 deposit below about $125,000), the leak is named as coupon reinvestment, and the rejected 60/40 alternative's failure is defined as breaking either promise. What holds it back is the same as Entry 1: a very low equity share for a client the case calls willing to take thoughtful risks, with the cost in expected contribution never weighed, and branding that spends words the strategy does not need.
- Not understood on first reading: the 60/40 comparison still left me unsure whether the alternative applied to the whole portfolio or only to the growth money, though the definition of what it "breaks" helped.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reach my table stakes (a Treasury ladder for the ten payments, stocks for the rest), so the ranking was decided by what each team did with the money after the ladder and by how honestly it argued the trade-off. The best entries (6 and 1) separated themselves not with the pill name but with a reading of the client I had not made myself: her outside income and the price of safety move together, so the promise should rest on the deposit she already holds. They then argued their design against alternatives they had modelled and attached probabilities to each claim, which is what Wharton's rubric calls reasoning, assumptions and trade-offs. Entry 5 was the only other entry to make its mechanism and its promise the same object and to name how the mechanism fails; Entry 3 was sound but descriptive; Entry 2 had the boldest idea and the least analysis behind it; Entry 4 was the competent median. To win rather than place, the best entry would need to confront the one question it avoids: what Laura gives up, in expected facility contribution, for a gift she can name with 97-in-100 confidence, and why that price is right for a client who is described as a risk-taker. A single sentence weighing that cost would turn a very well-argued conservative plan into a complete decision.
