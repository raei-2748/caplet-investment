### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well: The plan runs in a clear order: the payments first, then a facility floor, then a growth fund, with no trading until 2033. Client tailoring is strong: it says her tolerance for a missed payment is low because others would bear the loss, separates her living costs (covered outside the portfolio) from the portfolio, and sets a higher tolerance above the floor. - What it does well: It is close to Entry 1 but states its key assumption (world stocks at 7.5% a year) and makes the comparison with a 60/40 mix explicit. ## My overall view
Entries 2 and 6 are the two that answer the five IPS questions in order and show their trade-offs. Entry 2 wins on discipline and on showing its assumptions and the confidence behind the 2031 minimum, which is what Wharton asks the portfolio analysis to show. Entry 6 wins on client insight and on the capped-range point, but leaves a few figures unexplained. Entries 1 and 6 are almost the same plan, so I judged them on their differences. Entry 3 is a sound plan presented plainly, and Entry 4 is competent but leaves out the confidence number that co-sponsors need. Entry 5 is the most original of the six, but its staged facility design runs against the case's 2033 decision. The best entry in this set would need the confidence level for its 2031 range stated in plain words, a clear account of the figures it uses, and one sentence on how much growth Laura gets for the risk she takes.
