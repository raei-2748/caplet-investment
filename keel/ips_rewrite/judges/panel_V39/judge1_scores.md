### Entry 2

The steps for 2031 (floor locked, top set by the stocks), 2033 (the ladder becomes the reserve, one payment a year) and the facility amount (floor plus at most half the rest) line up with what the case asks, and I could explain it to a friend in a minute. ### Entry 3

### Entry 5

- What it does well and what holds it back: A calm, sensible plan with a memorable image: roots that pay the ten cheques and a branch that grows the gift. ### Entry 6

- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: Strong on the risk side. The three buckets (reserve, growth sleeve, flexibility) and the reserve getting less risky each year show a plan that changes as the needs approach, which is what the case asks for. But I lost the middle of it: the growth sleeve runs on a rule called CPPI with a multiplier of three, and I could not explain it to a friend afterwards. "Gap risk" and "cash-lock" are not explained beyond their names. The pitch's "stock-or-Treasury rule" tells me nothing. "Floor = Promise" is a catchy line the team could carry through the whole competition, but the writing asks me to trust the mechanics rather than explaining them.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: CPPI, the "multiplier of three" and how stocks held equal "three times the cushion", "gap risk" and "cash-lock", and the "present value" of the minimum.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest, for me, was whether I could repeat the plan in two sentences afterwards and still say what would break it. Entry 3 did both: a plain story, numbers with their assumptions, and a candid account of what it gave up. Entry 2 was the easiest plan to explain but left its confidence number unstated, which is the gap I would most want closed. Entries 1, 5 and 6 each had a good idea and then lost me to a missing number or to jargon. The best entry would need one plain sentence on how sure the 2031 range is, with a number, and the mechanics of any rule it uses explained in words a friend would follow.
