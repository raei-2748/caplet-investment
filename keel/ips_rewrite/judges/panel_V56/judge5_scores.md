# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid (decision science). Marked against Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions only. Method: for each entry I wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a round-robin of pairwise comparisons (15 pairs) and derived the ranking from the wins.

Note before the ranking: Entries 1, 5 and 6 are the same strategy, and 5 and 6 are the same text apart from one rule. I scored them as one strategy and ordered them only on the few places where they differ. Their order relative to the other three entries is therefore a judgement about one strategy, not three.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1 - the gift built as a capital-protected note without the option, the client's income seen as a stock, and the plan's own weak point named and pre-funded; 5 wins of 5.
2. Entry 5 - the same strategy as Entry 1, with a defined worst-case rule for the reinvestment gap but without the alternative it rejected spelled out; 4 wins.
3. Entry 6 - identical to Entry 5 except that its gap rule relies on an undefined "margin"; 3 wins.
4. Entry 4 - the one genuinely different mechanism in the field (a pre-committed floor rule tied to the 2031 promise), best quantitative framing, held back by complexity and a silence on the ladder's own cash flows; 2 wins.
5. Entry 2 - the competent table-stakes design, complete and tidy, but it taught me nothing new; 1 win.
6. Entry 3 - a good definition of certainty attached to a plan whose central sizing decision is never made; 0 wins.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things I had not seen framed this way. First, the gift is engineered as a capital-protected note with the option stripped out: the 2028 deposit buys a Treasury maturing in 2032 as the floor, and only the interest that floor should earn is put at risk in shares, so stock exposure is sized by the floor's yield rather than by appetite. Second, the client's own income is treated as a stock-like asset that is correlated with the market, which is the real argument for keeping market risk away from the payments: a downturn would hit her 2028 deposit and a share-heavy portfolio at once. The entry also names its weak point (coupon reinvestment on the ladder), states a pre-committed rule for funding the gap out of the flexibility share before the gift, and moves the promised top of the range into Treasuries before Laura speaks to co-sponsors, so the range she announces is already owned. The trade-off it chose (credibility over size) is stated with the rejected alternative, which is exactly what Wharton asks for and what the other table-stakes entries omit. What holds it back: with under a tenth of the money in shares, growth has been very nearly surrendered, and the case says Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks and wants a balance; the entry argues for its choice but a judge could fairly say the balance has tipped too far. It also gives no confidence level for the top of the range and little on how the growth holding is reviewed, and the pharmaceutical name does more work as a rule ("never sell a rung early") than as an explanation.
- Did not understand on first reading: I had to re-read to see that "the interest that floor should earn" sizes the stock sleeve (the floor's interest to 2032, not the ladder's coupons). "Funds that mature like bonds" left me unsure what instrument is meant. The size of the "cushion" added to the gap price is not defined.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already expect. This is the ladder-plus-growth design most competent teams will reach, and it is executed well: the ladder sized with coupons, coupons parked in short Treasuries, an 80/20 growth engine with a rebalancing band, an annual review, and a stated set of return and inflation assumptions tested in three scenarios. It answers every one of Wharton's questions in order, which earns it a solid pass on strategy and portfolio analysis. What holds it back is the absence of a decision that cost the team something. The floor is not fixed until 2031, which means the minimum Laura can promise is whatever the market has left her by then; the entry does not confront that or argue why it is acceptable. The claim that stocks are her best defence against Taiwan construction costs is asserted, not reasoned. Client knowledge is adequate (credibility, entrepreneur) but generic; nothing here could only have been written about Laura. It reads as a well-organised document rather than as a team that saw something.
- Did not understand on first reading: what sets the bottom of the 2031 range. The entry says the bottom is moved into Treasuries, but the bottom seems to be chosen at that moment, so the sentence is circular on first pass.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: one useful idea, which is the definition of a high degree of certainty as independence from any forecast ("we are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question"). That is a sharper epistemic standard than a percentile and I would have liked the rest of the entry to live up to it. It does not. The central allocation decision of the whole plan, how the 2028 deposit is split between the 2032 Treasury (the minimum) and the world stock fund, is never stated; nor is the "share" of the stock fund that sets the top of the range, nor the "part" that stays as flexibility. For a decision scientist that is the plan declining to decide. It also names specific products, which Wharton told teams not to spend the IPS on, and spends a paragraph on inflation that says the obvious. The structure is clear and the voice is honest; the plan beneath it is Entry 2 with the numbers left out.
- Did not understand on first reading: how much of the 2028 deposit goes to the 2032 Treasury versus the stock fund, and therefore how large the minimum is relative to the upside.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the one genuinely different mechanism in this set. The announced minimum is made the quantity the portfolio is built to protect, and the growth sleeve runs a pre-committed constant-proportion rule whose floor becomes, from 2031, the present value of that minimum. Precommitment that removes the decision from the moment of fear is a decision-science idea, and the entry argues its parameter choice (multiplier of three, not five, against a stated one-month gap risk) and accepts the cash-lock consequence openly. It also lists the risks it accepts, including the variability of the 2028 deposit and the Taiwan-dollar exposure, with reasons. Portfolio analysis is the strongest in the field: simulated paths, a 95% target for the minimum, a median for the top, a volatility assumption. What holds it back: the rule is hard for a non-specialist to hold in her head, and Wharton scores whether recommendations can earn Laura's confidence; a floor at 60% of the sleeve with a multiplier of three means the sleeve begins fully in shares and the entry does not say so plainly, nor does it mention the known cost of such rules in choppy sideways markets (buying after rises, selling after falls). It also says nothing about the ladder's coupons or reinvestment, the one place where its "near-certainty" could leak. Still, it is the entry most likely to be remembered by a judge who has read two hundred ladders.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the announced minimum is chosen in 2031 (the accrued floor, or a number the team picks that the floor then tracks), and I had to work out myself that "three times the cushion" at the start implies 100% of the sleeve in shares.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same two ideas as Entry 1 (the gift as a capital-protected note minus the option; the client's income as a correlated, stock-like asset), explained at slightly greater length and, for a first-time reader, slightly more plainly: the "hurt her twice" reasoning on the income correlation is easier to follow here than in Entry 1. Its rule for the ladder's reinvestment gap is also better defined: it prices the shortfall as if nothing more were reinvested, which is a severe but auditable worst case rather than a discretionary cushion. What it gives up against Entry 1 is the explicit statement of the road not taken; the alternative (put the floor into shares and chase a bigger gift) is implied in the 2031 paragraph but never named, and Wharton asks for the trade-offs behind the decision, not only the decision. The same reservations as Entry 1 apply: very little growth, no confidence level on the top of the range, and a brand name that carries a rule but not an argument.
- Did not understand on first reading: the pitch's "Treasuries that mature one before each January" is garbled and I read it twice. I also wondered whether pricing the gap as if nothing were reinvested overstates it, since the entry gives no sense of scale.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: identical to Entry 5 in every respect but one. Everything said above about the strategy, its client fit and its limits applies here without change. The one difference is the rule for the reinvestment gap: it prices the shortfall at that day's rates and adds "a margin", which is a judgement call left to a future committee rather than a rule, and so is a small step away from the precommitment logic that makes the rest of the plan convincing. On its own that would not move a score, and it does not here; the two entries carry the same marks and I ordered them only because a ranking forces me to.
- Did not understand on first reading: the same garbled pitch phrase as Entry 5, and the size of the "margin".

## My overall view
Every entry bought the ten payments with Treasuries held to maturity, so that was never the contest. What separated the top from the rest was whether the team had seen anything beyond it. Entry 2 saw nothing and did the standard thing well; Entry 3 saw a good definition and then failed to size its own plan. Entry 4 saw that the promise could define the construction rule, and argued its parameters, which I respect, but it built a machine that Laura would have to take on trust. The Entry 1 family saw the client: her income is itself a risky asset correlated with the market, so the portfolio should add no correlated risk to it; the gift can be built as a protected note with the option replaced by owned shares; the ladder's own weak point is coupon reinvestment, so fund that gap from the flexibility share before it touches the promise; and move the announced top into bonds before it is announced. Those are four decisions, each with a reason, in plain language a co-sponsor could follow. For the best entry to actually win, it would need to meet the growth question head-on rather than leave a judge to notice that under a tenth of the money is in shares: either show that this is a deliberate position on the balance Wharton asks for, with a confidence figure for the top of the range, or give the growth holding a little more room and say what that costs in certainty. It would also need the Final Report to carry the numbers the IPS rightly leaves out.
