# Judge J3 scores

(Priya, pension actuary. Method: trace $300,000 in 2027, $150,000 in 2028, ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042, the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution through each plan, and find where it breaks or where the reasoning is hand-waved.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: names the ladder's one real hole (coupon reinvestment) and bounds it with a worst-case price in 2031 that can only be too cautious. The range is locked and funded before anyone hears it.
2. Entry 2: same plan and the same fix for the hole, but the cushion is not sized. Clearest on the trade-off it turned down, and on how her income risk ties back to the ladder.
3. Entry 1: same plan again, with the reinvestment fix resting on an unsized margin. Sound, a little less explicit about what it gave up.
4. Entry 5: the most quantitative growth design here (CPPI, a reasoned multiplier, gap and cash-lock risks named). It misses the ladder's reinvestment hole, and its confidence statements don't quite agree with each other.
5. Entry 4: clean liability matching and a good inflation split, but it says the only risks left are default and execution, which is false for a coupon ladder.
6. Entry 6: sensible "promise first" frame, but the 2031 floor is never sized, the reinvestment risk is treated as solved, and the growth sleeve's design is generic.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Every dollar has a job, and the order is right: the liabilities are cash-flow matched with Treasuries held to maturity, the 2028 deposit makes up any ladder gap first, then sets a principal floor for the gift, and only roughly that floor's interest goes into equities. Tracing the flows, it holds: the payments never touch shares, the 2031 range is funded in Treasuries before it is spoken, and her flexibility comes last. Unlike most entries, it finds the actual hole in a coupon ladder (the bond cost assumes coupons are reinvested at today's yields) and says which pot pays for it and in what order: her spare shares first, then the whole range. What holds it back is that the fix rests on "a margin" whose size is never given, and rates can keep falling between 2031 and 2033, so the 2031 pricing isn't final. The equity sleeve is tiny, so the gift is close to fixed at about the 2028 deposit plus a little. That is defensible for this client, but it puts little weight on her stated willingness to take thoughtful risks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what the pre-2033 coupons are reinvested in, and how big the margin is. Also, once half the shares move to Treasuries in 2031 the gift is almost fixed at the top, so I wasn't sure why the bottom of the range stays as low as the bare floor.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same architecture as Entry 1: matched ladder, a principal floor from the 2028 deposit, interest-sized equity, the promised half of the shares turned into bonds before the 2031 announcement, and the reinvestment gap priced and funded from her flexibility first. Two things lift it above Entry 1 for me. It states the alternative it rejected (stocks under the floor for a bigger gift) and why: a figure told to co-sponsors can't be walked back. And it ties her income risk to the ladder itself, since a downturn that shrinks her 2028 deposit is also the kind that makes ladder rungs dearer. That correlation is real and most teams miss it. Weaknesses: the reinvestment cushion is unsized, like Entry 1's. And if the first $300,000 doesn't complete the ladder, a cheque is not "already bought" until 2028, which is the very year the deposit is at risk. The text slides past that. The certainty definition is sound, but it is asserted rather than tested.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the "cushion" on the reinvestment gap is, and what happens if the first deposit leaves rungs unbought and the second one arrives small.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same plan as Entries 1 and 2, but it closes the one gap they leave open. In 2031 the reinvestment shortfall is priced as if nothing further were reinvested. That is a hard upper bound on the gap: it can only overstate it, so later rate falls can't break the promise. With that, the chain holds from 2031 onward with no forecast in it. The payments are matched, the stated range is held in Treasuries, and the worst-case gap is set aside from her spare shares, with the range itself as the last line of defence. That is exactly how I would want a liability-driven plan to fail: flexibility first, promise last, and nothing left to luck. It also states the equity share against the full $450,000, which makes the scale of the risk plain. What holds it back is shared with its siblings. The equity share is very small, so the "growth" half of the balance is thin. The range will come out almost a point once the 2031 moves are made. And the worst-case bound will cost her real flexibility, which the plan accepts but never sizes even roughly.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: I had to stop and check that "as if nothing more were reinvested" meant pricing the coupons at a zero return. It does, and it is the right bound, but it takes a second read.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A tidy, well-ordered liability match: maturity-dated Treasury funds for the early payments, individual bonds for the later ones, the 2028 deposit filling any gap, then a 2032 Treasury floor and one world equity fund. The inflation split is the most honest in the set: the payments are nominal so a nominal ladder matches them, while facility costs are real so the equity fund carries that risk. The flaw I was looking for is here. It says the only risks left are default and execution, but a coupon ladder bought for under $300,000 depends on reinvesting coupons for years before the first payment, so falling rates can leave it short. That risk is never priced, and the plan's certainty claim rests on ignoring it. The 2031 top is a share of an equity fund that stays fully invested to 2033, which is acceptable because the bottom is locked. But neither the share given nor the share kept is defined, and it defers the likelihood of the top to the Final Report.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how much of VT goes into the top of the range and how much stays as her buffer. Also whether the 2032 floor fund's interest is reinvested or paid out.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The most serious attempt here to make the growth sleeve itself protect a promise. A CPPI rule with a stated multiplier and the reason for it (a breach needs roughly a one-third fall in a month at full exposure), a floor tied to the present value of the announced minimum, the margin ban reflected in the 100% cap, and both gap risk and cash-lock named and accepted. That is genuine portfolio analysis, and the assumption list (returns, volatility, timing, nominal payments, currency) is the most complete of the six. But it repeats the classic hole on the reserve: rungs "sized to deliver $50,000" and never sold, with no word on how coupons received before 2033 are reinvested. The 2031 statements also don't line up for me. A floor "mechanically built to protect" the minimum sits beside a 95% confidence target. And the top is the median outcome, while the gift is not said to be capped at the top, so the chance of landing inside the range is unclear. The rule-based machinery is defensible but heavy for a plan where most of the money is already in a static ladder.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the announced minimum is chosen in 2031 from a floor that starts at about 60% of the sleeve and grows at the short rate; whether the gift is capped at the top of the range; and why the minimum carries only 95% confidence if the rule protects it.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The frame is correct and the order of claims is right: payments matched first, a 2031 floor locked in Treasuries before co-sponsors hear it, and the gift capped at the top with half the growth kept as flexibility. Tracing the money, though, the key quantities are missing. It never says how much of the growth portfolio moves into the 2031 floor or how that is decided, so the bottom of the range has no rule behind it. Coupons received early are parked in short Treasuries, which is reinvestment at unknown future rates, yet the entry claims rate swings never touch the payments. The growth sleeve's 80/20 split with a drift band is a standard balanced fund, and there is no reason given for holding bonds alongside a floor that is locked separately later. The "good, average and severe" testing is named but not described, so the analysis reads as asserted rather than done.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the size of the 2031 floor is set; and why the growth sleeve carries 20% intermediate Treasuries when the floor is bought separately in 2031.

## My overall view
Every entry here matches the ten payments with Treasuries, so matching alone separated nobody. What separated them was whether the team saw where a matched ladder can still fail and priced it. A coupon ladder that costs less than the $500,000 it pays depends on reinvesting coupons through years when nothing is paid out. Three teams named that, put a price on it before the 2031 announcement, and said which money absorbs it first. The other three either ignored it or claimed it away. Entry 3 went one step further and priced the gap at its worst case, so nothing after 2031 can break the promise. That is the only entry where I could trace every flow from 2027 to 2042 without finding a forecast holding up a commitment. To win, the best entry would need two things. First, an order-of-magnitude sense of what that worst-case set-aside costs her flexibility, so Laura knows what the certainty is costing her. Second, a sharper answer on the growth side: a sleeve under a tenth of her money is defensible, but the team should show it weighed her stated appetite for thoughtful risk, not just the safety of the promise.
