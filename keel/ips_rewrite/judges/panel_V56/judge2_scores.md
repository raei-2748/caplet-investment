### Entry 1

- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe

But it hands a client who "takes thoughtful risks" and has six to sixteen years a portfolio with under a tenth in shares, and it never counts what that safety costs her. The "banks sell this as a capital-protected note" line is a comparison, not an analysis. The 7.5% share assumption has no stated downside case. It reads as timidity with a good explanation.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The ladder carries the certainty and a rule-based sleeve lets stocks run when the cushion is large, which is the right instinct for this client. It states its return and volatility assumptions, a confidence target, and its own failure modes (gap risk, cash-lock, currency). The cost: CPPI is dense for judges who want a simple, elegant idea, and the cash-lock means a bad early year can park the growth sleeve in Treasuries for years. The top of the range at "roughly even odds" is honest. The sleeve is also small, so the upside it manages is modest in dollars.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "floor starts near 60% of the sleeve" and how it ties to the announced minimum from 2031.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the plan counted what safety costs. Entries 1, 2 and 5 are one idea, a ladder, a floor, and shares under a tenth of her money, told well but leaving a risk-tolerant client with a bond portfolio. Entry 4 is the clearest about doing the obvious right thing: buy the promise, then grow the surplus properly. Entry 3 is more ambitious and analytical but risks losing judges in the machinery. To win, Entry 4 would need to put a number on its confidence in the 2031 range, say what the 80% stock tilt gives up against a lower tilt, and show one worked good/bad market case.
