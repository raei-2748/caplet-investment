# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5 - Fully quantified growth sleeve with a pre-committed rule, a stated confidence level and an honest list of what the rule costs.
2. Entry 6 - Ladder first, then a real 80% equity engine with a stated review and stress approach; simple and growth-minded, less rigorous on the numbers.
3. Entry 4 - Clean floor-plus-VT design that names the downside (-30% years) and the assumed return, but gives away its upside quietly.
4. Entry 3 - Most ambitious growth tilt, but it rests on a thematic forecast (AI, semiconductors) and concentrated satellites.
5. Entry 2 - Same design as Entry 1, but it at least admits the stock slice is about a fifteenth of her money.
6. Entry 1 - Sophisticated floor design, but the growth sleeve is tiny, not sized, and the upside given away is never counted.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The ladder for the ten payments is sound and well tied to Laura's income pattern (the 2028 deposit behaves like a stock). The floor-plus-"interest on the floor" idea is a reasonable structure, and it is honest that the 2031 range is sold to co-sponsors as near-certain. But it never says how little goes into stocks. The result is a plan where the growth engine is a rounding error for a client who is "willing to take thoughtful risks" with six years of runway. The cost of that safety (the upside Laura gives away) is waved off in one sentence ("we could put the floor into stocks and chase a bigger gift") and is not counted. The reinvestment-risk handling is good analysis, but the plan's analytical core is thin on return assumptions and downside scenarios. The capsule framing takes up space that could have gone to numbers.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how large the stock holding actually is, and whether "the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn" means a few percent or a large slice of the 2028 deposit. I had to work it out myself.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same architecture as Entry 1 but with the key disclosure: stocks are "about a fifteenth of her money, small on purpose". That is the honesty I want, though it also confirms the plan is timid; a fifteenth in stocks is not meaningful growth for a risk-tolerant client with six years. It states the 7.5% assumption and that only the gift shrinks if stocks disappoint. The reinvestment shortfall now lowers the whole range, which is more honest than reducing only the bottom. The plan protects the payments and the promise very well, but it is mostly a bond purchase with a lottery ticket attached. Better than Entry 1 on analysis, still short on quantifying downside and upside outcomes.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the "capital-protected note minus the option" argument is clever but I could not tell whether the stock slice is meant to be the interest on the floor or an ordinary allocation; the fifteenth clarified it.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The Lock-then-Launch split is clear and correct: certainty for the fixed payments, returns for the flexible money. It is the only entry that really tries to grow the 2028 deposit with conviction. But the growth sleeve is 30% in tech, semiconductor and Taiwan satellites justified by a forecast that AI will drive the next decade; that is a prediction, not a framework, and Wharton warns against picking investments you think will perform well. Diversification is weaker than it looks (three overlapping tech-heavy satellites on top of an S&P core that is already tech heavy). Taiwan equity as a hedge to Taiwan cost is a thoughtful touch but it is not explained as a hedge. There are no return or volatility assumptions and no downside scenarios, so portfolio analysis is thin. The 2031 floor mechanism is good, and it correctly notes the contribution is true surplus. It names specific tickers, which Wharton says to avoid.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "expected value of the remaining equity" sets the top of the range, since no confidence level is given.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A clear, disciplined plan: match each payment, define high certainty as "does not depend on any forecast", then put the rest in one cheap world fund and leave it alone. It states the return assumption (6-7%) and the severity of the bad years (-30% or worse), which is the kind of honesty I want. It covers inflation, the flexibility share and how the reserve shortens. What holds it back is that the split between floor and VT is never sized, so I cannot tell how much growth Laura actually gets, and the cost of choosing a near-guaranteed minimum over more stocks is not counted. The promise to state the likelihood of the top "in the Final Report" defers the key number. Solid and readable, but it plays safe.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the VT holding is relative to the Treasury floor.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry that treats the facility contribution as something to be grown with discipline rather than something to be fenced off. The sleeve starts effectively fully invested in a world ETF and cuts stocks only as the cushion shrinks, so it takes the risk Laura can bear and says exactly when it stops. It states the assumptions (7% return, 16% volatility, 4.5% on Treasuries), the simulation basis for the top of the range, the confidence in the minimum (95%) and the roughly even odds at the top, and it lists what the rule costs (gap risk, cash-lock, the unreliable 2028 deposit, currency). It ties the 2031 promise directly to what the portfolio is built to protect, which speaks to the co-sponsor credibility point in the case. Weaknesses: a monthly CPPI is complex for judges to follow and cash-lock does give up recovery; the 2033 giving rule ("at most half the gain above") is a little vague. Even so, it is the only entry I would trust to explain its trade-offs to a sceptical room.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor "from 2031 equals the present value of the announced minimum" interacts with the 60% starting floor; I had to read it twice.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A straightforward and well-argued plan: ladder first, then an 80/20 global stock and intermediate Treasury engine, with stocks justified as the best defence against Taiwan construction cost inflation. That is a real growth position and the one I find most appropriate for a client with six years and covered living costs. It plans the changes by date (2031 lock of the range floor, 2033 reserve shortening), caps the contribution at half the remaining growth money and keeps the rest as flexibility, and tests good, average and severe markets. The weak spots: a "shock absorber" of 20% intermediate bonds is a small hedge and is not argued, it never counts what the ladder costs in forgone growth, and the model-based confidence level is promised, not given. It also treats the 2028 deposit as certain income when the case says it comes from variable sources.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why 20% intermediate Treasuries rather than the short Treasuries used elsewhere in the plan.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the entry actually counted the price of safety. Entries 1 and 2 are elegant but turn the facility money into mostly bonds, and Entry 1 does not even say how little is in stocks. Entries 4 and 6 are sensible and honest about the stock volatility, but they do not size the trade-off. Entry 3 is the boldest but bets on a forecast and a concentrated tech theme. Entry 5 gives Laura real equity exposure, quantifies the assumptions and the odds, and says in plain terms what the rule gives up. To win, Entry 5 would need a simpler explanation of the rule, a clear number for how much of the sleeve is in stocks on day one, and one sentence on what the floor costs in expected facility money versus an all-stock sleeve.
