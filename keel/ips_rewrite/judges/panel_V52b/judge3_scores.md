# Judge J3 scores

(Priya, pension actuary. Method: trace the $300,000 in 2027, the $150,000 in 2028, the 2031 range, the 2033 contribution and the ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042 through each plan, and find where it breaks or where the reasoning is waved through.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: the only plan where I could not find an unpriced hole. It names the ladder's real weak point (coupon reinvestment) and says who pays for it, and everything it promises in 2031 is already in bonds.
2. Entry 5: the same plan as Entry 3. Its fallback for a shortfall the kept stocks can't cover ("comes off the bottom") is less coherent than Entry 3's "lowers the whole range".
3. Entry 2: the most serious risk work (gap risk, cash-lock, deposit risk, currency), but the 2031 range's confidence is badly defined, and the ladder's reinvestment risk is never priced.
4. Entry 1: complete, sound and sized correctly, but it claims the only risk left is default and leaves the 2031 floor's size undefined.
5. Entry 4: a sound skeleton that overclaims certainty, leaves the key split unsized and doesn't protect the top of the range.
6. Entry 6: the ladder's sizing doesn't add up, and the growth engine is a concentrated bet on a forecast.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The cash flows trace cleanly. The ladder is sized so principal plus coupons covers each payment, and the "roughly two-thirds of total contributions" checks out against today's yields. The 2028 deposit fills any gap first. In 2031 the bottom of the range goes into bonds maturing before 2033, the gift is capped at the announced top, and part of the growth money is kept back for flexibility. It answers every IPS question Wharton asks: allocation over time, how the reserve self-liquidates, a rebalancing rule, return and inflation assumptions, and good/average/severe testing. Where it slips is the claim that rate swings "never touch the payments" and that default is the only risk left. Coupons that arrive before 2033 have to be reinvested at rates nobody knows yet, and that hole goes unpriced. It also never says how much of the growth sleeve becomes the 2031 floor, so I can't tell how big the protected promise is. There's no plan for a smaller 2028 deposit either. It's a competent, standard design that understands why it works, but it isn't distinctive.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This team clearly understands that a protection rule has failure modes. It names gap risk and works out roughly what fall would breach the floor, which is why it picked a multiplier of 3. It accepts cash-lock knowingly, and it also deals with the 2028 deposit's dependence on Laura's income and the Taiwan-dollar exposure. That is more real risk thinking than any other entry. Two things hold it back. First, the range: the top is set at roughly the median outcome and the gift isn't stated to be capped there. So the chance that the 2033 contribution lands inside the range is only about a coin flip, which isn't what the case asks the confidence statement to deliver. The "95% confidence" in a minimum that is supposedly mechanically protected also sits awkwardly. Second, the ladder: "each sized to deliver $50,000" says nothing about coupons received before 2033 and the rate they'll be reinvested at. A monthly CPPI is also more moving parts than the goal needs. The logic holds, but the plan carries complexity that a simpler bond floor would get almost for free.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor jumps in 2031 from "accrues at the short Treasury yield" to "the present value of the announced minimum". It isn't clear whether the minimum is picked to equal the floor already accrued, or whether the cushion can shrink overnight when the number is announced.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The chain is coherent all the way through. The $300,000 buys the ten payments. The 2028 deposit first completes any rung, then buys a late-2032 Treasury floor, and only about the floor's expected interest goes into stocks. By 2031 the half of the stocks being promised has moved into bonds too, so every dollar named to co-sponsors is already owned. It is also the only entry to name the hole I came looking for: coupon reinvestment if yields fall and stay low. It says who pays for that shortfall (Laura's kept stocks) and what happens if they can't cover it (the whole range comes down), set aside before any number is said aloud. Linking Laura's 2028 deposit to her career income is real client insight, not decoration. My worries are about balance, not logic. Stocks are about a fifteenth of the money, which is thin for a client "willing to take thoughtful risks". Diversification beyond one world fund isn't discussed. And Laura's kept half of the stocks is also the shortfall reserve, so her flexibility after 2033 could be close to nothing. Inflation on facility costs gets only a passing nod through the Taiwan-dollar line. Even so, of these six, this is the plan whose promises I would sign off.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: which "funds that mature like bonds" sit in the ladder. Why the floor is "rounded down" (I assumed the fund rungs don't pay a fixed amount, but it isn't said). The XR naming took a moment to connect to the strategy.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The structure is right: matched rungs (term-dated Treasury ETFs for 2033-2036, individual bonds after), a late-2032 Treasury minimum funded from the 2028 deposit, and one low-cost world fund left alone. Its principles are clearly stated. But the definition of certainty overclaims. It says every rung pays its known value "whatever happens to ... interest rates", yet the ETFs pay out income along the way and the bonds pay coupons, both of which have to be reinvested. That risk is waved away. The split of the 2028 deposit between minimum and stocks is never sized, so the size of the promise is unknowable. The top of the range is set from VT's 2031 value while VT stays in stocks until 2033, so the top isn't protected. That's acceptable only because the confidence is pushed to the Final Report. Nothing covers a smaller 2028 deposit or currency risk. It's sound enough, but too thin on Portfolio Analysis to stand out.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "a share of VT's value" means for the top of the range, and whether the gift is capped at that top.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strategy is Entry 3's: ladder bought outright, 2028 floor bought in Treasuries, stocks sized to the floor's expected interest, and the promised half of the stocks moved to bonds before the range is named. So it has the same strengths: no unprotected promise, the reinvestment hole named and paid for, and genuine insight on the deposit depending on Laura's career. It also has the same weaknesses: very little growth, thin flexibility and little on diversification or facility inflation. Where it loses a little to Entry 3 is the fallback when the kept stocks can't cover the reinvestment shortfall. Saying the residual "comes off the bottom" suggests only the floor moves. But if money is genuinely lost, the top has to fall by the same amount, so the range's mechanics read less tightly. It doesn't say how large the stock sleeve is, either, which leaves me to work out how much growth Laura is actually getting.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: same as Entry 3 (which funds mature like bonds; why the floor is rounded down). Also whether "off the bottom" means the top stays put.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The idea of separating certain money from growth money is right, but the cash flows don't trace. Each rung is sized so its face value is $50,000. At yields near 4.5-5%, coupon Treasuries trade close to par, so ten $50,000 face amounts cost far more than $300,000, which doesn't fit "close to Laura's $300,000". The coupons on top would be a large, unexplained surplus. So either the cost or the sizing is wrong, and this is the foundation of the plan. The growth engine is a concentrated bet on a forecast: S&P 500 plus Nasdaq, semiconductors and Taiwan, chosen on a conviction about AI. Wharton says the goal isn't to pick what will perform, and the diversification is weak. From 2031, half the engine is locked as the floor. But the top is "the expected value" of the other half, while the gift can draw only part of that half because a quarter of the engine is kept back, so the top doesn't match what can actually be given. No return, inflation or confidence assumptions are stated, and a smaller 2028 deposit isn't considered.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a face-value-$50,000 ladder costs about $300,000. The satellite rule (capped at 30% in total, each at 10%, trimmed above 15%). How the top of the range relates to the at-least-25% that is kept.

## My overall view
Every entry here buys a Treasury ladder for the ten payments, so having one didn't separate anyone. What separated them was what the team believed was left over. Most of these plans claim the ladder leaves "only default risk". It doesn't: coupons and fund distributions received before each payment have to be reinvested, and the headroom under $300,000 is thin. Entries 3 and 5 were the only ones to see that hole, price it, and say who pays if it opens, and they were the only ones to make sure every dollar named to co-sponsors is already in bonds when it's named. That is the actuary's standard, and they meet it. Entry 2 has the deepest risk thinking but builds a complicated engine and then leaves its range confidence ill-defined. Entry 1 is the safe all-rounder. To win, the Entry 3 plan would need to show it isn't buying certainty by starving Laura of growth and flexibility. It should say plainly how large the stock sleeve and the kept money are, why that balance suits a client who takes thoughtful risks, how facility inflation is absorbed, and what Laura still has to spend on overruns after 2033 once the reinvestment reserve is drawn.
