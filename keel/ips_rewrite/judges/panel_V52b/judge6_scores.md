# Judge J6 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6: the only entry that states its rules, trade-offs and 2031 confidence together, and answers all five IPS questions.
2. Entry 3: the sharpest client insight (her 2028 deposit moves with the same markets) and the most original thinking, but the 2031 mechanics read tangled.
3. Entry 4: near-identical to Entry 3; clearer on the size of the growth fund and on the downside, but with no inflation point.
4. Entry 2: clean principles and the only entry to handle inflation directly; thinner on the path to 2033 and on numbers.
5. Entry 1: sound, diversified and complete at a glance, but few assumptions are tested and the 2031 logic stays broad.
6. Entry 5: clear lock-then-launch central idea, but almost no numbers, and a thematic US-tech and Taiwan growth sleeve that sits awkwardly with the balance and diversification Wharton asks for.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is easy to follow in one read. The promise is dated, matched to Treasury maturities and held to maturity, and the growth engine is broad, with a stated rebalancing rule and stated return assumptions (6-7% stocks, 4-5% Treasuries, 2.5% inflation). It touches the five IPS questions at headline level, including the reserve running down by 2033 and a flexibility share. It holds back on depth: the 2031 top of the range is described only as a "modelled confidence level", the good, average and severe market tests are named but never shown, and the 2028 deposit's dependence on Laura's own income is not mentioned. Articulation shows little of the team's research or learning, though that may be the format's limit.
- Did not understand: what the 2031 "modelled confidence level" is, and whether the top of the range is a dollar figure anywhere in the IPS.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The most explicit about principles: it names two, defines "highly certain" precisely (held to maturity, no forecast needed), and is the only entry that deals with inflation directly, placing facility-cost risk with the growth fund. The minimum is locked by 2031 at a stated near-certain level, and the VT holding is kept untouched to 2033 with a flexibility share left with Laura after that, which together cover balance and flexibility. The path is thinner. The IPS says VT is "held" until 2033 but not how its weight or its role changes as 2033 nears, the share of VT that sets the top of the range is never given as a figure, and the "why this fits Laura" paragraph is short. The tone is plain and the arguments are easy to check.
- Did not understand: how the "share of VT's value in 2031" is set, and whether anything happens to VT between 2031 and 2033 beyond waiting.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strongest client reading in the set: it sees that Laura's 2028 deposit comes from publishing, speaking and licensing income that falls in the same downturns that would hurt stocks, and it builds the plan so the cheques never depend on that income. The "capital-protected note" framing explains a floor-plus-upside structure in plain terms, and the entry names its own weak point (reinvestment risk) and says what gives way when it bites. The cost is the 2031 paragraph: the top of the range seems to be locked into Treasuries and also to be "half her stocks valued that day", and the reinvestment shortfall is taken off the bottom, so I had to reread it to work out what Laura can actually promise. The name and the pharmacist analogy are cleverness that adds nothing to the strategy, and I did not score them. Numbers are sparse (one 7.5% return assumption, no outcomes).
- Did not understand: whether the half of the stocks that backs the top of the range is in Treasuries or still in stocks, and how the 2033 gift is set between floor and top.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is nearly identical to Entry 3 in pitch and most of the IPS, so I judged the differences. It is clearer on proportion: the growth fund is about a fifteenth of her money, "small on purpose", and it says plainly that if world shares earn less only the gift shrinks, not the payments or the floor. The reinvestment shortfall is handled by lowering the whole range, which is a simpler rule than Entry 3's. It keeps the same client insight about the 2028 deposit and the same clear order of priorities (cheques, then floor, then gift). It says nothing about inflation or the cost of the facility rising, which Wharton's guide asks teams to address, and the 2031 mechanics have the same tangle as Entry 3.
- Did not understand: how far the whole range drops when the reinvestment shortfall is large, and whether the 2033 gift sits at the floor, the top or somewhere in between.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The central idea is stated at the start and is clear: the commitment needs certainty and the facility money needs returns, so separating them from day one is a real strategy choice. The ladder is built from individual Treasuries held to maturity, with coupons parked in short Treasuries, and the 2031 move (half the growth engine into a late-2032 Treasury, with the top set from the remaining equity) is easy to follow. The growth sleeve, though, is a conviction bet on AI, semiconductors and Taiwan, with QQQ, SMH and VOO overlapping heavily and EWT adding single-country exposure to the same place as the residency. That is hard to square with the "appropriate diversification" and balance Wharton asks for. The entry gives almost no numbers: no return assumption, no reinvestment figure, no view on the range and the "expected value of the remaining equity" is never stated.
- Did not understand: whether the 15% trim on any single satellite ever applies when each satellite is weighted at 10%.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The most complete answer to Wharton's questions: a central idea ("Floor = Promise"), three named buckets with rules, a reserve that holds no stocks and gets safer each January, and a planned 2031 move and 2033 contribution. Its portfolio analysis is the strongest here: stated return and volatility assumptions, a 10,000-path simulation behind the range, a stated confidence level for the floor and for the top, and a gap-risk trade-off that explains why the multiplier is three and not five. It also names its trade-offs openly (the cash-lock that gives up a recovery, the 2028 deposit's dependence on Laura's income, the currency point) and says why it accepts them, which is what Wharton's guide asks judges to look for. The weaknesses are that the CPPI mechanics are dense for a reader who does not already know the method, the "at least 95% confidence" is asserted rather than shown, and the team's own process is barely visible.
- Did not understand: how the floor accrues and then switches to the present value of the minimum in 2031. I followed the idea but would want a worked example.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether an entry showed the reasoning and trade-offs behind its promise, not how well it was written. Entry 6 did that with numbers and named costs, so a reader can test it. Entries 3 and 4 had the best client thinking and the most original argument, but made me reconstruct the 2031 and 2033 mechanics myself, which costs marks under Portfolio Analysis and Creativity. Entries 1 and 2 had sound structures with few assumptions to test, and Entry 5 had a clear idea that was not backed by numbers and leaned on a concentrated growth bet. To win, the best entry (6) would need a worked example of the floor and the 2033 contribution in dollars, a plainer account of how the floor accrues, and the 2028 income link stated as a named risk with its effect on the range.
