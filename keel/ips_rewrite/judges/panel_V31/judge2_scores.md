### Entry 1

- The payments are secured properly with a held-to-maturity Treasury ladder, and the plan recognises that Laura's 2028 deposit comes from income that could shrink in a downturn. That is good client insight. But the facility contribution is built as a capital-protected note: a large Treasury floor with only a thin slice of stock, and then half of even that slice is moved into the first payment's fund the day the range is named. In practice Laura's growth sleeve ends up almost entirely bonds, for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks, with six or more years and her living costs covered elsewhere. The reasoning is careful, but it protects against a danger that is already mostly protected against.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most intellectually complete entry. It answers the "stocks beat bonds" objection directly. It names the specific condition under which the payments would fail (yields drop sharply and the 2028 deposit more than halves), which is a concrete and credible way to define "high certainty". It also states the odds of reaching the top of the range (about three in four), and why a range set too high is worse than one set too low. It also keeps the stocks until 2033, so the upside is not given up early. My criticism is the same as for the whole family: most of the facility money sits in a floor, so the growth engine is small and the range is correspondingly narrow. A client who takes thoughtful risks would be better served by more stock exposure. I mark it down for that, but it is honest about the trade, and I will not pretend the other entries are more honest.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Why the floor is described as "the fund that pays the first cheque" and how that sizes to the whole remainder by 2032; it took a second read. Also unclear how large the stock position ends up in dollars.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A clean, readable plan. It buys the ten payments with a Treasury and bond-fund ladder, defines high certainty clearly ("it does not depend on any forecast"), then splits the 2028 deposit between a late-2032 Treasury, which sets the minimum, and a single global stock fund held untouched. It gives realistic return assumptions and says openly that -30% years are possible. Its weaknesses are that it does not say how the 2028 deposit is split, so the reader cannot tell whether the growth part is large or token, and that it promises to state the odds of reaching the top "in the Final Report". Even so, the stock position is more plainly a growth engine than in Entries 1-3, and the plan's logic is easy to follow and defend. Laura could be confident reading this.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How big the floor is compared with the stock fund; the reader is left to guess.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 4/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 4/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The early 70% stock allocation is the right instinct for a long horizon, and I like that. But the plan is a standard target-date fund aimed at this client. It does not recognise that the ten payments are fixed dated liabilities that can be matched, and it defines "high certainty" as surviving the historical stress periods tested, which is not what the case asks for: the portfolio could still miss a payment in a scenario not tested. It also de-risks the whole portfolio, including the facility money, down to 30% stock by 2033, which cuts the growth the facility contribution depends on just when the money is about to be used. The reserve is held 30% stocks to 2040, which puts payments at market risk. A client who must pay fixed dollars with a high degree of certainty would not be reassured. The plan is simple, but it is simple in the wrong place.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How the reserve at "110% of the present value" is computed and with which discount rate; it is not stated.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the plan I would want to see. It secures the ten payments first with Treasuries maturing before each date, defines high certainty plainly, and then puts everything above the ladder into a real growth portfolio (about 80% global stocks, 20% intermediate Treasuries) instead of tying most of it up in a floor. It is also sensible about when to protect the facility money: only in 2031, once Laura names the range, and only the amount she is promising. The facility rule (locked floor plus at most half the remaining growth money, kept below the top of the range) is clear, and the rest stays as flexibility, which fits the case. The weak points are that the figures stay qualitative (no stated odds of reaching the top of the range, no numbers for good and bad markets), and the line that rate swings "never touch" the payments slightly overstates it, since coupons reinvested at lower yields are a real, small risk. But the balance between risk and protection is the one this client asked for.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: None. The only gap is how much money lands in the growth engine versus the ladder, which I worked out from the contributions.

## My overall view
What separated the better entries from the rest was whether Laura's money was left with a real chance to grow. All five liability-matching entries secure the ten payments sensibly, and I do not mark any of them down for that. The difference comes after. Entries 1 to 3 build the facility gift as a capital-protected note: a big Treasury floor, a thin slice of stock, and in Entries 1 and 3 half of that slice locked as soon as the range is named. That is careful and honest in places (Entry 2 is the best written of them), but it protects against a risk the case does not ask her to avoid, and it leaves a risk-taking client with a nearly-bond portfolio and a narrow range. Entry 5 grows early but never really secures the payments. Entry 6 puts the payments in a ladder and the rest of her money in stocks, and only locks the part she announces. To win, Entry 6 would need to put numbers on its good, average and bad markets, say how big the growth sleeve is, state the odds of reaching the top of the range, and acknowledge that a coupon-paying ladder has a small reinvestment gap. Entry 2's candour about what its plan can and cannot do is the quality I would most like to see added to it.
