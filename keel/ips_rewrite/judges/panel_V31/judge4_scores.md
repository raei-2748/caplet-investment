# Judge J4 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - plain, honest, complete: promise bought first, growth on top, a clear 2031 lock, reads like students who did the work.
2. Entry 5 - the deepest thinking of the set (income-risk of the 2028 deposit, floor plus stocks, lock after naming), with a candid admission of a failed first plan; slightly too polished and neat.
3. Entry 6 - simple, credible, well fitted to the case, honest about -30% years; thin on flexibility and on how the 2031 range is sized.
4. Entry 4 - strong reasoning but crowded and over-engineered, with several mechanisms stacked; the most "built" of the set.
5. Entry 1 - sophisticated and complete, but brochure-like in voice, leaning on a conceit and a repeated refrain; asks a lot of a reader to believe teenagers wrote it.
6. Entry 2 - a standard target-date idea; sound and readable, but it does not deliver the high certainty the case demands.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8, Client Knowledge 8, Portfolio Analysis 7, Articulation 5, Creativity & Presentation 6, Overall 7
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The thinking is real: ten dated debts matched by Treasuries, a floor plus stocks for the facility gift, and a range built around the cost of overpromising versus underpromising. It deals with the main risks (coupon reinvestment, inflation, the Taiwan dollar) and says where the plan breaks. What holds it back, for me, is the voice. It is clever and rhetorical in a way that reads like a pitch deck, and the pharmacist conceit and repeated refrain feel like packaging rather than a team talking. The mechanism (the capital-protected note with the bank taken out, "sized to grow back") is sophisticated and a little hard to follow cold. I believe the ideas, I am less sure whose they are.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor, the fund that pays the first cheque, and "half the stocks" fit together in practice; and why the plan changes once in 2033 to set aside a coupon gap.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5, Client Knowledge 5, Portfolio Analysis 5, Articulation 6, Creativity & Presentation 5, Overall 5
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- This is the most obviously student-written entry and it is clear and organised. The glide path is a recognisable idea, and the stress-test definition of certainty is at least stated. But it does not meet the case: the fixed $50,000 payments need high certainty, and holding 30% stocks in the reserve, tested on past crashes rather than matched to the payments, is not that. It also treats the 2031 range as something to be worked out later. A glide path for a retirement does not recognise that Laura's payments are ten dated debts. Honest and tidy, but generic.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a 110% cushion and two years of cash amount to high certainty when stocks are still in the reserve; and why the 2000-2002 and 2008 tests cover payments running to 2042.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8, Client Knowledge 8, Portfolio Analysis 7, Articulation 6, Creativity & Presentation 7, Overall 8
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This reads like a team that understands the case. It separates the promise from the vision, buys a Treasury ladder first, defines high certainty in plain words, and states the one residual risk, a US default. The "two-thirds" cost check is right and it covers the 2028 gap. The 80/20 growth sleeve, 2031 lock of the range floor, the 2033 handover to the reserve and the cap on the contribution all line up, so the strategy changes as needs approach. Weaker spots: the claim that stocks are the best defence against Taiwan construction costs is thin, and it misses that the 2028 deposit is itself income-dependent. Still, it is cohesive, honest about assumptions and written in a believable student voice.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none really; the 2031 lock is described briefly and I would want to know what happens if stocks have fallen by then.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8, Client Knowledge 8, Portfolio Analysis 7, Articulation 6, Creativity & Presentation 6, Overall 7
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Real reasoning again, and a good admission that the first plan, three-quarters stocks, failed in the model. The thought about ten independent bets versus ten debts of one government is a genuine insight, and so is the observation that a capped range keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. But too much is stacked in 500 words: a floor, a sized fund, a 2031 lock, a coupon settlement, a quoted bottom adjustment, a 2033 top-up. A reader has to hold a lot in their head, and the voice is again very finished and slogan-like ("each promise is made safe no later than the day it becomes one"). It is thoughtful but it reads more like a product spec than a team explaining itself.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the sequence of what moves into the first cheque's fund on which date, and how anything the cushion can't cover "comes off the quoted bottom".

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8, Client Knowledge 9, Portfolio Analysis 7, Articulation 7, Creativity & Presentation 7, Overall 8
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The best client reasoning of the six: Laura's career is already a stock, so her 2028 deposit never carries a cheque; the ladder is bought first; the gift sits on a floor with stocks on top; the range is locked once named. It explains the price of the approach ("a smaller boom") and admits the failed first plan, which is the honesty I look for. The ladder-versus-$500,000 arithmetic is clear. It is a little too neat, with headings and refrains that sound polished, and the mechanism of the floor takes careful reading. But the argument is easy to follow and consistent from the pitch to the end.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the "cushion" is, and what exactly "the promised half moves into Treasuries" means in dollars.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7, Client Knowledge 7, Portfolio Analysis 6, Articulation 6, Creativity & Presentation 6, Overall 7
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, plain-spoken plan: a ladder matched to each payment, using iBond ETFs for the early years and individual bonds for the later ones, with the 2028 deposit as the gap filler and then a floor plus VT. The definition of certainty is clear ("we are removing the market from the question") and it admits that stocks can fall 30% or more. It reads like students who did the work. What holds it back is that the facility side is thin: how the 2031 range is sized, how much of VT stays with her, and how the plan reacts if things change are left vague, and it never notices that the 2028 deposit depends on her income. Sound, but less distinctive.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big "a share" of VT is in the range, and how a floor bought in 2028 is described as already "locked" in 2031.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the team treated the ten payments as ten dated debts and then thought honestly about the other half of the case, the facility gift and the 2031 range, rather than just bolting stocks on. Entries 3 and 5 do both. Entry 5 has the deepest understanding of Laura, but I trust Entry 3 slightly more because it sounds like students explaining what they decided and why. Entries 1 and 4 have the same deep ideas but wrap them in a polish that makes me doubt who wrote them, and they are crowded. Entry 2 is the only one that does not secure the payments, so it falls behind. The best entry would need to keep Entry 3's plain voice and add Entry 5's insight about the 2028 deposit, plus a clearer statement of what happens to the 2031 range if markets have fallen.
