INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them.

Caplet XR in one rule: each promise is made safe no later than the day it becomes one.

The cheques are promises from page one: $50,000 every January, 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars. So her first $300,000 buys them in January 2027: Treasury bonds and funds that turn to cash before each January. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush. To us, X is ten and R is redemption: ten holdings, each redeemed in time for one cheque.

Our first plan, three-quarters stocks, missed cheques in the model. A fixed-percentage withdrawal can ride out a crash; a fixed-dollar withdrawal is a forced sale at the bottom. We spread the stocks across the world, but not the cheques: Ten independent bets, each 99% safe, would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one.

And one reason only Laura has: her 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – income that can shrink in the same slump that makes bonds dearer. So money she has buys certainty; money she expects carries risk.

That deposit fills any gap in the ladder, then buys the floor – more of the first cheque's fund – sized to regrow what's left by 2033 and backing the cheques until she names a range. The floor's expected interest, about one dollar in fifteen, buys a world index fund. Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque is worse off – only the gift.

Her 2031 range can embarrass her twice over. Too low, and co-sponsors give less – bigger lead gifts draw bigger gifts. Too high, and she breaks her word, which we judge three times worse. So the bottom is the floor she owns, rounded down; the top adds half the stocks, and the other half stays hers.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once named, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into the first cheque's fund.

Our one soft spot is coupons reinvested as rates fall, so it is settled in 2031 too: the gap today's yields imply, plus a cushion, moves from spare cash and her half into the first cheque's fund, and anything they can't cover comes off the quoted bottom. In 2033 the reserve tops up once, then never changes.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less; it cannot make $50,000 cost more. It and the Taiwan dollar can hurt the building – that is what the rest of Laura's half, sellable any day, is for. If needs change, cheques first; floor and stocks are bought smaller together.

No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush. The residency gets ten Januaries it can count on, and a building promised only from Treasuries she owns.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
Laura has made her residency one promise and has one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone. The hope is a gift for the building, big enough to convince co-sponsors it is real. The two need different kinds of money, so Caplet XR keeps them apart.

One dose, every January

Pharmacists call a pill that releases the same dose every hour, however much is left, extended release – XR. Laura's cheques are like that: $50,000 is due after a good year or a dreadful one. Stocks are terrible at this. An endowment spending a fixed percentage can sit through a crash, but anyone owing a fixed amount must sell at the bottom. (We learned this the hard way: our first plan, three-quarters stocks, missed cheques in our model.)

So her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques in January 2027: a ladder of Treasury bonds and iBond Treasury funds, each maturing before its January. That's our XR, too – X for ten, R for redemption. The cheques add up to $500,000 but cost about $300,000; the government's interest pays the rest, provided nobody sells early.

A hope, with a floor

Laura's career is already a stock. Her 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – exactly the income a recession shrinks – so it never carries a cheque. It first plugs any gap in the ladder; the rest builds her gift.

Most of it buys her floor: more of the fund paying the first cheque, sized to grow back to the whole amount by 2033. The interest it should earn, about a fifteenth of everything she owns, buys a world index fund. It is a bank's capital-protected note with the bank, and its fee, taken out. Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque loses a cent. The price is a smaller boom.

Say it, then lock it

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range: low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws big gifts after it. So the bottom is the floor she owns, and the top adds half her stocks.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once it's named, any fall in stocks comes off the gift, and any rise past the top goes to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries, with a cushion for the one thing they can't promise: what reinvested coupons will earn. Every number she says aloud is already owned.

Do not crush

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Nothing is sold before it pays; each turns to cash the December before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more. Taiwan's costs and currency threaten the building instead – that is what the other half of her stocks is for. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope.

ELEVATOR PITCH (max 50 words):
Laura's ten $50,000 payments are a promise, so we buy the promise first: a ladder of US Treasuries, each maturing just before a payment, held to the end. Only money left over is invested for growth in one global stock fund, giving co-sponsors a guaranteed minimum and an honest upside.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
What we are trying to do
Laura has an obligation and an ambition. The obligation is ten fixed $50,000 payments, 2033 to 2042, funded with high certainty and no outside help. The ambition is a facility contribution big enough to bring co-sponsors in. We secure the obligation first, at today's prices, and only then pursue growth.

Principle 1: Match each payment to a maturity
In January 2027 Laura's $300,000 buys a ladder of ten rungs. Payments due 2033-2036 are matched with iShares iBonds Treasury ETFs maturing the December before; they hold only Treasuries and pay out at maturity. Payments due 2037-2042 are matched with individual Treasury bonds maturing a few months earlier. With 2027 Treasury yields around 4-5%, $500,000 of future payments costs a little under $300,000 today. If the first deposit falls short, the gap is the first claim on the 2028 deposit.

Our definition of high certainty
A payment is "highly certain" when it does not depend on any forecast. Held to maturity, every rung pays its known value whatever happens to stocks, interest rates or inflation. The only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution, which we control by checking every maturity date. We are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question.

Principle 2: One growth engine, left alone
After any gap is filled, the 2028 deposit is split in two. One part buys a Treasury maturing in late 2032; this sets a minimum facility contribution Laura can promise in 2031 with near-certainty. The rest goes into Vanguard Total World Stock (VT): thousands of companies worldwide for under 0.1% a year. We hold it untouched until 2033: over six years, cost and discipline beat prediction. We assume roughly 6-7% a year with years of -30% or worse possible.

How the portfolio changes as 2033 approaches
In 2033 the ladder becomes the operating reserve: ten rungs for ten payments, one maturing each year, with any cash awaiting a payment held in short-term Treasuries. The reserve shortens by itself, as a shrinking obligation should, and nothing is ever sold to meet a payment.

The 2031 range and the facility
Laura's range runs from the minimum already locked in Treasuries to that minimum plus a share of VT's value in 2031. The bottom is close to guaranteed; the top depends on markets, and we will state its likelihood in the Final Report. Good markets raise the contribution; bad markets can only shrink the upside, never the payments or the minimum.

Flexibility
Part of the VT holding stays with her after 2033, invested, as a buffer for overruns and new ideas.

Inflation
The payments are fixed in dollars, so a nominal ladder matches them exactly. Facility costs will rise with inflation; that risk sits with the growth fund, the asset best placed to outpace it.

Why this fits Laura
She takes risks with ideas, not with promises to other people. This strategy lets her do both.

Glide Path 2033: a target-date strategy built for one date. Laura's portfolio starts at 70% stocks to grow while time is on her side, then glides to 30% by January 2033. Risk falls as her ten $50,000 payments approach, and keeps falling as they are paid.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
Central idea
Laura's goals have a date on them. The first $50,000 payment is due in January 2033 and the last in January 2042. Target-date funds solve exactly this problem: take more risk when the deadline is far away, and less as it gets close. We built Laura one aimed at her residency, not a retirement.

Why this fits Laura
Laura has six years with no withdrawals and her living costs are covered outside the portfolio, so early on she can afford stock market swings. She takes thoughtful risks but asked for a balance between growth and protection. A glide path gives her both, at different times, and it removes emotion from the decision: the schedule tells us when to reduce risk, not the headlines.

The glide path (stocks / Treasuries and cash)
2027-2028: 70 / 30
2029: 65 / 35
2030: 55 / 45
2031: 45 / 55
2032: 35 / 65
January 2033: 30 / 70
Stocks are held through VT (total world) with a tilt to VTI, so about 60% of the stock portion is U.S. and 40% international. Treasuries are held through IEF, SHY and individual Treasury notes; cash through SGOV. The 2028 contribution is invested straight into the current target mix. Each January we rebalance to the new step; between steps we rebalance if the mix drifts more than five points.

The operating reserve (2033-2042)
In January 2033 we split the portfolio. The operating reserve is sized at 110% of the present value of the ten payments, so it carries a cushion for bad years. It keeps the balanced character of the glide path: 30% stocks and 70% Treasuries, with the next two payments always held in SGOV. The reserve keeps gliding, cutting stocks by about four points a year, so by 2040 it is entirely Treasuries and cash. A modest stock share keeps the reserve earning.

What "high certainty" means
We modelled Laura's $450,000 growing over the six years to 2033 using conservative returns (6% stocks, 3.5% Treasuries) and tested the glide path through the worst historical periods for a 30/70 portfolio, including 2000-2002 and 2008. In each one the reserve still covered every payment, because the 10% cushion and two years of cash meant no stocks had to be sold in a crash. We define high certainty as funding all ten payments in every historical stress period we tested.

Facility contribution and flexibility
Everything outside the reserve is the facility pool. Laura should contribute up to 80% of it and keep 20% invested 30/70 as flexibility for overruns, delays or her next idea. Because the glide path has already cut stocks to 45% by 2031, her 2033 portfolio is far more predictable when she talks to co-sponsors: a 20% stock market fall in 2031-2032 would reduce the portfolio by less than 10%. We will build the 2031 range from the glide path's low and expected scenarios in the Final Report.

Discipline
Low-cost ETFs and Treasuries only. No single stocks and no market timing.

ELEVATOR PITCH (max 50 words):
Promise First, Then Grow: we lock in Laura's commitment before chasing her vision. U.S. Treasury bonds maturing as each $50,000 payment falls due secure all ten residency payments; everything else grows in diversified global stocks, so her facility contribution rises with markets while her promise never depends on them.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
Objective. Laura's portfolio has two jobs of very different kinds. The first is a promise: ten fixed $50,000 payments, 2033 to 2042, funded with a high degree of certainty. The second is a vision: the largest facility contribution she can make responsibly while keeping flexibility. Our strategy never lets the second job put the first at risk. We fund the promise first, then let everything else grow.

Why this fits Laura. Laura is an entrepreneur who takes thoughtful risks, but her residency's credibility depends on keeping her word to collaborators and co-sponsors. Because the payments are fixed in dollars and dated exactly, they can be matched almost perfectly with U.S. Treasury bonds. Gambling money already spoken for would be risk without reward.

The Commitment Ladder. In 2027 we buy individual Treasury bonds maturing just before each payment date from 2033 to 2042, sized so principal plus coupons covers every $50,000 payment. At current yields this costs roughly two-thirds of Laura's total contributions; if the first deposit falls short, the 2028 deposit completes the ladder before anything else. Coupons received before they are needed are held in short-term Treasuries. We define "high certainty" as every payment backed by a Treasury maturity on or before its due date, so the only remaining risk is a U.S. government default. Because we hold every bond to maturity, rate swings never touch the payments.

The Growth Engine. Everything above the ladder is invested for growth through low-cost, broadly diversified ETFs: roughly 80% global stocks (U.S. and international) and 20% intermediate Treasuries as a shock absorber. With a five-year horizon to 2033 and the payments already secured, Laura can afford equity volatility here, and stocks are her best defence against rising construction costs in Taiwan. We rebalance once a year, only when weights drift more than five percentage points.

Planned changes as needs approach.
- *2031:* Before Laura speaks to co-sponsors, we move the bottom of her announced range into Treasuries maturing just before 2033. That floor is then locked; she can never be forced to break it. The top of the range reflects the remaining stocks with a stated, modelled confidence level, so she promises only what she can defend.
- *2033:* The ladder becomes the operating reserve with no rebalancing required. Each year one rung matures and pays one $50,000 payment, so the reserve shortens and self-liquidates as payments are made.
- *Facility contribution:* Laura contributes the locked floor plus at most half of the remaining growth portfolio, capped at the top of her announced range. The rest stays invested as her flexibility fund for overruns, delays or new opportunities.

Risk and assumptions. We assume long-run returns near 6–7% a year for global stocks and 4–5% for Treasuries, 2.5% inflation, and no outside money for the payments. We test outcomes in good, average and severe markets; a crash shrinks only the facility contribution, never the payments. Each January we review the plan against Laura's needs.
