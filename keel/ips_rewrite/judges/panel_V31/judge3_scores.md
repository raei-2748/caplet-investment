# Judge J3 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4: buys the ten payments outright, defines high certainty by naming the exact stress that breaks it, and prices the 2031 range against an owned floor. One residual gap is sized only loosely.
2. Entry 2: the same matched-liability design, and the most complete on residual risk (the reinvestment gap is settled before the range is quoted, with a fallback), but its reasoning is compressed and harder to follow.
3. Entry 3: the same design, explained most clearly for a lay reader, but it never formally defines high certainty, and it is unclear whose reinvestment risk the cushion covers.
4. Entry 5: a conventional, sound ladder-plus-growth plan with stated assumptions and a clear 2031/2033 path. It overclaims that rates "never touch the payments" while it reinvests coupons in bills.
5. Entry 6: a clean ladder and an honest definition of certainty, but it leaves the deposit split and the flexibility size unspecified and treats fund payouts as fixed.
6. Entry 1: a glide path that never matches the liabilities. The payments are exposed to markets until 2033 and to sequence risk after it, and certainty rests on a handful of historical periods.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It answers "how will the allocation change as needs approach" more directly than anyone, with an explicit schedule and rebalancing rules, and it reads clearly. The problem is that the ten fixed-dollar payments are never secured. From 2027 to 2032 the money for them sits in a 70-to-35% stock portfolio, so a bad first few years shrinks the pot that has to fund the reserve, and nothing in the plan says what happens if the 2033 portfolio cannot cover 110% of the payments' present value. The reserve then keeps 30% stocks against fixed-dollar outflows, which is exactly the sequence-risk exposure a liability-matcher avoids. The 70% in Treasuries is held through funds and notes with no maturity matching, so it also carries rate risk. "High certainty" means surviving the historical windows they chose to test, which is a small, backward-looking sample, and the test seems to cover only the reserve phase, not the 70%-stock accumulation phase where the real risk sits. The 2031 range is deferred entirely, and the 80/20 facility split is asserted, not derived.
- Not understood on first reading: the discount rate behind "110% of the present value of the ten payments" (at a risk-free rate or at the portfolio's expected return?), which decides whether the cushion is real.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This team understands the liability. The first deposit buys a maturity-matched Treasury ladder, the second fills any gap before anything else, and the leftover surplus is built as a floor plus a small equity sleeve. The floor keeps backing the payments until the range is named, a genuinely actuarial touch. It is the only entry that prices the residual reinvestment risk fully: the gap is settled before the range is quoted, from named sources, and "anything they can't cover comes off the quoted bottom". So the range can never be bought at the expense of the payments. Locking the promised half once it is named removes the asymmetric risk to the gift between 2031 and 2033. The client reasoning is sharp: the 2028 deposit is pro-cyclical income, and fixed-dollar outflows cannot ride out a crash. What holds it back is balance. Equity is roughly a fifteenth of the money, so "thoughtful risks" and growth get little room, and Laura's flexibility after the gift (half a small stock sleeve plus spare cash) looks thin against "committing all remaining assets could limit her flexibility". The argument is also compressed enough that a judge has to reconstruct several steps, and high certainty is never stated as a definition.
- Not understood on first reading: the "ten independent bets, each 99% safe" argument (I had to work out that it is about concentration in one issuer, not about diversification helping), and where the "three times worse" weighting comes from.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It has the same sound architecture as Entries 2 and 4: payments bought first with a matched ladder, the pro-cyclical 2028 deposit kept off the payments, a floor plus an equity sleeve for the gift, and the promised amount locked once named. It is the easiest of the three to follow. It explains why $500,000 of payments costs about $300,000, why fixed-dollar outflows make stocks unsuitable, and why the reserve runs itself down. Where it falls short for me is the evidence on funding reliability. It never defines high certainty or says how it was judged, and "every number she says aloud is already owned" asserts the safety rather than testing it. The reinvestment-coupon cushion appears only in the gift-locking step, so I cannot tell whether the ladder's own reinvestment risk on the long rungs is covered. The same thin-growth and thin-flexibility concerns as Entry 2 apply.
- Not understood on first reading: whether "a cushion for ... what reinvested coupons will earn" protects the operating payments or only the announced gift.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- It is the only entry that does what an actuary would ask first: it defines high certainty by naming the joint stress that breaks it, a large fall in yields before purchase combined with a halved 2028 deposit. That is a funding test with a stated failure point, not a slogan. It meets the obvious objection head on ("ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill"), ties the 2028 deposit's correlation with markets to the order of purchases, and gives the range a stated likelihood of reaching its top. The gift structure is coherent: an owned floor, a small equity sleeve, and a bottom that is close to guaranteed. Its one hand-wave is the reinvestment gap on the long bonds. It is called "small" and covered from Laura's half in 2033, but the plan never shows that her half is big enough in the scenario where rates and stocks both fall, nor what gives way if it is not. Like its siblings, it leaves little room for growth and modest flexibility, and it shows little of the team's process or learning.
- Not understood on first reading: none, beyond wanting the size of the "small gap" set against the size of "Laura's half".

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is a sound, complete, conventional plan. Every IPS question has an answer: a matched ladder first, an 80/20 diversified growth sleeve, a 2031 floor moved into Treasuries before the range is quoted, a contribution rule capped at the top of the range, an explicit flexibility fund, stated return and inflation assumptions, and an annual review. It gives Laura more growth and more diversification than Entries 2-4, which fits her "thoughtful risks". My problem is the funding claim. It sizes the ladder on principal plus coupons and parks early coupons in short-term Treasuries, then says the only remaining risk is default and that rate swings never touch the payments. That is untrue, because the bill rate on those coupons is unknown, and it is exactly the unpriced hole I look for. It does not see that the 2028 deposit may shrink in the same downturn, and the "good, average and severe" testing is mentioned without saying what it showed.
- Not understood on first reading: none.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure is right, and its definition of high certainty (a payment that depends on no forecast) is honest and easy to defend. The ladder's mechanics are explained concretely, including how the deposit closes any gap, and the 2031 range follows logically from a Treasury-backed minimum. But key quantities are left open. How the 2028 deposit is split between the minimum and VT, what "a share of VT" in the range means, and how much stays with Laura for flexibility are all unstated, so the facility and flexibility trade-off is not really decided. It also says every rung "pays its known value whatever happens to ... interest rates". For the bond funds and for reinvested coupons that is not strictly true, and the plan does not price it. It does not notice the 2028 deposit's link to the economy.
- Not understood on first reading: whether the individual long bonds are sized on face value alone (coupons as surplus) or on face value plus coupons; the answer decides whether reinvestment risk exists.

## My overall view
For me the field split on one question: did the team buy the ten payments, or forecast them? Entry 1 forecasts them, and that is the hole I expected to find. The other five buy them. Among those five, the difference was whether the team chased down the residual risks that remain once you own a Treasury ladder: reinvested coupons, a gap rung funded a year late by an income that falls in recessions, and a gift exposed to markets between naming it in 2031 and paying it in 2033. Entries 2 and 4 found all three. Entry 2 closed every one with a funded fallback. Entry 4 defined certainty by its breaking point, which is what the case asks for. To win, the best entry would combine the two: Entry 4's named stress test, plus Entry 2's rule that any uncovered gap comes off the quoted range, plus a sentence showing the leftover flexibility is large enough to matter. It should also make the case that a liability-matched design still leaves Laura room for the "thoughtful risks" she wants, since the low equity weight is the first thing a growth-minded judge will attack.
