# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Marking scheme: Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions only.
Method: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know; then I ran a full pairwise
tournament (15 pairs) on Wharton's criteria and ranked by wins.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 (5 wins) - the ladder-plus-floor strategy argued end to end: the sceptic answered, the failure condition of "high certainty" named, the range asymmetry priced; the most reasoning per word that a reader can still follow once.
2. Entry 2 (4 wins) - the same family of strategy with the richest decision-theoretic content (why not to diversify the liability assets, an explicit loss ratio for the range, the reinvestment gap settled in 2031), held back by density.
3. Entry 1 (3 wins) - the most readable of the three floor-and-ladder entries; carries the core ideas (human-capital correlation, capital-protected-note construction, lock on the day she names) with a little less argument behind them.
4. Entry 6 (2 wins) - clean ladder, a crisp forecast-free definition of certainty, and one real idea: buy the range's bottom in 2028, not 2031. Leaves sizing and reinvestment unsaid.
5. Entry 4 (1 win) - the textbook answer done well: complete, orderly, every Wharton question visibly answered, assumptions and scenarios stated. Nothing in it I did not already know.
6. Entry 5 (0 wins) - the one different idea (a glide path), but it is a blunter frame for this client's problem, not a sharper one; funding certainty rests on a backtest and the co-sponsor range is deferred.

Pairwise record: 3 beat 1, 2, 4, 5, 6. 2 beat 1, 4, 5, 6. 1 beat 4, 5, 6. 6 beat 4, 5. 4 beat 5.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the second deposit can be built as a capital-protected note with the issuer removed (a Treasury floor regrowing the whole remainder, only its expected interest at risk in stocks), and that Laura's own earnings are already equity-like, which is a client-specific reason to hold very little equity. Both are genuine ways of seeing the problem that go beyond the standard ladder. The strategy is disciplined across the horizons: buy the cheques, build a floor, lock the promised half when the range is announced, let the reserve run itself down. The trade-off is owned rather than hidden: a smaller boom in exchange for a gift that is almost entirely owned when it is spoken. What holds it back is that the argument is thinner than in the two sibling entries: the reinvestment problem is mentioned as a cushion but not explained, the equity share is so small that a reader may wonder whether Laura's "thoughtful risks" have anywhere to live, and the quantitative side is mostly gestured at (about $300,000, about a fifteenth). The pharmacy metaphor earns its keep as an organising device but is introduced before it is explained.
- Did not understand on first reading: what the floor is "sized to grow back to" (the remainder of the 2028 deposit, I eventually inferred); and whether the cushion for reinvested coupons comes from Laura's half or from the floor.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things I had not seen framed this way in a student IPS. First, that diversification is the wrong tool for the liability side: ten independent 99%-safe claims fail more often than ten claims on one issuer that fail together, so the cheques should be concentrated, not spread. Second, an explicit loss function for the 2031 range, with over-promising weighted roughly three times under-promising, which is exactly how a decision scientist would want the range set. The principle that money already owned buys certainty while expected money carries risk is a sharp reading of the client's two deposits. The reinvestment weakness is not only admitted but settled with a dated mechanism. What holds it back is the reading experience: the entry packs an idea into nearly every sentence, several mechanisms (gap, floor, lock, coupon top-up, Laura's half) are introduced in quick succession, and a judge reading once will lose the thread at least once. Wharton asks for a well-organised narrative; this is a well-organised argument compressed to the limit.
- Did not understand on first reading: what it means for the floor to be "backing the cheques" until the range is named (is the floor a second cushion for the ladder, or only the gift's minimum?); and what the 2033 one-off top-up of the reserve is funded from.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that "high degree of certainty" can be defined by naming the joint condition under which the plan breaks (a large fall in yields before the purchase together with a much smaller second deposit), which is more honest and more useful than quoting a probability; and that the common objection, stocks beat bonds over a decade, rests on treating ten one-year liabilities as one ten-year one. The entry takes the sceptic seriously, answers it, then builds the floor and the stock sleeve the same way as its siblings but with the cleanest explanation of what the floor regrows and when. The range logic is complete: an asymmetric loss, a bottom already owned, a top with a stated frequency of being reached, and a dated fix for the reinvestment gap. Client fit is strong throughout: the fixed-dollar nature of the cheques, the correlation of her earnings with markets, inflation's effect on the building rather than the payments, the Taiwan dollar. What holds it back: naming specific funds sits slightly against Wharton's request to focus on framework rather than individual investments; the equity share is small and the entry does not say in dollars what the gift might be, so a reader must trust the structure; and, as with its siblings, Laura's own appetite for risk is argued away rather than given a place.
- Did not understand on first reading: none of substance. The phrase about the cheques being questions for a contract rather than a forecast took a second look.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already know, which is the point. This is the strategy I expected most competent teams to reach, and it is executed well: a dated ladder with a clear definition of certainty, a diversified growth engine with a stated mix and a rebalancing rule, a 2031 lock of the range's bottom, a self-liquidating reserve, a contribution rule with a retained flexibility fund, stated return and inflation assumptions and scenario testing. Every Wharton question is answered in order, and a reader never loses the thread. It also gives Laura more genuine growth than the floor-based entries, which fits her stated willingness to take thoughtful risks. What holds it back is the absence of any idea that is the team's own, and a few places where the reasoning stops short: the floor is locked only in 2031, so its size depends on where markets are that year; the range's top is deferred to a confidence level to be modelled later; coupons are parked but the reinvestment risk is not sized; and the client paragraph could describe almost any careful client with a promise to keep. A good, safe entry that I would expect to sit near the edge of the Top 50 rather than inside it.
- Did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: that a team can borrow the target-date structure and aim it at 2033, and that a 110% reserve with two years of payments in cash and a continuing glide is their definition of certainty. The entry is honest about what it tested and states its return assumptions, and the glide-path table is easy to follow. But as a way of seeing the client's problem it is blunter, not sharper. Laura does not have one date; she has ten fixed-dollar dates and a credibility problem in 2031, and a reserve that still holds 30% stocks against those fixed payments turns "high certainty" into "survived the historical periods we chose." Letting the whole $450,000 ride at 70% stocks for two years also exposes the operating commitment itself to a bad start, and the schedule-driven de-risking will sell stocks after falls as readily as after rises, a cost the entry does not weigh. Client knowledge is thin: nothing on the fixed-dollar nature of the liability, inflation, Taiwan, or the correlation between her income and markets, and the range for co-sponsors, central to the case, is pushed to the Final Report. A coherent fund design, but for a different client.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the 10% cushion and the 30% stock allocation in the reserve are meant to coexist with the requirement for a high degree of certainty, since the cushion is roughly one bad year for the stock portion; and what happens to the glide if a crash arrives in 2027-28, before any de-risking has occurred.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: one thing worth having, that the bottom of the 2031 range can be bought in 2028 as a Treasury maturing in late 2032, so the minimum Laura will promise is owned five years before she promises it rather than locked at whatever price 2031 offers. The definition of certainty as "nothing depends on a forecast" is the cleanest in the set, and the entry's reading of Laura, risks with ideas rather than with promises to other people, is a client insight that fits the case. The ladder is well specified (iBond funds for the near rungs, individual bonds for the far ones), inflation is handled correctly, and the reserve runs itself down. What holds it back is what it leaves unsaid: how the 2028 deposit is split between the floor and the stock fund, what happens to the stock holding between 2031 and 2033 once a top has been announced, and how coupons on the long bonds are handled (the claim that every rung pays its known value is slightly stronger than the holdings support). The portfolio analysis is deliberately unquantified, which is a defensible stance but gives the judge little to evaluate under Wharton's third criterion.
- Did not understand on first reading: the sizing rule for the 2028 split, and whether "a share of VT's value in 2031" at the top of the range is protected in any way once announced.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reached what I regard as table stakes, a maturity-matched Treasury ladder for the ten payments with stocks for the rest, so the competition here was decided by what each team saw beyond it. Entries 1, 2 and 3 share one strategy and the same handful of ideas, and those ideas are the best in the set: the client's own earnings are already equity, so the second deposit should carry the risk and the first should not; the gift is best built as a capital-protected note with the issuer removed; a capped range becomes a one-sided bet the day it is named, so the promised part must be locked that day; and the one weakness of the plan, reinvested coupons, is named and dated rather than hidden. Entry 3 wins because it carries that argument with the least friction and defines certainty by its failure condition. Entries 6 and 4 are good professional work with one idea and none respectively. Entry 5 is the only genuinely different design, and I gave it credit for that, but it is a frame imported from retirement saving and it fits this client worse, not better. For the best entry to win outright it would need to give Laura's appetite for risk a place in the design instead of arguing it away, to say in rough dollars what the gift might be so the judge can weigh the trade-off rather than trust it, and to let the strategy stand with a little less reliance on the pharmacy metaphor to organise it.
