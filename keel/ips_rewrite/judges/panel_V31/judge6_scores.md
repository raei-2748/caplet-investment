## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the entry defines and tests "a high degree of funding certainty" for the ten cheques, and then tells the judge what happens to the facility gift once it is promised. Entries 2 to 6 all fund the cheques with Treasuries matched to their dates, which is the right answer to Wharton's first requirement. Entry 1 holds 70% stocks and still has 30% in stocks in 2033, so it answers a different question. Among the Treasury-ladder entries, Entry 6 is the most convincing on assumptions and the only one to put a number on the range; Entry 5 is the easiest to follow and locks the gift on naming day, but leaves the certainty claim undefined; Entry 4 has the sharpest reasoning but needs a second read; Entry 2 is clean but thin on evidence. To win, the best entry would need Entry 6's certainty definition and stated confidence, Entry 5's lock rule in plain headed prose, and a stated facility formula with the 2028 deposit split given. A note on the set itself: Entries 4 and 6 open with the same sentence, and Entries 4, 5 and 6 share one architecture (Caplet XR, a Treasury floor, half the stocks, a lock on naming). I judged each on its own, but a programme manager would want that known before the entries are compared side by side, since Wharton's creativity criterion asks for an authentic team voice.
