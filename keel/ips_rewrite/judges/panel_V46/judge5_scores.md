## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already expect, which is the problem. The plan is exactly my prior: individual Treasuries matched to the ten payments, the rest in a diversified 80/20 growth sleeve, a floor locked into Treasuries in 2031, a half-the-upside rule for the gift and the remainder kept as a flexibility fund. Everything Wharton asks for is present and in order: a definition of high certainty (a Treasury maturity on or before each date, residual risk is federal default), how the reserve changes (self-liquidating), how the sleeve is managed (annual rebalancing with a five-point band), assumptions with numbers, scenario testing, the 2028 deposit as the ladder's backstop. It reads cleanly and a co-sponsor could follow it. What holds it back is that there is no trade-off argued anywhere: why 80/20 and not 60/40 or 100/0, why half of the growth and not a third, why a five-year horizon justifies that much equity. The "stocks defend against rising Taiwan construction costs" claim is asserted, not reasoned. It would earn Laura's confidence; it would not make her think differently about her problem.
- Not understood on first reading: how the bottom of the 2031 range is sized. The text says the bottom is "moved into Treasuries", but the bottom is defined by whatever is moved, so I could not tell what rule sets it. Also "roughly two-thirds of total contributions" made me pause to work out that the ladder absorbs essentially the whole first deposit.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned, and this entry taught me four things. First, that "high degree of certainty" can be operationalised as an enumerated list of failure modes rather than a probability adjective, and that one of those modes is correlated: a recession before the 2027 purchase both raises the ladder's cost and shrinks the entrepreneurial income behind the 2028 deposit that has to plug the gap. That is a sharper reading of the client than anyone else managed. Second, that the 2028 deposit can be structured as a capital-protected note built by hand: Treasuries that grow back to the whole deposit by late 2032, with only the expected interest at risk in one world fund, so the floor exists from 2028 rather than being bought in 2031 at whatever stocks fetch. Third, the capped range has an asymmetry (falls come off the gift, rises above the top go to Laura) and the entry both names it and neutralises it by moving the promised half into Treasuries on naming day. Fourth, and most important for me, the trade-off is argued against the strongest rival the team could construct (keep the ladder, put the deposit in stocks, buy the floor in 2031), with the admission that the rival usually wins by about $15,000 and the price of that, a $90,000 promise versus $140,000 in the worst twentieth of paths. That is exactly the "reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs" Wharton says it wants. What holds it back: the growth half of Wharton's balance is thin. Only the interest on the 2028 deposit ever meets equity risk, and for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks a judge could reasonably ask whether the team has chosen the least risky plan rather than the appropriate one. As a decision scientist I also note that the tail the team pays $15,000 to avoid is a smaller promise, not a broken one; the case's credibility concern is about over-promising, so the loss function is arguable. Diversification is dealt with in one clause. The prose is compressed and a human judge reading once has to work; the closing flourish adds nothing to the strategy.
- Not understood on first reading: "Matched in our model to name our $145,000 median bottom" took two reads before I saw that the rival had been calibrated to make the same typical promise. "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" I took to mean target-maturity ETFs but was not told. And if the gift reaches its top 97 times in 100, I wondered what the range is for and what happens in the other three.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the number Laura tells co-sponsors can be made the quantity the portfolio is mechanically built to defend, through a pre-committed rule that cuts stock exposure as the cushion shrinks "before anyone is tempted to decide". As a decision scientist I warmed to this: it is a commitment device, it refuses to be rewritten because markets moved (Wharton's own words), and the team names its failure modes honestly, gap risk (with the reasoning for a multiplier of three rather than five), cash-lock, the variability of the 2028 deposit and the Taiwan-dollar exposure, and says which it accepts. Confidence levels are stated (95% for the minimum, roughly even odds for the top), assumptions are numeric, liquidity is covered, the flexibility rule is clear. What holds it back: the client is served less well than the mechanism. The starting floor of "near 60% of the sleeve" is unexplained, and in the cash-lock path Laura's protected minimum would be far smaller than what Entries 2, 3 and 5 secure by simply owning the floor from 2028 with no path dependence at all; the entry never compares its dynamic protection with that simpler static one, so the central trade-off it has chosen is not argued. The text is also the hardest in the set for a human reading once: CPPI, cushion, multiplier, present value, accrual at the short yield, all in one paragraph. Wharton asks the IPS to earn Laura's confidence; I think this one would earn her analyst's confidence and leave her asking what she just agreed to. I went back and forth between this and Entry 3 and gave Entry 3 the win because its trade-off is argued against a real alternative and its floor does not depend on monthly rebalancing working.
- Not understood on first reading: why 60%; how the announced minimum in 2031 is set (it seems to be whatever the floor has become, which makes "Floor = Promise" partly circular); and what the sleeve looks like in 2033 to 2042 once the gift is made.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same architecture as Entries 2 and 3, with two things I did not get elsewhere: the observation that Laura does not owe one ten-year bill but ten one-year bills, which is the cleanest statement of sequence risk in the set and the real reason a drawdown portfolio fails where an accumulation portfolio would not; and an explicit priority rule (cheques, then floor, then hope) that answers Wharton's principles question directly. The stated confidence in the top of the range (about 97 in 100) is kept. The trade-off is argued with numbers: a 60/40 portfolio ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead on average but misses a cheque or breaks its word about one time in three, against under one in sixty here. Why it sits below Entry 2: the rival is the cruder one. A 60/40 whole-portfolio plan is a fair proxy for what many teams will submit, so it is not a straw man in this competition, but it is not the strongest alternative, and a decision scientist wants the strongest. Entry 2's rival keeps the ladder and risks only the deposit, which is the comparison that actually tests the team's choice. The growth-balance reservation applies with more force here, because the entry itself shows the expected cost of its caution is about $200,000, and then does not ask whether a middle path exists.
- Not understood on first reading: the rival paragraph arrives before the floor and the range have been introduced, so "breaks its word to co-sponsors" referred to a promise I had not yet read about; and I could not tell what failure the "one in sixty" counts for a plan whose payments are all Treasury-backed.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reach the same base camp: Treasuries matched to the ten payments, stocks for the rest, something locked before 2031. From there the question I asked of each was whether it saw anything about Laura's problem that I had not, and whether it argued a trade-off rather than announcing a choice. Entries 2, 3 and 5 did both: they treated certainty as an enumerated set of failure modes, one of them correlated through Laura's own income; they built the floor into the 2028 deposit itself instead of buying it in 2031 at the market's mercy; they named the asymmetry of a capped promise; and they put a price on their own caution and chose to pay it. Entry 4 had the one truly different mechanism and the honesty to list how it fails, but never compared it with the simpler static protection its rivals used. Entries 1 and 6 executed the base-camp plan, one cleanly and one charmingly, and taught me nothing. What the best entry (Entry 2) would need to win outright is the other half of Wharton's balance: it has made the case for protecting the promise beyond doubt, but a client who takes thoughtful risks deserves to be shown that the team looked for a middle path between its own plan and the rival it beat, and either found none or found one and said why it was still wrong. It should also give a human judge reading once a little more room to breathe; the ideas are good enough not to need compression.
