# Judge J3 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5: the only entry that finds the plan's own leak, prices it, puts it in a priority order and states a confidence for the range.
2. Entry 4: the most rigorous dynamic rule (CPPI) with honest failure modes, but the ladder's coupon reinvestment is never addressed.
3. Entry 2: same sound plan as Entry 5, but it gives no stated confidence for the range and no rule for what gives way first.
4. Entry 6: same core plan, but it tests itself against a weak 60/40 rival and leaves open whether its own failures include a missed cheque.
5. Entry 3: complete and orderly, but it says rate swings "never touch the payments" while it reinvests coupons at rates nobody knows.
6. Entry 1: the right structure, but the range bottom is a scenario rather than money she owns, and the gift rule and the margin are never sized.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 2/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The bones are right. The 2027 money buys a dated Treasury ladder matched to the ten payments, the 2028 deposit fills any gap, and everything else sits in a diversified growth sleeve, so a crash between 2028 and 2031 hits the facility and never the commitment. Tracing the cash flows, though, the 2031 range is where it gets vague. The bottom is "locked value plus the rest after a severe fall", so it is a scenario estimate, not money she owns. The sleeve only moves into 2033 Treasuries "gradually" after 2031, so a fall worse than "severe" in 2031-33 breaks the number she gave co-sponsors. No confidence is given for the range. The facility contribution has no rule (how much of the sleeve is given, and how big is the "bleed"?). Coupons the long bonds pay before 2033 have to be reinvested at unknown rates, and the entry never mentions it. The 15% Taiwan slice through one country fund is a real concentration bet, defended only by where the building is. The graphic-novel frame is tied to Laura and does organise the plan, but it carries more of the page than the analysis does.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "sized so principal and coupons equal $50,000" means for bonds that pay coupons years before their payment date; and how big the "bleed" is, and whether it comes out of the gift or out of what she keeps.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The cash flows hold all the way through. The 2027 money buys the ladder. Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that grow back to the full $150,000 by late 2032, so the bottom of the range has been owned since 2028 and is never bought in 2031 at whatever the market gives. Only the expected interest goes into stocks. The entry does what I look for first: it names the hole in its own ladder (coupons paid early earn less if yields fall) and says when it will be priced and where the money comes from. It also sees that a recession hits the ladder's cost and Laura's 2028 income at the same moment. The rival test is the honest kind: a strong alternative that keeps the same ladder, a stated trade-off (about $15,000 more on average against about $90,000 instead of $140,000 in the bad twentieth), and a reason to choose. What holds it back: no confidence is stated for the range, which the case asks for. The return assumptions behind "our model" are never given. Stock exposure is small (roughly the interest on $150,000), which leaves Laura, who takes thoughtful risks, little upside and not much flexibility after she gives the floor plus half her stocks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "Sell low, by appointment" is heading, given where it sits; "Do not crush" and the capsule name took a second read; and how the 2031 "set aside" for the reinvestment shortfall is held.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 2/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- A complete, well-ordered plan that answers every IPS question: a ladder bought first, the 2028 deposit completing it, an 80/20 growth engine, the bottom of the range locked into pre-2033 Treasuries in 2031, the gift as floor plus at most half the rest, capped at the top, and the reserve running itself down. The assumptions are stated (6-7%, 4-5%, 2.5% inflation), which is more than most entries give. The logic has two weak points. First, it holds early coupons in short Treasuries and still claims rate swings never touch the payments, but those coupons are reinvested at rates nobody knows, and that is exactly the unpriced leak. Second, the floor is locked in 2031 at whatever the sleeve is worth that day, so a crash in 2030 sets her range at the bottom, and the entry doesn't weigh that against any alternative. It promises a "stated, modelled confidence" without stating one, and "if the deposit falls short, 2028 completes it" ignores that the ladder will be repriced by then. It is the textbook answer done neatly; it does not show me the team found where its own plan bends.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The most technically ambitious entry, and it mostly understands its own tool. A CPPI rule on the growth sleeve (multiplier 3, monthly, no leverage) ties the announced minimum to a floor the rule defends mechanically. The entry names the real ways it fails: gap risk (a one-month fall of about a third, correctly read off the multiplier), cash-lock after a crash, a cyclical 2028 income, and the Taiwan dollar with no hedge allowed. Assumptions and confidence levels are stated (95% for the minimum, roughly even odds at the top), and keeping half the gain above the minimum gives Laura real flexibility. The holes: coupon reinvestment on the ladder is never mentioned. Repricing the ladder gap in 2028 is waved through. The switch from a floor that "accrues at the short yield" to "the present value of the announced minimum" in 2031 leaves unclear how the minimum is chosen and whether there is a jump. Because the top is set at the median and the gift is not capped, "confidence within the range" means little. A monthly CPPI also gives back return in choppy markets, a cost the entry does not weigh against a simpler locked floor.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the 2031 announced minimum relates to the floor the rule was already defending, that is, whether the floor is reset to the minimum or the minimum is read off the floor.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same structure as Entry 2, and it traces cleanly: a ladder from the 2027 money, a floor of the full $150,000 owned from 2028, stocks bought only with the expected interest, half the promised stocks moved into Treasuries the day the range is named, and a reserve that runs itself down. What lifts it above its near-twin is what an actuary asks next. It states a confidence for the range (top reached about 97 in 100). It sets an order for when things go wrong: cheques, then floor, then gift. It gives the tail number after the reinvestment set-aside, so the leak is counted in the promise and not just mentioned. The rival is a fair one (same ladder, stocks bought at the 2031 price) and the trade-off is priced in both directions, which is exactly what Wharton means by "tradeoffs behind those decisions". It still doesn't state the return assumptions behind its model. It doesn't explain what goes wrong in the 3 in 100 once both ends of the range are in Treasuries. And it doesn't face the cost of so little equity for a client who takes risks, or how thin her flexibility is after she gives the floor plus half the stocks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the top is only about 97 in 100 when, as I read it, both the floor and the promised half are in Treasuries by then; "Do not crush" as an ending.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The same sound plan as Entries 2 and 5, with the reinvestment leak named and priced, a floor owned since 2028, a stated 97-in-100 for the top and a priority order. The "ten one-year bills" point on sequence risk is a real insight. The rival test is weaker, though. A 60/40 portfolio with no ladder is not the strongest alternative to a liability-matched plan, so beating it says little. The "almost $200,000 ahead" figure, given without the assumptions behind it, invites the question of whether Laura is overpaying for certainty. Worse, "ours, under one in sixty" for "misses a cheque or breaks its word" leaves me unsure whether the plan itself can miss a cheque, which contradicts its own definition of high certainty (only a US default). On the core promise that ambiguity matters more than anything else in the entry. As in the other two, stock exposure and the flexibility left over are small and not defended.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether any part of the "under one in sixty" is a missed operating payment or only a broken range; and what the 60/40 rival holds for the payments.

## My overall view
Every entry buys the ten payments with a Treasury ladder, so the ladder separated nobody. What separated them was what happens around it. The coupons the ladder pays before 2033 must be reinvested at unknown rates. The 2028 deposit both completes the ladder and comes from income that shrinks in the same recession that makes the ladder dearer. And the bottom of the 2031 range is either money already owned or a price taken on the day. Entries 2, 5 and 6 (they read as versions of one plan) were the only ones to put a price on the leak in their own plan. Entry 4 was the only one to model a dynamic protection rule and own its failure modes. Entries 1 and 3 have the right shape but assert certainty they haven't checked. To win, Entry 5 needs four more things: the return and rate assumptions behind its model in one sentence, an explanation of what fails in the 3 in 100, an honest case for why so little equity and so thin a margin are right for a client who takes thoughtful risks, and Entry 4's habit of naming every failure mode the plan accepts.
