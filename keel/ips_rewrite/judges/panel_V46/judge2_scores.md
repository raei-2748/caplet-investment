# Judge J2 scores

(Marcus, former equity PM. Marked only against Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - Floor = Promise with a pre-committed CPPI rule: the only plan that lets the 2028 money run at full equity and still protects the announced minimum, with its risks and the upside it forfeits stated plainly.
2. Entry 5 - Ladder, then a floor bond plus a small stock fund; counts the price of safety ($15,000) and names a modelled chance (97 in 100) of reaching the top, with a clear priority order if things change.
3. Entry 1 - Same engine as 5; honest about the roughly $200,000 that a 60/40 usually gains, but the comparison is against a rival that puts the ladder money at risk too, which flatters the safe plan.
4. Entry 6 - The most growth-oriented structure (80% stocks on everything above the ladder) and clear on planned changes, but there are almost no numbers behind the facility range or the stock bet.
5. Entry 4 - Same engine as 5 with a good rival test, but it drops the modelled probability of reaching the top and the priority order, so it is the thinner version of 5.
6. Entry 2 - Sound bones (ladder first, diversified sleeve, Taiwan slice) but vague on how the range is built, thin on analysis, and the graphic-novel framing takes space that should carry reasoning.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The plan buys the ten payments with Treasuries and lists the specific ways the "high certainty" could fail, including the recession-before-we-buy and 2028-deposit link, which is a good reading of Laura's case. It is honest that a 60/40 usually ends about $200,000 ahead, and that is the kind of price tag I want to see. What holds it back is the comparison: the 60/40 rival puts the ladder money itself at risk, so "one time in three it misses a cheque" is a weak straw man. A fairer rival (keep the ladder, take more equity risk with the surplus) is what entries 4 and 5 use. The stock sleeve is tiny, about the interest on the floor bond, so for a client "willing to take thoughtful risks" this gives up a lot of growth for a gift range that is mostly her own money returned. The capped-range catch is stated clearly.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Sell low, by appointment" and "Do not crush" are sign-offs I didn't need; the shortfall that is "priced and set aside" in 2031 isn't sized, so I couldn't tell how much of the hope it eats.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The ladder-first logic is right and the growth sleeve is properly all-in on equities (US, international and a 15% Taiwan slice), which I like as a growth stance. But the analysis is almost absent: no stated returns beyond "moderate", no probabilities, and the range ("value already locked in plus the rest after a severe market fall") is hand-waved, with no confidence level, which Wharton explicitly asks for in 2031. Moving "a growing share" into Treasuries from 2031 is described but not sized. Naming individual funds is the sort of detail the IPS instructions say to avoid, and the Taiwan sleeve is justified on sentiment more than risk. The comic metaphor takes up half the document and I'd have preferred the words spent on trade-offs and assumptions.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "bleed" is sized relative to the half-and-half or any other rule; what "severe market fall" means numerically.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one plan that actually puts the surplus to work: a CPPI rule with a multiplier of three starts nearly fully in equities and de-risks only as the cushion shrinks, so the facility contribution can grow with markets while the announced minimum is protected. It states stock and Treasury return and volatility assumptions, uses 10,000 simulated paths, targets at least 95% confidence on the minimum and calls the top "roughly even odds", which is a straight answer to Wharton's request for confidence in the range. The risk section is the most honest in the set (gap risk, cash-lock missing a recovery, 2028 deposit variability, currency), and I credit the team for saying what they give up. It is more complicated than a judge may like, the monthly rebalancing needs to be operable, and "half the gain above the minimum" caps the upside for Laura further than I'd choose, but the complexity earns its place. Dollar numbers for the range would make it stronger, though the IPS is told not to give a final range.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can start "near 60% of the sleeve" when the sleeve is nearly empty until 2028; I read it as applying once the 2028 deposit arrives.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same ladder and floor-bond engine as entries 1 and 5. The rival test is fair and specific (a stock-heavy 2028 deposit usually wins by about $15,000 but can only name about $90,000 in the worst twentieth of paths versus $140,000), and "We'd pay the $15,000" is a clearly priced trade-off, which I respect. What I would mark down: it gives no modelled chance of reaching the top of the range, no statement of what comes first if something goes wrong, and the equity exposure is small. A $15,000 cost is cheap for the protection claimed, but a judge who wants growth will notice the plan never shows what the whole facility contribution could be on a good outcome.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "cash paid early earns less if yields fall" is clear enough, but how the 2031 set-aside is chosen is not stated.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Of the three versions of the ladder-plus-floor plan, this is the most complete: it has the failure list, the capped-range catch, a modelled top-of-range probability (about 97 in 100), a fair stock-heavy rival priced at about $15,000, a worst-twentieth comparison, and a clear priority order (cheques, then floor, then hope). That is the reasoning-and-tradeoffs Wharton asks for. My reservation as an equity man is that only the interest on the floor bond is in stocks, so the gift is mostly her own capital returned and the upside is thin. The team does say so, and it prices the alternative, which is more than most do. Fine for the semifinal, but not a bold answer to a client who takes thoughtful risks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the "twentieth of our paths" language took a second read; the phrase "buys its bottom" is jargon.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Structurally this is the most growth-friendly of the ladder plans: everything above the ladder is 80% stocks, with the floor locked into Treasuries only in 2031, and it correctly links equities to rising Taiwan construction costs. The planned changes are laid out cleanly (2031, 2033, facility contribution). But it is thin where Wharton wants evidence: no probability, no range, no sense of how large the growth sleeve or the contribution is, and "we test good, average and severe markets" with no result. A 20% Treasury slice inside the growth sleeve is also an unexplained hedge on a sleeve with a five-year horizon. It is a sensible, safe-sounding plan a competent team would write, but it does not show its work.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "locked floor" is sized in 2031 and whether the contribution can be below it.

## My overall view
What separates entry 3 is that it treats safety as something to be priced and managed rather than bought wholesale: it protects the minimum with a rule and lets the rest of the money earn, and it then says what the rule gives up. The ladder-and-floor-bond family (1, 4, 5) is carefully reasoned and gives honest numbers on the price of safety, but it keeps only a small slice in stocks, so most of the "gift" is Laura's own money coming back. Entry 6 has the right instinct on growth but shows no work, and entry 2 is mainly style. To win, the best entry would need to attach dollar-scale figures to its range and probabilities (even approximate), show how much more the rule earns than the plain ladder-plus-floor plan in typical and bad paths, and trim the complexity so a judge can follow it on one read.
