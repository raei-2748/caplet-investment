### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: It takes Wharton's five IPS questions in order, with headed sections for the central idea, reserve, growth sleeve, flexibility, reserve over time, the 2031 range, assumptions, liquidity, risks and Laura. It is the only entry that states both the stock and Treasury return assumptions, with a volatility figure, and the diversification is explicit (thousands of companies across many countries). Some terms are used with little plain-English help, and the statement about the floor starting near 60% of the sleeve is hard to place in dollars.

## My overall view
The top three share one core design: Treasuries for the ten payments, the 2028 deposit split into a Treasury floor and one world stock fund, and a 2031 range capped by that floor. What separated them is whether the entry shows the price of its own plan and the rival it rejected. Entries 1, 2 and 6 each do this, and they are the entries a reader can follow from the central idea to the 2033 payments without having to guess. Entry 2 is the most complete on framework, assumptions and risk; Entry 1 is the most complete as a judgement, with a stated cost and a visible change of mind. To win, the best entry would need Entry 1's clear priorities and honest price, Entry 2's stated assumptions and confidence levels (with the 2031 range given as a real number), and a plain sentence on what the team learned. Entries 1, 4 and 6 are close enough in text that I judged them on what differs between them. Entries 3 and 5 are sound but do not show the outcome analysis or the confidence level Wharton asks for, and neither makes the trade-off of its own plan visible.
