# Judge J3 scores

Judge: Priya (pension actuary, asset-liability matching). Method: traced the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028),
the ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042), the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution through each plan, looking for the
point where it fails or where the reasoning is waved through.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: the only plan where every leak I found is named, priced, given a funding source, and taken off the range before it is quoted.
2. Entry 5: a clean, honest structure with a range it cannot miss and a named shortfall waterfall, but thin on evidence.
3. Entry 1: the same sound liability match as Entry 3, but the coupon-reinvestment gap has no named source of money.
4. Entry 4: close to Entry 1, with a stated range confidence, but it contradicts itself on the 2028 deposit.
5. Entry 6: a conventional, sensible ladder-plus-growth plan that denies the one risk a coupon ladder actually has.
6. Entry 2: a duration mismatch until 2031, a reserve that costs more than the first deposit, and a "match" that mixes up two kinds of risk.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The liability side is right. The ten fixed nominal payments are bought with dated Treasuries in January 2027, so the operating commitment is matched in timing and amount. The failure points are named: a US default, or yields down two points combined with a much smaller 2028 deposit. The team also sees the leak a coupon ladder really has, which is reinvestment at lower yields. They explain why a single issuer is safer here than ten "safe" ones, and they argue that Laura's income shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear, which is a real asset-liability point and not decoration. They also name the trade-off that locking the promised half gives up its upside. What holds it back: the reinvestment gap is "priced and set aside in 2031" but I can't tell whose money pays for it, and the plan never says whether that comes off the range. More broadly, the plan commits almost everything above the reserve (the floor plus half a small stock sleeve). That leaves Laura very little flexibility after 2033, and the case explicitly warns against that. The 60/40 comparison is useful evidence, but it is hard to read what "breaks a promise, mostly the gift" means for a plan with an unlocked range.
- Confusions on first reading: "about a fifteenth of her money" took a moment to tie to the floor's interest. I wasn't sure whether the 2031 set-aside for the coupon gap reduces the range or comes from somewhere else.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The understanding of the client is genuine: the lead-gift signalling argument and her reputation with co-sponsors are real features of the case, and a moderate growth sleeve follows from them. On the liability side, though, the plan has holes nobody priced. From 2027 to 2031 the reserve sits in an intermediate Treasury fund whose duration is well short of the payments'. A fall in rates raises what the payments cost faster than the fund gains, so the reserve can drift short exactly when it is supposed to be safe. A 5% margin is a guess, not a hedge. Sizing the reserve at present value plus 5% also costs more than the $300,000 first deposit, and the plan doesn't say where the rest comes from. "97% probability ... even in a recession or a large fall in rates" contradicts itself. The matching pledge is creative, but it treats a match that depends on co-sponsors as if it covered market risk. If co-sponsors give and markets have fallen, the match can't be paid, and that is exactly the credibility failure the case warns about. Flexibility is "a portion", with no size and no rule.
- Confusions on first reading: how the 20%-a-year move into short Treasuries from 2031 squares with a growth sleeve that is meant to fund the 2033 contribution. Also, what the cap on the match is measured against.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry where my trace found no unfunded hole. The payments are bought in 2027. The 2028 deposit first fills any gap, then buys a floor, and only the floor's expected interest takes equity risk. The reinvestment leak is sized at 2031 yields with a cushion, paid for from named money (spare cash and Laura's half), and anything left uncovered is taken off the range before she quotes it. That is how an actuary would close it. The reasoning shows the team understands why: a fixed-dollar draw turns a crash into a forced sale; the arithmetic on ten independent 99% bets is right; the income-correlation argument explains why expected money should insure and owned money should commit. A fixed nominal liability is immune to inflation while the building is not, and the plan sends that risk to the money it leaves her. Every rule fires on a date the case set, and changes land only on what isn't yet promised. What holds it back: the prose is compressed to the point that a judge reading once has to work for it. Flexibility after 2033 is still only half a small stock sleeve, which is thin for a client the case says should not commit everything.
- Confusions on first reading: "each end as sure as its miss is costly" needed a second read. "More of the first cheque's fund" as the floor's holding isn't obvious. "Topped up once" in 2033 doesn't say from what.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The strategy and the liability match are the same as Entry 1, and just as sound. It adds two things an actuary values: a stated confidence for the range once it is locked, and an explicit order of priority if anything changes (cheques, then floor, then the hope). The "ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill" framing shows the team understands sequence risk. What holds it back: it says the 2028 deposit "never carries a cheque", then has that deposit plug any gap in the ladder, which is carrying a cheque. A judge checking the logic will notice. The coupon-reinvestment gap is set aside in 2031, again with no named source. A residual miss of "about $1,000" on a range whose top has already moved into Treasuries isn't explained, so I can't tell what risk remains. It also drops the argument for relying on one issuer. As with Entry 1, the plan commits nearly everything above the reserve, which leaves Laura little flexibility.
- Confusions on first reading: why a range that is locked into Treasuries on the day it is named can still miss, and where the coupon-gap money comes from.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The cash flows trace cleanly. The $292,000 cost is dated to a curve, and the ladder is bought latest-first because those rungs carry the most rate risk, which is the right priority. The 2028 deposit completes the ladder if needed and then buys a 2032 floor. Any expected 2033 shortfall has a stated waterfall: the 2027 leftover, then Laura's half, then the contribution above the floor. The range runs from the floor (rounded down after the expected shortfall) to the floor plus half the fund, and she pays the floor plus half the fund capped at the top, so the 2033 gift cannot fall outside it. That is a range built to hold. The split between ability and willingness to bear risk (high capacity, near-zero tolerance for a missed payment because the artists bear it) is the most precise client reading here. The plan also says it matches the WInS book. What holds it back is Portfolio Analysis. There is no measure of certainty beyond "depends mainly on the US government", and no alternative tested against the plan, so the claim that this beats a balanced portfolio rests on assertion. Flexibility after 2033 is half a small fund, the same weakness as Entries 1, 3 and 4.
- Confusions on first reading: none of substance. "Income reinvested in the holding that paid it" is clear in intent, but it doesn't say what happens to the reinvestment shortfall if rates fall.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clear, conventional liability-driven plan. A dated Treasury ladder covers the payments, the 2028 deposit backstops it, everything else goes into an 80/20 growth portfolio, the bottom of the range is moved into Treasuries before the 2031 announcement, and the contribution is capped at half the growth portfolio. The return and inflation assumptions are stated. It keeps more for Laura's flexibility and takes more growth than the floor-based entries, which arguably suits a client "willing to take thoughtful risks" better. What holds it back is the hole I was looking for. The ladder is sized so "principal plus coupons" covers each payment and the coupons sit in short Treasuries, so the plan depends on reinvestment rates through 2032. Yet it says "rate swings never touch the payments". That is the one risk a held-to-maturity coupon ladder has, and here it is denied rather than priced. The size of the 2031 floor is never set, so I can't tell how the bottom of the range is chosen. "Good, average and severe markets" are named but no results are given, so the analysis is asserted rather than shown.
- Confusions on first reading: how the bottom of the range is determined in 2031, and whether "the remaining stocks with a stated, modelled confidence level" means a percentile or something else.

## My overall view
Every entry matched the payments with dated Treasuries. At this level that is the entry ticket, not the differentiator. What separated them is whether the team found the leak in their own plan and paid for it. A coupon ladder bought in 2027 has to reinvest six years of coupons at rates nobody knows. The best entry sized that gap, funded it from named money, and cut it from the range before Laura says a number. The weaker ones either ignored it, called it a non-issue, or set money aside without saying whose. The second dividing line was the 2031 range: whether the plan makes it impossible for the 2033 gift to fall outside what was quoted, and says what was given up to get there. To win, the top entry (Entry 3) needs two things. It has to read clearly in one pass, because a judge should not have to decode the logic I had to decode. And it needs a real answer on flexibility. All four floor-based plans leave Laura only half a small stock sleeve once the gift is paid, and the case says plainly that committing nearly everything limits her. A plan that kept Entry 3's rigour and showed, with a modelled number, how much Laura keeps and why that is enough would be hard to beat.
