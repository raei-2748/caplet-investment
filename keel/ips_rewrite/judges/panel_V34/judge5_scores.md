## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: nothing I did not already hold as my prior. This is the default plan done properly: a ladder of individual Treasuries matched to each payment, held to maturity, with the rest in a broadly diversified 80/20 growth sleeve, an annual review, a 2031 floor moved into Treasuries, and a contribution of floor plus half the growth sleeve capped at the announced top. Every one of Wharton's questions gets an answer and the structure is easy to follow. What holds it back is that the reasoning is asserted rather than earned: the client section tells me Laura takes thoughtful risks and values her word, which is the case restated; the stress test is described as good, average and severe markets with no sense of what those did to the numbers; and the trade-off that matters most here, how much of the 2028 deposit should carry equity risk when the facility gift is the only thing at stake, is never discussed. It would earn Laura's confidence, but it would not make her choose this team over the two thousand others who wrote something very similar.
- Not understood on first reading: why the growth sleeve needs 20% intermediate Treasuries as a shock absorber when the entry has just argued that the payments are already fully secured and the sleeve exists to take risk; the entry does not say what that 20% is protecting.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: three things, which is more than any other entry taught me. First, a real correlation argument for why the liability-matching asset should not be diversified: ten independent near-certain claims fail more often than ten claims on one borrower that stand or fall together. Second, the point that Laura's 2028 deposit is itself risky and correlated with the same recession that makes bonds expensive, which is why money she already has should buy the certainty and money she merely expects should carry the risk. Third, the asymmetry of a capped range once it is announced (she keeps every fall and gives away every rise), and the fix of locking the promised half the day she names it. The coupon-reinvestment leak is also identified and dealt with before the range is quoted. That is a strategy with genuine decision-theoretic content. What holds it back is severe: the prose is compressed to the point where a judge reading once will not follow the mechanics of the floor, the fund or the 2028 deposit's path; the pharmacy metaphor takes space the mechanics needed; and the opening generalisation about what every other team will do is a claim the writers cannot know and judges will not enjoy. The ideas are top-50 ideas; the entry as a whole is not reliably readable enough to be scored as one.
- Not understood on first reading: what "more of the first cheque's fund" is and how it becomes a floor for the facility; what it means in practice that the floor's expected interest buys the index fund, and roughly how large that fund then is; and how the coupon-leak top-up interacts with the range if spare cash and Laura's half cannot cover it.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the reframing of Laura's obligation as ten one-year bills rather than one ten-year bill, which is the right way to see why a fixed-dollar withdrawal schedule punishes equity holders in a way a fixed-percentage one does not; the capital-protected-note structure for the 2028 deposit (a Treasury floor sized to grow back to the whole remainder, with only the implied interest at risk in world stocks), which is a clear and defensible answer to the growth-versus-protection balance Laura asked for; and the asymmetry fix on the announced range. Crucially this entry shows me the decisions it rejected and roughly what they cost: the first plan missed a payment about one future in thirty, a balanced 60/40 breaks a promise about one in four, and after the 2031 lock the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100 with a tiny miss. Those are the kind of modelled, falsifiable statements Wharton's Portfolio Analysis criterion asks for, and they are given at the level of strategy rather than calculation. The definition of high certainty is explicit and honest about the two ways it can fail. Client fit is argued, not asserted: Laura has already taken her big risk, and her income is correlated with the very recession that would hurt her. The priority order at the end (payments, then floor, then hope) is the kind of decision rule a client can actually hold the team to. What holds it back: inflation and the Taiwan-dollar cost of the building get no mention at all, and the plan leaves Laura with very little equity overall, a choice it justifies by the cost of a broken promise but which a judge who reads Laura's risk willingness more generously will question.
- Not understood on first reading: the floor "sized to grow back to the whole remainder" took a second pass to see that the discount is what funds the stocks; and the $85,000 threshold in the certainty definition arrives without saying what it is a threshold for until the sentence is re-read.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: the observation that people who fund buildings rarely pay to run them, which is the sharpest single piece of client insight in the set and explains why the operating commitment is the hard problem rather than the facility. Also a sensible ordering rule (buy the most distant payments first because they are the most rate-sensitive), an explicit acceptance of single-issuer concentration with the reason, and an honestly argued refusal to move floor money into equities even though it would raise the expected gift. This is the most complete answer to Wharton's list: objectives, ability and willingness to bear risk, construction, reserve composition over time, review policy, substitution rule, shortfall waterfall, range, flexibility, and the link to the WInS book. It reads like a real policy document. What holds it back: the certainty claim rests on the issuer and never on a model, so the Portfolio Analysis is thinner than Entries 3 and 5; the range is built so it cannot be missed only because it is wide, and the entry does not notice the asymmetry (Laura keeps the downside of the half she has quoted and gives away the upside) that three other entries fix; and the formal voice, while appropriate, gives the judge less sense of a team that argued and changed its mind.
- Not understood on first reading: how large the branch (the equity fund) actually is once the 2032 Treasury ETF has been sized to return the whole 2028 remainder; it is implied to be only the discount, but the entry does not say so, and the pitch's description of the branch sounds larger than that.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: largely the same as Entry 3, which it mirrors almost paragraph for paragraph, plus three things Entry 3 lacks: a stated equity return assumption behind the comparison with a balanced portfolio, the point that every ladder holding is sellable any day (liquidity, which Wharton names and most entries ignore), and the observation that the kept half is flexibility against a building priced in Taiwan dollars. It also states plainly that the team gives up the upside on the promised half as the price of naming a number, which is the honest way to present that trade-off, and closes with a rule that decisions fire on dates rather than on market moves, which is exactly the discipline Wharton's guide asks for. What holds it back relative to Entry 3 is one missing step: having argued for the 2031 lock, it never tells me how often the range is then hit or how large a miss would be, so the credibility of the announced range rests on logic alone where Entry 3 gives evidence. It also drops the explicit priority order at the end. These are small differences; on a different day I could rank 5 and 3 the other way round.
- Not understood on first reading: none of substance. The description of the floor as Treasuries bought at a discount and worth the whole remainder by late 2032 is the clearest statement of that mechanism in the set.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: the one idea in this set I had not seen before and would cite in a seminar: split the 2031 announcement into a committed lead gift (already in Treasuries, promised with near-certainty) and a conditional matching pledge (paid only as co-sponsors give, capped by what the growth sleeve can provide in most outcomes), so that market uncertainty is reframed as a reason for others to give rather than to hesitate. That is a sharper way of seeing the co-sponsor problem, it draws on real evidence about how donors behave, and it answers Wharton's criterion about communicating uncertainty credibly more directly than anyone else. It also gives the only quantified definition of high certainty expressed as a probability (97% in simulation) with the stress scenarios named. What holds it back is the investment design underneath. The reserve sits in an intermediate Treasury ETF with a 5% margin for four years and is only converted to matched maturities in 2031, so the team deliberately carries rate risk on the payments during 2027-2031 and ends up with a 97% probability where a matched ladder bought in 2027 would have given something close to certainty for the same money; the entry never explains why. The growth sleeve is described as a list of tickers, which is the thing Wharton told teams not to do, and the flexibility section is a sentence rather than a policy. The co-sponsor idea is top-50; the portfolio it sits on is mid-table.
- Not understood on first reading: why the reserve is not matched from the start; and how the matching pledge behaves if co-sponsors give generously in a year when the growth sleeve has fallen, since the match is conditional on both and the entry describes only the case where one of them fails.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reached my prior (match the payments with Treasuries, grow the rest) and one, Entry 6, deliberately stopped short of it. What separated the top from the rest was not the ladder but what the team did with the second deposit and the announced range. Entries 2, 3 and 5 saw two things the others did not: that the 2028 deposit is itself a risky, recession-correlated asset and should not be the thing a payment depends on, and that a capped range, once spoken, has an asymmetry that only a 2031 lock removes. Entry 3 wins because it carries those ideas in prose a judge can follow once and then shows, with a handful of modelled odds, what the rejected plans would have cost and how often the chosen one keeps its word. Entry 5 is the same plan with slightly different emphases and could easily have been first. Entry 4 is the best pure policy document and has the best single client insight. Entry 2 has the most ideas and the least chance of being understood. Entry 6 has the one new idea in the set and would have been a serious contender for the top had it bought the payments outright in 2027 and spent its words on the lead-gift mechanism instead of tickers. Entry 1 is what everyone else would write.

For Entry 3 to win outright rather than merely reach the top 50, it needs to say something about inflation and the Taiwan dollar (the case asks for it and two rivals in this set manage it in a sentence), to meet head-on the objection that a plan holding one-fifteenth in equities is too timid for a client who says she takes thoughtful risks, and to make the floor mechanism understandable without a second reading. Those are edits, not redesigns. The strategy itself is the best-argued in the set.
