# Judge J2 scores

(Marcus: former equity PM. Marked only against Wharton's five criteria and IPS questions.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - Treasury ladder for the promise, then a genuinely equity-led surplus (about 80% stocks) with the range floor locked in 2031; plain, tested against good/average/severe markets.
2. Entry 6 - Same safe-first design as Entry 4 but gives real numbers on the range (top reached about 97 times in 100, miss about $1,000) and admits the balanced 60/40 alternative usually leaves her richer.
3. Entry 4 - Same design, honest about what the cap and the floor cost Laura in upside, but fewer numbers on the 2031 range.
4. Entry 2 - The only other entry that keeps a real equity sleeve (65%), with a clever donor-signalling idea, but the certainty and the range are underspecified and it de-risks into 2033.
5. Entry 5 - Careful, client-aware and plain, but almost all of the money is Treasuries and it declines on principle to put floor money to work; no tested scenarios.
6. Entry 1 - Most defensive of all, hard to follow on a single read, and very little said about what the safety costs.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The plan buys the ten payments with the first deposit, then puts the 2028 deposit into Treasuries too and risks only the interest in a world index fund. So roughly 14 dollars in 15 are in government bonds, and in 2031 even the "promised half" of the stock fund is moved into Treasuries. It takes the sequencing risk on fixed-dollar withdrawals seriously, and the point that her 2028 income is correlated with the recessions that make bonds dear is a good client insight. But for a client who is "willing to take thoughtful risks" with up to sixteen years to run, this is safety bought at a price the entry never counts. It does not say how much upside is given away, how likely any outcome is, or what confidence sits behind the range. A good deal of the text goes on a metaphor and on a mechanism ("the first cheque's fund") that a reader has to work to follow. It reads as careful, but not as a strategy that grows the facility contribution.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "the first cheque's fund" is and why the floor is "more" of it; why wiping out every share would leave the cheques unaffected but the gift not (I worked it out, but it took effort); how the coupon cushion is sized and what is "taken off the range".

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The idea that a firm lead gift plus a conditional match turns market uncertainty into a reason for co-sponsors to give is original and ties the strategy to the co-sponsor purpose in the case. It also has a moderate growth sleeve (65% stocks) that actually does some growing, which I like better than the all-Treasury plans. Against that, the operating reserve is sized to present value plus 5% and held partly in a fund with a duration far shorter than the payments until 2031, and a 97% simulated success rate is not obviously "a high degree of certainty" for a fixed promise. The glide of 20% a year out of stocks from 2031 gives back growth just as the facility gift is being built, and the 2031 range never gets a dollar figure, a confidence level or a statement of how bad markets would bite. It names individual ETFs, which Wharton says is not the point of the IPS, and the donor research is asserted rather than shown. Good instincts, thin analysis.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "matching pledge" is communicated as a range with a stated confidence level, which is what the case asks for; what exactly the growth sleeve holds by 2033 after three years of 20% moves.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6.5/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the plan I would fund of the six. It does the safe thing cheaply (a bond ladder matched to the payments, defined as "backed by a Treasury maturing on or before due date") and then lets the surplus run in global stocks at about 80%, with a modest Treasury cushion, which is what a client with thoughtful-risk appetite and outside living income should have. It says why equity volatility is affordable (payments already secured, five years to run) and links stocks to the Taiwan construction-cost risk. The 2031 floor-lock, the "floor plus at most half the remainder" gift rule and the annual review fit the time horizons in the case, and stated return, inflation and stress assumptions are given. What holds it back: it does not put a number on the upside given up by locking the floor, it promises a "stated, modelled confidence level" without stating one, and it says nothing about coupon reinvestment or rates falling before the ladder is bought. The metaphors are mostly absent, which suits me. It is a solid, readable plan that would be stronger with two or three actual figures.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none of substance; I would have liked the confidence level on the range actually stated.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 6.5/10, Articulation 6.5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure is the same as Entry 6: ladder, floor, interest-only equity, lock the promised half in 2031. What I credit this version for is that it says out loud what safety costs: a balanced 60/40 would usually leave her richer but break a promise one time in four, and by naming the range she "gives up that half's upside". It also defines high certainty in terms of the failure conditions (US default, or yields falling and the 2028 deposit being too small). I still think the result is timid: with about a fifteenth of her money in the index fund, the facility contribution is mostly a Treasury number. The stated one-in-four failure for the balanced alternative is a modelled number that seems to decide the whole question, and the analysis does not explore a middle point. It gives few numbers on how likely the 2031 range is to hold, which Wharton wants. The pill metaphor takes up space I would rather see used on the risk trade-off.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "worth everything the deposit had left by late 2032" means in dollars; how the ladder, floor and index fund fit together as proportions of the $450,000.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 5.5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5.5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The client work is the best of the six: it separates Laura's ability to bear risk (high) from her willingness (high for the gift, low for a missed payment), builds the ladder latest-payment-first for rate sensitivity, says what happens if yields fall before January, and has a priority order for a 2033 shortfall. It is plain and well organised. Where I mark it down is that it chooses to keep the floor money out of equities on principle ("we do not") and then has an index fund that is a small residual, so the facility contribution barely grows. The range is defined so she cannot miss it, which is safe but makes the stated confidence trivial: every outcome lands between floor and top, so the range says little to co-sponsors about the real uncertainty. There is no modelling shown of good and bad markets, no probability, and "held without trading until 2033" gives up any discipline of rebalancing or de-risking at all. It is careful and credible but does not reach for much.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "a 2032 Treasury ETF sized to return that remainder" does in dollars; how big the equity "branch" is; why the top of the range is the floor plus half the fund as of 2031 rather than a figure the fund could actually exceed.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6.5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6.5/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same safe-first design as Entry 4, but it gives me more to evaluate: the range top is reached about 97 times in 100 on the model and the rare miss is about $1,000, and a balanced 60/40 breaks a promise about one in four times. That is the kind of figure Wharton asks for under "funding reliability, the facility contribution and flexibility under varying market outcomes". It also sets a clear priority order if anything changes (cheques, then floor, then hope). My objection remains the one I have to the whole family: nearly all the money sits in Treasuries and the expected facility contribution is conservative, with the cost of that caution presented as a virtue rather than priced against the upside. I do give it credit for at least stating the 60/40 comparison. It is a good, clear delivery of a plan I would not choose.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how large the equity position actually is in dollars (about a fifteenth of everything she owns, but of what base); the "dearer building" reasoning for keeping half the stocks.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the surplus was actually put to work. Five of the six treat Laura's stock allocation as a footnote on top of a bond ladder and floor, and talk about safety as if it were free. Entries 4 and 6 at least tell me what the safety costs and give modelled odds, and Entry 5 understands the client very well, but all three leave a client who can take thoughtful risks with a facility contribution that is mostly Treasuries. Entry 3 is the only one that builds the promise cheaply and then lets the rest grow in stocks, and it explains why that is affordable. To win, Entry 3 would need numbers: the stated confidence for the 2031 range, an estimate of the expected facility contribution against the more cautious alternatives, and a sentence on what locking the floor in 2031 gives up. It would also be worth dealing with falling rates before the ladder is bought. With those, it would be a clear top-50 plan.
