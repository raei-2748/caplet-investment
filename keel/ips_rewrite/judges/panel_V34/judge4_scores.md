## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A sensible, easy-to-follow plan: buy Treasuries for the ten payments, grow the rest, tighten things up in 2031, review each January. It states its return assumptions and defines "high certainty" in a way a teenager could defend, and it deals with every question Wharton lists. What holds it back is that it reads like the standard textbook answer in a tidy template (headed sections, "Growth Engine"). It shows little sign of the team having tested anything or having been surprised by something. The risk testing is a single sentence ("good, average and severe markets") with no result. It also says little about Laura herself beyond "entrepreneur who takes risks". A competent entry that a judge will like and then forget.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. The "locked floor" in 2031 is described in a few words, so I'd want to see how it is sized.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thinking is real. The team started with a growth-heavy plan, modelled it, found it missed a payment about one time in thirty, and changed course for a stated reason: the payments are ten separate one-year bills. It ties Laura's 2028 income to the recession risk, which is the sharpest client insight in the set. It also admits the costs, with the 60/40 breaking a promise one time in four and the cap giving away upside. Against that, the extended-release pill joke is a brand more than an explanation. The "capital-protected note minus the bank" line, the neat paragraph endings and the polish make me suspect help, and I distrust that. The strategy is dense for 500 words, and the "about 1 in 30" and "$85,000" figures are not explained.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the gift risks "only the interest", and the "$85,000" yield-fall condition. It took two readings to see that the gift is protected by a Treasury floor and only its expected interest goes to stocks.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The ideas are good: cover each promise by the day it becomes a promise, protect the range, and note that inflation cannot make $50,000 cost more. But I find it the least human. It is full of slogans and rules-of-one-sentence ("Every team will promise... We would rather buy them", "do not crush"), and the boasting opening is exactly what I distrust. The prose gets so compressed that I had to decode phrases like "moves into the first cheque's fund", and a judge reading it once will lose the thread. There are few numbers that come from the team's own work. It also leans on the same pill gag as the others. Strong cleverness, weak plain reasoning.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "the first cheque's fund" (which fund, and why that one?), how the 2031 coupon-shortfall fix works, and what "topped up once" in 2033 means.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The clearest of the three that share an approach. It admits the first plan failed in modelling, and explains why in a way an economics class would follow: ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill. It gives a priority order if things change (cheques, then the floor, then the hope), says how often the gift reaches its top and how large a miss would be, and handles the cap problem by moving the promised half into Treasuries. That is disciplined planning with numbers. It also shows the same client insight about her 2028 income. What holds it back is the same gimmick and polish as Entry 2, plus the close text overlap with Entries 2 and 3, which makes the three read as one template. If I were told a teenager wrote it unaided I would be sceptical.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a floor "sized to grow back to the whole remainder" works, and how "balanced 60/40" relates to the rest of the allocation.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The best idea here is not about markets at all: the lead gift and matching pledge as a way to show co-sponsors a number she can keep. It is original and it is aimed at the co-sponsor audience Wharton cares about. It also reads as the plainest voice of the six. But the investment side is weak. The reserve sits in intermediate bond funds for years, which leaves interest-rate risk on payments that are supposed to be certain. It lists individual funds (IEF, VTI, VXUS, SCHD, BND, SHY), which Wharton said to avoid. "Fundraising research consistently finds" is asserted without anything behind it. No range, no stress result, and little on how the facility contribution is sized or what "97%" rests on. The growth sleeve is a generic 65/35 mix. Original, but I cannot tell the plan would hold up.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "matching pledge" is paid if co-sponsors give more than expected, and how the reserve is sized once "5% margin" meets falling rates.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one that sounds like people who did the work. It separates Laura's ability to bear risk (high, since living costs are covered elsewhere) from her willingness (low for a missed payment, because the artists would bear the loss), which is real economics. It owns its weak point ("relying on one issuer may look like a weakness, but we accept it") and says why. It lays out a plain order for absorbing a shortfall, a calm review rule, and a reason for wanting a wide range ("missing a narrower one would harm her credibility more"). The plan is simple enough to defend aloud. Weaknesses: little modelling shown, with no stated confidence that the range will hold. Saying the range cannot be missed is too strong for 500 words of market risk, and the pitch is clumsy. But it is honest, tailored and understood.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the pitch's opening ("co-sponsors who fund the building rarely pay to run it") took a second read to see as the reason for the design, and "sized to return that remainder" is vague.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether I believed the writers understood what they were saying. Entry 6 earns trust by being plain, naming Laura's real circumstances and admitting its weak point. Entries 2 and 4 contain the deepest reasoning in the set, including a failed first attempt and honest odds, but a tidy joke and over-polished prose make me less sure whose words they are, and three entries sharing the same gag is a warning sign. Entry 3 pushes that cleverness furthest and becomes hard to read. Entry 1 is safe but unremarkable. Entry 5 has the best fresh idea and the weakest analysis. To win, Entry 6 would need one quantified result of its own (how often the range holds, and what happens in a bad decade), a dropped "cannot miss the range" claim, and a clearer pitch, with no new cleverness. Entry 4 would win if it said the same things in less compact, less performed prose.
