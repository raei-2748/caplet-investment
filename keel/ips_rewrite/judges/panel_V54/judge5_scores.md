Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Marked only against Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions in WHARTON_WORD.md, with the client case open beside me.

Method, as promised: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know (my prior being that a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes), then ran a round-robin of fifteen pairwise comparisons on Wharton's criteria and ranked by wins.

Pairwise record (wins out of 5): Entry 6: 5. Entry 1: 4. Entry 4: 3. Entry 5: 2. Entry 3: 1. Entry 2: 0.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 ... the only entry whose growth money runs on a pre-committed rule with its failure modes named, priced and accepted; the promise to co-sponsors is the portfolio's design target, not an afterthought.
2. Entry 1 ... the sharpest reading of the client (her income and the ladder's cost move together; the 2028 deposit is the fragile one) and the only entry that confesses its own weak point and builds a mechanism for it; thin on quantified uncertainty.
3. Entry 4 ... the same strategy and almost the same text as Entry 1, slightly less complete on where the reinvestment gap money sits and what the reserve contains.
4. Entry 5 ... the complete textbook answer: every Wharton question covered, assumptions and scenarios stated, nothing I had not already expected.
5. Entry 3 ... clean, well-ordered and honest about what certainty means, but it leaves its one real allocation decision (how to split the 2028 deposit) unstated and offers no view of varying market outcomes.
6. Entry 2 ... a sound ladder bolted to a conviction portfolio of sector bets, with a bond-sizing claim that does not add up and no stated assumptions or scenarios.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things. First, that the size of the stock sleeve can be set by a principle rather than a preference: put at risk only what the Treasury floor is expected to earn, so the 2028 money comes back whole in nominal terms even if stocks go to zero, and the team explains why this beats the bank's version of the same structure. Second, that Laura's second deposit is itself a risky asset correlated with the very conditions that make the ladder more expensive, which is a genuine piece of client knowledge that no other entry saw. The entry also names its own weak point, coupon reinvestment, and gives a dated mechanism for pricing and plugging the gap in 2031 out of the flexibility money before the gift is touched, which is exactly the kind of planned adjustment Wharton asks for. What holds it back: the portfolio is very conservative for a client who says she takes thoughtful risks and asked for a balance between growth and protection, and the trade-off is argued in one breath rather than weighed; the uncertainty around the range is described qualitatively (the bottom is owned, the top depends on half the stocks) with no confidence level or scenario; and the pharmacy metaphor spends words a judge is not scoring, though it does double as a commitment device not to sell rungs early, which is substantive. Nothing about the team's process.
- Confusion on first reading: the ladder is described as paying exactly what it promises and, later, as depending on reinvested coupons; the two reconcile once the weak-point paragraph is read, but on a single pass the first claim feels stronger than the second allows. I also could not tell how large the gift might be in a normal market, since the stock sleeve is under a tenth of the money and the floor is roughly the 2028 deposit less any top-up.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: nothing new. The separation of promised money from growth money is well explained and the 2031 half-lock and 25% flexibility floor are sensible. But the growth engine is a conviction portfolio of sector and single-country funds justified by a view that one industry will drive the next decade, which is precisely what Wharton says the goal is not; the Taiwan fund is linked to the residency by geography rather than by any argued purpose such as currency exposure. The ladder description claims each rung is sized so that its face value delivers the payment and that the whole ladder costs about $300,000; at the yields quoted, ten coupon-bearing Treasuries with $500,000 of face would cost far more than that unless the coupons are counted toward the payments, and the entry does not say so. There are no return assumptions, no scenarios, no inflation view, no mention of the 2028 deposit being uncertain, and reinvestment risk is waved off as very small. It reads clearly, which is why it is not lower.
- Confusion on first reading: how the ladder can be both face-value-sized and cost roughly $300,000; and what the top of the range being the expected value of the equity half means for how often Laura would fall short of her own top.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: one useful idea, that a high degree of certainty is best defined by what the payment does not depend on, taking the market out of the question rather than quoting a probability. The structure is the standard one done carefully: a ladder mixing defined-maturity Treasury funds and individual bonds, a late-2032 Treasury as the announced minimum, one world fund left alone, inflation reasoned through for both the fixed payments and the facility, and a reserve that shortens on its own. It is well organised under headings that map onto Wharton's questions. What holds it back: the only real decision in the plan, how much of the 2028 deposit goes to the floor and how much to stocks, is left as "one part" and "the rest"; the range's top is deferred to the Final Report with no sense of likelihood; there is no view of what unfavorable outcomes do to anything except "the upside"; the variability of the 2028 deposit is not mentioned; and the certainty claim quietly assumes coupons and fund payouts land where needed without saying how. Competent, and it would be in my top fifty in a weak year.
- Confusion on first reading: none of substance; I simply could not find the number or rule that fixes the floor-versus-growth split.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is Entry 1 with a handful of sentences changed, so everything I said about Entry 1 applies: the same sharp view of the client's second deposit, the same principle for sizing the stock sleeve, the same honesty about reinvestment. Judged on its own text, it adds a clear statement of priority order (payments, then floor, then everything above) that I liked as a decision rule. It also repeats its pitch's central sentence inside the IPS, which costs words without adding reasoning. Same weaknesses on quantified uncertainty and on the growth-versus-protection trade-off.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already expect, but I found nothing wrong either. This is the most complete coverage of Wharton's list: the ladder is sized correctly on principal plus coupons, the growth engine has a stated mix with a reason for its bond portion, rebalancing has a band, assumptions for stocks, Treasuries and inflation are written down, good, average and severe markets are said to be tested, the 2031 lock, the 2033 contribution cap and the flexibility fund are all specified, and there is an annual review. The client fit is argued through her credibility with collaborators, which is right. What holds it back for a top score is that the central decision rule is circular: the floor is whatever the team decides to lock, and what it locks is the bottom of the range, with no principle for how large that should be relative to the sleeve; the 2028 deposit's own uncertainty is not mentioned; currency is not mentioned; and the voice is a competent summary rather than a team thinking in front of me. The framework would work for almost any client with a dated liability and a surplus, which is both its strength and its ceiling.
- Confusion on first reading: none.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the 2031 communication problem can be turned into the portfolio's objective function. The announced minimum is not estimated from the portfolio; the portfolio is built to protect it, by a pre-committed rule that cuts stock exposure mechanically as the cushion above the floor shrinks, so that nobody has to decide anything in a falling market. That is a genuine idea about decision-making under uncertainty, and it is the only entry that takes Wharton's warning against rewriting strategy when markets move and makes it structural. The analysis is the strongest here: the multiplier is chosen against a stated monthly loss that would breach the floor, the cash-lock failure mode is named and accepted with a reason, the confidence in the minimum and the odds on the top are both stated, the 2028 deposit's variability and the Taiwan-dollar exposure are each given a home, and assumptions carry volatility as well as return. The reserve is the standard ladder, correctly described. What holds it back: the starting floor at about 60% of the sleeve is asserted rather than justified, so the growth sleeve is fully in stocks on day one with only a fraction of the 2028 money protected and the reader has to trust the number; monthly rule-based trading is more machinery than any other entry and the IPS never asks whether Laura would understand or want to be run by a rule; and the writing is a specification with bullet points rather than a narrative, which Wharton's presentation criterion does ask for. Articulation is a little higher than the others because the entry says what it chose and why in several places.
- Confusion on first reading: how the announced minimum in 2031 is actually determined (I infer it is roughly the floor that has been accruing since 2028, so the minimum is whatever the rule has protected by then, but the entry does not say this in one place); and whether the half-of-gain rule for the 2033 contribution is measured from the minimum or from the original sleeve.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reached what I expected every competent team to reach: buy the ten payments with Treasuries held to maturity and invest the rest. What separated the top from the middle was whether the team had an idea about the part that is actually uncertain, the growth money and the promise built on it. Entry 6 did: it made the promise the thing the portfolio is engineered to protect, chose a rule rather than discretion, and told me how the rule fails and why it accepts that. Entries 1 and 4 did too, in a different register: they understood that the client's own income is the risky asset in this case and sized the stock sleeve by a principle rather than a preference, and they were the only entries honest enough to name the ladder's reinvestment weakness and plan for it. Entry 5 answered every question and taught me nothing; Entry 3 was tidy but left its one real decision blank; Entry 2 drifted into picking winners, which Wharton explicitly says is not the task. I note that Entries 1 and 4 are substantially the same text and judged them as submitted. For Entry 6 to win outright it would need to justify its starting floor, show in plain prose that Laura herself could live with a rule that may park her growth money in bills for two years, and say in one place how the announced minimum is fixed. For Entry 1 to overtake it, it would need to put numbers on the range's uncertainty and argue, rather than assert, why so little of a risk-tolerant client's money should carry risk.
