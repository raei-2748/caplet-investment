# Judge J3 scores

(Priya, pension actuary. Method: trace the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the 2031 range, the 2033 contribution and the ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042 through each plan, and find where it breaks or where the reasoning is waved through. Reference point I used for the ladder: at Treasury yields of about 4.5-5%, ten $50,000 payments six to fifteen years out are worth roughly $300,000-$320,000 at the start of 2027, so the first deposit buys the commitment with almost nothing to spare, and only if the coupons paid before 2033 are reinvested.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5: the only plan where every dollar has a home, and it prices the ladder's one real hole (coupon reinvestment) and puts the money set aside for it inside the operating reserve.
2. Entry 1: almost the same plan and the same insight, but the money set aside for the reinvestment gap is left floating ("from the stocks Laura keeps") and never joins the reserve.
3. Entry 2: a genuine pre-committed rule (CPPI) with stated parameters and an honest breach analysis, but the ladder's reinvestment risk is missed and the promised minimum is only ~95% safe.
4. Entry 3: sound "promise first, then grow" structure with a cap and a flexibility rule; the floor's size is never pinned down and it claims rate swings "never touch" the payments.
5. Entry 4: right present-value intuition and a floor bought early, but it overclaims certainty and leaves the upside share and flexibility unsized.
6. Entry 6: the ladder as described (face value $50,000 a rung, coupons as a spare cushion) cannot cost "close to $300,000"; the growth sleeve is concentrated in assets correlated with the project.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The cash flows trace cleanly: the $300,000 buys the ladder, any shortfall is the first claim on the 2028 deposit, most of the rest buys a Treasury maturing late 2032 as the floor, and only about the floor's expected interest goes into one world stock fund. The 2031 range is built so that every promised dollar is already in bonds before it is spoken, which is the right instinct for a public commitment. It is the only entry besides Entry 5 that sees the ladder costs less than $500,000 *because* coupons are reinvested, that this is a rate risk, and that it must be priced and funded before the range is named, with a clear order of who absorbs the loss (flexibility first, then the range). It also links her deposit risk to her career risk, which shows real client thinking. What holds it back: the reinvestment cushion is "set aside from the stocks Laura keeps" but never said to be moved into bonds or added to the operating reserve, so the reserve definition in 2033 (ladder only) does not include the money meant to plug the ladder's gap. Flexibility is also very thin: about half of a stock sleeve that is under a tenth of her money, minus the gap cushion, so in a falling-rate world she has almost nothing left after the gift; they choose that trade-off knowingly, but they do not discuss whether it is enough for her.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: since the top of the range is also moved into Treasuries in 2031, the 2033 contribution is nearly fixed by then; I had to work out that the "range" is really only covering the reinvestment gap. No confidence level is stated.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This team uses a real portfolio-insurance tool and specifies it properly: floor, multiplier, monthly rebalancing, the 100% cap forced by the margin ban, and the size of a one-month fall needed to breach it (about a third), with a reason for choosing three over five. They name cash-lock, gap risk, the 2028 deposit's income source and Taiwan-dollar currency risk, and say who pays for each. That is the most honest risk register of the six. But the reserve section says the only remaining risk is US default, which is not true: the coupons paid 2027-2032 have to be reinvested for the ladder to cover $500,000 from about $300,000, and nothing prices that. "Maturing each January" also leaves open whether a mid-January maturity arrives after a start-of-January payment. On the facility side, a minimum protected to "at least 95%" by a trading rule is weaker than a minimum already sitting in a Treasury, for a number Laura says aloud to co-sponsors; and a top set at the median means only about even odds of landing in the range, which the case asks them to state with confidence.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor moves from "60% of the sleeve, accruing at the short yield" to "the present value of the announced minimum" in 2031: who picks the minimum, and can it be set above the floor the rule has been defending?

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure is sound and the ladder is described correctly ("principal plus coupons covers every payment"), with the 2028 deposit as backstop and a cost that matches the arithmetic (about two-thirds of total contributions). The facility rule is coherent: lock the bottom of the range into Treasuries before 2031, give the floor plus at most half the rest, capped at the announced top, and keep the remainder as flexibility; there is a rebalancing band and an annual review. What holds it back for me: coupons held in short-term Treasuries until needed are exactly the reinvestment exposure, yet the entry says rate swings never touch the payments. How much of the growth sleeve becomes the floor in 2031 is never sized, so I cannot tell how much of an 80% equity sleeve the promise rests on. "Good, average and severe markets" is three scenarios, not a confidence level, and the 2028 deposit's own uncertainty and the Taiwan-dollar exposure are not discussed.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The team understands present value ($500,000 of future payments costing a little under $300,000) and builds the ladder with care, checking maturity dates and mixing maturing Treasury funds with individual bonds. Buying the facility minimum in 2028 instead of 2031 protects the promise from three extra years of equity risk, and the inflation point (the payments are nominal, facility costs are not) is put in the right place. But "we are removing the market from the question" overclaims: the maturing funds pay out monthly and end at a value that is not contractually fixed, the bonds maturing "a few months earlier" leave cash to reinvest, and the pre-2033 coupons that make the ladder affordable are never mentioned. The split of the 2028 deposit between floor and stocks, the "share" of the stock fund in the top of the range, and the "part" kept as flexibility are all left unsized, and the likelihood of the top is put off to the Final Report, so the framework is clear in shape but not in the numbers that would show it holds.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the share of the stock fund counted in the top of the range is moved into bonds in 2031 or left exposed until 2033.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry where I could not find an unpriced hole. Each cash flow has a stated destination and a stated order of priority: ladder first, then the floor, then a small stock sleeve sized to the floor's expected interest, with the 2028 deposit finishing any unbought rung. It identifies the one risk a held-to-maturity ladder still carries, coupon reinvestment, prices it in 2031 at that day's yields with a cushion, moves that money into Treasuries and makes it part of the operating reserve, so the reserve's composition in 2033 actually matches the liability. It also forbids selling a rung for the building, and every dollar of the announced range is in bonds before Laura names it, so a bad market can only shrink money that was never promised. The career-as-a-stock argument for why the 2028 deposit is unreliable is real client analysis. What holds it back: this is a very low-risk plan for a client the case calls "willing to take thoughtful risks", and the flexibility left after the gift is thin and is the first thing the reinvestment gap eats; the team states this trade-off but does not test whether what remains is enough for overruns. No confidence level is given for the range, though their structure makes it close to certain.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: same as Entry 1: with the top locked in 2031, the range is mostly a cushion for the reinvestment gap rather than a market range; it took a second pass to see that.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The separation of "certain money" and "growth money" is the right starting point, and moving half the growth sleeve into Treasuries before 2031 is a reasonable protection for the floor. But the ladder does not work as written: if each rung's face value is $50,000, the ten bonds carry $500,000 of face plus coupons on top, which costs far more than $300,000 (roughly $400,000 or more unless coupons are near zero, and zero-coupon STRIPS are banned). Either the coupons are needed to make the payments, in which case reinvestment risk is not "very small", or they are a spare cushion, in which case the ladder eats most of the 2028 deposit and the growth engine the plan depends on is much smaller than shown. The growth sleeve then puts 30% into Nasdaq, semiconductors and Taiwan, which largely overlap the S&P core and tie the facility money to the same region and industry as the project's own risk, presented as a market conviction, not a diversified plan. The top of the range is the expected value of all remaining equity while the rules keep at least a quarter of the engine back, so the top cannot be reached by their own rule; no return assumptions, confidence level, inflation or 2028-deposit risk are given.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a ladder of $50,000-face rungs, with coupons left over as a cushion, costs "close to" $300,000.

## My overall view
Five of six teams reached the same sound skeleton (a Treasury ladder for the ten payments, growth money for the facility), so the skeleton was not what separated them. What separated them was whether they understood that a coupon ladder bought six years before the first payment is not risk-free: about $300,000 covers $500,000 of payments only because the coupons are reinvested, and that reinvestment is exposed to falling rates. Entries 1 and 5 saw it, priced it and said who pays; the rest called the payments certain and stopped. After that, the question was whether the 2031 promise is already money in bonds or a model's estimate, and whether the team stated plainly who absorbs each loss. Entry 5 is the best because it closes every loop, including placing the gap money inside the operating reserve. To win outright it needs to defend its weakest client fit: show that the small stock sleeve and the thin flexibility left after the gift are a deliberate, tested choice for a risk-taking entrepreneur, not just the cautious result of protecting the promise, and give the range a stated confidence level.
