# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1 - the only plan that treats the growth sleeve as a real risk budget, with equity exposure that scales with the cushion and an honest account of the upside it gives away (cash-lock).
2. Entry 2 - clean ladder plus an 80% equity growth sleeve; ambitious where Laura can afford it, with a clear glide path and a stated risk test, though it never prices the safety it buys.
3. Entry 3 - sound, plain ladder-plus-world-stock plan that covers every question, but the equity share is never sized and the growth ambition is muted.
4. Entry 5 - the most equity-hungry on paper, but the growth is thematic bets (semis, Nasdaq, Taiwan) with no return, risk or downside reasoning behind them.
5. Entry 4 - careful on the ladder and the reinvestment gap, but stocks are under a tenth of her money; this is timidity dressed up as prudence.
6. Entry 6 - same plan as Entry 4 with slightly less reasoning on the client's income risk; same timidity.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Reading it as a whole, this is the one entry where the growth money is actually managed as growth money. The ladder secures the ten payments, and the sleeve then runs a pre-committed rule that holds more stock when the cushion is large and cuts it when the cushion shrinks, capped at 100% because margin is banned. That gives Laura meaningful upside participation over six years while still defending the number she tells co-sponsors in 2031. It names what the approach costs (cash-lock, missing a recovery, gap risk, and why the multiplier is three and not five), which is exactly the honesty about given-away upside I look for. It also links the range, the 95% confidence on the minimum and the roughly even odds on the top to simulated paths, so the confidence statement has a basis. What holds it back is density: a monthly rule, floors and multipliers are a lot for a 500-word statement that Wharton wants framed as a decision framework, and the "floor starts near 60% of the sleeve" figure is not explained. Some judges will find it heavy, but the logic holds together and fits a client who takes thoughtful risks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Why the floor starts at roughly 60% of the sleeve and then switches in 2031 to the present value of the announced minimum; how those two floors connect.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A well-organised answer to every question Wharton asks: the ladder is explained and defined as "high certainty", the growth sleeve is about 80% global stocks with a sensible rebalancing band, the 2031 floor-locking and the 2033 handover are stated, and the facility rule (floor plus at most half the growth, capped at the announced top) protects flexibility. I like that it is willing to hold real equity risk for five years on the grounds that the payments are already secured, and it says plainly that a crash shrinks the gift, not the payments. It falls short because it never prices the safety it buys (what the ladder costs Laura in expected growth versus a more aggressive split) and the 20% intermediate-Treasury shock absorber is not justified against the horizon. The "stocks as best defence against Taiwan construction costs" claim is asserted rather than tested. Still a competent, credible plan that a judge could follow in one read.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: None.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A sound, readable plan: ladder first, with a sensible split between iBond ETFs for the early rungs and individual Treasuries for the later ones, then a floor Treasury for 2032 and the rest in one low-cost world fund left alone to 2033. It gives a thoughtful definition of high certainty ("not depending on any forecast"), addresses inflation and flexibility under their own headings, and fits Laura's profile (risk with ideas, not with promises). The weakness is that the growth side is thin: no size for the equity piece, only a loose 6-7% assumption and a passing mention of -30% years, and no stated confidence for the range (deferred to the Final Report). It is competent, fairly conservative, and does not show much about the upside being given up, so it reads as safe rather than analysed.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How big the VT holding is relative to the floor Treasury; "part of the VT holding" for flexibility is not quantified.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The client reasoning is the best of the conservative entries: it spots that Laura's 2028 deposit is correlated with the very downturn that makes a ladder costly, and it names the coupon-reinvestment shortfall and sets money aside for it before the range is announced. But strategically, the stock allocation is only the interest the floor should earn, under a tenth of her money, for a woman described as willing to take thoughtful risks with six years to 2033 and living costs covered elsewhere. It acknowledges a bigger gift is possible by putting the floor in stocks and waves it away in one sentence, without sizing what that safety costs. The capital-protected-note framing is a tidy way to say the same thing, but the result is a very small expected gift. The brand story (XR, "do not crush") takes up a lot of the word budget and does not add analysis. A careful, honest plan, but it leaves most of the growth on the table.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How a floor of "most of the rest" plus a remainder equal to "roughly the interest" ends up as under a tenth of her money; the arithmetic is implied, not shown.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It separates the two kinds of money well, and the ladder logic is fine. But the growth engine is a 70% S&P 500 core with 10% each in QQQ, a semiconductor ETF and Taiwan, justified by a conviction that AI will drive the next decade. That is a forecast, not a strategy, and Wharton explicitly says the goal is not to pick investments you expect to perform well. The satellites double up on US large-cap tech already inside the core, the whole sleeve is nearly all US plus a Taiwan bet, and there is no return assumption, volatility, downside case or confidence level anywhere. The 2031 top is "expected value of the remaining equity", with no statement of how likely it is. It calls reinvestment risk "very small" without support. Appetite for growth is right; the reasoning to earn it is missing, and the bets add concentration risk to a client whose payments are meant to be safe.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Why Taiwan equity exposure is meant to help (hedge for the facility cost, or a return bet); and how the 25% "at least" flexibility share relates to the floor.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the plan took the growth half of Laura's problem seriously. Everyone can buy a Treasury ladder to cover ten fixed payments; that part is table stakes. The question is what you do with the roughly $150-190k that is left and six years to run it, for a client who takes thoughtful risks and whose living costs are covered elsewhere. Entry 1 builds a rule that lets the sleeve work harder when it is safe to and says in plain terms what it gives up. Entry 2 is not far behind, with a plain 80% equity engine. Entries 4 and 6 are well-argued but shrink the equity to under a tenth of her money and never count the cost of that safety. Entry 5 is the reverse failure: lots of equity, but chosen on a hunch about semiconductors. To win, Entry 1 would need to cut the mechanics down to a framework a judge can hold in one read, explain the 60% starting floor, and put a number on what the ladder costs against a fuller-equity alternative.
