# Judge J1 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: the easiest to remember and repeat. The "do not crush" idea and the plain rule that the cheques come first, with the stock fund kept small, stuck with me, and the entry explains why the 2028 deposit should not fund the cheques.
2. Entry 5: almost the same entry as Entry 1, with a cleaner "the cheques, then the floor, then everything above it" order. I would not be able to tell them apart a week later.
3. Entry 3: the most orderly read. Two numbered principles, a clear definition of "high certainty", and a plain account of how the portfolio changes by 2033.
4. Entry 2: clear, sensible and well signposted, but it reads like a standard textbook split of bonds and stocks, with little to make it stand out.
5. Entry 6: a bold theme (Taiwan and AI as the growth story) that gives the entry some personality, but the growth engine is concentrated and the 2031 and 2033 steps are hard to follow.
6. Entry 4: the most rigorous on paper, and the one I understood least. The growth-sleeve rule and the simulation language lost me, and I would not feel able to explain it to a friend.

## My overall view
The top three were the ones I could explain back to a friend after one read. Entries 1 and 5 are almost the same strategy with the same hook, and what separates them from the rest is that the promise comes first and is explained as a promise, not as an investment bet: Laura's cheques are bought with Treasuries, the stock money is small and kept away from them, and the entry says why her income is a reason not to gamble the 2028 deposit. Entry 3 is the most orderly and gives the clearest structure. The middle group (2, 6) is sensible or bold but generic or hard to follow, and Entry 4 is rigorous but written for a finance reader. For the best entry to win me over it would need to keep this plain logic, explain the 2031 range and the reinvestment gap in one clear picture a friend could repeat, and show what the team itself learned along the way, since no entry does that well.
