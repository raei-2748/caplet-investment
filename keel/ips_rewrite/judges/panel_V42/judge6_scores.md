# Judge J6 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: answers all five of Wharton's questions with one clear mechanism, and is the only entry that states a confidence figure for the 2031 range and shows the cost of the ladder's one weakness in plain numbers.
2. Entry 6: the most rigorous entry (a rule fixed in advance, a stated confidence for the range floor, and every accepted risk written down), but it reads more like a model specification than a story.
3. Entry 2: the same plan as Entry 1 in a sharper voice; it covers the range protection well but gives no range confidence figure.
4. Entry 5: the same plan again; it has the best account of the team's own learning, but the top of its range is not protected once named.
5. Entry 3: clear, conventional and well diversified; the 2031 range has no stated confidence figure and the thesis is not distinctive.
6. Entry 4: the most original idea (staged options), but its facility tranche runs past the 2033 decision point, the growth sleeve is concentrated, and the range has no confidence level.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: The entry answers Wharton's five questions in an order a judge can follow: the promise and the hope are kept apart, the ten cheques are bought up front, the 2028 deposit is the backstop, the surplus goes to one world index fund, the reserve runs itself down from 2033, and the 2031 range is stated with its failure route. Its client insight is the strongest in the set: Laura's advances and speaking income fall in the same recessions that make bonds dear, so her own risk already sits in the residency and the cheques should not depend on markets. It gives a stated confidence (the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100) and says what the rare miss costs, which is the kind of uncertainty statement Wharton asks for. What holds it back is clarity in a few places: the "$125,000" line does not say whether it is the deposit used or the amount left, and the "one in thirty" for the three-quarters plan sits oddly beside "one in three" for the 60/40, which are different tests. The growth piece is one fund, and the model's volatility and other assumptions are not given, so the numbers have to be taken on trust.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: Whether "about $125,000 still does" means the amount of the deposit used or what is left over. What money is "priced and set aside in 2031" for the ladder's leak. Why the 75%-stock plan misses one cheque in thirty while the 60/40 misses one time in three.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the same plan as Entry 1, told with more punch: "we don't invest the promise, we buy it" is a thesis a judge will remember. The calendar argument (ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill, so a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom) is the clearest reason in the set for why a stock-heavy plan is wrong for this client. ### Entry 4

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the most rigorous entry and the only one that uses investment tools in a way the judge can test. It sets a rule fixed in advance (a floor, a multiplier of three, a monthly check, the sleeve capped at 100% because margin is banned) and gives a reason for the multiplier. What holds it back is readability: it reads like a model specification more than a plan for a client, the jargon (CPPI, cushion, present value) is not explained, and Laura's story is thin, with a short "why Laura" paragraph. The growth sleeve is one world ETF, and the entry shows little of the team's own learning.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: The rule itself was clear, but not how a present value in 2031 becomes the 2033 minimum, or why the floor accrues at the short Treasury yield.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the entry shows how the range Laura gives co-sponsors is protected and how sure the team is that it holds. Entries 1 and 6 do this with figures and a stated confidence. The others describe the mechanism but leave the confidence out, and Entry 4 never gets to a confidence level at all. Entries 1, 2 and 5 are the same plan, with near-identical figures and several phrases in common. I scored each on its own text, as the brief asks, but a panel should not see one plan three times, and the office should check whether these drafts came from different teams. To win, the best entry would need what Entry 1 nearly has: one clear mechanism for the 2031 range, a stated confidence, the cost of its one weakness shown in plain figures, and a voice a co-sponsor could repeat. It would also need the team's learning to show in the IPS itself, since Articulation can only be read from what the IPS shows.
