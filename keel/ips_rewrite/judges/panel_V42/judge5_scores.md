# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid (decision science). Method: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a round-robin of pairwise comparisons on Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions, and ranked by wins. My prior, stated up front: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is the baseline most competent teams will reach. It earns a pass, not a prize. Only a genuinely new idea, a sharper way of seeing Laura's problem, or a well-argued trade-off moves an entry up.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 2 (5 wins) - the announced 2031 minimum becomes the mechanical floor of a pre-committed rule; the only entry that keeps real growth and still explains, with numbers, which risks it accepts and why.
2. Entry 3 (4 wins) - the sharpest reading of the client's problem (her 2028 income and the ladder's price move against her together); a home-made capital-protected note, with a priced counterfactual; growth nearly abandoned.
3. Entry 4 (3 wins) - the same strategy as Entry 3, argued cleanly, with the 2031 de-risking step but without a stated confidence level or the coupon-reinvestment leak.
4. Entry 6 (2 wins) - the same strategy a third time; the best version of the argument against 60/40, the thinnest on what actually happens between 2031 and 2033.
5. Entry 5 (1 win) - the most genuinely new idea in the set (stage-gated contribution as real options), let down by thin analysis, an unexplained seed size and a growth sleeve chosen by past performance and affinity.
6. Entry 1 (0 wins) - the baseline done well: complete, orderly, nothing I did not already expect.

Pairwise results (winner first): 2>1, 2>3, 2>4, 2>5, 2>6; 3>1, 3>4, 3>5, 3>6; 4>1, 4>5, 4>6; 6>1, 6>5; 5>1. The closest calls were 2 vs 3 and 6 vs 5; I explain both below.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe (leaning no)
- What I learned: nothing I had not assumed a competent team would reach. The entry is the baseline: a maturity-matched ladder, an 80/20 growth sleeve with annual rebalancing, the floor of the 2031 range moved into Treasuries, facility gift = floor plus at most half the growth sleeve, the rest kept for flexibility. It answers every one of Wharton's IPS questions in order, states its assumptions, and the reasoning is sound throughout; a client would understand it. What holds it back is that the reasoning is asserted rather than argued: "we test outcomes in good, average and severe markets" is the only sign of analysis, and no trade-off is weighed against an alternative. The claim that stocks are Laura's best defence against Taiwanese construction costs is offered without support, and the 80/20 choice is not justified over any other split. Of the six entries this is the one I could have written before opening the file, which is exactly the problem at a 2% cut.
- Confused on first reading: the ladder costs "roughly two-thirds of Laura's total contributions", which is the whole first deposit, yet the next clause plans for the first deposit "falling short"; I had to work out that the two statements are about the same margin. Also, what "stated, modelled confidence level" the top of the range would carry is left open.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the number Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 can be made the thing the portfolio is mechanically built to protect, rather than a forecast of it. The reserve is a standard ladder, bought with the first deposit, and the entry says plainly that certainty means dependence on no stock return and no market price. The growth sleeve runs a pre-committed constant-proportion rule whose floor becomes, from 2031, the present value of the announced minimum; stock exposure is cut by rule, not by a committee, when the cushion shrinks. This is the only entry that keeps real growth capital for Laura while still being able to say how the facility promise is protected, and it is the only one that lists the risks it is accepting with reasons: the multiplier is three rather than five because of one-month gap risk, cash-lock is accepted because the promise still holds, the 2028 deposit is claimed by the ladder first, currency is paid from the flexibility share. Confidence is stated at both ends of the range (at least 95% on the minimum, roughly even odds on the top). What holds it back: the starting floor of about 60% of the sleeve is not justified, the entry is dense for a once-through reading (cushion, multiplier, CPPI), and it says little about Laura herself beyond the deposit and the currency. Articulation is modest but real: choosing three over five is a visible decision.
- Confused on first reading: how the 60% starting floor was chosen, and what happens at 2031 if the present value of the minimum Laura wants to announce is above the floor the rule has been carrying, i.e. whether the announcement is constrained by the rule or the rule reset by the announcement.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things. First, that Laura's two apparently separate risks are one risk: the recession that makes the ladder dearer is the same recession that shrinks the publishing, speaking and licensing income behind her 2028 deposit, so the deposit is weakest exactly when the gap is largest. That is a sharper way of seeing the client than any other entry managed, and it drives the design (the payments use money she already has). Second, the reframing of the liability as ten one-year bills rather than one ten-year bill, which is why a diversified 60/40 fails here when it would succeed for a patient investor. The structure built on these is a floor of Treasuries that grow back into the second deposit by 2032 with only the expected interest in a world index fund, which the entry correctly identifies as a capital-protected note without the bank. The trade-off is argued with numbers from the team's own model: 60/40 usually leaves her richer, but about one time in three misses a cheque or lands below its own range; the ladder's coupon-reinvestment leak is named and dealt with in 2031; the top of the range is reached about 97 times in 100 and the de-risking step on announcement day is explained. What holds it back: growth is all but abandoned (stocks around a fifteenth of the portfolio) and the flexibility that remains, half of a small stock holding, is thin against a case that explicitly worries about limiting her flexibility; the entry argues she has spent her risk budget on the residency, which is a position, but it means the "balance" Wharton asks for is answered mostly on one side. The pill metaphor and acronym cost me a moment and add nothing to the argument.
- Confused on first reading: "even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does" took two readings to parse as "a deposit of $125,000 would still fill the gap"; "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" is not explained; and I could not tell where the money for the coupon leak set aside in 2031 comes from.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes (marginal)
- This is the same strategy as Entry 3 with the same two insights (correlated recession risk; ten one-year bills), the same capital-protected-note structure, the same 60/40 counterfactual with roughly the same numbers, and the same first-cheques-then-floor-then-hope priority. It argues it more cleanly and defines high certainty as "a short list of the ways it fails", which is a good decision-science habit. It keeps the 2031 de-risking step (the promised half moves to Treasuries on naming day) and names the Taiwan-dollar exposure as the use of the retained half. What it has less of than Entry 3: no stated confidence for the range, no mention of the coupon-reinvestment leak, and nothing on inflation or on what happens to coupons before 2033. The same reservations apply about near-zero growth and small flexibility. Of the two near-identical entries I rank it below 3 because 3 shows more of its analysis; a judge who reads once would otherwise find them interchangeable.
- Confused on first reading: "a named top can only be missed" needed a second pass; whether "the whole sum" the floor grows back into is the full second deposit or what remains after plugging the ladder.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution need not be one decision in 2033. Treating it as three tranches, each released by a gate tied to markets and to evidence about the project, is the most genuinely new idea in the set, and the entry is right that this is how co-sponsors and venture investors already think. The runway (ladder) section is standard and sound. What holds it back is execution. The seed tranche, which is the whole of Laura's credible promise, has no stated size or sizing principle, and the Gate 2 "target" is undefined, so the entry never actually says how the range would be derived or how confident it is. Gate 2 makes her second tranche conditional on co-sponsors committing first, which inverts the signalling logic the case describes (her commitment is meant to draw theirs), and Gate 3 in 2034-2035 sits outside the 2033 facility decision the case frames. The growth sleeve of 75% world stocks and 25% a technology index is justified by past leadership and by Laura's own career, which is performance-chasing plus affinity, and it reduces diversification without a stated reason tied to her goals. Portfolio analysis is a single sentence ("about 7% a year with deep drawdowns possible"). Had the staging idea been given the analytic care Entry 2 gave its rule, this would have been my favourite.
- Confused on first reading: how big the seed tranche is; what "above its target" means; whether a tranche paid after the residency opens still counts as the facility contribution Laura promises in 2031.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The third rendering of the Entry 3/4 strategy. Its strength is the way it builds the case against itself first: the 60/40 alternative is given its due (usually $200,000 richer by 2042) before the one-in-three failure rate and the timetable argument are brought in, and the point that Laura's risk appetite has already been spent on the residency is made well. The correlated-recession insight and the two-item failure list are here too. What holds it back is the second half: once the range is named there is no de-risking step, no stated confidence for the floor or the top, and no account of what happens to the stock half between 2031 and 2033, so the question "how does the portfolio change as funding needs approach" is answered only by "rungs mature". It spends words on quotation and metaphor that its siblings spent on mechanism. Against Entry 5 it was a close call: 6 has deeper client reasoning and real numbers; 5 has a newer idea and a clearer timeline. I gave it to 6 because Wharton asks for recommendations that can earn Laura's confidence and for credible communication to co-sponsors, and 6's owned floor does that where 5's conditional tranches do not.
- Confused on first reading: what the de-risking plan is after 2031; what confidence attaches to the range; the closing instruction, which I took as a metaphor for the hold-to-maturity rule but had to infer.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries reach the baseline I expected: buy the ten payments with Treasuries held to maturity, define certainty as dependence on the US government alone, and keep the rest for the gift. That part no longer separates anyone. What separated the top from the rest was whether the team had seen something about Laura's problem that the baseline misses and then paid for it with an argued trade-off. Entry 2 saw that the 2031 announcement can be engineered rather than forecast, kept genuine growth in play, and told me which risks it was accepting and why; that is the closest to a complete decision framework. Entries 3, 4 and 6 saw that her income and the ladder's price are one correlated risk and reframed the liability as ten short bills, which is the best piece of client insight on the table, but they bought safety at the price of nearly all growth and most flexibility, and only Entry 3 told me how confident to be. Entry 5 had the newest idea and the least analysis behind it. For Entry 2 to win outright it would need to justify its starting floor, say in one sentence what Laura's flexibility looks like in dollars rather than in mechanism, and be readable once by someone who has never heard the word cushion. For the Entry 3 family to beat it, it would need to answer the growth question rather than dismiss it: show what the retained half is worth in the bad and good cases, and explain why a fifteenth in stocks is the right amount for a client the case calls a thoughtful risk-taker, not just a safe one.
