# Judge J4 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5 - same capital-protected idea as 1 and 6, but told most plainly, with the team's changed mind, the catch in the capped range and a cost-benefit it admits.
2. Entry 4 - the most technical and the most candid about how its own rule can fail; reads like students who ran the simulation and found the cracks.
3. Entry 6 - honest case against itself and good reasoning, but the headings and refrains make it read as performed.
4. Entry 1 - the same substance as 6; the "short list of ways it fails" lines and headings sound more like a copywriter than a team.

## Per entry
### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is a real strategy: buy the ten payments with a Treasury ladder, use the 2028 deposit for a floor that grows back to its own value, and send only the expected interest into one world index fund. It ties the payments, the 2031 range and the 2033 reserve together and says what happens first if something breaks. It is honest that a 60/40 portfolio usually ends richer and says why the team still declines, and it names the one real weak point (a bad recession before purchase hitting both ladder cost and the 2028 deposit). What holds it back for me is the voice. The headings and the lines that announce their own cleverness make it read as written to be admired, not to explain, and I trust a plain explanation more. The numbers carry "our model" or "by our sums" labels, which helps, but I cannot see the working.
- Confusion: "even after a two-point fall in yields, about $125,000 of it still does" - I had to reread to see that $125,000 is the deposit size that still fills the gap. Also, "a named top can only be missed" took a second read.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 4/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- A sensible, conventional plan: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments, 80/20 stocks and Treasuries above it, a floor locked in 2031, and a half-of-the-gain rule for the gift. It defines high certainty, states return assumptions and says a crash shrinks only the gift. All of that is correct, and none of it tells me what these students found out. The 80% stock sleeve is justified by "best defence against rising construction costs" and "shock absorber" without any test that I can see. The bold-label structure and the language are those of a template. Little in it is wrong, and little in it is evidence of thinking.
- Confusion: none, except how "roughly two-thirds" of contributions arises and how the 2031 floor is sized.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The ladder for the payments is right, and the idea of releasing the gift in stages is a creative way to think about flexibility. But the surplus is 75% VT and 25% QQQ justified by "innovation-driven companies have led global returns", which is hindsight and a concentration bet, and a poor fit for a client whose 2028 deposit already depends on her own creative business. The gates depend on co-sponsor commitments and "portfolio above its target", which are not investment facts the team can model, and a tranche in 2034-35 sits oddly against a 2033 facility. No stress test, no probabilities and no numbers to judge it by. The startup metaphor does more work than the analysis.
- Confusion: how a later tranche is "paid" when co-sponsors have not yet committed, and how the 2031 range is sized.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The central idea (the number told to co-sponsors is the number the portfolio is built to protect) is genuinely connected to the case, and a CPPI rule is a thoughtful way to deliver it. The strongest thing is the "Risks we accept" list: gap risk with a stated multiplier choice, cash-lock, the variable 2028 deposit and the currency problem. Few teams here admit their own mechanism can fail. The cost is complexity: a monthly rule with a 60% starting floor needs real trust from a reader, the team never says why it beats simply buying the floor in 2028, and the sleeve could end up in cash for years. Reads as written by students who built it, a little dense for a first-time reader, and the voice is more engineer than team.
- Confusion: how "half the gain above it" in 2033 fits the 2031 top of range, and why 60% was the starting floor.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- I believe these students ran something and changed their minds. They started with three-quarters stocks, saw about one future in thirty miss a cheque, and say so plainly. The reasoning is in ordinary words: ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill; Laura "has already taken the big one: the residency"; her income falls in the same recessions that make bonds dear. It also names a catch in its own capped range (a fall comes off the gift, a rise past the top goes to Laura), says what they do about it, and gives a size for the rare miss. The 60/40 comparison is stated both ways. The pill jokes are a little much, but they are lighter here than in the others. This reads closest to something a teenager could write after doing the work.
- Confusion: "Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does" - still does what? I worked out it means a deposit of $125,000 still fills the ladder gap. The "one leak" about early cash earning less could be clearer.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The same strategy as 1 and 5, and the argument is good: it states the best case against itself (60/40 usually ends almost $200,000 ahead), then answers it with the one-in-three failure and the calendar problem. Quoting the case on certainty and thoughtful risks shows it read the client. But three jokey headings, a closing "Do not crush." and the repeated "We don't invest the promise. We buy it." give the feel of a campaign, not a team's explanation. Substance is equal to Entry 1; trust is a notch lower than Entry 5.
- Confusion: "A bird in the hand, half the bush" as a heading did not tell me what the section would say; the $125,000 vs $150,000 line is clear enough.

## My overall view
Three of these six are the same plan, which tells me it is a good one; I judge how each team explains it, not that it won. What separates them for me is whether I hear students working something out or a team selling an idea. Entry 5 says what the team first believed, what broke it, what the catch is in its own design and what it costs, in sentences a teacher could imagine a seventeen-year-old writing. Entry 4 earns respect for admitting where its rule fails. Entry 2 is safe and anonymous; Entry 3 is a metaphor without a model. To win, Entry 5 would drop the remaining pill gags, clear up the $125,000 sentence, and show one more piece of the working behind its numbers in plain words (where the "one in thirty" and "97 in 100" come from). Entry 4 would need to say why it beats simply buying the floor in 2028.
