# Judge J3 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: the only entry that finds and prices the ladder's hidden leak, locks what it promises when it promises it, and says how often the range holds.
2. Entry 6: the most rigorous risk mechanics (a stated dynamic rule, its failure modes named and sized), but the range top is a coin flip and the ladder's reinvestment leak is not seen.
3. Entry 4: the same sound liability-first design as Entry 1, with the 2031 lock, but it does not price the reinvestment leak or state how reliable the range is.
4. Entry 2: the same design again, and it sets out its trade-off honestly, but it never says when the "half her stocks" is fixed, so the 2031 range is loose.
5. Entry 3: complete and sensible, and it answers every question, but almost none of it is quantified, and it says rate swings never touch the payments, which is not quite true.
6. Entry 5: an original framing, but the gift is pushed past 2033 and tied to co-sponsors in a circular way, and the stock sleeve is tilted toward Laura's own industry.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- When I trace the cash flows, this is the plan that holds. $300k buys the ten payments in 2027. The 2028 deposit fills any gap first, then buys a floor that grows back to its own size by 2033, and only the floor's expected interest (about $30k, which fits "a fifteenth" of ~$450k) goes into stocks. It is the only entry that spots the leak I look for first: coupons received before they are needed must be reinvested at unknown rates. It sets that money aside in 2031, before the range is named. It also sees the asymmetry of a capped range (losses come off the gift, gains past the top go to Laura) and closes it by moving the promised half into Treasuries on the day the range is named. It then gives a modelled confidence for reaching the top. Its point about risk capacity is good actuarial thinking: her 2028 income shrinks in the same recessions that make the ladder dearer. What holds it back: total equity is very small for a client "willing to take thoughtful risks", and flexibility after 2033 is only about half a ~$30-40k stock pot. That is thin against the case's warning about committing all remaining assets, and the plan doesn't argue that the amount is enough.
- Not understood on first reading: "Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does." It is unclear whether $125k is the smallest deposit that still fills the ladder gap or the money left after filling it. On my rough numbers a two-point fall widens the gap by roughly $60k, so neither reading fits cleanly. I also could not tell whether "one time in three" for the 60/40 counts missed payments or only landing below the range. The two failures are very different.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A sound, conventional plan that answers every IPS question in order. There is a Treasury ladder for the payments, a diversified 80/20 growth sleeve, a 2031 lock of the range's bottom, and a gift of floor plus at most half the growth, capped at the top. The rest is kept as flexibility, which is a better flexibility share than Entries 1, 2 and 4 keep. The equity weight fits a client who takes thoughtful risks, and putting the floor at whatever is locked in 2031 means a crash before then shrinks the promise rather than breaking it. What holds it back is that it asserts more than it shows. "We test outcomes in good, average and severe markets", but no result is given: no probability for the payments, the range or the gift. That is weak on funding reliability under varying outcomes. It says rate swings never touch the payments, yet it keeps pre-2033 coupons in short Treasuries. If rates fall, those coupons earn less than the ladder was priced on, and that hole is not priced. The size of the 2031 floor (what fraction of the growth sleeve) is never stated. There is almost no sign of how the team reached its decisions.
- Not understood on first reading: how large the 2031 floor will be, and what confidence the "stated, modelled confidence level" turned out to be.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The payments are handled correctly with a held-to-maturity Treasury ladder, and staging the gift as real options is an original idea that speaks Laura's founder language. But when I trace the gift, it breaks against the case. The case asks for a 2033 contribution and a 2031 range for it. Here part of the gift is pushed to 2034-2035, and part depends on co-sponsors having already committed their share. Co-sponsors are meant to be drawn in by her commitment, so the condition is circular. It also means the range's chance of holding depends on things no market model can price, and no confidence is given. It says every promise is sized to a secured tranche, then says the top depends on markets. The growth sleeve adds 25% QQQ because tech "led global returns", which is chasing past winners. It also doubles down on the industry her own income comes from, which is the opposite of what her career risk calls for. Funding analysis beyond "about 7%" is absent, and the coupon reinvestment hole on the ladder is not seen.
- Not understood on first reading: what "the growth portfolio above its target" means at Gate 2 (what target, and set when?). Also, what Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 about the tranches that depend on the project.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The most technically serious entry. Ladder first, then a stated CPPI rule on the growth sleeve: the floor accrues at the short yield and, from 2031, equals the present value of the announced minimum, with a multiplier of three, monthly rebalancing, and a cap of 100% because margin is banned. That ties the 2031 promise directly to a mechanical floor ("Floor = Promise"). It gives far more upside than a static floor-plus-interest design. Better still, it names its own failure modes and sizes the main one: gap risk (a one-month fall of about a third) is why the multiplier is three, not five. It also covers cash-lock, a smaller 2028 deposit, and currency with no hedging allowed. Assumptions are stated with numbers, and liquidity is addressed directly. What holds it back: the top of the range is set at roughly even odds, and the gift is not explicitly capped at the top. So the chance of landing inside the range is only around a half, which is a weak range to put before co-sponsors. Like most entries, it says nothing about reinvesting the ladder's coupons before 2033. There is little sign of how the team got here.
- Not understood on first reading: whether the gift is capped at the announced top or can run above it. Also, how the floor set at "near 60% of the sleeve" in 2028 hands over to the announced minimum in 2031 if the two don't match.

## My overall view
Every entry gets the first step right: buy the ten payments with Treasuries before taking any risk. So that step separated nobody. What separated them was what happens after it. Does the team see that a coupon ladder still has reinvestment risk? Do they lock what they promise at the moment they promise it? Can they say how often the range they give co-sponsors will actually hold? Entry 1 is the only one that does all three. Entry 6 brings the best risk engineering but leaves the range itself loose. Entries 2-4 are sound but leave one or more of those steps hand-waved, and Entry 5 changes the case's timeline to fit its idea. To win, Entry 1 needs to defend its very low equity and thin post-2033 flexibility against a client "willing to take thoughtful risks". It should give a reason why that flexibility share is enough, and it should make its 2028-deposit stress figure unambiguous. As it stands, a judge who works the numbers will stop at that sentence.
