## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5 - Ladder for the ten payments, then a rule-based stock/Treasury sleeve that keeps real equity exposure while protecting the announced 2031 minimum; the risks it accepts are named.
2. Entry 1 - Plain ladder plus an 80/20 diversified growth engine; simple, defensible, the most growth-oriented of the clearly reasoned plans, but thin on numbers.
3. Entry 6 - The capital-protected-note plan with the most complete numbers (97-in-100 on the top of the range, the cost of the cap, the ladder's reinvestment leak). Too timid on growth.
4. Entry 2 - Fully invested surplus (75% VT / 25% QQQ) and an inventive staged-gift idea, but the stages blur the 2031 range and depend on outsiders, and there are almost no numbers.
5. Entry 3 - Same strategy as 6 with less quantification of the top of the range and more rhetoric.
6. Entry 4 - Same strategy again; the least complete on the range and its confidence, and the most rhetoric.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Treasuries are matched to the ten payments, and the whole surplus goes into a diversified 80/20 global stock/Treasury mix. That mix has enough stock to build a real facility contribution, which is what I want for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks. The planned changes are clear: lock the bottom of the range into 2032-33 Treasuries in 2031, convert the ladder into the reserve in 2033, then give the floor plus at most half the growth portfolio. That half-and-half split is a sensible flexibility rule. What holds it back is the analysis. It names no confidence level for the range ("a stated, modelled confidence level" is a placeholder), it never says what the ladder-first choice costs in expected upside, and "rate swings never touch the payments" skips over reinvesting coupons before they are needed. A judge is told the strategy is sound and is not shown how sound. The 20% Treasury "shock absorber" is mild timidity but defensible.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. It was the easiest of the six to follow.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The ladder is sound and the surplus is invested 100% for growth, which I like. The founder and runway framing is a fair way to explain it to a client with Laura's background. But 25% QQQ is a concentrated technology bet justified by one sentence ("innovation companies have led returns"), and that sits uneasily with the diversification criterion. The staged gates are the main weakness. The case asks for one range for the 2033 contribution, with stated confidence, that protects the payments. This plan adds tranches paid in 2034-35 and makes one depend on co-sponsors having committed, which is outside money and outside what the portfolio decides. There is no confidence figure and almost no analysis beyond "about 7%". It is original, but the originality works against the case's own question, and the "real option" language does not replace a number.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the 2031 range is meant to be stated as dollars with a confidence level when the later tranches depend on events that cannot be forecast, and what the seed tranche size would be.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is the capital-protected-note design: a ladder, then Treasuries that grow back to the whole 2028 deposit by late 2032, with only the interest, about a fifteenth of the portfolio, in a world index fund. To its credit it is honest about the price. It says a 60/40 plan usually ends almost $200,000 richer and gives the one-in-three failure rate that persuaded the team. Its list of what can break a cheque is specific. That honesty is why it scores as high as it does. But I read the result as timidity with good arguments. Perhaps 7% of the portfolio ends up in stocks. "She has already taken the big one" is not an investment argument, and there is nothing on the 2031 range's confidence beyond describing its shape. The one-in-three figure for a 60/40 plan is also hard to believe without more explanation. The tone is more literary than analytical.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Sell low, by appointment" as a heading, and how a 60/40 portfolio "misses a cheque" about one time in three when the ladder funds the cheques in both designs.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry that treats the facility contribution as a growth problem and still protects the promise. The idea is clear and tied to what the case asks: the minimum Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 is the number the portfolio is built to protect, and the payments sit in a reserve that never holds stocks. The growth sleeve starts close to fully invested and cuts stock exposure as the cushion shrinks. It also gives real figures (7% return, 16% volatility, 4.5% for Treasuries, 10,000 simulated paths, at least 95% confidence in the minimum, roughly even odds on the top). Its stated risks are the right ones: gap risk, getting stuck in Treasuries, the variable 2028 deposit and the Taiwan dollar. Currency risk is a client-specific point that most entries missed. Weaknesses: CPPI is a lot of machinery for an IPS that is meant to leave out detailed calculations, and a mechanical rule can sell stocks into a dip and miss the rebound, which it admits. "Half the gain above it" is loosely worded. But it gives away upside knowingly and says how, which is what I want to see.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor is set before 2031, since it says "near 60% of the sleeve", and what "gain above" the minimum means in 2033.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- It is the most complete version of the note idea. It gives a confidence figure (the top reached about 97 times in 100, a typical miss about $1,000), names the ladder's one real leak (early cash earning less if yields fall) and commits to dealing with it in 2031, and explains the catch in a capped range: falls come off the gift and rises past the top go to Laura. It also ties her income to recessions, which is real client knowledge. But the 97-in-100 is high precisely because the promised half is moved into Treasuries when the range is named, so the plan is mostly a bond plan. I would mark that down as timidity; the upside given away is described honestly but is large, and the stock allocation is a small fraction of the portfolio. The claim that 60/40 fails one time in three alongside "one in thirty" for the 75%-stock plan is stated without enough explanation to trust. Still a rigorous, well-argued conservative plan, and a judge who values funding certainty will like it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the 60/40 alternative misses a cheque about one time in three when the 75%-stock plan misses about one in thirty.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the team counted the cost of safety and still left room for the facility gift to grow. Entry 5 does both: the payments are safe, the stock exposure is real, and the cap on the upside is explained in numbers. Entry 1 has the right shape but never proves it. The three note entries (3, 4, 6) are honest about what they give up. They give up a great deal, though, and the argument that Laura has "already taken the big risk" is a rationalisation. Entry 6 is the best of those. Entry 2 is original but is short on the numbers the case asks for and builds its gift on conditions outside the portfolio. To win, Entry 5 needs to trim the machinery to what a lay judge can follow, say plainly what the rule costs in a strong market, and state its range and confidence in dollars. Entry 1 needs to quantify what the ladder-first choice costs and name a confidence level for its range.
