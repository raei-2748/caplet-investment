# Judge J3 scores

Judge: Priya (pension actuary, asset-liability matching). Method: trace the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042), the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution through each plan and find where it breaks or where the reasoning is waved through.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5: the clearest account of where a matched ladder can actually fail (correlated recession, coupon reinvestment) and what the safety costs. It also shows the team learned from its own first model.
2. Entry 4: the same sound structure and the same priced holes as Entry 5, without the team's own learning.
3. Entry 1: a structure close to 4 and 5 that prices the same leaks, but the reasoning about the 2028 deposit is muddier and it never says what the safety costs.
4. Entry 6: the most technically ambitious entry (a CPPI rule with its gap risk quantified), but the "protected" floor is held in the wrong asset and the range's top is a coin flip.
5. Entry 2: a competent, conventional ladder-plus-growth plan whose central claim, that rate swings never touch the payments, is not true as written.
6. Entry 3: a vivid framing, but the staging runs past the case's 2033 contribution, the range has no confidence attached, and the tech tilt doubles a risk Laura already carries.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The money flow works: the first deposit buys a Treasury ladder against the ten payments, the 2028 deposit first fills any gap and then buys a floor that grows back to roughly the deposit by late 2032, and only the expected interest goes into world stocks. Unlike most plans, it names the coupon-reinvestment leak in a ladder and sets money aside for it before the range is announced. It also links Laura's income to the recessions that raise the ladder's cost, which is a real wrong-way risk most teams miss. The tradeoff against a 60/40 portfolio is stated, but only as "usually leaves her richer" with no size, so the reader can't weigh what the certainty costs. What holds it back for me is that almost the whole 2028 deposit is committed to the gift. After 2033 her flexibility is about half of a small stock sleeve. Given the case's warning about committing all remaining assets, that deserved a sentence on whether it is enough. The model figures (one in thirty, 97 in 100, about $1,000) are given as model output, which is right, but the IPS never says what they were tested against.
- Did not understand on first reading: the line that "about $125,000 still does" after a two-point fall in yields. I could not tell whether that is the size of deposit needed to fill the gap or what is left after filling it, and my own rough sizing of a two-point gap is well under $125,000. I also wondered why it is a range at all: if the promised half of the stocks moves into Treasuries the day the range is named, the top is close to locked, and it was unclear what the remaining uncertainty is.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The structure is sound and every IPS question gets an answer: the ladder for the payments, the 2028 deposit completing it, an 80/20 growth sleeve with a rebalancing rule, a 2031 lock of the range's bottom, the reserve running itself down, and a cap that leaves half the growth as flexibility. Stating return and inflation assumptions is good practice. The hole is the definition of certainty. It says coupons received early sit in short Treasuries and that rate swings never touch the payments. But early coupons reinvested at lower rates are exactly how rate swings reach a matched ladder, and the plan neither prices that nor sets money aside for it. It also treats the 2028 deposit as a dependable backstop without asking whether a recession that makes the ladder dearer would also shrink Laura's publishing and speaking income. The "good, average and severe" tests are mentioned, but no result is reported, so I can't see how reliable the funding actually is. It reads like a plan built from the right parts, not one tested against Laura's own cash flows.
- Did not understand on first reading: none. My doubts are about whether the certainty claim is true, not about what it means.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 4/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- Treating the facility money as staged, option-like tranches is an original idea, and the operating ladder at its base is the right instinct. The trouble is that the case asks for a 2031 range for her 2033 contribution with a stated confidence, and the third tranche is paid in 2034-2035. Gate 2 also depends on co-sponsors committing first, when they are waiting on her signal. The plan gives no confidence level for the range at all. Its closing claim, that every promise is sized to a tranche already secured, contradicts its own design, since the top of the range rests on tranches that are not secured. The 25% QQQ tilt is justified by Laura's career sitting in technology and creative industries, which is backwards: her 2028 deposit and her income already depend on that sector, so the portfolio doubles her exposure instead of offsetting it. Like Entry 2, it says the ladder's payout is fixed whatever markets do, and ignores coupon reinvestment. It gives no assumptions for Treasuries, inflation or currency.
- Did not understand on first reading: how a tranche released in 2034-2035 fits a 2033 contribution range, and what "the growth portfolio is above its target" means, since no target is defined.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This entry does what I look for first: it defines certainty as a short list of named failure modes, not an adjective. The list includes the correlated one: a recession that both raises the ladder's cost and shrinks the income behind the 2028 deposit. It also names the coupon leak and funds it before the range is set. It makes the case against itself and puts a number on it: a 60/40 portfolio usually ends about $200,000 ahead, but breaks a promise about one time in three against under one in sixty here. That is the tradeoff a judge needs to see. The floor-plus-interest design for the gift is the right shape for a fixed date two years out, and moving the promised half into Treasuries when the range is named closes the gap between what is promised and what is owned. What holds it back is shared with Entries 1 and 5: flexibility after 2033 is thin (half a small stock sleeve), inflation and Taiwan-dollar costs get one clause, and the IPS shows nothing of how the team got here.
- Did not understand on first reading: the same point as Entry 1. Once the promised half is locked in Treasuries, I am not sure what can still make the gift miss its top three times in 100.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- On substance this entry matches Entry 4: a matched ladder from the first deposit, a 2028 floor that repays the deposit in 2032 with the expected interest in one world fund, a 2031 range from the owned floor to floor plus half the stocks, and the promised half locked on naming day. It also has the same honest list of failure modes, including the correlated recession and the reinvestment leak, and the same priced comparison with a 60/40 portfolio. It edges ahead because it shows the team testing a three-quarters-stock plan in its own model, seeing it fail, and changing course. That is evidence the team understands why the structure is what it is. It also explains what "sell low, by appointment" means: fixed payment dates force sales on a known timetable. That is the actuarial core of the case. The weaknesses are the same as in Entry 4: thin post-2033 flexibility, little on inflation and facility cost, and a range whose top is nearly locked, so the confidence statement does less work than it seems to.
- Did not understand on first reading: what remaining risk produces the three-in-100 miss of the top once the promised half is in Treasuries.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the most quantitatively literate entry. A pre-committed CPPI rule protects the announced minimum. It explains that a multiplier of three means a breach needs a one-month fall of about a third, and it admits the cash-lock cost. It states assumptions for returns, volatility, timing, liquidity, inflation and currency. It is also honest that the minimum is held to about 95%, not to certainty. But the floor is the present value of a fixed 2033 amount, held in short Treasuries. If rates fall, that present value rises while the short bills barely move, so the floor can be breached by rates as well as by stocks. A pension fund would hold a bond maturing in 2033 here, not bills. The range is the weaker part: the top is set at even odds and the gift is not capped at it, so the chance that the 2033 contribution actually lands inside the range is under one half. That is a weak answer to the case's demand for a credible range. The ladder ignores coupon reinvestment, and the 2028 deposit's tie to recessions is noted but not linked to the ladder's cost. The name "Floor = Promise" oversells a floor the team itself rates at 95%.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the floor moves from "about 60% of the sleeve, accruing at the short yield" to "the present value of Laura's announced minimum" in 2031. If the minimum is announced above the running floor, the cushion and the stock holding drop overnight, and the entry doesn't say how the minimum is chosen to avoid that.

## My overall view
Every entry here pairs a Treasury ladder with a growth sleeve, so the structure separated no one. What separated them was whether the team had found the holes in its own ladder. Entries 2, 3 and 6 call the ladder "fixed whatever markets do". That is not true while early coupons have to be reinvested, and none of the three asks what happens when the recession that makes the ladder dearer also cuts the 2028 deposit meant to fill the gap. Entries 1, 4 and 5 name both, price them, and fund them before Laura says a number to co-sponsors. They are also the only entries that tell me what the certainty costs her in expected wealth. That is the "reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs" Wharton asks to see. To win, the best entry (5) needs three more things. First, a frank answer on flexibility: after 2033 almost everything is committed to the gift and the reserve, and the case warns about exactly that. Second, a line on inflation and Taiwan-dollar facility costs. Third, an explanation of why the range is still a range once the top is nearly locked in Treasuries, and what the 97-in-100 figure is actually measuring.
