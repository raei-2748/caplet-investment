# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 2 - Ladder for the payments, then a rules-based stock-or-Treasury sleeve that keeps real equity exposure while defending the announced minimum; the most honest about what the safety costs and where it fails.
2. Entry 1 - Clean ladder plus a genuine 80/20 growth portfolio, a half-the-gain cap and a locked floor in 2031; sound and easy to follow, but thin on numbers and on what the caution gives up.
3. Entry 6 - Fully self-contained version of the capital-protected-note idea; it counts the price of safety (about $200k) but then pays it, leaving very little in stocks.
4. Entry 3 - Same strategy as Entry 6 with slightly less explanation of how it was reached; same objection.
5. Entry 5 - Same strategy again, shortest on the quantified trade-off, and the weakest on the stock exposure the client could afford.
6. Entry 4 - Growth-heavy but thin analysis. The staged-gate gift also conflicts with the case: the 2031 range is meant to describe the 2033 contribution, not stages stretching to 2035 that depend on co-sponsors.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The structure is right for this client. Ten fixed, dated payments are matched by held-to-maturity Treasuries, with a clear and defensible definition of "high certainty". Everything above the ladder goes into a real growth portfolio, roughly 80% global stocks, with a sensible rebalancing band. That is the first thing I want to see for someone who is "willing to take thoughtful risks" and has twelve years or more to run. The plan changes over time: the floor is locked in 2031, the ladder becomes the reserve in 2033, and the facility gift is the floor plus at most half the growth. It is a plan a co-sponsor could follow. What holds it back is that the "never put the promise at risk" line is asserted rather than counted. It never says how much expected growth the half-the-gain cap and the 20% Treasury sleeve give away. There is no simulated confidence figure for the range, only "a stated, modelled confidence level". The risk section is short, and it is not clear what happens to the sleeve once the floor is locked in 2031.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. I was not sure whether the locked 2031 floor is carved out of the growth portfolio or sits on top of it.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most intellectually serious entry, and the one that tries hardest to earn the client more than the Treasury rate. The ladder takes the first $300k. The growth sleeve then runs a pre-committed rule (CPPI) that holds stocks at a multiple of the cushion above a floor tied to the number Laura will announce. That lets the sleeve start nearly fully invested and keep participating, while the minimum stays protected. The entry is unusually frank about where the rule can fail: gap risk, with a stated reason for choosing a multiplier of three rather than five; cash-lock, which would miss a recovery; the variable 2028 deposit; and currency exposure. Assumptions are numbers (7% return, 16% volatility, 10,000 paths, 95% confidence in the minimum, roughly even odds on the top). It also adds an explicit "will not rewrite the rule because markets moved", which matches Wharton's wording. Against it, a monthly dynamic rule is more machinery than a high-school team can defend, and a judge could ask how it behaves in a choppy, falling-then-recovering market. The "floor starts near 60% of the sleeve" figure is not explained. The text is dense, and a few terms (cushion, multiplier) are loosely defined for a lay co-sponsor reader.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor moves from "60% of the sleeve" to "present value of the announced minimum" in 2031, and whether the two are meant to be the same number.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The ladder reasoning is excellent: a list of ways to fail rather than an adjective, the coupon-reinvestment leak named, and a plan to price it in 2031. It also does something almost no one does, which is count the price of safety: a 60/40 mix ends about $200,000 ahead "usually", but misses a payment or breaks its word about one time in three, against under one in sixty here. I respect that honesty. But after counting the price it pays it in full. The 2028 deposit is mostly Treasuries grown back to itself, so only the expected interest, a small share of the whole, goes into stocks. For a client with six to sixteen years to run, living costs covered elsewhere, and a stated appetite for thoughtful risk, I think that gives up too much. The "one time in three" comparison is against a 60/40 that mixes the payments with the growth, which is not the fair alternative. The fair one is a ladder plus a stock sleeve like Entry 1's. The capped-range logic (move the promised half into Treasuries once the range is named) is thoughtful and well explained, and the "97 in 100" figure is welcome. The headings and wordplay add no analysis.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Sell low, by appointment", "Have your cake and invest the icing" and "Do not crush" are not explained until later, and the last one never is within the entry. I also couldn't tell how much of the portfolio ends up in stocks.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It does the right basic thing, a ladder for the payments plus a stock-heavy surplus, and I like that it does not cower in bonds. But the growth sleeve is 75% world stocks and 25% QQQ on the argument that innovation companies have led returns and Laura worked in tech. That is a performance bet and a concentration, not diversification, and it is the "investments you think will perform well" thinking Wharton warns about. The staged-gate gift is a creative idea that does not fit the case. Laura must give co-sponsors a credible range in 2031 for her 2033 contribution, yet tranches are released in 2034-35 and depend on co-sponsors committing and on the portfolio "recovering", which is not something she controls. The "real option" language is used loosely, with no sizing, no probabilities and no simulation of any of it. There is no stated confidence in the range, no assumption beyond "about 7%", and no stress case beyond "deep drawdowns possible". The startup analogy is neat, but the money mechanics behind it are thin.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the seed tranche is, how the gates actually trigger, and how Laura can state a 2031 range if the top depends on later gates.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is the same plan as 3 and 6: the ladder, a floor of Treasuries for the 2028 deposit, only the interest in a world index fund, and a range of floor plus half the stocks. It is the best written of the three at saying why the 75%-stock start was dropped (about one future in thirty missed a cheque), and it ties the client's income risk neatly to recessions. It also gives concrete figures: about $125,000 of the 2028 deposit would still cover the gap if yields fall two points, and the rare miss on the gift is about $1,000. My objection is the same and stronger here: the stock exposure is about a fifteenth of everything she owns, and the "a 60/40 usually leaves her richer" admission is not quantified the way Entry 3 and 6 do. A client who can take thoughtful risks is being given a bond ladder with a small equity garnish and told it is a growth plan. The comparison with a 60/40 is again a straw man for the same reason.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what the "rare miss is about $1,000" is measured against; "about a fifteenth of everything she owns" took me a moment to convert into a dollar amount.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is the same strategy as Entries 3 and 5, and the most complete statement of it. It names the abandoned 75%-stock first plan, counts the roughly $200,000 price of safety against the one-in-three versus under-one-in-sixty failure rates, and notes that the shares in the replacement note cannot expire worthless the way an option can. It covers the 2031 repricing of the reinvestment shortfall, the "cheques first, then floor, then hope" priority order, and the reserve running itself down from 2033. My disagreement is with the choice itself, and I mark it down for that. The plan prices safety honestly and then buys all of it, leaving a client who is willing to take risks with almost no equity exposure on her new money. It is well argued and well disclosed, but cautious dressed as rigorous. It sits above 3 and 5 only because its reasoning is the most self-contained.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: I was not told the actual proportion of stocks in the final portfolio. "Do not crush" is a closing flourish I could not tie to anything in the strategy.

## My overall view
What separated the top from the rest was whether the entry kept real growth in the plan and was honest about what the protection costs. Entries 1 and 2 both fund the payments properly and then run an actual stock portfolio for the facility gift, so the gift can grow with the market. Entry 2 goes furthest. It builds a rule that keeps stocks on while defending the number Laura will tell co-sponsors, and it lists the ways the rule can fail. Entries 3, 5 and 6 are carefully reasoned, and they are the only ones that put a dollar figure on the cost of their caution. I still mark them down for then spending almost the entire 2028 deposit on Treasuries for a client with a long horizon, living costs covered elsewhere, and stated appetite for risk. Entry 4 has the opposite problem, equity enthusiasm with no analysis behind it and a gift structure that does not match the case. To win, the best entry (Entry 2) would need to say in plain terms what the rule gives up compared with simply holding stocks, show a small table of good, average and bad outcomes for the facility gift in dollars, and explain the mechanism in words a co-sponsor could repeat. Entry 1 would need the same numbers and an explicit figure for the confidence in its range.
