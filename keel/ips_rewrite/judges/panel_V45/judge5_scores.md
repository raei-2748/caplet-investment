## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4: the sharpest reading of the client's problem (her 2028 income and the ladder's cost fall together), a quantified trade-off against the obvious alternative, and a range mechanism that explains its own catch.
2. Entry 1: a genuine pre-commitment mechanism (the 2031 promise becomes the floor the portfolio mechanically defends), with its failure modes named and a confidence level stated; harder to follow on one reading.
3. Entry 6: the same strategy as Entry 4 with a cleaner narrative and the team's own change of mind shown, but fewer numbers on how the sleeve is sized and stressed.
4. Entry 2: near-twin of Entry 6; the same reasoning, slightly less evidence of process and of why shares beat the bank's option.
5. Entry 3: the one genuinely new idea in the set (the facility gift as staged real options), let down by almost no portfolio analysis and a growth sleeve whose justification runs backwards.
6. Entry 5: competent, complete and clear, and exactly what I expected every competent team to reach; it taught me nothing.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the number Laura announces in 2031 can be made the floor of a pre-committed rule, so the portfolio defends her word mechanically rather than by someone's judgement in a falling market. That is a real idea, and it is a decision scientist's idea: it removes the human from the moment of temptation. The entry is also unusually honest about what the mechanism costs: it names gap risk and explains why the multiplier is three, and it names cash-lock and accepts it, which is exactly the "reasoning, assumptions and trade-offs" Wharton says it wants. The ladder, the self-liquidating reserve, the floor-at-95% and top-at-even-odds range, the currency and 2028-deposit risks are all covered. What holds it back: the growth sleeve is the most complex construction in the set (monthly rebalancing, a floor that accrues then re-anchors in 2031, a leverage multiplier), and a judge reading once will work to follow it; the client paragraph is the shortest and least specific of the six; and it never tells me what it gives up relative to a simpler plan, so the trade-off is argued inside the rule but not between rules.
- Not understood on first reading: the circularity between the announced minimum and the floor. The floor "from 2031 equals the present value of Laura's announced minimum", but the minimum must itself be chosen from the sleeve's value then; I could not tell which is set first. I also could not tell what "near 60% of the sleeve" means in dollars for the gift floor.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that a recession before 2028 hurts Laura twice, by making the ladder dearer and by shrinking the publishing and speaking income that funds the second deposit; that "high degree of certainty" can be defined as an enumerated list of failure modes rather than a probability; and that a capped range has an asymmetry (falls come off the gift, rises above the cap go to Laura) that justifies locking the promised half on the day it is named. The capital-protected-note framing (floor Treasuries plus the interest in shares) is a tidy way of seeing the second deposit, and the 60/40 comparison states what the plan gives up in the median and what it buys in the tail, which is the trade-off Wharton asks for. The reserve's run-down and the priority order (cheques, floor, hope) answer the composition-over-time question directly. What holds it back: the growth exposure is very small and the entry never says how small, so I cannot judge whether "balance between growth and protection" has been struck or simply avoided; the coupon-leak set-aside comes "from the hope" without saying what happens if the hope is too small to cover it; and the team's own process is only implied.
- Not understood on first reading: how large the stock holding is relative to the whole, and therefore how wide the 2031 range would be. Also whether "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" are a distinct instrument from the bonds or a stand-in for rungs that cannot be bought directly.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution can be treated as a sequence of real options with gates tied to both market state and project state, which is a genuinely different way to answer "preserve flexibility" and the only entry that gives flexibility a mechanism rather than a leftover bucket. The founder framing fits who Laura is, and the runway/growth-capital split is a clean way to explain the two kinds of money. What holds it back is nearly everything Wharton puts under Portfolio Analysis: no sizes for the tranches, no confidence level for the range, no stress of the ladder's cost or the 2028 deposit, no mention of inflation, and "about 7% a year with deep drawdowns possible" standing in for projections. The growth sleeve's 25% in a technology-heavy fund is justified by Laura's career being in technology, which is the wrong direction: her human capital already carries that exposure, so the portfolio should not add to it. Two gates also sit awkwardly with the case: making the second tranche conditional on co-sponsors committing first may read to co-sponsors as hedging rather than commitment, and a third tranche in 2034-35 answers a question (a 2033 contribution range) the case did not ask.
- Not understood on first reading: what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio at Gate 2, how the bottom of the range is sized in 2031, and whether the team intends the 2031 range to include the Gate 3 money or not.

### Entry 4

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same core ideas as Entries 2 and 6 (buy the promise rather than invest it; the second deposit as a capital-protected note without the bank; the capped-range asymmetry and the fix), but here each is pinned to evidence. ### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the ladder costs roughly two-thirds of Laura's total contributions, which is the one useful magnitude in the entry. Everything else is the plan I expected before I opened the file: ladder the payments, hold to maturity, define certainty as a Treasury maturity before each date, invest the rest in a diversified stock-heavy mix, lock the bottom of the range in 2031, give the floor plus half the remainder, keep the rest as a flexibility fund, review yearly. The client paragraph could be about any entrepreneur with fixed commitments. Among 2,300 entries this is the median competent plan done neatly, and the top 50 will need more than neatness.
- Not understood on first reading: none. That is partly the point: there was nothing in it to work at.

## My overall view
Five of the six entries ladder the ten payments in Treasuries and put the rest in stocks, which confirms my prior that this is where any competent team ends up; it is the entry fee, not the prize. What separated the top from the rest was whether the team had seen something about Laura's problem that the ladder alone does not reveal, and whether it had argued a trade-off rather than described a plan. Entry 4 saw that Laura's second deposit and the ladder's cost move together in a recession, quantified what its caution costs against a 60/40 alternative and what it buys in the tail, and explained why a capped range needs the promised half locked the day it is named; Entry 1 saw that the 2031 promise can be built into a rule the portfolio defends without anyone having to be brave. Entries 6 and 2 share Entry 4's ideas with less evidence; Entry 3 had the only truly new mechanism but did not do the analysis to back it; Entry 5 did everything correctly and nothing memorably. For Entry 4 to win outright it would need to put its test before its conclusion, say plainly how much growth it has chosen to forgo and why that is the right amount for this client rather than a cautious one, and size the Taiwan-dollar exposure instead of promising to sell into it.
