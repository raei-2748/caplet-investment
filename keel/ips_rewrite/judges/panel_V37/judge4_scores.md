## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The team separates Laura's promise (ten payments) from her hope (the gift) and builds each from a different kind of money. That is a thesis, not a list of holdings. The reasoning is mostly honest: the team says they started with three-quarters in stocks, their model showed that missed a payment in about one future in thirty, and they changed course. They also name the leak in their own ladder (reinvested coupons) and say what they do about it, and they list what could go wrong with the 2028 deposit and the second contribution. Laura is handled well: her income falls in the same recessions that hurt other assets, and the residency is already her big bet. Two things hold it back. The "XR pill" conceit is more decoration than explanation, and I am not sure a team of teenagers would write it that way. It also never shows what the stock-heavy alternative would have given up, so the reader can't weigh the trade-off. The numbers (97 in 100, about $1,000, one in thirty) are tied to a model, which helps.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The "interest on the floor goes into stocks" structure took two reads, and I wasn't sure at first why the half Laura promised moves to Treasuries on the day she names the range. It makes sense once you see the one-way catch in a capped range.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the same strategy and the same voice as Entry 1, with one important difference: it states what the alternative (a balanced 60/40 portfolio) would do. The team says it usually leaves her richer, but breaks a promise about one time in four. That is what Wharton asks for, the trade-off and the reasoning behind it, and it shows the team chose with its eyes open. It drops the paragraph on what would happen if the 2028 deposit went into stocks, which is a small loss, but the 60/40 comparison is worth more. The same pill gimmick is there and I still find it slightly too cute. Overall this is the entry where I most believe the team understands what it built, and where the funding-certainty definition, risk, flexibility and glide path all work together.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Same as Entry 1: the move of the promised half into Treasuries when the range is named. "Bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee" is clever but needed a second look.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A careful, internally consistent plan. Payments are matched with Treasuries, the latest ones are bought first because they are the most rate-sensitive, and the 2028 deposit completes any gap. The team separates Laura's ability to bear risk (high) from her willingness (lower for a missed payment, since artists would bear the loss), which is real client thinking. The range is built to hold, and the team says plainly why it prefers a wide range to a narrow one it might miss. It is also honest about the single-issuer risk. The weakness is the voice: formal and a bit stiff, with few numbers showing how the team tested its claims. There is little to show how reliable the funding is under different markets, and the "cannot miss the range" claim is stated rather than argued. Good, but it doesn't stand out among strong entries.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The sentence about a "range quoted rounded down after any expected shortfall" took me a moment.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The central idea, that the number Laura announces should be the one the portfolio is built to protect, is clear and good. The risk section is honest (gap risk, cash-lock, the 2028 deposit, currency). But the way it is carried out is a monthly CPPI rule with a multiplier of three, and that is the kind of mechanism I would expect from an adult quant, not a student team. Wharton asked for the framework, not detailed calculations, and I suspect many co-sponsors and judges would not follow it. A rule that moves everything into Treasuries and misses any recovery is a real cost; the team accepts it, but gives little sense of how often that happens. The headings, bullets and bold type read like a brochure. Laura herself appears mostly at the end.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How the "floor" ties to the announced minimum while also being 60% of the sleeve, and whether the rule changes after 2031; I had to piece that together.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The idea of a committed lead gift plus a conditional match is the most unusual co-sponsor idea in the set and ties to Laura as a storyteller. But the claim that "fundraising research consistently finds" this is not shown, so I cannot tell whether the team found it or just heard it. The portfolio itself is a list of tickers and fixed percentages (65/35, VTI, VXUS, SCHD, BND, SHY) that is not explained and not tailored to Laura; the safe reserve in the early years is a bond fund with rate risk, and BND brings in corporate credit. The 97% certainty is stated, but there is no way to see how it was reached. Much of the space goes on co-sponsor framing that the brief says belongs in the Final Report. The text also includes "simulator notes" that should not be in an entry.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How a "matching pledge" works when co-sponsors give less, and why a moderate growth sleeve is needed if the reserve already protects the payments.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Plain, readable and sensible. A bond ladder funds the promise, the rest goes to an 80/20 growth mix, the floor is locked in 2031, and the contribution is the floor plus at most half of the rest. It reads as something a student team could have written. It also gives a clear definition of "high certainty". But it is the default answer to this case: it doesn't show a problem the team found, an alternative they rejected, or a number from their own testing beyond "good, average and severe" markets. The claim that rate swings "never touch the payments" ignores the reinvestment of coupons before they are needed, which the better entries notice. The 80% stock figure is justified only by a general argument about construction costs. A solid mid-field entry.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: None.

## My overall view
The best entries are the ones where I can see the team changed its mind because of something it tested, and said so. Entries 1 and 2 do that, and Entry 2 goes further by naming the alternative and what it would cost. They also fit Laura's circumstances rather than any client. My reservation is that their writing is polished and a bit clever, which is what makes me cautious, but the thinking underneath is real. To win, I'd want the best entry to drop the pill conceit, keep the numbers tied to the model and show a little more of how the team's early idea failed. Entry 3 is the safest alternative; Entries 4 and 5 are ambitious but lean on mechanisms or claims the team does not explain.
