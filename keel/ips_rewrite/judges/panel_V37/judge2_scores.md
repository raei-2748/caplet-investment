# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1 - Ladder for the ten payments, then a rules-based stock-heavy sleeve that keeps real upside, with the cost of safety stated.
2. Entry 4 - Same capital-protected-note design as Entry 2, but it counts what the safety costs (60/40 usually richer, breaks a promise 1 in 4).
3. Entry 5 - Clean, sensible ladder plus an 80/20 growth portfolio; right instinct on growth, but thin on testing and on pricing the safety.
4. Entry 2 - Same design as Entry 4 and well quantified, but it puts only the interest into stocks and de-risks the promised half in 2031, which is a lot of upside to give away.
5. Entry 6 - Original co-sponsor angle, but a weaker certainty layer, fund tickers, brief-drift into fundraising, and a growth sleeve that gets de-risked from 2031.
6. Entry 3 - Competent and client-aware, but it has the least analysis and the most timid shape, with no probabilities and no test of the range.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The central idea ties the promised minimum to what the portfolio is mechanically built to protect. The first $300k funds the ten payments with held-to-maturity Treasuries, and the 2028 money goes into a stock sleeve run by a pre-committed rule. That rule is the part I like as a stock man. It lets the sleeve run up to 100% in stocks when the cushion is big, so the facility contribution can grow with the market instead of being capped by a bond-heavy design. It also says plainly what that costs: it can lock into Treasuries and miss a recovery, and it cannot react within a month. Assumptions (about 7% and 16% for stocks, 4.5% for Treasuries, 10,000 paths) are stated and are clearly the team's own. The multiplier choice shows they thought about gap risk rather than just borrowing a technique. The certainty definition, the half-the-gain flexibility rule and the 2031 range logic all hang together. It is somewhat technical for a judge who is not a markets person, and the sleeve is a small part of the money, so the headline upside is modest. It still gives away its upside knowingly.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the cushion-times-three mechanism took a second pass. The floor "starts near 60% of the sleeve", and I was not sure what that figure rests on.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is strong on the client: the payments are paid first, the 2028 deposit plugs any ladder gap, and her income is argued to fall in the same recessions that make bonds dear. The certainty definition is concrete and the leak (reinvested coupons) is priced and handled. It also tests the original growth-heavy design (a missed cheque about 1 in 30) and reports a 97-in-100 chance of reaching the top of the range. My problem is the shape. Only the expected interest, about a fifteenth of her assets, is exposed to stocks. For a client willing to take thoughtful risks with 6 to 16 years to go and living costs covered elsewhere, that is a timid design. On top of that, the half she promises moves into Treasuries when the range is named, so even more upside is surrendered. It does admit a stock-funded deposit would usually have a higher top, which is honest, but it never sets that against the cost. The gift is mostly decided at purchase, and the portfolio is not working hard for her.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "the half she promised moves into Treasuries" took a re-read to see why. The "capped range has a catch" paragraph is dense. I also noticed this entry and Entry 4 share the same pitch and most of the same body.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- A sound structure: a ladder of Treasuries or defined-maturity ETFs, a 2032 Treasury ETF that returns the 2028 remainder as the floor, and an index fund as the branch, with half of it kept by Laura. The risk-capacity versus willingness discussion is a genuine plus. But there are no probabilities, no stated return assumptions, and no test of how often the top is reached. The sentence "she cannot miss the range" is a capped-payout design presented as a virtue, with nothing said about the upside surrendered. As in Entry 2, the stock exposure is small. The pitch claim about co-sponsors rarely paying for running costs is asserted, not supported. It reads as a decent outline of a strategy, not a tested one. It mentions the WInS portfolio, which is fine but adds little.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor ETF is "sized to return that remainder" and what "quoted rounded down after any expected shortfall" means in dollars.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is the same design as Entry 2, with the same strengths: ladder first, a clear certainty definition, the coupon leak priced, a 97-in-100 top-of-range statistic. What separates it is that it puts the price of safety on the table. A 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer but breaks a promise about one time in four, and the team chose the protected design knowing that. That is the kind of trade-off statement I want from a team, even though I would have chosen differently. It still carries the small stock exposure and the 2031 de-risking, so it is a timid plan with an honest explanation. The weakness is that it does not say how much richer, so the reader cannot judge whether one in four is worth it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the same "capped range" paragraph as Entry 2. I could not tell from the text how much richer the 60/40 median is.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Clean and easy to follow. The ladder covers the ten payments, which is roughly two-thirds of the money. The rest goes into 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries with an annual drift rebalance. It also ties stocks to Taiwan construction-cost inflation, a useful reason to hold growth. I like the instinct: after the promise is funded, the rest is allowed to grow. But the 2031 step moves the bottom of the range into Treasuries and the facility contribution is capped at half the growth portfolio, so the upside is again given away without saying how much. The risk section is generic ("good, average and severe markets") with no numbers, no probability of reaching the range, and no test of the certainty definition beyond default risk. The strategy is right-sized, but it is a plan stated rather than a plan tested.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how "at most half of the remaining growth portfolio" interacts with the locked floor, and whether the floor is inside or outside that half.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5.6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The lead-gift-plus-conditional-match framing is original and fits the co-sponsor purpose. But the IPS brief says the draft communication and range belong in the Final Report, and the entry spends a large share of its words there. The research claim is asserted without a source. The certainty layer is weaker than the others: for years the reserve is held in an intermediate Treasury fund, which carries rate risk, and the 5% margin and 97% target are stated without showing how they were checked. The growth sleeve (65/35) has tickers, a dividend tilt that is not justified, and a bond fund that brings credit risk into the safe side. It then moves 20% a year into short Treasuries from 2031, which is more timidity. It names individual funds when Wharton asks for the framework, not individual investments. A good idea on the donor side, but it is carried by a thinner investment case.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the reserve is rebuilt as a ladder from 2031 and what happens to rate risk in the years before that; the explanation of the "match" amount in dollars.

## My overall view
The entries that separated themselves did two things: they tested the plan with numbers, and they said what the safety costs. Entries 1 and 4 do it best, and Entry 1 does it while keeping the most real stock exposure. Four of the six end up with a protected floor and a small stock sleeve, and none of them works out what Laura gives up in expected dollars for being that cautious. For a client who takes thoughtful risks, with her living costs covered and 6 to 16 years to run, that is the missing piece. The best entry (Entry 1) would win by turning its stated cost into a number: the median and 5th-95th range of the contribution under its rule versus a plain 60/40, so the judge can see the price of the promise and agree with the trade. It would also help to give a bigger share of the money to the growth sleeve and to explain the rule with fewer technical steps.
