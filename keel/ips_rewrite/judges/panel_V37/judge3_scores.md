# Judge J3 scores

(Priya, pension actuary. Method: trace the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042), the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution through each plan and look for the place it breaks or gets hand-waved. Entry 2 arrived with an appended "SIMULATOR NOTES" block; it is not part of the IPS and I ignored it.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - every dollar of the surplus runs under a stated rule; assumptions and its own failure modes are named and sized.
2. Entry 6 - the tightest liability matching in the field, with the ladder's leak priced; flexibility and growth are thin.
3. Entry 1 - same plan as Entry 6; its trade-off with the alternative is given as a number I can't pin down.
4. Entry 5 - a clean cash-flow waterfall and a range that can't be missed, but no projections or return assumptions.
5. Entry 4 - sound and standard, but it says rates "never touch the payments" while it relies on reinvesting coupons.
6. Entry 2 - the payments sit on a duration mismatch until 2031, and the conditional match opens a new liability.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The liability side is excellent. The $300,000 buys a dated Treasury ladder, the 2028 deposit fills any cost gap first, and the entry states exactly when a payment fails: a two-point yield fall before purchase combined with a 2028 deposit under about $125,000. It also finds the coupon-reinvestment leak most ladders hide and pays for it before the 2031 range is named. That's the hole I came looking for, and they found it themselves. Moving the promised half of the stocks into Treasuries once the range is named shows they understand that a capped promise leaves the downside with the gift and the upside with Laura. What holds it back is the rest of the balance sheet. The 2028 floor is sized to the whole remainder, so the minimum gift takes up nearly all of Laura's surplus. Her flexibility comes down to half of a small stock sleeve plus whatever headroom is left from 2027, which looks thin against the case's warning about committing everything. No return or inflation assumptions are stated, so the probabilities can't be checked. The comparison with 60/40 ("breaks a promise about one time in four") counts breaking "the gift it named" as a failure without saying what range a 60/40 plan would have named, so the number rests on a definition that's never shown.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: "about a fifteenth of everything she owns". I had to work out that this means the 2028 remainder less the cost of the zero-style floor, relative to all $450,000. And when the leak is "set aside in 2031", I couldn't tell which pot the money comes out of.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The client thinking is genuine: a lead gift as a signal, and the point that a gift which shrinks destroys trust. The glide path is spelled out year by year. The cash-flow trace breaks in two places. First, a reserve "sized to present value with a 5% margin" costs more than the $300,000 first deposit at today's yields, so the reserve isn't fully funded until 2028, and the plan never says so. Second, from 2027 to 2031 the reserve sits in an intermediate Treasury fund whose duration is clearly shorter than the payments'. If rates fall, the liability grows faster than the asset, and the plan locks in matched bonds only in 2031 at whatever yields hold then. That is an unhedged rate bet on money that is supposed to be certain. The 97% target is asserted, never derived. The matching pledge is creative but creates a new problem: if markets fall while co-sponsors do give, Laura owes a match the sleeve can't fund, and calling the match "always conditional" doesn't remove that promise. There are no return assumptions and no facility range or confidence statement, and flexibility gets one sentence.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: the growth sleeve begins moving 20% a year into short Treasuries in 2031, the same year the "lead gift" is defined as "the amount already moved into Treasuries". I couldn't work out how large the committed lead gift would be at the moment she announces it.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry where the growth money has a mechanical rule that actually trades growth off against protection. The floor starts near 60% of the sleeve, accrues at the short Treasury yield, and resets to the present value of the announced minimum from 2031. The team understands its own rule's weak points and sizes them. A one-month fall of about a third breaks it, which is why they chose a multiplier of three. Cash-lock is accepted with a reason. The 2028 deposit's dependence on her creative income is named, and the ladder claims it first. Currency risk falls on the flexibility share. Return, volatility and Treasury assumptions are stated, so I can rebuild the numbers. Flexibility is a rule (the minimum plus at most half the gain) rather than a wish, and liquidity and diversification are both covered. My reservations are smaller than elsewhere. The ladder is "sized to deliver $50,000", and the plan never says what happens to the coupons paid before 2033. The range puts the minimum at 95% confidence and the top at "roughly even odds", but the 2033 rule isn't capped at the top, so I can't read a single confidence that the gift falls inside the range. Client knowledge is correct but more generic than in the best entries.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: whether the "half of 10,000 simulated paths" top is measured from 2028 or from 2031, and whether a gift above the announced top is allowed.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The structure is sound: a dated ladder, an 80/20 growth engine, the floor moved into 2032 Treasuries before the 2031 conversation, and a contribution rule of floor plus at most half the growth, capped at the top. The assumptions are stated, it's tested in three scenarios, and the reasoning for equity (no withdrawals before 2033, construction-cost inflation) holds. The hole: the ladder is sized so that "principal plus coupons" cover each payment, and the coupons received early are parked in short Treasuries. That is reinvestment risk on money meant to be certain, yet the entry claims rate swings "never touch the payments". The team hasn't noticed its own leak. The 2031 floor size is never pinned down: how much goes into the bottom of the range after a bad 2028-2030? No confidence level is given for the top. Taiwan-dollar risk isn't mentioned. It reads like a competent plan many teams could write, with little that shows why it fits Laura in particular.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The cash-flow logic is the most careful after Entry 1/6, and in places more careful. The 2027 deposit buys the latest payments first, for the right reason: they are the most rate-sensitive, so any unfunded gap sits in the short rungs. A shortfall has an explicit order of payment (2027 leftover, then Laura's half, then the gift above the floor). The floor is quoted "rounded down after any expected shortfall", which handles the reinvestment leak without making a fuss of it. The range runs from the floor to the floor plus half the fund, with the 2033 gift capped at that top, so the gift lands in the range by construction. That's a credible answer to the credibility problem. The ability-versus-willingness split is real client analysis. What holds it back is Portfolio Analysis. There are no return assumptions, no projected outcomes and nothing about how wide the range is likely to be, so "under varying market outcomes" is argued in words but never measured. Like Entry 1/6, the floor takes the whole 2028 remainder, which leaves little growth and only half a small fund as flexibility.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: "acting only if ... a holding becomes unavailable, when we substitute a Treasury maturing earlier". I wasn't sure whether that applies to the ladder, the floor or both.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- It's the same plan as Entry 1 and has the same strengths: a dated ladder, a stated failure condition, a priced coupon leak, the 2028 deposit claimed first, and the promised half locked once the range is named. It also has the same weakness: a floor sized to the whole 2028 remainder leaves Laura very little flexibility or growth, and there are no stated return or inflation assumptions behind the 97-in-100 figure. Where it differs, I prefer it. Instead of the 60/40 comparison, it states the trade-off directly: putting the 2028 deposit into stocks would usually give a higher top but an unprotected bottom. That's the honest cost of the design, and it doesn't lean on a failure measure I can't define. It also makes clear the floor has been owned since 2028, which tells me when the protection begins.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: as in Entry 1, which pot pays for the 2031 set-aside for the leak, and what "a fifteenth of everything she owns" is measured against.

## My overall view
Every entry protects the ten payments with Treasuries, so the payments are not what separates them. What separates them is whether the team found the leaks in its own plan and whether the money above the payments follows a rule. Entries 4 and 2 each have a hole in the "certain" money they never priced (reinvested coupons in one, a duration mismatch until 2031 in the other). Entries 1, 5 and 6 found and paid for the ladder's leak, which is rare and to their credit. But they protect so hard that the 2028 deposit is almost all promised floor, so growth and flexibility, two of Wharton's five balancing words, are left with very little. Entry 3 is my first because it is the only plan whose surplus genuinely balances growth, a protected minimum and kept flexibility under one stated rule, with its assumptions shown and its breaking points named and sized. To win outright, Entry 3 needs Entry 1/6's liability discipline: say what the ladder's early coupons do, cap the gift at the announced top (or say why not), and give one clear confidence that the 2033 gift lands inside the range. (Entries 1 and 6 are nearly identical word for word; I scored each on its own text.)
