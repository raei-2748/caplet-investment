## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already expect. This is the design my prior says most competent teams reach: a held-to-maturity Treasury ladder for the ten payments, an 80/20 growth sleeve for the rest, a floor moved into Treasuries in 2031, contribution equals floor plus half the remainder. It is executed cleanly, the certainty definition is sensible (every payment backed by a maturity, residual risk is the US government), and the self-liquidating reserve is correctly described. What holds it back is that the reasoning stops at assertion: 80/20 is stated rather than argued, "stocks are her best defence against rising construction costs" is a claim without a mechanism, and the modelled confidence behind the top of the range is promised but never given. The client fit is generic: the entry could be written for any client with dated fixed payments and does not use what is specific to Laura (the source of the 2028 deposit, her credibility with co-sponsors as a decision variable). It would earn her confidence, but not her attention.
- Not understood on first reading: what "capped at the top of her announced range" adds when the top already reflects the remaining stocks; and what confidence level the 2031 top actually carries.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the number Laura tells co-sponsors can be made the thing the portfolio is mechanically built to defend, and that a pre-committed rule (constant-proportion insurance with multiplier three) removes the temptation to decide in a falling market. This is the only entry whose growth sleeve has a rule rather than a weight, and the only one that writes its own failure modes down and accepts them in advance: gap risk sized against a one-third monthly fall, cash-lock, the variability of the 2028 deposit, currency. The multiplier-three-not-five choice is exactly the kind of argued trade-off Wharton asks for. What holds it back: the floor before 2031 ("near 60% of the sleeve") protects nothing the client has promised yet, so for three years the rule is machinery without an objective; the sleeve it governs is roughly a third of her money, which makes the apparatus heavy for the stakes; and a monthly rebalancing rule is harder to explain to Laura than the entry admits. Client knowledge is adequate but mostly in the last paragraph; the entry is about the mechanism more than about her.
- Not understood on first reading: how 60% was chosen as the starting floor and what it is protecting between 2028 and 2031; and whether "about half of 10,000 paths would beat" the top is compatible with telling co-sponsors a range she is confident about.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: two things. First, that co-sponsors who fund buildings rarely pay to run them, which is a real reason to treat the operating commitment as the thing that matters most and is a better justification for the ladder than "the payments are fixed". Second, that buying the longest rungs first is the right order when the deposit may not cover the ladder, because they are the most rate-sensitive. The capacity-versus-willingness split, with willingness depending on which objective is at stake, is proper IPS thinking and is well tailored (the artists bear the loss of a missed payment). The structure itself, a 2032 Treasury sized to return the whole 2028 remainder with only the discount going into a world fund, is the same capital-protected idea as Entries 4 and 6, but this entry does not say why the whole remainder should be protected rather than some of it, so the reader has to supply the argument. The shortfall waterfall and the "range built to hold" are sound. Overall it reads as a careful, slightly under-explained document: more decisions than reasons.
- Not understood on first reading: "sized to return that remainder" took a second read before I saw that all of the remainder is protected and only the interest grows; and "the 2027 leftover" appears in the shortfall waterfall without having been introduced.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: more than from any other entry. Three reframings I had not seen put this way: that Laura owes ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill, so the risk is sequence risk in the wrong year, not average return; that her 2028 deposit comes from income that shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear, so her ability to plug a ladder gap is weakest when the gap is biggest, which is why the promise must be paid from money she already has; and that once a capped range is announced the payoff becomes asymmetric (falls hit the gift, rises past the top go to Laura), so the promised half should be moved to Treasuries on the day she names it. Each rejected alternative is priced in the team's own model (75% stocks misses a cheque one future in thirty; 60/40 usually leaves her richer but breaks a promise one time in four), and the coupon-reinvestment leak in the ladder is identified and set aside before the range is named. The trade-off is argued, not assumed: the entry accepts a small gift in exchange for never breaking a word, and says why that is the right trade for this client. What holds it back: only about a fifteenth of her money is in stocks, so the "balance between pursuing growth and protecting capital" the case asks for is tilted hard to one side, and the entry should expect a judge to push on whether this honours her willingness to take thoughtful risks (it answers that she already has, which I accept but not everyone will); diversification is one world fund plus Treasuries; and the product-name paragraph spends words that the reasoning did not need. Articulation shows a team that modelled, rejected and revised, which is as much as an IPS can show.
- Not understood on first reading: "yields drop two points before we buy" when the buying is in January 2027 (a short window for a two-point move); and the "about a fifteenth" figure, which I had to reconstruct rather than read.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution can be designed as a fundraising instrument, not just a number: a lead gift already locked in Treasuries, plus a matching pledge that is conditional on co-sponsors giving, so that a bad market shrinks only the part that was always conditional. That is a real mechanism-design idea and the most original client thinking in the field; it turns uncertainty into an incentive rather than a caveat. The certainty definition (97% in simulation) is explicit and the glide path (20% a year into short Treasuries from 2031) is clear. What holds it back is the portfolio under the idea. The reserve sits in an intermediate Treasury fund with a 5% margin for four years rather than in matched maturities, so until 2031 the "protected" payments carry duration mismatch and the 97% is a simulation result, not a construction; the growth sleeve's bond share includes a broad bond fund that brings corporate credit into the safe side; the dividend tilt is not justified; and "moderate, not aggressive" is defended by a reputational cost that the conditional match has already neutralised, which is a tension the entry does not notice. The entry also leans on fundraising research as a premise without showing how it was tested against Laura's situation. Strong on why, weaker on how.
- Not understood on first reading: whether the matching pledge sits inside the announced range or alongside it; why a 5% margin and not some other; and why the reserve is not matched from 2027 when the entry already knows it must be by 2031.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same three reframings as Entry 4 (ten one-year bills; income that falls when bonds are dear; the asymmetry of a capped range once named), the same priced rejection of a 75%-stock start, the same leak in the ladder set aside before 2031, and the same capital-protected floor. Everything I said in Entry 4's favour applies here. The paragraph where it differs replaces the priced 60/40 comparison with a counterfactual: had the 2028 deposit gone into stocks, the top of the range would usually be higher and the bottom "whatever a 2030 crash left". That is a good argument for the floor, and "the floor she has owned since 2028" is a clearer statement of what the bottom of the range is. But it is qualitative where Entry 4 is quantitative, and in a tournament between two otherwise identical entries the one that prices its alternative wins on Portfolio Analysis. The same reservations hold: a very small growth share for a client who says she is willing to take thoughtful risks, thin diversification, and a product-name paragraph that earns nothing under Wharton's criteria.
- Not understood on first reading: as for Entry 4; and the counterfactual about the 2028 deposit gives no number, which stands out when the other rejected alternative does.

## My overall view
What separated the top from the rest was not the ladder; four of six entries lock the payments with held-to-maturity Treasuries and every one of them treats the remainder as the facility money, so that part of the problem was solved by the field and earned nobody a top mark. The separation came from whether a team saw something in Laura's situation that the ladder alone does not address. Entries 4 and 6 saw three such things (the sequence-risk shape of her liability, the correlation between her 2028 income and the cost of the ladder, and the payoff asymmetry of a capped promise) and priced the alternatives they rejected, which is what "reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs" means in practice. Entry 2 saw one thing (make the announced minimum the mechanical floor) and built honest machinery around it. Entry 5 saw one thing about co-sponsors that nobody else did, then under-built the portfolio beneath it. Entries 3 and 1 built sound portfolios and explained less. For the best entry to win outright it would need to meet the obvious objection head-on: with roughly a fifteenth of her money in stocks, a judge will ask whether this is a balance between growth and protection or a surrender of growth, and the answer ("she has already taken the big risk") needs one more sentence of evidence, perhaps the size of the gift it expects against the size a 60/40 portfolio would expect, so that the trade is visible in dollars and not only in probabilities. It should also drop the product-name paragraph and spend those words there.
