## Per entry
### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: I could tick off all five IPS questions in order (reserve, growth, flexibility, reserve over time, 2031 range), with assumptions, liquidity and named risks after them. The central idea, that the 2031 minimum is the floor the portfolio is built to defend, is clean and tied to what Laura must protect. The growth rule is pre-committed and the entry says it will not be rewritten because markets moved, which is the discipline Wharton's Guide asks for. The 2031 range comes with stated confidence (at least 95% on the minimum, roughly even odds on the top), and the return assumptions are on the page. What holds it back is that the growth engine (a floor that accrues at the short yield, a multiplier of three, a monthly cap) takes real effort to picture year by year, and the entry gives almost no projected outcomes under good and bad markets beyond the one-month fall and the range odds. Nothing in the IPS shows what the team learned along the way.
- Anything I did not understand: how the floor "accrues at the short Treasury yield" and then "equals the present value of the minimum from 2031", that is, whether it moves in 2031 or drifts before then; and why the top of the range is the amount "half of 10,000 paths would beat" rather than a stated target.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the best reading of Laura's position. It separates her ability to bear risk (high, because other income covers living costs) from her willingness (low tolerance for a missed payment, because "the artists would bear the loss"), and the floor-then-growth order follows from that. The "held without trading until 2033" rule and the reserve that shrinks as each payment is made answer the allocation-over-time question in plain words. It also states its trade-off openly: moving floor money into equities would raise the expected gift but could break a number Laura has quoted. What holds it back is that the portfolio analysis barely exists. There are no return assumptions, no confidence level for the 2031 range ("we prefer a range built to hold" is not a number), and no outcomes under favourable or unfavourable markets, which the Guide asks for. The case for diversification is one sentence.
- Anything I did not understand: what the "2027 leftover" is (it is never defined), and how the range is "rounded down after any expected shortfall" when that shortfall is never quantified.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: The two-jobs framing (a promise and a vision) gives a central idea a judge can repeat, and "never let the second job put the first at risk" is the right principle. The ladder is defined exactly ("every payment backed by a Treasury maturity on or before its due date"). The growth engine has weights (about 80/20), a rebalancing rule (five points, once a year) and a reason for the risk (five years to 2033 with the payments already secured). The 2031 locked floor and the rule "floor plus at most half the remaining growth, capped at the top" give a readable facility rule. What holds it back is that the growth sleeve stays at about 80% stocks right up to 2033 with no glide path after 2031, so only the floor changes as the needs approach. The top of the range rests on "a stated, modelled confidence level" that is never stated, and the test of good, average and severe markets gives no outcomes. "Stocks are her best defence against rising construction costs in Taiwan" is asserted, not shown, and the currency exposure is not addressed.
- Anything I did not understand: where the range's confidence level is stated (I could not find it), and whether the growth sleeve is still about 80% stocks in 2032 and 2033.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: This has the strongest client story. "She owes ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill" is the reason the ladder exists, and the team says it dropped a three-quarters-stock draft after modelling it (it missed a cheque in about one future in thirty). That shows the response to a challenge that the Articulation criterion asks about, and it shows it inside the IPS. The trade-off is explicit and quantified: a balanced 60/40 usually leaves her richer but breaks a promise about one time in four. High certainty is defined by its failure conditions (a US default, or a two-point yield drop with the 2028 deposit under about $125,000), and the one leak (coupon reinvestment) is priced before the range is named. The capped-range catch is a sharp point about credibility. What holds it back is that growth is small: after the floor is bought, only the interest on the 2028 remainder goes into stocks, about a fifteenth of her money by the team's sums, so the balance leans heavily towards protection. No return assumptions are stated at all, and the range mechanics take two readings to follow.
- Anything I did not understand: how Treasuries "sized to grow back to the whole remainder" pay off (is that a discount bond, and is that consistent with the rule that individual STRIPS are not permitted?); and whether the promised half of the stocks moves into Treasuries on the day of naming, which would make the top a floor, and how that fits the "97 times in 100" claim.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: The lead-gift-plus-conditional-match idea connects the portfolio to something the case itself says matters: a committed personal contribution signals that the residency is viable. The certainty definition (at least 97% in the team's simulations that every payment is met from the reserve alone) is explicit. The moderate 65/35 mix is justified, with a reason that is about reputation, not only about money. What holds it back is that much of the IPS is about how to pitch co-sponsors, which the IPS instructions place in the Final Report. The lead-gift amount is never stated, the "capped by what the growth sleeve can provide in most outcomes" is vague, and flexibility ("keep a portion invested") has no size. Five tickers are named, and the reserve is in a rate-sensitive bond fund until 2031, which sits awkwardly with a high-certainty promise. There are no return assumptions and no projection.
- Anything I did not understand: how big the lead gift is and how it is set (the entry says it is "the amount already moved into Treasuries" but gives no number); and why the early reserve is in a bond fund when the payments are dated.

### Entry 6

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: It shares Entry 4's backbone and its honest account of why the early all-stock draft was dropped and why the payments are one-year bills. Growth is still small, return assumptions are still missing, and the range mechanics carry the same two-reading problem.

## My overall view
The best entry is the one that gives the most mechanical answer to each of Wharton's questions while keeping growth real. Entry 1 does that: its central idea is a floor the 2031 minimum is built to defend, its rules are fixed in advance, its reserve gets safer every year, and it states both its assumptions and its range confidence. Entries 4 and 6 are the most persuasive and the most candid about trade-offs, which is what Wharton says judges want, but they make growth small, leave return assumptions out, and need their range mechanics made plain. Entry 3 is sound and clearly written, but it has no late glide path and no stated range confidence. Entries 2 and 5 have good ideas (willingness versus capacity; a lead gift that signals), yet Entry 2 has almost no analysis and Entry 5 spends the IPS on fundraising and leaves the central number out. To win, the best entry would need to state its return assumptions and projected outcomes, put the range confidence in one sentence, show growth above zero before 2031, and keep the co-sponsor material for the Final Report.
