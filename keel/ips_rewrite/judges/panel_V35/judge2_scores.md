## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4 - Ladder for the payments, then a real 80/20 growth portfolio on the rest; the only entry that lets the facility money actually work for Laura. Thin on numbers.
2. Entry 1 - Best-quantified of the Treasury-floor plans, honest that a 60/40 mix usually leaves her richer, but the growth sleeve is tiny.
3. Entry 3 - Same strategy as Entry 1 with slightly less on the coupon leak and ordering of claims; states its 7.5% return assumption.
4. Entry 6 - Growth-minded (75% VT, 25% QQQ) and original, but the staged tranches are loosely tied to the case and nothing is quantified.
5. Entry 5 - Clear, careful and fully bond-floored; says "growth" but gives almost none, and gives no probabilities.
6. Entry 2 - Same timid core as 1 and 3, but dense and hard to follow, with a lot of machinery for very little upside.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The payments are handled well: a ladder bought in 2027, the 2028 deposit as backstop, and a stated meaning of "high degree of certainty". It also comes with figures for the one leak (coupon reinvestment) and for how the 2031 range behaves. It is honest that a balanced 60/40 portfolio usually leaves Laura richer, and that it chose against that. My problem is the choice itself. Everything above the ladder goes into a Treasury floor, with only the floor's expected interest, about a fifteenth of her money, in stocks. For a client "willing to take thoughtful risks", with six to sixteen years and living costs covered elsewhere, that is a bond fund with a small stock garnish. The facility contribution it leaves her is about the size of the money she put in, and the entry does not count what that safety costs her. The risk side is handled thoughtfully, but the growth side is where this strategy falls short.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a gift "reaches its top about 97 times in 100" when the stock sleeve is so small was not obvious until I worked out that the range itself must be narrow. The "XR" passage reads as decoration.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The strategy underneath is the same as Entry 1: ladder first, floor for the gift, a small stock sleeve from the floor's interest, then a range and a de-risking at naming. It has the sharpest argument that ten cheques on one government are one bet, and it handles inflation and the Taiwan dollar well. But it is written in a compressed shorthand ("the first cheque's fund", moves of money into it from several places), and a judge reading once has to rebuild the mechanics. There are almost no numbers, so I cannot see what Laura gives up in growth, and the plan is the most defensive of the group. Lots of rules, very little upside, and I cannot see how the result compares with a more growth-oriented plan.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "the first cheque's fund" is, and why spare cash and "her half" are both moved there in 2031. The claim that each end of the range is "as sure as its miss is costly" was not explained.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Nearly the same plan as Entry 1, and equally clear. It states its stock return assumption (7.5%), explains why one borrower is deliberate, and says plainly what breaks a cheque. It admits that a 60/40 portfolio usually does better and that its own plan sacrifices that, which is the honesty I look for. But, as in Entry 1, the case it makes against more equity is a one-in-four chance of breaking "a promise, mostly the gift". That treats a missed upside target as if it were a missed payment. For a client with years and outside income, giving up nearly all the upside for the gift to protect against that is timidity dressed as prudence. It has slightly less than Entry 1 on the coupon leak, so I put it just behind.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the reserve "runs itself down" is clear, but how the floor is "bought in 2028" yet repays "what's left of the deposit in late 2032" took me a second pass.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry that treats Laura as the client described: payments secured with a ladder, then roughly 80% world stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries on roughly two-fifths of her money for the stretch to 2031. It then locks the bottom of the announced range in Treasuries before she speaks to co-sponsors, so the growth is real but the promise is protected where it matters. The glide path (2031 lock, 2033 reserve, half of the remaining portfolio capped at the range top) answers "how does the portfolio change as needs approach" directly. The weak point is analysis: no probabilities, no sense of how the range is set or how often it holds, and "rate swings never touch the payments" overlooks coupon reinvestment and a possibly short 2028 deposit. It also spends the 2028 deposit, which comes from income that can shrink in a slump, on equities without discussing that. Still, it chooses the right risk for this client and explains why, and judges can follow it in one read.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "a stated, modelled confidence level" is promised but never stated.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Plain, correct and careful: payments latest-first, a 2032 Treasury ETF to back a facility floor, and a world index fund as the "branch". It spells out client risk willingness and ability and gives a sensible order for covering a 2033 shortfall. But it is timid in the way I dislike. It declines to put floor money in equities to protect a number, caps the range so she "cannot miss it", and says it prefers a range built to hold. The result is a bond-floored plan with a small equity sleeve, an expected facility contribution close to the floor, and no figures for what is given up. It uses "tolerance above average" for the facility without ever putting money behind it. Competent, not ambitious.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the pitch's opening about co-sponsors who "rarely pay to run it" is an assertion with no support in the case; I could not tell where it came from.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is the only other entry that wants to grow the surplus (75% VT, 25% QQQ, about 7%), and the founder framing of runway versus growth capital is original and does connect to Laura. The payments are properly funded. But the facility plan departs from the case: Laura must say in 2031 what she expects to give in 2033, and here later tranches are paid in 2034-2035 and depend on co-sponsor commitments and project milestones, which makes the "range" hard to quote credibly. The QQQ tilt concentrates the stock sleeve in a way I would want justified. Nothing is quantified (no confidence in the range, no shortfall test, no downside), so the "real options" language does not show how much she can responsibly give. Good instincts on growth, weaker on the client's actual questions.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the 2031 range is sized from the three tranches, and what "above its target" means in Gate 2.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the plan lets the money for the facility work. Entries 1, 2, 3 and 5 are one idea in different clothes: pay for the cheques with bonds, then back the gift with a Treasury floor, with only the interest in stocks. They are well argued, and Entries 1 and 3 are honest that a more balanced portfolio usually leaves Laura richer. But in the end they are bond-heavy plans for a client who takes thoughtful risks, with years to go and outside income. They give up the upside almost completely and call that discipline. Entry 4 gets the structure right: lock the promise, grow the rest, and lock the range floor only when she has to speak. To win, it would need numbers: how often its range holds, what a bad 2028-2031 does to the floor, how the 2028 deposit's own risk is handled, and a plain statement of what the 80/20 sleeve gains over the bond-floored alternatives.
