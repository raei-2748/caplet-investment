## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thing I trust most here is the sequence of thought: the team began with a stock-heavy plan, tested it, found that it could miss a payment, and changed course. That is how real students learn, and the text says so plainly. The central idea is easy to hold: ten dated payments are bought outright with Treasuries, and the gift grows on top of a protected floor. It is honest about trade-offs, for instance that a 60/40 mix usually leaves her richer but breaks a promise about one time in four, and that a capped range has a catch. It also admits the ladder's one leak, reinvested coupons, and says when it will be dealt with. Client fit is good: it uses the point that her 2028 deposit comes from income that shrinks in recessions. What holds it back is the pill branding ("XR", "R for redemption"), which feels like a gimmick, and the number of cheerful turns of phrase; it also asks a judge to follow a fairly intricate floor-and-fund structure in few words.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the sentence calling the floor "a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee" took a second read; and I was not sure what "sized to grow back to the whole remainder" means in practice.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Essentially the same sound strategy as Entry 1, and it reasons well in places: putting every cheque on one borrower on purpose, and defining "a high degree of certainty" as exactly two failure conditions, is the sort of plain argument I want to see. The Taiwan-dollar point for the building is a good small touch of client knowledge, and the assumption of 7.5% for world stocks is stated, which helps credibility. It is slightly weaker than Entry 1 because it does not mention the coupon reinvestment weakness, so the certainty claim reads a little cleaner than the evidence supports, and it has less of the "we tried something and it failed" voice. The pill joke is again more branding than thinking.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why "the difference" between the 2028 cost of the floor and the remainder is "about a fifteenth of her money"; I took it on trust.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5.5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, readable, correct answer: fund the payments with matched Treasuries, grow the rest, lock the floor of the range in 2031, and keep half the remainder as flexibility. A student could well have written it, and I like that it defines "high certainty" and says a crash only shrinks the facility. But it feels like the expected answer rather than a thought-through one. The growth sleeve at 80% stocks is asserted, not tested, there is no sign the team tried alternatives or found trouble, and the coupon and rate risks are brushed aside ("rate swings never touch the payments"). The client appears mainly as a risk-taker and as someone needing Taiwan cost protection; the recession-sensitive 2028 deposit is not considered. Headings and bullets make it tidy but add little voice.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4.5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The payments are handled sensibly, and the startup lens is a genuine attempt at an original idea that connects to Laura's career. But the idea bends the case: Laura's facility contribution is decided when the reserve is set aside at the start of 2033, and co-sponsors want a credible range in 2031, not tranches released in 2034-35 on "project milestones" the team cannot know. Gate 3 would be a promise that depends on things outside the portfolio. The growth sleeve of 75% world stocks and 25% Nasdaq-100 is justified only by the claim that innovation firms "have led global returns", which is hindsight, and no numbers or testing of bad outcomes appear. The "real option" language sounds clever without being worked through. I would credit the creativity but not the fit to the case.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the flexible tranches fit with the requirement that she give co-sponsors a dollar range in 2031 with stated confidence.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7.5/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- There is a lot of real reasoning here: why one borrower beats ten, why fixed-dollar withdrawals are different from fixed-percentage ones, the coupon weakness settled in 2031, inflation hurting the building but not the payments. The client fit is strong. My doubts are about authenticity and readability. It opens with a boast about what every team will do, repeats "do not crush", and compresses many ideas into short abstract sentences ("each end as sure as its miss is costly", "the first cheque's fund"), so I had to work to follow it. It reads like something polished by a professional rather than explained by students. A judge who does not read it twice may lose the structure of the floor and the fund. The depth earns marks; the voice and density cost them.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "the first cheque's fund" as a name for the floor-plus-ladder pot, and how exactly the shortfall is "taken off the range before it is quoted".

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 6.5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5.5/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This one reads like an actual policy a careful adviser would write, in plain language. The split between Laura's ability and willingness to bear risk is a good piece of client thinking, with the nice point that artists, not Laura, bear the loss of a missed payment. The order in which any shortfall is absorbed, and the decision not to move floor money into equities even though that would raise the expected contribution, are real trade-offs honestly stated. It admits the single-issuer weakness. Against it: it is dry, there is no sense of a team that tried things and changed its mind, and the numbers are few (a $292,000 cost and nothing on probabilities). Saying she "cannot miss the range" overstates what a range tied to uncertain markets can promise. It also mentions the WInS portfolio, which the IPS does not need.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a 2032 Treasury ETF "sized to return that remainder" works if the ETF is not a held-to-maturity bond.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was visible thinking. Entries 1 and 2 tell me what the team first believed, what the model showed, and what they changed, and they are straight about where the plan can still fail. The others either give me a tidy correct answer (3, 6), a clever idea that does not fit the case (4), or very dense reasoning with the feel of a finished brochure (5). My reservation with the top two is the pill-and-slogan packaging, which a sceptical judge like me finds a little too pleased with itself. For Entry 1 to win outright it would need to cut the branding, say in plain words how many of the numbers are from the team's own model and how they were produced, and trust the ideas to carry it without the wit.
