# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Method: for each entry I wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a pairwise tournament on Wharton's five criteria and derived the ranking from the wins. My prior is that a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes; all six entries reach it, so the ranking turns on what each adds beyond that.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5: the clearest statement of the two-jobs design, with a quantified definition of certainty, an honest trade-off against the obvious alternative, and the one leak in the ladder named and handled. Won all five pairings.
2. Entry 4: the same design and nearly the same quality; loses to Entry 5 only because it leaves one risk unmentioned and its ordering of priorities is implicit. Won four pairings.
3. Entry 3: the most carefully reasoned of the formal entries, with two insights about the client I had not seen elsewhere; held back by one step in the construction that a reader has to reconstruct. Won three pairings.
4. Entry 1: the richest in ideas per word and the sharpest single decision rule in the set, but compressed to the point where a one-pass reader loses the mechanism. Won two pairings.
5. Entry 2: competent, consistent, well organised, and almost entirely what my prior predicted. Won one pairing.
6. Entry 6: the only genuinely different framing in the set, but the framing fits the client's problem less well than it first appears and the portfolio analysis is thin. Won no pairings.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: a single decision rule that governs the whole plan (a promise is made safe on the day it becomes a promise), the observation that Laura's 2028 deposit is itself a risky asset correlated with the very conditions that make the ladder dearer, and the argument that spreading the payments across several issuers would make a missed January more likely rather than less. These are the kind of ideas my prior says earn a top score, and the entry also sees the asymmetry a capped range creates once announced and fixes it. What holds it back is how it is written: nearly every paragraph carries two ideas in the space of one, several mechanisms are stated rather than explained, and a judge reading once will finish with the impression of cleverness rather than a plan they could repeat back. The team's own learning (an equity-heavy first plan that failed in the model) is mentioned but not developed. The opener presumes to speak for other teams, which I did not hold against it but did not reward either.
- Not understood on first reading: what "the first cheque's fund" is and why the 2028 deposit buys more of it; how large the world index fund is relative to the floor; and how the ten-independent-bets arithmetic relates to the one-issuer choice (I worked it out on a second pass, which a competition judge may not take).

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: very little that my prior did not already contain. The entry does the fundamentals properly: a ladder bought in 2027 and completed by the 2028 deposit, a growth sleeve with a stated mix and a rebalancing rule, a 2031 floor moved into Treasuries, a 2033 contribution rule with a cap and a retained flexibility fund, stated return and inflation assumptions, and a statement that outcomes were tested across good, average and severe markets. That last point is a real strength under Portfolio Analysis and the plan is internally consistent, so I believe a client would understand it and accept it. But the tailoring to Laura stops at "entrepreneur who keeps her word": there is no use of the facts that make her case unusual, such as the nature of her 2028 income or what the range is for, and the 80/20 growth sleeve is asserted rather than argued against an alternative. It would be a sound IPS from any of several hundred competent teams.
- Not understood on first reading: none.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that co-sponsors who fund buildings seldom fund operations, which is a sharp reason for the ordering of the whole plan and is drawn from how Laura's world actually works rather than from the portfolio; and a clean separation of Laura's ability to bear risk (high, since nothing is withdrawn before 2033) from her willingness, which the entry correctly says differs by objective because the artists, not Laura, bear a missed payment. The sequencing of purchases by rate sensitivity, the explicit refusal to move floor money into equities even though it would raise the expected gift, and the waterfall for any shortfall are all disciplined reasoning of the kind Wharton asks for. The entry reads as a policy document rather than a story, which costs it a little on narrative voice but nothing on credibility. What holds it back is one construction step the reader has to infer (see below), the absence of any uncertainty figures around the range it says is built to hold, and a thin showing of the team's own process.
- Not understood on first reading: where the money for the global index fund comes from. The 2028 remainder is said to buy a 2032 Treasury ETF sized to return that remainder, and then "the rest" forms the branch; I eventually took this to mean the Treasury costs less than it repays and the difference is the branch, but the entry does not say so.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: a definition of "high degree of certainty" stated as the two specific conditions under which a payment could fail, one of them a joint condition involving both a yield move and a shortfall in Laura's 2028 deposit, which is exactly the kind of operational definition the case asks for and almost no team gives. The insight that Laura's second deposit is correlated with the recessions that make bonds dear, and that she has therefore already taken her big risk, tailors the plan to this client specifically. The entry also runs the trade-off I most wanted to see: against a balanced 60/40 portfolio that is usually richer, it reports how often that alternative breaks a promise, and chooses the floor anyway with its reasons. The capped-range asymmetry and its fix are explained so that a reader can see why the fix is needed. The team's learning (the equity-heavy first plan sinking in the model) is told as a decision, not a confession. What holds it back relative to Entry 5 is that it does not mention coupon reinvestment, the one risk the ladder still carries, and that the order of priorities if things change is left implicit.
- Not understood on first reading: the base for "a fifteenth of her money" (of the deposit, or of all $450,000), and what counts as breaking "the gift" in the 60/40 comparison, since in that alternative no range would yet have been named.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: everything I learned from Entry 4, plus the reframing of the commitment as ten one-year bills rather than one ten-year bill, which is the right way to see why a fixed-dollar liability is more dangerous than its size suggests; the naming of the one remaining leak (coupons reinvested at lower yields) and a stated decision about when it is priced and funded; and the comparison of the floor-plus-interest construction to a capital-protected note without the bank's fee, which told me in one stroke what the structure is and why it is cheap. The entry ends with an explicit order of priorities if circumstances change, which closes a question Entries 1 and 4 leave open. It defines certainty operationally, tests the obvious alternative and says how often it fails, explains the range mechanics and the fix for the capped-range asymmetry, and keeps the client's circumstances (the nature of her 2028 income, the building priced in another currency) in view throughout. What holds it back from a higher score: the uncertainty analysis is reported as a few headline figures rather than as a view across market outcomes, the team's process appears only as the failed first plan, and it gives up the single-issuer argument that Entries 1 and 4 make.
- Not understood on first reading: the base for "a fifteenth of everything she owns"; otherwise none.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: a genuinely different way of seeing the facility contribution, as a sequence of real options exercised against evidence rather than one number, and the point that a single 2033 commitment forces a bet on one market moment. As a decision scientist I am sympathetic to the framing, and the entry argues it with conviction. But the framing fits the case less well than it appears. The case says Laura's own contribution is what signals commitment and draws co-sponsors in; making her second tranche conditional on co-sponsors committing first inverts that logic. The third tranche arrives in 2034-35, while the case asks what she contributes toward establishing the facility in 2033, so the top of the announced range includes money that may not arrive when the building is paid for. The growth sleeve's tilt to a technology index is justified by Laura's career, which is a reason for less exposure there, not more, if her 2028 income depends on the same sector. There are no figures on how the plan fares across market outcomes, no handling of a short 2028 deposit, and no statement of how the top of the range is set or with what confidence. The creativity is real; the analysis behind it is not yet.
- Not understood on first reading: how the top of the 2031 range is determined when two of its three parts depend on future gates; and whether a tranche paid in 2034-35 counts as the 2033 facility contribution the case describes.

## My overall view
All six teams reached the ladder; that is where my prior said they would be, and it earned none of them anything on its own. What separated the top from the rest was whether the entry saw something about Laura's problem that the ladder does not solve by itself. Entries 4 and 5 saw that her second deposit is a risky asset correlated with the bad state of the world, that a capped range creates an asymmetry the day it is named, and that the obvious balanced alternative is usually richer and still wrong for her, and they said how often. Entry 3 saw how co-sponsors behave and why that orders the plan. Entry 1 saw as much as any of them but did not give the reader time to see it. Entries 2 and 6 sit at opposite ends of the same failure: one has the discipline without a new idea, the other has a new idea without the discipline. For Entry 5 to win outright it would need to show the uncertainty analysis as a view across outcomes rather than three headline figures, carry the single-issuer argument it dropped, and let the team's own reasoning process show a little more, since Wharton scores that too.
