## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the second deposit can itself be the floor, bought outright in 2028 as Treasuries that grow back into the full amount, with only the expected interest put at risk in stocks. That is the capital-protected-note structure without the bank, and it is a sharper way of seeing the facility problem than "ladder plus growth sleeve". I also learned the "ten one-year bills" framing of why the operating commitment cannot be left in a balanced portfolio: the danger is not average return but a forced sale in a particular year. Client fit is the best in the field: it notices that the same recession that raises the ladder's cost also shrinks the publishing and speaking income behind the 2028 deposit, which is exactly the correlation the case plants, and it keeps half the stocks liquid against a Taiwan-dollar building. What holds it back is the comparison it chooses to run. Its rival is a 60/40 portfolio with no ladder at all, which "usually" ends far ahead but misses payments or breaks its word one time in three. That argues for the ladder, which I already accept, rather than for the interesting part of the plan, the pre-bought floor. The priority order (payments, then floor, then gift) is a decision rule I can audit, and the operating reserve's run-down is stated plainly. The section heading "Sell low, by appointment" floats above a paragraph about something else and the pharmacy ending asks the reader to do work that Wharton's criteria do not reward.
- Did not understand on first reading: "ours, under one in sixty" sits after "misses a cheque or breaks its word", so on first pass I could not tell whether this plan misses an operating payment about 1.7% of the time (which would sit badly with "high degree of certainty") or only falls short of its announced range. I also had to re-read "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" to work out what instrument was meant.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already know. That is not a complaint about competence; it is the whole verdict. The ladder is correctly specified (maturity on or before each date, coupons parked short, held to maturity so rate moves do not matter, the 2028 deposit tops it up first). The growth engine is a standard 80/20 with a five-point rebalancing band. The 2031 step (move the bottom of the range into Treasuries maturing before 2033) is sensible and the facility rule (floor plus at most half the remainder, capped) preserves flexibility as the case asks. Assumptions are stated and three scenarios are named. It reads clearly and in a plausible student voice, and the credibility argument for why the payments must not be gambled is well put. But every design choice is the default choice, and none is defended against an alternative: why 80/20 and not 100% stocks given the payments are already secured, why move the floor only in 2031 rather than earlier, what the modelled confidence on the top of the range actually is. The claim that stocks are the best defence against Taiwan construction costs is asserted, not argued. Among 2,300 teams this entry will have many near-twins; a judge cannot pick it out.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the "stated, modelled confidence level" on the top of the range is set, and why the 20% intermediate Treasuries in the growth sleeve are there if the 2031 de-risking step already exists. Neither is fatal.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: everything I learned from Entry 1 (the deposit as an owned floor, the recession that hits both the ladder's price and the deposit's source, the asymmetry of a capped range once it is named, which is why the promised half is de-risked on the day of the promise), plus the one thing I most wanted: a controlled comparison. The rival keeps the same ladder and differs in one respect only, it leaves the second deposit in stocks and fixes the floor in 2031. Holding everything else constant, the entry states the price of its floor (about $9,000 in a typical 2033) and what it buys (the chance of having to promise under $100,000 falls from about one in thirteen to about one in 150), and then says it would pay that price. That is a trade-off argued the way Wharton asks for: assumptions, cost, benefit, decision. It also reads the 2031 moment correctly as the credibility moment, and adds the observation that the rival's bottom falls in exactly the years donors give less, which is a correlation insight about the client's world rather than about markets. "High degree of certainty" is defined as a list of failure modes with a plan for each, including the coupon-reinvestment leak that most teams will not notice. The reserve's run-down and the priority order are explicit. What holds it back: the growth sleeve is small, so the plan takes less of the "thoughtful risk" the case invites than Entry 4 does, and the entry relies on the reader trusting its model numbers without any statement of the return assumption behind them (Entries 1 and 5 at least name 7.5%). The pharmacy ending remains a flourish rather than content.
- Did not understand on first reading: how a floor "owned since 2028" can ever lead to promising under $100,000 (the one-in-150 figure). I eventually inferred it is the case where the 2028 deposit first has to complete the ladder and the coupon shortfall is also set aside, but the text made me work for it.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that one can make the 2031 announced minimum the explicit target of a mechanical rule rather than a number read off a portfolio, and that the team understood the costs of doing so. The sleeve runs a constant-proportion rule with a multiplier of three, and the entry explains why three and not five (a breach needs a one-month fall of roughly a third while fully invested), accepts cash-lock as the price, claims the 2028 deposit first for the ladder, and names currency as a risk paid from the flexibility share. As a decision scientist I value "we will not rewrite the rule because markets moved" and "falls cut stock exposure before anyone is tempted to decide" more than most judges will; it is a pre-commitment device against the team's own future discretion. The range construction (protected minimum at 95% confidence; top at the median of 10,000 paths, described as even odds) is the most precisely specified in the field. What holds it back: the client is nearly absent until a two-sentence coda, the residency and Taiwan appear only as a currency line, and the rule's floor before 2031 (60% of the sleeve, accruing at the short rate) protects nothing Laura has promised, so for four years the machinery runs without a purpose a client could name. More seriously, the plan's central claim is "Floor = Promise", but it never says how the promise is chosen in 2031; if the minimum is simply whatever the rule has protected by then, the slogan is circular. The owned floor in Entries 1, 3 and 5 is a simpler and stronger version of the same protection, with no gap risk and no monthly trading, so the extra complexity has to earn its place through higher upside, and the entry never shows the upside in dollars. A reader meeting CPPI for the first time will not come away able to explain it to Laura.
- Did not understand on first reading: where the 2031 announced minimum comes from (see above); what "the floor starts near 60% of the sleeve" is protecting before any promise exists; whether monthly rebalancing is meant to continue after 2033 for the flexibility share.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same structural idea as Entries 1 and 3, defended here by sequence risk rather than by the price of insurance. The rival again keeps the ladder and differs only in leaving the second deposit in stocks until 2031 and then buying a floor; it "usually" gives about $4,000 more, but one time in twenty stocks are down a quarter on the date it must convert, and its gift falls to about $115,000 against about $155,000 here. The point that a rival which de-risks on a fixed future date is "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" is a correct and under-appreciated argument: a date-triggered conversion is itself a bet on one day's prices, and the pre-bought floor removes that bet entirely. The failure-mode definition of certainty, the recession that hits both ladder cost and deposit source, the capped-range asymmetry, the de-risking of the promised half on the day of the promise, the liquid half kept against a Taiwan-dollar building, and the explicit priority order are all here. Where it sits against Entry 3: the $4,000 figure is a cost but is not framed as a cost paid for a stated benefit the way Entry 3's $9,000 is, and the benefit is given as a gift size in a bad year rather than as the probability of having to make an embarrassing promise, which is slightly further from what the case says Laura fears. The section heading "Sell low, by appointment" appears before a paragraph about the team's abandoned first plan, and the reader only discovers what it refers to a paragraph later.
- Did not understand on first reading: the floating heading, as above; and whether the rival's floor in 2031 is the same size as this plan's or set from whatever the stocks are worth that day (I assumed the latter, which is what makes the comparison bite).

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What I learned: that the facility contribution can be framed as staged venture funding, released against milestones, with each later tranche a real option Laura holds rather than a commitment she owes. That is a genuinely different way of seeing the problem and it speaks to who the client is. The runway section is a sound ladder with a clean definition of certainty. But the idea is not carried through on Wharton's terms. The case asks for a range and a stated confidence that the 2033 contribution falls within it; this entry gives neither a confidence level nor any modelled outcome, and its only quantitative content is "about 7% a year with deep drawdowns possible". Gate 3 pays money in 2034-2035, which is outside the 2033 contribution the case defines, and Gate 2 makes Laura's own tranche conditional on co-sponsors having committed first, which inverts the case's logic that her contribution is the signal that brings co-sponsors in. The growth sleeve's 25% in a technology index is justified by past leadership and by Laura's own career being in technology; the first reason is the performance-chasing Wharton explicitly warns against, and the second is backwards, since her 2028 deposit and ongoing income already depend on that sector, so the tilt adds correlated risk rather than diversification. A fine pitch with the analysis still to be done.
- Did not understand on first reading: what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio at Gate 2; whether the 2034-2035 tranche is meant to count toward the 2033 facility contribution the case asks teams to range; and what the announced top of the range would be in dollars or probability.

## My overall view
Five of six entries bought the ten payments with a Treasury ladder, and all five defined "high certainty" the same correct way, so the ladder decided nothing. What separated the top three from the rest was the second deposit: Entries 1, 3 and 5 saw that the 2028 money can be the floor itself, bought outright and owned from 2028, with only its expected interest at risk, which turns the 2031 range from a forecast into a statement about something Laura already holds. Entry 4 reached a defensible alternative by machinery rather than by ownership and was honest about its costs, but left its own key number unexplained and the client out of the room. Entry 2 did everything right and nothing distinctive; Entry 6 had the freshest lens and did not do the work beneath it. Among the three near-identical top entries, I rewarded the one whose comparison held everything constant but one choice and then priced that choice against the client's stated fear (an embarrassing promise in 2031), because that is the shape of a well-argued trade-off and it is rare at this level. To win outright, the best entry would need to state the return assumption its model numbers rest on, show in one line what the conservative floor costs in expected gift size against a plan that takes more of the thoughtful risk the case invites (Entry 4's kind), and resolve the small confusions a first-time reader hits: how an owned floor can still fall short, and why a plan this careful ends on a joke.
