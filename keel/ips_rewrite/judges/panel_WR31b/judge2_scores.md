# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 - the only entry that names the full price of its own caution (almost $200,000 behind a 60/40 in a typical year) and then argues, with odds, why Laura should pay it.
2. Entry 3 - the one plan that actually keeps Laura in stocks early (the rule starts near fully invested) and protects the minimum mechanically; candid about cash-lock and gap risk.
3. Entry 4 - same plan as 6, with a smaller, more honest-sounding price ($9,000 in a typical 2033) and a rival that is closer to fair.
4. Entry 1 - the most growth-minded plain plan (80% stocks above the ladder, floor locked in 2031), but the price of its safety is never counted.
5. Entry 2 - same plan as 4 and 6, but its stated cost of caution (about $4,000) makes the safety look cheaper than it is, and the growth side is thin.
6. Entry 5 - startup metaphor carrying an under-argued plan: an unexplained 25% tech tilt, gates tied to project events, and no numbers.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean and sensible plan: buy the ten payments with Treasuries, put everything above that into roughly 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries, lock the bottom of the range in 2031, and give at most half the growth pot. For a client who "takes thoughtful risks", it is the plan that leaves the most money working for the facility, and I like that it says stocks are the better defence against Taiwan construction-cost inflation. What holds it back is analysis. It says "test good, average and severe markets" but shows no result, gives no odds for the range, and never says what the ladder costs Laura in forgone growth. The half-of-growth rule is asserted rather than defended. Reads as competent, not distinguished.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The same Caplet XR plan as Entries 4 and 6. The client reading is strong (the 2028 deposit depends on the same income a recession would hurt, Taiwan-dollar pricing, a range she can defend). The strategy is, though, a protected-note design in which stocks are bought only with the interest the 2028 Treasuries should earn, then half the gain is given up in the range. That is a lot of caution for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks. The rival it tests is a fair one, but it reports the rival as only about $4,000 better in a usual case, which understates what the safety costs, and it measures the downside with a single 1-in-20 scenario. The "do not crush" ending and the "buy it, don't invest it" framing are memorable, but I mark on the strategy, not the style.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Sell low, by appointment" and "Do not crush" read like stray labels, and I had to work out the "capital-protected note" analogy (what the bank's option is and why shares replace it) before the growth leg made sense.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The ladder pays the ten payments, and the growth sleeve starts near fully invested in a world ETF and cuts stock exposure only as the cushion above a rising floor shrinks. In effect it takes risk early, when the client has time, and defends the minimum she will announce, which is how I would want a risk-willing client's money run. It states its assumptions (about 7% stock return, 16% volatility, 4.5% Treasuries), names the failure modes (gap risk, cash-lock, the 2028 deposit income, currency) and says it accepts them. The cost is jargon and mechanics (CPPI, multipliers, monthly rebalancing) in a document Wharton wanted to be about framework, and a cash-lock that could leave Laura out of a recovery. It also gives up half the gain above the floor, which is a generous cushion, and it never shows what the rule costs against simply holding stocks.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor "accrues" and what "present value of Laura's announced minimum" means in practice; I had to reconstruct that the sleeve starts at a high stock share because the cushion is large.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same base plan as Entries 2 and 6. Its test against the rival is the more useful one here: it sets the rival to promise the same $145,000, shows that its bottom falls below $100,000 about 1 time in 13 against about 1 in 150, and states the price as about $9,000 of Laura's own money in a typical 2033. A figure like that is what I want from a plan that holds back on risk, because it lets the client decide whether the insurance is worth it. The sum is small against $450,000 of contributions, which is itself a sign that the plan gives up little growth. The weakness is the same as its siblings: stocks are bought with the interest only, so the facility money has limited growth, and half the gain above the floor is held back.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the rival's floor "falls with the market, in the years donors give less"; I took it to mean it would have to be set after stocks have already fallen, when co-sponsors are also tighter.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The ladder for the ten payments is sound and the 2031 seed tranche is a reasonable way to lock the bottom of the range. Everything else is weakly argued. A 25% weight in a Nasdaq-100 fund on top of a world fund is a concentrated tech bet justified by "innovation-driven companies have led returns" and Laura's career, which is a hindsight argument and not a diversification case. The staged gates tie the contribution to co-sponsor commitments, residency performance in 2034-35 and portfolio recovery, none of which the client's case asks the portfolio to decide. The case needs a dollar range and a confidence level in 2031, and here there is neither. No modelled outcome, probability or cost of the lock-in appears anywhere, so an investor reading it cannot tell what Laura gives up or gains. A growth tilt is welcome in principle, but this one is not supported.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "real option" to contribute more later lines up with a range she must announce in 2031, and how the tranches decide the amount she actually pays in 2033.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry that counts the price of safety: in its own model a 60/40 usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead, and it says so in plain numbers before explaining why it still prefers the ladder, namely that about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word, against under one in sixty here. Being specific about the upside given away is the thing I look for and almost nobody does. I do think the rival is a little generous to my side of the argument, since it puts the whole portfolio in 60/40 and not just the money above the ladder, so some of the $200,000 is a flattering gap. And the plan still grows the gift only with the interest on the 2028 Treasuries, which is cautious. But it is honest about it and has the odds behind its choice, which most entries lack. The conclusion is justified by the model, not by adjectives.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the 60/40 comparison includes the ladder, i.e. whether "ours" and the rival are fully comparable; the "one in three" miss depends on forced sales in a bad year, which I accepted but would want shown.

## My overall view
What separated the better entries is whether they count the cost of safety and then defend it with numbers, not whether they chose the safe route. Entries 2, 4 and 6 share a conservative design (buy the promise, grow the gift from the interest), and the one that put the largest and most honest dollar figure on what that caution costs, with odds on the other side, is the one I trust most. Entry 3 is the only plan that gives stocks real room early and then protects the minimum by rule, and I would back it more if it explained itself without the machinery. Entry 1 is the most growth-minded of the plain plans but never does the arithmetic. Entry 5 offers a story where the analysis should be. To win from my chair, the best entry would need to keep Entry 6's candour about price while putting more of the 2028 money into stocks in the years 2028-2031, and show in a number what that would add to the middle of the range and what it would cost in the bad tail.
