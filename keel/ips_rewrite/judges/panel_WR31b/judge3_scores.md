# Judge J3 scores

Judge: Priya (pension actuary, asset-liability matching). Method: traced the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the
2031 range, the 2033 contribution and the ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042) through each plan, looking for the unpriced hole.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: the ladder-plus-floor plan that finds its own leaks (coupon reinvestment, a recession that hits both ladder cost and the 2028 deposit) and prices what the floor costs Laura in her own terms.
2. Entry 5: a CPPI rule on the growth sleeve, with the most honest list of how the rule itself can fail; misses the reinvestment leak in the ladder.
3. Entry 2: same plan as Entry 3, tested against a credible rival, but the trade-off is expressed less in terms of what the client must protect.
4. Entry 1: same plan again, but argued against a 60/40 straw man, so the comparison proves little.
5. Entry 6: a sound, conventional ladder-plus-growth plan that claims rates "never touch the payments" and never puts a number on its range confidence.
6. Entry 4: a creative stage-gate story with no quantified range, a tech tilt correlated with Laura's own income, and tranches that drift past 2033.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The cash flows trace cleanly: $300,000 buys the ten payments, any shortfall is a first claim on the 2028 deposit, most of the $150,000 buys a Treasury floor that grows back to $150,000 by 2033, and the remainder goes into one world fund. The team also sees the things most students miss: coupons that arrive before 2033 must be reinvested, and a recession both raises the ladder's cost and shrinks the income behind the 2028 deposit. The weakness is the rival it argues against. Nobody who matches liabilities would hold a whole-portfolio 60/40 against ten dated payments, so beating it says little about why this design and not a near neighbour. Flexibility is thin (half of a small stock sleeve), and that cost of all the safety is never weighed against "willing to take thoughtful risks" except through the straw man.
- Not understood on first reading: if the promised half moves into Treasuries on the day the range is named, the top should be close to certain, so I could not see why it is reached only "about 97 times in 100". It is also unclear whether the floor shrinks when the 2028 deposit has to fill a ladder gap first.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same plan and the same strong cash-flow tracing as Entry 1, with a much better test: the real rival keeps the ladder but waits until 2031 to buy the floor. That is exactly the timing question an actuary would ask. The comparison is honest. The rival gives a little more in a typical outcome, but it has to lock its floor in on a fixed date whatever the market does, and the entry puts a number on that tail. What holds it back is that the trade-off is framed as gift size rather than as the credibility of what Laura tells co-sponsors, which is what the case says is at stake. It shares Entry 1's thin flexibility and its unexplained 97-in-100.
- Not understood on first reading: the same 97-in-100 question as Entry 1. The stock return assumption is given, but not the bond or reinvestment-rate assumptions behind "price that shortfall".

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry whose analysis is aimed at the failure the case cares about most after the payments: having to announce a smaller minimum in a bad year, when donors are also giving less. It prices the protection both ways, as a rare failure and as a cost of about $9,000 of Laura's own money in a typical 2033, and then says it would pay that. That is a trade-off stated the way an actuary would want it. The ladder logic is the same sound structure as Entries 1 and 2: the first deposit is matched to the dated payments, a gap is a named first claim on the 2028 money, and the correlated recession risk and the reinvestment leak are both identified, with the leak funded from the hope before any gift is named. The reserve runs down by maturity, so it needs no rebalancing in the payment years. What holds it back is how little equity risk the plan carries for a client who takes thoughtful risks, and how small her flexibility pool is after 2033. It also states fewer explicit assumptions than I would like (no return figures at all).
- Not understood on first reading: the same 97-in-100 question, and which return and rate assumptions sit behind "in our model".

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The milestone idea is genuinely creative, and the payment ladder is correctly built and held to maturity. But the plan never quantifies anything a co-sponsor or a judge could check. Seed tranche size, range, confidence level and any shortfall if the 2027 money does not buy the whole ladder are all left open. A one-year gap before 2028 is exposed to rates and to the same income risk, and it is not discussed. The 25% QQQ tilt is justified by Laura's career in technology and creative industries, which is the wrong direction: her 2028 deposit and her livelihood already depend on that sector, so the portfolio should diversify away from it. Paying tranches in 2034-2035 moves the facility decision past the 2033 point the case sets. And "every promise is sized to a tranche already secured" sits uneasily with a range top that depends on tranches not yet secured.
- Not understood on first reading: how big the seed tranche is, how the top of the range is set, and how a contribution paid in 2034-2035 fits a case that sets the decision in 2033.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most technically literate risk management in the set. Tying a CPPI floor to the announced minimum makes the 2031 promise something the portfolio actually defends, and the team understands its own tool. It explains why the multiplier is three, says what size of monthly fall breaks the floor, admits the cash-lock cost, and names the deposit, currency and liquidity risks. It also states its assumptions and gives confidence figures for both ends of the range, which most entries do not. The ladder is where it has a hole: "each sized to deliver $50,000, held to maturity and never sold" ignores the coupons that arrive years before they are needed and the rate they will be reinvested at. It also does not say how the 60% starting floor relates to the minimum Laura can announce in 2031. A monthly dynamic rule is also more machinery than the problem strictly needs, though the team does justify it.
- Not understood on first reading: whether the gift can exceed the announced top (the top is set at even odds, and the gift is the minimum plus half the gain), so it is unclear what "confidence that it falls in the range" actually is.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is a well-organised, complete answer to the IPS questions: a matched ladder, a growth sleeve with a bond shock absorber, a 2031 floor lock, the floor plus half the gains as the gift, and the rest kept as flexibility. It is the hole my prior expects, though. The plan says coupons are held in short Treasuries and then says rate swings "never touch the payments", and that is not true. Falling rates lower what those coupons earn, and if the first deposit falls short, they raise the price of the rungs bought in 2028. Because the floor is only locked in 2031, its size falls with the market in the bad years, and the entry neither notices nor prices that. The range confidence is promised as "a stated, modelled confidence level" but never stated.
- Not understood on first reading: none.

## My overall view
Every entry here buys the payments with a Treasury ladder, so the ladder did not separate them. What separated them was whether the team found the places where a matched ladder is not quite matched, and whether it priced the floor under the 2031 promise rather than just asserting one. Entry 3 did both. It caught the reinvestment leak, the fact that a recession hurts the ladder price and the 2028 deposit at once, and the pro-cyclical floor of the obvious rival, and it put a cost on its own choice in Laura's money. Entry 5 understood dynamic protection better than anyone but missed the leak in its own ladder. To win, Entry 3 needs to state its return and rate assumptions plainly. It also needs to explain why a top that is locked into Treasuries is reached only 97 times in 100, and to show that the thin post-2033 flexibility is a deliberate trade-off rather than a by-product of so much safety.
