## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strategy is coherent from end to end. The promise is bought with Treasuries, the 2028 money builds a floor that grows back to the gift's bottom, and the surplus sits in one world fund. It deals with the real ways a "high degree of certainty" can fail, including the lower-yield recession that raises the ladder's cost just as the 2028 deposit weakens. I like that it names a serious rival and admits that rival "usually" does a few thousand dollars better, then explains why that still loses on the bad-year tail. It also admits the catch in a capped range. What holds it back is the voice. It is very polished and aphoristic, with short punchy lines and a coined plan name, and it reads more like an advertisement than a team working things out. The probabilities are stated but I cannot see where they came from.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The "capital-protected note minus the option" comparison and the "about 97 times in 100" claim took a second read. I also wasn't sure what the closing "Do not crush." refers to.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The Treasury ladder for the ten payments is sound and clearly explained. Everything after that is weak. The growth money goes 75/25 into VT and QQQ on the grounds that "innovation-driven companies have led returns", which is a bet on a past trend and a tilt to one narrow index with no discussion of the risk. The tranche gates are tied to co-sponsor commitments and to the residency "operating on plan", but the case says the facility contribution depends on the portfolio, so the gates import outside events. A tranche paid in 2034-35 also goes beyond what the 2031 range has to cover. There is almost no quantification: no confidence level for the range, no downside test, and 7% a year is stated without a basis. The startup language ("runway", "burn", "seed") feels borrowed from a pitch deck rather than from the work done.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How the 2031 range gets its top, and what "above its target" means for Gate 2.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same architecture as Entry 1, and it is a strong one for Laura: the promise bought outright, a floor built in 2028, a range whose bottom is already owned. The difference is the trade-off paragraph. It ties Laura's willingness to take thoughtful risks to a rival that puts the rest in stocks, and it argues in terms of the number that matters for the co-sponsor promise, how often the bottom of the range would have to fall below $100,000. It then says plainly that the floor costs about $9,000 of Laura's own money in a typical 2033 and that the team would pay it. That is the honest cost-of-safety statement I look for. It loses a little for the same brochure feel as Entry 1, and the "one in thirteen" versus "one in 150" comparison is hard for a reader to check.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Set to promise the same $145,000 in a typical year" - I took it to mean both plans are tuned to give the same 2031 promise on average, but it took effort.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is a different and thoughtful answer. The 2031 promise should be what the portfolio is mechanically built to protect, and a pre-committed rule (CPPI) protects it. The team defines high certainty, gives its return and volatility assumptions, and has a real "risks we accept" section: gap risk, cash-lock, the 2028 deposit, currency. That honesty about what could go wrong is its best feature. Against that, it goes into more detail than Wharton asks for (multipliers, monthly rebalancing, 10,000 paths), and it reads like a quant desk note, so I am less sure a student wrote it. The strategy also risks being too complicated for the insight, and it is hard for a lay reader to see why the rule beats a simpler floor. Client fit is real, but the voice is flat and the structure is a checklist.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Three times the cushion" and why that multiplier is safe; and why a floor starting at "near 60% of the sleeve" is the right start.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A sensible, readable plan that a team could genuinely have written: ladder first, then 80/20 global stocks and Treasuries, a floor locked in 2031, half of the remaining growth portfolio as the most she gives. It follows the case closely, includes inflation and construction costs, and has an annual review. But it is the plan many teams will submit. It doesn't say what alternative was considered or rejected, and the claims ("rate swings never touch the payments", "we test outcomes in good, average and severe markets") come with no numbers or findings. The 2031 floor is also moved into Treasuries at that point, which leaves open what happens if stocks have fallen by then. It is honest and plain, but it shows little of the thinking behind it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How "roughly two-thirds of total contributions" fits with the ladder being funded by the first $300,000 alone.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same strong backbone as Entries 1 and 3, and the "ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill" point is a clear way of explaining why timing risk matters. The trade-off paragraph picks a 60/40 portfolio as the rival and says it usually ends nearly $200,000 ahead, which is honest. But it is not the best alternative to this plan. A ladder plus a stock portfolio is the natural comparison, and a plain 60/40 missing a cheque one time in three is an easy win, so the case against the plan feels easier than it should. It also has the same slogan-heavy feel as the others.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Why a 60/40 portfolio would "break its word to co-sponsors" at the same rate as it misses a cheque.

## My overall view
Three of these six are the same plan with a different rival paragraph, so what separates them is how honestly the team argues with itself. Buying the promise outright with Treasuries, building a floor in 2028, and naming a range whose bottom she already owns is a real answer to Laura's case, and the best entries say what it costs her in dollars and where the tempting alternative would win. Entry 3 does that most cleanly. My worry is voice. All three are so polished and aphoristic that I can't be sure real teenagers wrote them, and I would trade a few lines of cleverness for a plain account of what the team tried, got wrong and changed. Entry 4 has the most candid risk section and a different idea, but it overreaches on mechanism. Entry 5 is honest but unremarkable, and Entry 2 is a metaphor without enough analysis behind it. To win, the best entry would need to keep its reasoning, lose some of the slogans, and show a little more of how the numbers were produced and what the team got wrong along the way.
