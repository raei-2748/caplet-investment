## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: The entry has one clear idea, that the first $300,000 buys the ten payments and the growth money is kept apart from them, and it defends that idea against the obvious alternative of a 60/40 mix with stated failure rates and a stated return assumption. It states the reasons the payments could fail (US default, a recession that lowers yields before the ladder is bought, a weaker 2028 deposit) and prices the coupon reinvestment gap in 2031, which is honest and useful. It answers the five IPS questions in a readable order: the 2028 floor, the 2031 range, and the 2033 reserve running down. The weak points are the confidence figure for the 2031 range, which is hard to read (it is not clear what the 97-in-100 measures or whether it covers landing inside the range), and the growth sleeve is a single world fund with little discussion of diversification. Laura's own circumstances are used mostly as the reason for the design, not as a set of tailored choices.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: What the "97 times in 100" figure measures, and whether a top that is hit that often is still a range or has become a promise. The phrase about which half of the stocks moves into Treasuries and when is also unclear on first read.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: This entry states a single central idea (the minimum Laura announces is the floor the portfolio is built to protect) and then sets out three buckets, which answers Wharton's questions directly. It gives the confidence levels Wharton asks for: at least 95% confidence in the minimum, and a top that roughly half of simulated paths would beat, with assumptions for returns and volatility stated. It is the only entry that names real risks with honest numbers, including gap risk, the cash-lock case and currency, and it shows the rules-based approach keeps the payments outside any stock return. It also covers diversification (one world fund spread across many companies) and the 2028 deposit as a source of risk. The drawbacks are that the rule is complex for a non-specialist judge and the presentation is dense; the team's own experience gets no space, which is acceptable because Articulation is mostly judged in the Final Report.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The exact mechanics of the rule that sets the stock share (the cushion and multiplier) took a second read. I also wondered why the top of the range is set at the median path, which means roughly even odds.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What it does well and what holds it back: The founder analogy (fund the runway first, then release growth money in stages) is original and suits Laura's entrepreneurial background, and the commitment to hold the payment bonds to maturity is clearly stated. The problem is the facility timing. Wharton and the client case put the contribution decision at the start of 2033, but Gate 3 releases money in 2034 to 2035, and the entry does not reconcile this with the 2031 range. The growth sleeve (mostly world stocks plus a technology-heavy fund) is justified by how innovation companies have performed, which is a hindsight argument rather than a reason. The gate sizes and the "growth portfolio above target" trigger are never defined, so the portfolio analysis is thin.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How big each tranche is, and what the target is for the growth portfolio at each gate. I also could not tell how the 2031 range relates to the later gates.

## My overall view
The best entries in this group did what Wharton asked: they named one central idea, stated the reasons the payments could fail, and gave a confidence level for the 2031 range along with the assumptions behind it. The three near-identical "ladder plus capped range" entries (1, 3 and 5) are strong on tradeoffs but differ mainly in which alternative they compare against, and only Entry 4 states a confidence level and assumptions clearly while also answering every question. To win, an entry would need to keep the plain structure of Entry 1, state how sure it is of the 2031 range in one clear sentence with its basis, keep the facility money in 2033 as the case describes, and explain the growth allocation rather than assert it. Entry 4 already does most of this, but it would need to be easier to follow for a judge reading it once.
