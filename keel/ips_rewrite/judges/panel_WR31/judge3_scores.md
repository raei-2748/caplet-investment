## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- I traced the money and it holds together. The $300k buys a dated Treasury ladder (about two-thirds of the $450k, which is right), any gap is filled from the 2028 deposit, and the surplus (about $150-160k) goes 80/20 stocks/intermediate Treasuries until 2031. Then the range floor is moved into bonds maturing before 2033, and in 2033 the gift is that floor plus at most half the growth, with the rest kept for flexibility. That is textbook asset-liability matching, and the assumptions (returns, inflation, no outside money) are stated. What holds it back is that it says rate swings never touch the payments while also holding coupons in short Treasuries. Those coupons are reinvested at unknown rates, so the hole exists and is not priced. The 2031 floor is also bought on a fixed date whatever the market is doing, so a crash in 2030 shrinks the floor, and the plan never weighs that. "A stated, modelled confidence level" is promised but no figure or method is given, and it does not ask what happens if the 2028 deposit comes in short.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This entry defines high certainty as a list of specific failures, which is how I would do it: a government default, a fall in yields before purchase that makes the ladder dearer while the 2028 deposit also shrinks (a correlated risk most teams miss), and the coupon reinvestment leak, which it prices in 2031 before any gift is named. The 2028 deposit becomes a Treasury floor maturing in late 2032, with the expected interest in one world fund, which is a sound and cheap way to build a protected floor. It weighs itself against two rivals and puts numbers on both, including a tail case. Its weak point is the 2031 range. The top is "floor plus half her stocks", but nothing says whether that promised half stays in stocks until 2033 or is locked, whether the gift is capped at the top, or how confident Laura can be of landing in the range. The case asks for exactly that confidence. Diversification is thin (one stock fund) but defended.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what happens to the "promised" half of the stocks between the 2031 announcement and 2033. Is it still exposed, and is the gift capped at the top of the range?

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most technically serious growth engine in the set. It runs CPPI on the surplus with a multiplier of 3, the margin-free cap, and a floor that becomes the present value of the announced minimum from 2031. It names its gap risk (a one-month fall of about a third), its cash-lock cost and the 2028-deposit risk, and it commits not to rewrite the rule after market moves. The range has an explicit confidence design: at least 95% on the minimum and even odds on the top. Assumptions, liquidity and the currency exposure are all stated. Two things hold it back for me. First, the ladder is "each sized to deliver $50,000", but coupon reinvestment is never mentioned, so the operating reserve's one real leak is unpriced. Second, the floor before 2031 ("near 60% of the sleeve, accruing at the short yield") is not tied to any liability, and the protected asset is rolling short Treasuries rather than a bond dated to 2033. That leaves reinvestment risk in the floor too. A 95% target for a promise to co-sponsors is also lower than I would want from a mechanism this tight.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the pre-2031 floor of "near 60%" was chosen, and how it hands over to the announced minimum in 2031 if the sleeve cannot then support the minimum Laura wants to name.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Every cash flow is accounted for. The $300k buys the ladder, the 2028 deposit first fills any gap, and the rest becomes a Treasury floor maturing in late 2032 plus one world fund. The range is named in 2031 and the reserve runs itself down from 2033. The certainty definition is a list of failure modes, including the coupon reinvestment leak, which is priced and set aside before the gift is named. That is the hole I expect students to miss, and this team found it. It is also the only entry to point out that a capped range is asymmetric (losses come off the gift, gains past the top go to Laura) and to fix it by moving the promised half into Treasuries on the day the range is named, then giving a confidence figure. It defends its key choice, locking the floor in 2028 rather than 2031, against the closest rival and shows the cost (about $4k in the median) and the benefit (about $155k against $115k one time in twenty). That is the tradeoff reasoning Wharton asks for. Its limits are thin equity diversification (one fund), no stated Treasury yield or inflation assumption, and a gift that rests mostly on returned principal, so the plan leans cautious for a client "willing to take thoughtful risks".
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: if the promised half moves into Treasuries when the range is named, I would expect the gift to reach the top almost surely. The "97 times in 100" figure is not explained (presumably the reinvestment set-aside).

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The operating ladder is standard and correct. Treating the facility contribution as staged real options is a genuinely original idea that fits how co-sponsors think. But when I trace the money, the gift side is hand-waved: the tranches are never sized, "above its target" is undefined, and the range top depends on later tranches that are not secured. That sits uneasily with the claim that every promise is sized to a secured tranche. Adding 25% QQQ because Laura's career is in technology and creative industries gets the risk logic backwards. Her 2028 deposit already depends on that income, so the portfolio doubles her exposure rather than hedging it, and the tilt concentrates the only growth asset. Apart from about 7% for stocks, there are no figures for funding reliability, range confidence or tail outcomes. Gate 2 also makes her contribution depend on co-sponsors committing first, which could be a chicken-and-egg problem for the 2031 pitch.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big each tranche is, what the "target" for Gate 2 is, and whether money held back for Gate 3 counts as flexibility or as a commitment.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the same structure as Entry 4, and it holds: the ladder from the first deposit, the gap filled from the second, a Treasury floor from the 2028 deposit, the reinvestment leak priced in 2031, and the promised half locked into Treasuries when the range is named, with a confidence figure. It adds what Entry 4 lacks, a funding-reliability number for the payments and the word to co-sponsors (under one in sixty against one in three for a 60/40). It also explains sequence risk clearly: ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill. But the rival it beats is a whole-portfolio 60/40, which no serious asset-liability manager would propose for fixed dated liabilities. It never defends the plan's real distinctive decision, buying the gift's floor in 2028 rather than waiting until 2031, so a sceptical reader is left asking what that choice costs Laura. As in Entry 4, Treasury-yield and inflation assumptions are not stated and the equity side is a single fund.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether "under one in sixty" counts the reinvestment leak after the 2031 set-aside, or before it.

## My overall view
Almost every entry buys the ten payments with a Treasury ladder, so the ladder separated nobody. What separated them was whether the team traced the money past the obvious. Does the ladder leak through coupon reinvestment? Is the 2031 range backed by assets, or only by hope? Is the plan's key design choice defended against its closest rival and not a strawman? Entries 4 and 6 found and priced the leak and locked the promised range. Entry 3 built the most disciplined engine but left its own floor loosely tied to the liability. Entry 1 is correct but takes the hard parts on trust, and Entry 5 is an idea without a balance sheet. To win, the best entry (4) needs to put Entry 6's payment-reliability figure next to its own rival comparison and state its yield and inflation assumptions. It should also show briefly that a mostly-Treasury gift is a deliberate choice for a client who takes thoughtful risks, not just caution.
