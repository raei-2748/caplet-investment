# Judge J4 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - buys the payments with Treasuries, names the strongest rival plan, admits what it costs, and owns the catch in its own range.
2. Entry 1 - same core plan, argued against a plain 60/40 portfolio; honest about giving up roughly $200,000 in the usual case, but leaves out the middle-way rival.
3. Entry 2 - same core plan; the rival comparison is good, but it never says what the capped range costs Laura.
4. Entry 5 - a real, thoughtful framework (CPPI) with the most honest risk list, but heavy for a student team and it reads like a textbook.
5. Entry 6 - sound, plain and client-appropriate, but conventional and thin on reasoning and trade-offs.
6. Entry 4 - the startup metaphor is more cute than convincing; the gates are vague and the 75/25 VT/QQQ growth sleeve is poorly justified.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strategy is clear: buy the ten payments with Treasuries, protect a floor for the gift, and put only the interest on that floor into stocks. It lists how "high certainty" could fail (default, a recession before the purchase, early coupons reinvested at lower yields), which is real thinking. It admits the team's first plan was three-quarters stocks and that a 60/40 mix usually ends nearly $200,000 ahead, then says why it still rejects that mix. That honesty about cost is what I most want from students. It also states the catch in the capped range, and the figures are tied to "our model". What holds it back is the voice. It is very finished and aphoristic, which makes me suspect heavy editing. It also sets up only one rival, a fairly easy one to beat.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The "capital-protected note" analogy took a second pass. I also wasn't sure why moving "the half she promised" into Treasuries on the naming day is needed.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes

It also tests a tougher rival, stocks held until 2031 and a floor bought then. It reports honestly that the rival usually gives about $4,000 more but can be badly hurt in a bad year. ### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8.5/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8.5/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most complete version of the shared plan. It tests the strongest rival, keeping stocks until 2031 and then buying a floor, and concedes it usually gives about $4,000 more. It shows the cost of the rival's forced sale in a bad year (about $115,000 against about $155,000). It also states the catch in the capped range and the modelled odds of reaching the top. The reasoning is honest, the trade-offs are named, and the numbers are tied to the model. The ordering of cheques, then floor, then hope, answers the question about protecting the payments while keeping flexibility. The voice still feels more crafted than teenage, and "Do not crush" and the section headings feel like brand lines. The reasoning is strong enough to outweigh that.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Do not crush" meant nothing to me. Maybe it is a reference to something the team knows.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The payments are locked in Treasuries sensibly, and the staged facility contribution is an original idea. But the gates are loosely defined ("above its target", "operating on plan"), and the metaphor of Laura as a startup founder does most of the work in place of analysis. The growth sleeve is 75% VT and 25% QQQ, justified by the claim that innovation companies "have led global returns" and by Laura's career in technology. That is a weak reason, and it concentrates the portfolio in a way the document never discusses. There are almost no numbers on how often the plan works or what it costs. The 2031 range depends on market and project milestones that Laura cannot control, which makes it hard to give co-sponsors a credible range. The tone is clear but sounds like a pitch deck.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How a "real option" differs from simply deciding later, and who judges whether the project is "on plan".

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7.5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6.5/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This has the most technical depth and the most candid risk section (gap risk, cash-lock, the uncertain 2028 deposit, currency). It states a confidence target and a simulation basis for the range, and it explains why the multiplier is three and not five. I respect the honesty. But CPPI with monthly rebalancing is a lot for a high-school team to run and defend, and the document reads like a lecture. The tone is closer to a quant textbook than to a team explaining its thinking, and the "Floor = Promise" slogan sits oddly beside it. It also gives little sense of what the strategy costs against a simpler one. I'm not sure the team could explain this unaided.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The "present value of the announced minimum" floor and how the cushion works in practice. I would not have followed it without outside knowledge.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5.5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, sensible, plain plan: Treasury ladder for the payments, 80/20 stocks and bonds for the rest, floor locked in 2031, half-the-remainder rule for flexibility. It follows Wharton's questions closely and everything is easy to follow. But it is the strategy I'd expect from many teams. It names no rival, admits no trade-off or earlier mistake, and gives no modelled odds, only "a stated, modelled confidence level" without a number. The headings ("Growth Engine", "Commitment Ladder") sound like a brochure. It is competent and readable but does not show the thinking behind the choices.

## My overall view
Entries 1-3 are close cousins and clearly the strongest, because they show real reasoning: they list how the plan could fail, put the team's own earlier mistake on the page, test rival plans, and say what the plan costs in the usual case. Those are the things that persuade me students did the work, though the finish is suspiciously polished. Entry 3 wins because it tests the hardest rival and states the catch in its own range, so the team looks as if it argued against itself. Entry 5 has the most depth but the least plausible authorship. Entry 6 is safe but generic, and Entry 4 leans on a metaphor in place of evidence. To win, the top entry would need to keep this level of honest argument while sounding less like a finished essay, with plainer sentences and fewer slogans, and it would need to show how it handles the 60/40 case as well as the stocks-until-2031 rival.
