# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, decision science. Method: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know (my prior being that a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes), then ran a full round-robin, two entries at a time, on Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions only, and ranked by wins.

Tournament wins (15 pairings): Entry 3: 5, Entry 1: 4, Entry 6: 3, Entry 4: 2, Entry 2: 1, Entry 5: 0.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: buys the floor in 2028 instead of forecasting it in 2031, argues that choice against the strongest realistic rival with numbers, and then fixes the asymmetry in its own capped range by de-risking the promised half on announcement day.
2. Entry 1: the same design, with the fullest defence of the central idea (two rivals, both quantified), but it leaves the promised half of the range exposed between 2031 and 2033 and names no confidence for the range.
3. Entry 6: the same design again, complete on the range mechanics and confidence, but it defends its central choice only against a 60/40 portfolio, which is a weaker opponent than the plan most competent teams will bring.
4. Entry 4: a genuinely new mechanism (a pre-committed rule whose floor becomes the number Laura promises), honest about its failure modes and the most explicit on assumptions, but the machine is path-dependent and sells after falls, and it reads as a specification rather than a case Laura could follow.
5. Entry 2: a sharper way of seeing the facility contribution (tranches unlocked by evidence, not one 2033 number), undermined by thin analysis, an unexplained seed-tranche size, no stated confidence, and a tech tilt justified by past leadership.
6. Entry 5: competent, complete and safe; it is exactly the table-stakes plan I expected most teams to reach, and it taught me nothing I did not already know.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the floor Laura promises in 2031 can be something she already owns since 2028 rather than something she buys that January, and that the difference matters precisely in the one-in-twenty markets where a promise is tested; also that the 2028 deposit and a pre-purchase rally are correlated risks because both are driven by a recession. The strategy is thought through across every horizon the case names (2027 purchase, 2028 completion and floor, 2031 range, 2033 reserve, run-off to 2042), and the trade-offs are argued twice with modelled numbers against two rivals, including the one most teams will submit; the team volunteers that its own first plan was three-quarters stocks and that its model killed it, which is the only credible sign of process in the field. What holds it back: the growth pot is small (only the expected interest on the 2028 deposit goes to stocks), and the entry relies on its modelled trade-off to persuade me that this is the right amount of thoughtful risk rather than too little; the range's top is not protected after it is named, so the entry is weaker on co-sponsor credibility than Entries 3 and 6; assumptions beyond a 7.5% stock return (volatility, inflation) are not stated; and the headings and sign-off, taken on their own, read as flourishes that a tired judge might not decode.
- Did not understand on first reading: what "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" are, exactly; whether the half of the stocks that defines the top of the range is protected in any way between 2031 and 2033 (the entry never says); and I had to read the heading in the middle twice before I saw it was a heading rather than a sentence that had lost its subject.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution need not be one 2033 number at all; it can be staged, with each tranche a right rather than an obligation and each unlocked by evidence about markets and the project. That is a real reframing of the client's problem and the only entry that treats the co-sponsor conversation the way co-sponsors themselves think. The founder analogy is also well matched to who Laura is, and the ladder-for-runway base is sound. What holds it back is nearly everything the Portfolio Analysis criterion asks for: there is no sizing of the seed tranche, no stated confidence in the range, no description of how the team evaluated funding reliability or flexibility under different market outcomes beyond "about 7% a year with deep drawdowns possible"; the 25% tilt to a tech index is justified by past leadership and by Laura's career, which is a reason to diversify away from technology, not toward it; and the third gate in 2034-35 sits outside the case's frame of a 2033 contribution. The floor is still bought in 2031, so the staging solves the 2033 problem but not the 2031 one. It would need twice the analysis to be a semifinal entry; the idea deserves it.
- Did not understand on first reading: how large the seed tranche is or how it is chosen; what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio at Gate 2; how confident Laura should be in the range she announces.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things. First, the same insight as Entries 1 and 6, that the floor should be owned from 2028, here argued against the strongest realistic rival (keep the ladder, stay in stocks until 2031, then buy a floor) with a quantified cost (about $4,000 of median gift) and a quantified benefit (a one-in-twenty gift of about $155,000 instead of about $115,000). That is exactly the kind of trade-off Wharton says it wants to see and the kind I give top marks for. Second, that a capped range has an asymmetric payoff for Laura (falls come off the gift, rises past the top go to her), and the clean fix is to move the promised half into Treasuries on the day she names it, which the entry says gets the gift to its top about 97 times in 100. The definition of high certainty as a list of specific failure modes, including the correlation between a recession, a dearer ladder and a thinner 2028 deposit, is the most thoughtful treatment of that question in the field. The entry covers every IPS question without a checklist: purchase, completion, floor, range, reserve run-off, priority order if anything changes, flexibility kept in the unpromised half. What holds it back: it says less than Entry 1 about why it rejected a conventional growth portfolio, so a judge who believes Laura should take more risk gets one argument instead of two; the stock allocation is small and the entry asks me to accept its model on that; volatility and inflation assumptions are not stated; and the same stylistic risks as Entry 1 apply.
- Did not understand on first reading: how a floor bought in 2028 with coupon-paying Treasuries "grows back into all of it" when the entry itself flags coupon reinvestment as a leak; and what "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" are. I also note, since I am told to judge blind, that Entries 1, 3 and 6 share a pitch and most of their text; I scored each on what it contains.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes, narrowly
- What I learned: that a pre-committed portfolio-insurance rule can be pointed at the co-sponsor problem, so that the minimum Laura announces in 2031 is by construction the number the portfolio mechanically defends. "Floor = Promise" is a sharp framing, and the team is unusually honest about the rule's known pathologies: gap risk inside a month, cash-lock after a fall, and why it chose a multiplier of three rather than five. It is the most explicit entry on assumptions (return, volatility, Treasury yield, timing, nominal payments, inflation in facility costs), it names the currency risk and the variability of the 2028 deposit, and its range (protected minimum at 95% confidence, top at even odds) is the clearest confidence statement in the field. What holds it back: the mechanism is path-dependent and sells stocks after they fall, every month, which is the behaviour the ladder was meant to avoid; it starts fully invested with a floor at only 60% of the sleeve, so the protection in the early years is modest and the cash-lock case, which the team accepts, is exactly the bad-market path co-sponsors would ask about; the 60% starting floor and the link between the 2031 announced minimum and the rule's floor at that date are asserted rather than reasoned; and the whole thing is written as a specification in bolded blocks, well organised but not a narrative that earns a client's confidence in the way Wharton describes. Nothing shows how the team worked or what it learned.
- Did not understand on first reading: why the floor begins near 60% of the sleeve; whether the announced minimum is chosen from the floor in 2031 or the floor is reset to a minimum chosen some other way; how coupons from the ladder are handled.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I did not already hold as my prior. Ladder for the payments, hold to maturity, complete it from the 2028 deposit if short; an 80/20 growth sleeve rebalanced on drift; move the bottom of the range into Treasuries in 2031; contribute the floor plus at most half the rest; keep the remainder as flexibility; test good, average and severe markets. Every IPS question is answered, the structure is clean, the assumptions are stated in ranges, and the point that stocks are the better defence against rising Taiwan construction costs is a sensible qualitative touch. What holds it back is that no decision in it is argued against an alternative: why 80/20 and not 60/40 or 100/0, why the floor is bought in 2031 rather than earlier or later, why half, what confidence the top of the range carries (deferred to "a stated, modelled confidence level"). It buys its floor in the one January the market may not cooperate, and does not notice. In a field of 2,300 this is the median competent entry; many teams will submit something very like it, and the judges will not be able to tell them apart.
- Did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same owned-floor design and the same capped-range asymmetry and fix as Entry 3, with the 97-in-100 confidence, plus the point from Entry 1 that Laura's liability is ten one-year bills rather than one ten-year bill, so a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom; the modelled failure rates (about one in three for a 60/40 portfolio against under one in sixty) make that concrete. The entry is complete on every IPS question, places the 2028 deposit's own risk inside its definition of certainty, and ends with a clear priority order. What holds it back: its central choice, to buy the floor in 2028 rather than stay in stocks until 2031, is defended only by the contrast with a 60/40 portfolio that nobody serious would run against fixed dated payments; the plan most teams will actually submit (ladder plus stocks, floor bought in 2031) goes unmentioned, so the one trade-off that would separate this entry from Entries 4 and 5 is left for the judge to infer. Same gaps on volatility and inflation assumptions, same stylistic risks, as its siblings.
- Did not understand on first reading: as Entry 3 (the floor's growth with coupon bonds; the maturing Treasury funds); and whether the $200,000 the 60/40 ends ahead by is total wealth in 2042 or the gift, which I resolved as the former on a second pass.

## My overall view
What separated the top from the rest was not the ladder, which five of six entries have and which I treat as the entry fee, but whether the team saw that the 2031 promise is itself a risk to be engineered. Entries 1, 3 and 6 saw it most clearly: they own the floor years before Laura speaks, they define certainty as a list of ways to fail rather than an adjective, and they put a number on what the safety costs. Entry 4 saw it too and built a machine for it, an honest and well-specified machine, but one that trades stocks against the market every month and whose worst case is the one Laura would be asked about. Entry 2 saw a different and valuable thing, that the contribution can be staged, and then did not do the work. Entry 5 did the work everyone does. For the best entry, Entry 3, to win outright rather than merely reach the semifinal, it would need to state its volatility and inflation assumptions in a sentence, say plainly why so little of Laura's money is in stocks in a way a client who takes thoughtful risks would accept (the rejected 60/40 argument from Entry 1 belongs in it), and be confident enough in its idea to let the headings and the sign-off do less; the idea is strong enough that it does not need them.
