# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4 - ladder for the payments, then a rule that starts fully in stocks and only de-risks as the cushion shrinks; the clearest growth plan with the downside named.
2. Entry 1 - ladder plus an 80/20 growth engine on everything above it; sound and growth-aware, but thin on numbers.
3. Entry 2 - rigorous and honest about the upside it gives away, but only the interest on the 2028 deposit goes into stocks.
4. Entry 6 - same plan as Entry 2, with the 2031 range explained more fully and the stronger rival left out.
5. Entry 5 - same plan again, with the weaker rival comparison and no admission of how much growth is surrendered.
6. Entry 3 - the staged-release idea is original, but the 75/25 VT/QQQ portfolio is under-diversified, the gates are vague, and there is no dollar range or confidence level.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean two-job structure. Treasuries match the ten payments, and the roughly $150k above the ladder goes into 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries. That fits a client who takes thoughtful risks and has a five-year horizon on the surplus. It also answers the questions: how the portfolio changes in 2031 and 2033, why diversification, what "high certainty" means, and what the facility contribution rule is (the floor plus at most half of the growth portfolio). It is honest that a crash only hits the facility contribution. What holds it back is the missing quantification. There is no stated confidence for the range, no sense of what the median contribution might be, and no cost to Laura of the safety it buys. The 2031 floor move is a forecast made at the time, and that risk is not weighed. The plan is competent and conventional, and it gives the judge little to remember.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. I was unsure whether "the bottom of her announced range" is sized before or after the stocks have run for four years, but the logic is recoverable.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the most analytically serious of the three variants. It lists the ways "high certainty" can fail, including the recession-lowers-yields-and-hits-the-2028-deposit link that ties to Laura's income, and it prices the coupon-reinvestment leak. It also names the 60/40 alternative and admits it "usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead". I credit that honesty. My objection is the price. Only the interest on a floor bond and the leftover (perhaps $40-50k out of $450k) ends up in stocks. A client who "takes thoughtful risks" with 6 to 16 years to run is put in what is effectively a capital-protected note. The "one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque" argument only works if the 60/40 is built on the payments themselves, which the text leaves unclear. The plan also never says how much the median facility contribution gives up against a more growth-oriented design. The defence, that the plan is sized to a loss of $115k versus $155k at the five-percent tail, is a good one but arrives at a very modest upside.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Sell low, by appointment" and "Do not crush." read like headings or stray notes. I could not tell what "or nearly" means for the ladder cost. I also had to work out that the 60/40 rival is separate from the ladder.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The idea of releasing the facility contribution in stages, as a founder would fund milestones, is original and it keeps the payment ladder safe. The growth side is the weak part. A 75% VT / 25% QQQ mix is a technology bet presented as diversification, and the justification ("innovation-driven companies have led global returns") is a backward-looking assertion. The gates are not defined. The 2033 tranche depends on co-sponsors having "committed their share", which is outside Laura's control. Gate 3 sits in 2034-35, which is not the time frame the case asks the team to plan. The case wants a dollar range and a confidence level in 2031, and I could not find either. Risk and assumptions get one line, with no tests of good and bad markets. The portfolio analysis is the thinnest of the six. It reads well as a story, but a judge cannot see what Laura would actually say to sponsors.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "seed tranche" is sized, and whether the later tranches come from the same portfolio or from the project's own success.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one plan I would want as a client who takes thoughtful risks. The ladder secures the ten payments. The 2028 sleeve starts fully in stocks, since the multiplier of three on a cushion of 40% is capped at 100%, and de-risks only as the cushion shrinks. Meaningful upside is kept while the floor is protected. It answers the questions directly: a stated confidence for the minimum (95%), "roughly even odds" for the top, the assumed return and volatility, and the risks it accepts (gap risk, cash-lock, the 2028 deposit's variability, currency). It states that margin is banned and that the rule will not be rewritten. It does not hide the cost of cash-lock. Weaknesses: monthly CPPI is mechanical detail where Wharton asks for a framework, and the cash-lock case locks in losses and misses recoveries, which an equity manager dislikes. The 2031 range still caps the upside at "half the gain above the minimum", which is more timid than it needs to be.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can "start near 60%" and then be reset in 2031 as the present value of the announced minimum, and whether those are the same number. The phrase "Floor = Promise" is a label for the range.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It has the same structure as Entry 2, with a different rival. Here the rival is the sensible one: keep the ladder, hold everything else in stocks to 2031, then buy a floor. The text concedes it "usually gives about $4,000 more", and it is beaten only because it forces a sale on a fixed date, with a $115k versus $155k tail. That is a fairer fight than the 60/40, but it also shows the plan's upside surrender is tiny, and the text frames $4,000 as a rounding error rather than the price of safety. The strong addition is the capped-range catch and the figure of about 97 times in 100 that the gift reaches its top. That is the kind of stated confidence the case asks for. A growth-oriented judge still sees a plan that puts most of the 2028 money into bonds.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the same "Do not crush." and "or nearly" as Entry 2. Also what happens to the "half she promised" after the day the range is named, and how the half moved to Treasuries works alongside the floor.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is Entry 2 with Entry 5's capped-range paragraph. It carries the 60/40 comparison, which is the clearest statement in the set of what the safety costs ("almost $200,000" ahead), and the 97-in-100 figure for reaching the top of the range. It is the most complete of the three variants in answering the case's 2031 requirement. It does not include the "strongest rival" test, so the choice of a bond-heavy floor rests on a comparison with a plan nobody would build. My complaint is the same as for Entry 2: a very small stock sleeve and a conclusion that says "usually" in the rival's favour and rests on the tails.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the same items as Entry 2, plus whether the "60/40" holds the ladder and a separate 60/40 sleeve or replaces the ladder.

## My overall view
Four of the six entries (2, 5, 6 and, to a degree, 1) fund the payments with a Treasury ladder, which is sound. The difference is what is done with the rest. Entry 4 is the only plan that treats the money above the ladder as a growth asset to be managed with a stated rule, and it states its confidence levels and the price of its protection. Entries 2, 5 and 6 are the best analysed, and they are honest about the upside they surrender, but they surrender it nearly entirely, and they defend that with tail risks, not with the client's risk appetite. To win, Entry 4 would need to shorten the mechanics (the judges asked for a framework), explain the cash-lock case in plain terms, and show what the median facility contribution looks like against the safer designs. Entry 1 would need numbers. Entry 3 would need a real portfolio and a real range.
