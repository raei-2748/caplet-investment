# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 - only plan that keeps real stock exposure on the growth money, states the price of its own safety (cash-lock), and quantifies confidence.
2. Entry 4 - clean ladder plus an honestly equity-heavy growth sleeve; sound, a little generic, thin on numbers.
3. Entry 2 - well-reasoned ladder with a stated return assumption, but under a tenth of the money is in shares.
4. Entry 5 - same plan as Entry 2, clear on the reinvestment gap, equally timid on growth.
5. Entry 3 - same plan again; no return assumption, less explicit about the cost of safety.
6. Entry 1 - same plan with the least quantification; leaves the upside given away largely uncounted.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Funds the ten payments fully with a Treasury ladder and uses the 2028 deposit mainly for a Treasury floor, with only the "interest" (under a tenth of her money) in world shares. It is coherent, and it spots the reinvestment weakness and says what is done about it. But for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks with 6 to 16 years to run, almost everything sits in bonds, and the plan never counts what that gives up. There is no return assumption and no sense of the range of outcomes, so the facility contribution is hard to judge. It reads as prudence rather than a growth strategy.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the "floor" and the share slice actually are in dollars; why the ladder needs "a margin" and how large it is.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same architecture as Entry 1 but better argued for the judge: it defines high certainty as "no payment depends on shares", ties the thin 2028 deposit risk to the building gift only, says shares are under a tenth of $450,000 and gives a 7.5% assumption. Still, a fund manager would say the client is badly short-changed: roughly 90% in Treasuries for a risk-tolerant client, with no estimate of the gift forgone. ### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7.5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6.5/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Funds the payments first, then puts everything above the ladder (roughly a third of her money) into an 80% stock, 20% intermediate Treasury mix, and says plainly that Laura can afford the volatility. It names the 6-7% and 4-5% assumptions, the inflation rate and a stress test across good, average and severe markets, and sets a clear glide path: lock the announced floor in 2031, cap the gift at half the remaining growth money. It is conventional and a bit generic, and it does not size the contribution range in dollars, but it actually takes the growth risk the client can bear and explains the reasoning. Its high-certainty definition is clear.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how large the growth portfolio really is after the ladder; the pitch's "chasing her vision" wording.

### Entry 5

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6.5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Ties the 2031 minimum to what the portfolio is built to protect, then runs a pre-committed CPPI rule on the growth sleeve that starts near fully invested in a world stock ETF, so Laura keeps real upside. It states its assumptions (7% return, 16% volatility, 4.5% Treasuries), uses simulation with explicit confidence (95% on the floor, about even odds on the top), and lists the risks it accepts, including gap risk and getting stuck in Treasuries after a bad fall, which is exactly the cost of safety most students skip. Weaknesses: the monthly rule is complex to explain and might be questioned as hard to run and as at odds with a no-rewriting principle, the pitch is hard to follow, and the sleeve is smaller than it looks. Overall it is the most analytical and the most honest about the trade.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can start at 60% of the sleeve yet equal the announced minimum from 2031; what happens in the transition; the pitch wording "stock-or-Treasury rule".

## My overall view
Four of these six are the same plan: buy the ten payments, put the rest in a Treasury floor and hold a sliver of shares. It is safe and tidy, but it hands a client with a long horizon and a risk-taking record a portfolio that is about nine-tenths bonds, and none of them says how many dollars of facility gift that costs. Entry 5 comes closest to admitting it, and Entry 2 at least states the stock share and a return assumption. The two that separate themselves are Entry 4, which simply lets the money above the ladder run in stocks and says so, and Entry 6, which also takes equity risk but wraps it in a rule and puts numbers and confidence levels on the outcomes. The best entry would combine Entry 6's quantified risk and disclosed trade-offs with Entry 4's plain explanation, and add a dollar range for the 2031 gift so a judge can see what the growth is worth against the bond floor.
