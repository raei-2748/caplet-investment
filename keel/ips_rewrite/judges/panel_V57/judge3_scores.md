### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7.5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- I traced the cash flows. The $300,000 is dedicated to the ten payments. The $150,000 first fills any rung the first deposit could not buy, then buys a Treasury floor maturing before 2033, and roughly that floor's interest goes into world shares. In 2031 half the shares are locked into Treasuries before the range is named, and in 2033 everything rolls into the reserve. I found no point where a payment fails. The team plainly knows the difference between immunising (matching duration and rebalancing) and dedicating (one bond per payment, held), and it says why it chose the second. That is the right instinct for fixed nominal liabilities. Most importantly, it names the reinvestment exposure and prices it as if nothing more were reinvested, which is a hard bound rather than a guess, and it does this before the range is spoken. So the range absorbs the hole instead of the payments. What holds it back is Portfolio Analysis: it gives no return assumption and no confidence level for the range, and it never says how big the kept shares are next to a possible shortfall. With so little in equities, the facility gift is close to the floor, and the entry does not admit how conservative that leaves a client who is "willing to take thoughtful risks".
- Confusions: once half the shares move into Treasuries in 2031, the top of the range looks almost certain, so I was not sure what uncertainty the range is meant to express or how confident the team is in it. "Treasury funds with fixed end dates" are treated like bonds that pay "exactly what it promised", but a target-maturity fund's final payout is not fixed. Only the floor is "rounded down" for this.

### Entry 2

- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It is well organised and answers every IPS question: a ladder for the promise, an 80/20 growth sleeve, a 2031 floor lock, a cap on the facility gift and a flexibility fund. It also defines "high certainty" and lists assumptions, which many entries will not. My problem is the hole. The ladder is "sized so principal plus coupons covers every payment", with early coupons held in short-term Treasuries. That is a bet on future short rates, so "rate swings never touch the payments" is false as written, and the team does not seem to see the exposure. The 2031 step is also hand-waved. - Confusions: how coupons held in short Treasuries fit with the claim that rates never touch the payments. Whether the yearly rebalance is a calendar rule or a threshold rule.

## My overall view
Every entry here protects the payments with a Treasury ladder, so the difference is whether each team saw the ladder's one weakness. A coupon ladder that costs about $300,000 for $500,000 of payments only works if its early interest is reinvested at rates nobody knows. Entries 2 and 3 never price that, and both claim a certainty the ladder does not quite have. Entry 3 put real analytical care into the facility minimum while leaving the payments with an unexamined assumption, which is backwards for this client. The four ladder-plus-floor entries all find the hole and cover it before the range is named. Entries 1 and 6 cover it at a worst-case bound, while 4 and 5 use an unsized margin. Entry 6 edges Entry 1 because it also states its definitions and assumptions. To win, Entry 6 needs three things. It should state its confidence that the 2033 gift falls in the range and what makes the range wide or narrow. It should show that the kept shares are big enough to cover the priced shortfall in a bad-rates path. And it should defend, with numbers, why so small an equity stake is the right balance of growth and protection for Laura.
