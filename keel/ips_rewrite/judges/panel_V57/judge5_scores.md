# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Marked against Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions only. Method: for each entry I wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a full pairwise tournament (15 pairs) and derived the ranking from the wins.

Prior, stated up front: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes. I expect most of the top few hundred teams to arrive there. What I pay for is a genuinely new idea, a sharper way of seeing Laura's problem, or a trade-off that is actually argued rather than asserted.

A note before the scores: Entries 1, 4, 5 and 6 are the same strategy, with the same name and most of the same paragraphs. I was told they come from different teams; I judged each on what is on its own page, but I say openly that they separated from each other by inches, and that the gaps between them should not be over-read.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: the sharpest view of the client's problem in the set (her income already behaves like a share; a fixed cheque cannot wait for a recovery), a design that needs no forecast, a priced answer to the ladder's one real weakness, and the most argued trade-offs per page.
2. Entry 5: the same entry as Entry 1 in almost every sentence; its method for pricing the reinvestment shortfall is looser ("add a margin") than Entry 1's worst-case rule, which is the only thing that put it second.
3. Entry 4: same strategy, states a return assumption and the size of the stock sleeve and names the alternative it rejected, but drops the pension-fund framing, the time-diversification point and the portfolio-insurance argument, so I learned a little less.
4. Entry 6: same strategy again, with the fewest supporting arguments of the four; still beats the two other designs on client fit and robustness.
5. Entry 2: a real idea (the announced minimum is what the portfolio is mechanically built to defend) and the most complete coverage of Wharton's questions, but the engine it chooses is path-dependent and pro-cyclical, and the entry does not see that its own ladder has the reinvestment problem the others address.
6. Entry 3: competent, complete and well organised, and exactly my prior; I learned nothing new about Laura or about how to fund fixed payments.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that Laura's 2028 deposit and a stock portfolio are exposed to the same downturn, so putting her portfolio in shares is a second bet on the same risk; that the ladder's cheapness comes from reinvested coupons, which is a risk the team prices under a worst-case assumption (no further reinvestment) before she names anything to co-sponsors; that the team chose cash-flow matching over duration matching and says why; and that it explicitly rejected both the bank's option-based structure and portfolio insurance, with a reason for each. That is four or five things I did not expect to learn from a high-school IPS, and the entry argues them rather than listing them. The design is coherent across Wharton's questions: how the allocation changes (only on fixed dates), how the 2031 range is made credible (the promised top is already in bonds before it is said), how flexibility is kept (the other half of the shares, liquid). What holds it back: the growth side is small, under a tenth of her money, and the entry does not quantify what that implies for the gift or state a return assumption, so Portfolio Analysis is argued qualitatively rather than shown. It is also so cautious that a judge could fairly ask whether Laura, who takes thoughtful risks, has been given enough upside.
- Did not understand on first reading: what "Treasury funds with fixed end dates" are (I inferred target-maturity bond funds); roughly how large the building floor is in dollars after the ladder is completed; and whether the ladder itself is sized to the full $50,000 per rung or relies on the reinvested interest for part of it.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: the idea of making the 2031 announcement the design variable, so the portfolio is built to defend the number Laura says rather than the other way round; and a worked trade-off on the rule's multiplier (three rather than five, because a one-month fall of a third is survivable). The entry is the most complete in the set against Wharton's checklist: assumptions, confidence levels, liquidity, currency, the deposit's variability and the reserve's path are all there, and it is honest about its own failure modes (gap risk, cash-lock). That honesty is worth real credit under Portfolio Analysis. What holds it back: the chosen engine is a pro-cyclical rule that sells stocks after they fall and can lock the whole sleeve into Treasuries for up to five years, so the "growth sleeve" can end with no growth at all exactly when Laura's own income is also weak; the floor's starting level (near 60% of the sleeve) is a parameter with no reason given; and the entry never notices that its ladder, like everyone's, depends on reinvested coupons. The client appears mostly in a closing paragraph; the strategy would read much the same for a different client with fixed liabilities. It reads as a specification, which is organised but not a narrative, and a human judge reading once will stumble on the mechanics.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the floor moves from "accrues at the short Treasury yield" to "present value of the announced minimum" in 2031 if those two numbers differ; what happens to the ladder's coupons between receipt and payment; and why 60% to start.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- A complete, orderly, well-written IPS that answers every one of Wharton's questions in turn and makes no mistakes I could find. The two-jobs framing is clear, the ladder is correctly described, the 2031 step of moving the bottom of the range into Treasuries is sound, the facility contribution rule (floor plus at most half the rest, capped at the announced top) preserves flexibility, and assumptions are stated. What holds it back is that all of this is my prior: a Treasury ladder plus an 80/20 growth portfolio with annual rebalancing is where a competent team lands by default, and the entry does not show me anything about Laura specifically that changes the design. The claim that stocks defend against Taiwanese construction costs is asserted, not argued. The bottom of the 2031 range, the one number that protects her credibility, is never derived; the entry says it will be locked but not how it is chosen. Reinvestment of coupons is mentioned but the risk in it is not. Among 2,300, this is a top-few-hundred entry, not a top-50 one.
- Did not understand on first reading: how the bottom of the announced range is set before it is locked; and what "a stated, modelled confidence level" for the top will actually be.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same strategy as Entries 1, 5 and 6. What this version adds: a stated return assumption, the size of the stock sleeve as a share of her money, the asymmetry that a smaller 2028 deposit shrinks the gift but cannot touch a cheque already bought, and an explicit statement of the alternative the team rejected (floor into stocks for a bigger gift) and why. The reinvestment shortfall is priced at the day's yields plus a cushion, which is reasonable but less auditable than a stated worst case.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is Entry 1 with a few words changed. Everything I said about Entry 1 applies: the human-capital argument, the choice of cash-flow matching over duration matching with a reason, the rejection of the option-based note and of portfolio insurance, the promised top moved into bonds before it is spoken, the flexibility kept liquid. The differences I could find are two: the ladder is described as needing no forecast or trading rather than no rebalancing (a slightly better claim, since it says what the plan does not depend on), and the reinvestment shortfall is priced at the day's rates "plus a margin" rather than as if nothing more were reinvested. The second is why it sits a hair below Entry 1: a decision rule that can be checked ("assume no further reinvestment") beats one with an unsized margin. The same reservations hold: growth is thin and unquantified, and no return assumption is given.
- Did not understand on first reading: the size of the margin; and, as with Entry 1, what the fixed-end-date Treasury funds are.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes, narrowly
- Same strategy again, with the shortest list of supporting arguments. It keeps the core that makes this design better than Entries 2 and 3: the human-capital reasoning, the floor-plus-interest construction with the shares owned rather than an option, the promised top locked into bonds before it is said, and the reinvestment gap priced under a worst-case rule. It adds a return assumption and the sleeve's size. It drops the pension-fund comparison, the time-diversification point and the portfolio-insurance rejection, and it does not name the alternative it turned down, so there are fewer places where I can see the team weighing one thing against another. It is still a sharper reading of Laura than Entries 2 or 3 and a more robust design than Entry 2's, which is why it beats both pairwise.
- Did not understand on first reading: none, beyond the shared question about what the fixed-end-date Treasury funds are. What puzzled me was that four of six entries read as near-copies of one another.

## My overall view
Three designs sat in front of me, not six. The weakest was the default: ladder the payments, put the rest in a stock-heavy mix, lock a floor in 2031. It is correct and I would sign it for Laura, but it tells me nothing a careful team would not reach in its first week. The second was a precommitted rule that defends an announced minimum by selling stocks as they fall; it is the most thorough entry against Wharton's checklist and the most honest about its own failure modes, but it chooses a path-dependent, pro-cyclical engine and does not see that its ladder has a reinvestment problem. The best design, in four near-identical versions, did three things the others did not: it read Laura's own income as an equity-like asset and refused to double that bet; it decomposed a capital-protected note and kept the shares instead of the option; and it found the ladder's one weakness, reinvestment, and priced it before she speaks to anyone. Those are genuine ideas and argued trade-offs, which is what I pay for. What the best entry would need to win, rather than to place: it should put a number on what its caution costs Laura (how big the gift is likely to be under its design versus a fuller stock sleeve) and say why that price is worth paying for a client who takes thoughtful risks; and it should state its return and rate assumptions plainly so a judge can see the projection behind the words, as Wharton's Portfolio Analysis criterion asks.
