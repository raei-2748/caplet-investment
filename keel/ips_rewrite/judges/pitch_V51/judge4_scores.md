## Per pitch

- The ladder of Treasuries for the ten payments and the 2028 floor for the gift are both there, so I can see the plan. The capsule opening takes up the first line without saying why it matters, and "only works whole" was a line I did not follow on first read. Nothing on the 2031 range or on flexibility.

### Pitch 2
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 9/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- This is the only pitch that starts from what Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 and makes the portfolio defend that number, which is the client's real problem. It also keeps money above the minimum with her, which speaks to flexibility. The "stock-or-Treasury rule" is never explained, so I could not say how the 2028 money is actually run.

### Pitch 3
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- The central idea is clear and easy to repeat: Treasuries pay the cheques, stocks only move the upside. "Never the floor" is a neat close, but it is a slogan and reads as written for effect. It names no 2031 range and no flexibility point.

### Pitch 4
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- The plan is there: a bond for each payment, a stock surplus, and a gift sized from the surplus only, which protects the payments. "Ten Lanterns" plus "lit by" reads as a designed image rather than a plan. I could not tell which deposit buys the ladder, since the text says "her two deposits" and then gives the surplus to stocks.

### Pitch 5
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- "Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise" is the most engaging line of the eight, and it makes the idea of matching the payments to the promise easy to follow. "Hopes for a gift" respects that the gift is uncertain. The 2028 Treasuries repaying in 2032 is clear. There is no word on the 2031 range or on what the stocks do beyond "for the gift", and "Caplet XR" is not explained.

### Pitch 6
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- Easiest to follow: buy the promise first with a Treasury ladder held to maturity, and invest only what is left. "A guaranteed minimum" gives co-sponsors the thing they need from the 2031 range. Two gaps: the 2028 deposit is never mentioned, so the changing horizons are not shown, and "guaranteed" is stronger than a judge would accept for Treasuries held to maturity. "An honest upside" is a little polished.

### Pitch 7
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- The ten Treasury holdings and the 2028 floor are clear, and it uses the full 50 words. "Her building gift" is loose wording for the residency's facility, and "the interest it should earn" hedges the core idea. No 2031 range.

### Pitch 8
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- Same first 33 words as pitch 3, so the structure is the same. The closing sentence adds a real risk: a long fall in rates could shrink the floor. That is the uncertainty a judge wants to see named, which is why I score substance higher. It still says nothing about the 2031 range or flexibility.

## Which one sounds most machine-written, and why
Pitch 4 sounds most machine-written to me. It opens with a title-like name and a colon ("Ten Lanterns:"), builds one extended image ("lit by a Treasury bond... bought with her two deposits") that it never explains, and closes on a tidy aphorism ("so no promise outruns the money"). The sentences are smooth, evenly weighted and built for effect rather than for telling a judge what the team will do, which is how a template sounds. Pitches 3 and 8 come close, since they share their opening and end on a parallel slogan, but pitch 4 has the most decorative construction for the least explained plan.
