# Pitch judge 2

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Pitch 8 - plain "buy the promise first" idea, in a team's voice, with a clear link to co-sponsors
2. Pitch 4 - the best client reading (the 2031 promise becomes the floor), a little abstract on the mechanism
3. Pitch 7 - clean, concrete and honest about what each deposit buys; a touch flat
4. Pitch 2 - specific and human, but the opening line ("doesn't invest the promise") is oblique
5. Pitch 6 - same skeleton as 5 but the closing line is a real caveat, not a slogan
6. Pitch 1 - sound strategy hidden behind a brochure device ("Ten Lanterns ... lit by")
7. Pitch 3 - the capsule analogy takes the opening and "only works whole" means nothing to me
8. Pitch 5 - slogan copy that also overclaims ("Never the floor")

## Per pitch

### Pitch 1
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- The strategy is sound and standard: match each payment with a maturing Treasury, grow the surplus in a stock fund, size the gift from the surplus. "Ten Lanterns" and "lit by" are brochure language, and a retired PM reads that as marketing. "So no promise outruns the money" is a decent line, but the pitch never says what the gift range rests on.

### Pitch 2
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- It states exactly what each deposit buys, and the 2028 Treasuries repaying in 2032 shows a real plan. "Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise" is a clumsy way to open, and I had to reread it. "Cheques" and "hopes for a gift" read as a person talking. The sentences run together a bit.

### Pitch 3
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 4/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- The first sentence is spent on a pharma metaphor that tells me nothing about the strategy. "Delivers its dose on schedule and only works whole" is cute, and I didn't follow what "works whole" means for a portfolio. The mechanics after that are fine: the ladder, then a floor for the building gift whose expected interest buys stocks. The "expected interest buys stocks" idea is clever but compressed to the point of cryptic.

### Pitch 4
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 9/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- It reads the case closely: the 2031 announcement is the danger to Laura's credibility, so the announced minimum is the thing to defend. "Near-certainty" and "anything earned above it stays hers, preserving flexibility" speak to the case's wording. "A stock-or-Treasury rule" is vague, and I would want to know what triggers it. It also leans on case phrases ("preserving flexibility"), which sounds a little like echoing the rubric.

### Pitch 5
- Substance 5/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 4/10, Sounds like a real person 3/10
- "Releases Laura's money on schedule" is product-brochure phrasing, and "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor." is a slogan. The floor does move, because the Treasury floor is priced off interest rates, so the absolute claim is an overclaim that I would hold against the team. The strategy underneath is the same as in the better pitches.

### Pitch 6
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- It matches 5 until the last line, "Only a long fall in interest rates could shrink the floor." That line is the honest caveat a PM wants to see, and it is better than 5's slogan. But "stocks move the ceiling" is still brochure rhythm, and the opening is the same polished product copy.

### Pitch 7
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- Plain and complete: the promise stated with dates, the ladder bought outright, the 2028 deposit as a floor for the building gift, with the interest it should earn going into stocks. "Treasury holdings" is a slightly odd phrase, and the language is neutral and a bit flat. Nothing here is false or inflated, though, and I would read the IPS to see how the floor is sized.

### Pitch 8
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- "The promise first, then only the leftover money for growth" is the central idea in one breath, and the "we" gives it a team's voice. "Held to the end" is a clear discipline, and the co-sponsor line ties it to the case. Weak points: "guaranteed minimum" overstates what Treasuries bought at market prices can promise, and it doesn't say what the 2028 deposit does.

## Which one sounds most machine-written, and why
Pitch 5. "Releases Laura's money on schedule", the fragment pair "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor.", and the tidy ceiling/floor antithesis are slogan constructions of the kind a language model reaches for. Pitch 6 shares most of that text, and Pitch 3's capsule metaphor and Pitch 1's lantern conceit are close behind. In all of those, the device comes before the strategy.
