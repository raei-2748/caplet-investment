# Pitch judge 3

Judge: Tom (English teacher, debating coach). Read: Wharton's criteria, the client case, the eight pitches. Nothing else.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Pitch 5 - plain, human, one clear idea ("buy the promise first"), and it speaks to the co-sponsors.
2. Pitch 6 - the clearest mechanics of the eight; "buys those cheques outright" tells me the whole plan in four words.
3. Pitch 2 - a real image (Ten Lanterns) and a real last line, slightly spoilt by "lit by".
4. Pitch 7 - the best single sentence of the set ("doesn't invest the promise") sits next to the most confusing one ("hopes for a gift").
5. Pitch 3 - fits the client best on the 2031 promise, but hides its method behind an unexplained "rule" and ends in rubric words.
6. Pitch 1 - the right plan in clipped slogan fragments; "Never the floor." is a tagline, not a sentence.
7. Pitch 4 - Pitch 1 with the punch removed and a rates caveat I could not follow on first read.
8. Pitch 8 - spends a third of its words explaining a brand name before it reaches Laura.

## Per pitch

### Pitch 1
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- The plan is there: Treasuries for the ten payments, a floor plus stocks for the gift. But the opening "Caplet XR releases Laura's money on schedule" reads like the first line of a product leaflet, and "buys world stocks with the expected interest" needed a second read before I saw it meant the interest the floor should earn. "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor." is a good thought delivered as an advertising fragment. Nobody talks like that across a table.

### Pitch 2
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- "Ten Lanterns" is a genuine image, and the pitch earns it: ten payments, ten bonds, each maturing when the money is due. "Lit by a Treasury bond" made me pause, since a bond does not light anything, but I knew what was meant. The last clause, "so no promise outruns the money", is the kind of line a sharp student writes and a machine does not. It says nothing about the 2031 range or flexibility, which keeps it short but leaves the client side thin.

### Pitch 3
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- This is the only pitch that starts from Laura's 2031 promise to co-sponsors, which is exactly where the case says her credibility is at stake, so the client fit is strong. But "runs a stock-or-Treasury rule that protects her announced minimum" tells me there is a rule without telling me what it is, which is the one thing a pitch should not do. "Near-certainty" and "preserving flexibility" are the marking scheme's words handed back to the marker. It reads like a summary written to a checklist.

### Pitch 4
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- Same text as Pitch 1 until the last sentence, so the same leaflet opening and the same dense middle. The new ending, "Only a long fall in interest rates could shrink the floor", is honest, and I like a pitch that admits a risk, but on first read I could not see how falling rates shrink a floor made of Treasuries; I had to work out it must be about reinvesting the interest. A caveat you have to decode is a caveat that costs you the reader. Pitch 1's ending at least landed.

### Pitch 5
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 9/10, Makes me read on 9/10, Sounds like a real person 9/10
- This one sounds like a student explaining the plan to a parent: "Laura's ten $50,000 payments are a promise, so we buy the promise first." Every sentence makes sense on first read, the ladder is described in plain words ("each maturing just before a payment, held to the end"), and it is the only pitch that names what co-sponsors get: "a guaranteed minimum and an honest upside". My one question is where the guaranteed minimum comes from if everything left over goes into stocks; the IPS will have to answer that, which is a reason to read it.

### Pitch 6
- Substance 9/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- The clearest account of the mechanics in the set. "Buys those cheques outright in 2027" is concrete and visual, and the last sentence finally made the floor-plus-interest idea plain to me: the 2028 deposit buys a Treasury floor, and the interest it should earn buys the stocks. Nothing here needed a second read. It does not mention co-sponsors or the 2031 range, and "Caplet XR" arrives unexplained, but I would rather an unexplained name than a sentence spent explaining it.

### Pitch 7
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- "Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise. Her first $300,000 buys it." is the best pair of sentences in all eight pitches: the central idea in nine words. The trouble is the first sentence: "hopes for a gift" made me think Laura was hoping to receive one, when she means to give it, and I had to re-read. "Repay it in 2032" also leans on a pronoun doing heavy lifting. Short sentences give it rhythm, but three or four in a row start to sound like a strapline rather than a person.

### Pitch 8
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 5/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- Seventeen of fifty words go on the name: an extended-release capsule that "delivers its dose on schedule and only works whole". I did not follow "only works whole" on first read and I am still not sure what it is claiming about the portfolio. The remaining two sentences are the same sound mechanics as Pitch 6, but the pitch has used its best space on branding rather than on Laura. A pitch should sell the plan, not the label.

## Which one sounds most machine-written, and why
Pitch 1 (and its twin, Pitch 4). It opens with a product name and a verb from a press release ("releases Laura's money on schedule"), compresses two ideas into one sentence that has to be unpacked ("builds a floor for the facility gift and buys world stocks with the expected interest"), and closes with two fragments arranged for rhythm rather than meaning: "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor." That staccato, slogan-first cadence is what over-edited text sounds like when every connecting word has been cut to save a count. A student talking would say "stocks can raise the gift but can't lower it", and would use a subject and a verb. Pitch 3 is the runner-up for a different reason: it does not sound like a slogan, it sounds like the rubric, with "near-certainty", "announced minimum" and "preserving flexibility" standing in for an actual explanation.
