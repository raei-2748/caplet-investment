## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The strategy underneath is sound: buy the ten payments, build a Treasury floor from the 2028 deposit, put the leftover interest into world stocks, give co-sponsors a range from floor to floor plus half the stocks. It also ties Laura's income risk to the plan sensibly. What holds it back is how much of the page is spent on the pharmacist metaphor, which reads as built rather than lived, and the reasoning about why not take more equity risk is a single sentence. The reinvestment shortfall is raised, which I like, but "whatever they can't cover comes off the bottom" is left unexplained, and no assumptions or return numbers appear at all. It reads like a polished piece of branding by someone who understands the product, not like a team showing its working.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why "ten holdings" are redeemed when the floor and the stock fund are separate holdings; how a capital-protected note without an option is meant to be better, which is stated but not argued.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same plan as Entry 1, but it gives more of the reasoning I want: why not put the floor in stocks (time makes a loss less likely without making it smaller), why the floor is a commitment rather than a forecast, and an honest "we accept that" on reinvestment risk. ### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This reads like students wrote it after doing the work: plain words, a clear definition of high certainty ("we are removing the market from the question"), a stated return assumption with a realistic bad-year warning, and a closing line about Laura taking risks with ideas but not with promises that fits her case well. - Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the minimum and the "share" of VT in 2031 are sized; the share is never given.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7.5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A complete answer to what Wharton asks. It separates the promise from the vision, defines high certainty, handles coupons, says how the portfolio changes in 2031 and 2033, caps the facility contribution at half the growth sleeve, and states return, inflation and rebalancing assumptions and a review habit. The 80/20 stocks and Treasuries sleeve is a plain, defensible choice, and it connects stocks to Taiwan construction cost. ### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one that shows a team thinking for itself. The central idea, that the minimum told to co-sponsors should be the number the portfolio is built to protect, ties the 2031 communication directly to the investment rule, which is what the case is about. It admits where its own rule can fail (a fast one-month fall, cash-lock that misses a recovery, the 2028 deposit being variable, the Taiwan dollar with derivatives banned) and says why the team chose a multiplier of three rather than five. It defines confidence levels and says the ranges come from simulation. The cost is complexity: a CPPI rule with a floor at 60% of the sleeve is a lot for a 500-word IPS, a few terms are jargon, and a reader may doubt whether students could run it. Even so, the honesty about trade-offs and the specific assumptions make it the most credible attempt at real analysis.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the floor starts at about 60% of the sleeve; what "about half of 10,000 simulated paths would beat" gives co-sponsors in plain terms; how the rule works if the 2028 deposit arrives smaller.

## My overall view
Four of the six entries are variations of one plan, and three of those share a cute capsule device that I find more clever than helpful. The strategy is good, but the page keeps stopping to admire itself, and none of them gives the numbers to check it. The ones that stood out were those that told me what the team assumed and what could go wrong. Entry 6 does that best, and Entry 5 covers the ground thoroughly. The best entry would need to keep Entry 6's honesty about risk, cut some of the machinery so it reads as something a teenager could explain aloud, and add one or two plain numbers about the range. The capsule entries would need to drop the slogan and put the saved words into assumptions and trade-offs.
