## Per entry
### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: This entry answers every one of Wharton's five IPS questions with something a reader can check: a central idea (the 2031 minimum is the number the portfolio is built to protect), a named rule for the growth money, a liquidity section, a reserve that shortens each year, and a 2031 range with a stated confidence level. It shows its assumptions openly (stock and Treasury returns and volatility, timing of flows, fixed nominal payments) and says plainly what it accepts: gap risk, a cash-lock if the growth money falls to the floor, a variable 2028 deposit and currency. It also explains why it chose a multiplier of three over five, which is the kind of reasoning Wharton asks for. What holds it back is density: a pre-committed multiplier rule, a floor that accrues at a yield and a simulated range are a lot to carry in 500 words, and the gap-risk claim (a one-month fall of about a third) is stated without the working. Articulation cannot be judged well from an IPS alone.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: The accrual of the floor and how the cushion sets the stock share; I had to work out the sequence for myself.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What it does well and what holds it back: The central idea is stated plainly (secure the promise first, then grow), and the balance between growth, risk and liquidity is explicit: an 80/20 growth mix with a stated annual rebalancing rule, an intermediate Treasury shock absorber, and a clear argument that equities hedge Taiwan construction costs. It names planned changes at 2031 and 2033 and gives return, inflation and scenario assumptions (good, average, severe markets). What holds it back is the 2031 floor: the money behind it was invested 80% in stocks from 2028, so the bottom is not a fixed amount until it is moved to Treasuries, and the entry does not say what happens if stocks have fallen by then. The "stated, modelled confidence level" for the range is never stated, and reinvestment risk on the coupons gets one sentence. Easy to follow, and it reads like an honest first draft of a real plan.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: How the 2031 bottom is set when the 2028 money is still in stocks.

### Entry 3

- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What it does well and what holds it back: The entry is candid about its single weak point (a long fall in interest rates lowering the floor) and explains the reinvestment gap and how it is covered before the range is named. Its client reading is well placed: it ties the 2028 deposit to publishing and speaking income that falls in the same downturns that make bonds dearer, which is the case's own funding source. What holds it back: the 2031 mechanism does not sit together on a first read (the top is floor plus half the stocks, that half moves into bonds, yet the pitch says stocks move the ceiling), and the 2033 contribution is never sized. ## My overall view
The best entry is the one that shows its working. Entry 1 does what Wharton asked: a central idea, the rules that guide the money, a liquidity and reserve path, and a 2031 range with stated confidence, all sitting on stated return assumptions. Entries 2 and 4 are sound and easy to follow but either leave the 2031 floor softer than it sounds or leave the growth plan untested. The Caplet XR entries have the best client reading and a memorable idea, but they give the reader almost nothing to check; a judge who is asked to score portfolio analysis cannot see a projection, a return assumption or a scenario anywhere in them. To win, an entry needs Entry 1's checkable mechanics, Entry 2's clear picture of balance over time, and a plain statement of what the floor is in every market outcome, in fewer words than Entry 1 uses.
