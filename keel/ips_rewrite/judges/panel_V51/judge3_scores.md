# Judge J3 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: buys the payments, buys the floor, and is the only kind of plan here that names the reinvestment hole, prices it before the promise and says who pays for it.
2. Entry 3: the same plan and the same honest handling of reinvestment; it is a little thinner on diversification and on what is locked in 2031.
3. Entry 4: the most technically serious growth sleeve (rule-based CPPI with the gap-risk maths done), but it treats the ladder as risk-free apart from default and is vague about how confident the range is.
4. Entry 2: the Entry 1/3 design, but it never says when the reinvestment gap is set aside, so an announced floor could still be dipped into. Its pitch ("Never the floor") contradicts its own body.
5. Entry 6: complete, sensible and well diversified, with a good 2031 lock. It counts on coupons being reinvested and then claims rates "never touch the payments".
6. Entry 5: specific and clean on the ladder instruments, but it leaves the stocks unhedged right through the period after the range is announced, leaves the split and the flexibility share unsized, and misses reinvestment risk.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- When I trace the cash flows, they hold. The $300,000 buys the ten payments outright, and any shortfall is the first call on the 2028 deposit. Most of the deposit buys a Treasury floor maturing before 2033, roughly its expected interest goes into a diversified world fund, and in 2031 the promised top is moved into bonds before it is spoken. So nothing promised to co-sponsors depends on a market price afterwards. The team saw the hole most student ladders leave unpriced: coupons and early maturities have to be reinvested at unknown rates. It says that gap is set aside from Laura's kept stocks before the range is named, and that any remainder comes off the bottom. That is the right order of claims (payments, then floor, then gift). Linking the 2028 deposit to Laura's career is a real client insight: her income dries up in the same downturn that makes bonds dearer, which is why the ladder is bought in 2027. What holds it back is that no return, inflation or currency assumption is stated, so I cannot check how big the upside or the reinvestment gap might be. The plan is also very conservative: total equity is a small share of her money and her flexibility is "half the stocks", which the reinvestment set-aside could largely use up. The case's concern about committing too much of what remains deserves a sentence on how large that kept share is.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The top half is locked in Treasuries in 2031, so the 2033 gift looks almost fixed at the top from that point on. I was unsure whether the "range" is really just the top minus a reinvestment allowance, and how much "rounded down" takes off.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the same sound structure as Entries 1 and 3: the ladder bought in 2027, a floor bought from the 2028 deposit, a small equity leg, and the top locked before it is announced. It also names reinvestment as the weak point, which most plans never do. But it stops short of the actuarial step that matters: it never says when the gap is measured and set aside. It says the gap comes from Laura's kept half "before the floor", so if the gap turns out bigger than the kept half after 2031, the floor already promised to co-sponsors gets cut. That is exactly the credibility damage the case warns against. The pitch's "Never the floor" contradicts that. The point that time makes a loss less likely but not smaller is a correct and useful reason for not putting the floor in stocks. Like 1 and 3, it gives no return or inflation assumptions, and its flexibility is thin and unsized.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: Whether the reinvestment gap is reserved before or after the range is announced, and therefore whether the floor really is a commitment.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The logic is the same as Entry 1 and it holds just as well. Payments are bought, not forecast. The floor is bought from the deposit that actually arrives. The reinvestment gap is set aside before the range is named, and anything left over lowers the bottom rather than breaking a promise. I value that its pitch admits the one thing that could shrink the floor: a long fall in interest rates. That is the honest risk statement a co-sponsor needs. It ranks just below Entry 1 because diversification is barely shown (the world fund is never described as broad) and because it is less explicit that the promised top is fully in bonds once named. The same gaps as Entry 1 hold it back: no stated return, inflation or currency assumptions, and a flexibility share that is small, unsized and the first thing the reinvestment gap eats.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: The same question as Entry 1. After the 2031 move, I could not tell what market outcome still separates the bottom of the range from the top.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- "Floor = Promise" is a strong central idea that ties the 2031 communication directly to how the portfolio is built. The CPPI sleeve shows real understanding: the leverage cap because margin is banned, the gap-risk threshold of about a third for a multiplier of three, the cash-lock risk accepted openly, and a rule fixed in advance so that markets do not trigger a rewrite. It also states its return and volatility assumptions, and it gives currency and facility inflation to the flexibility share, which is more than most entries do. But the reserve is described as carrying only US default risk. A $300,000 ladder for $500,000 of payments only works if coupons are reinvested, and that rate risk is nowhere priced. The range also confuses me. If the minimum is mechanically protected, why target only 95% confidence in it? And with the top set at the median, the chance of landing inside the range is close to even, which the entry never states plainly. Client knowledge is thin: the deposit risk is named, but it is not connected to how Laura earns her money.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: How the 2031 minimum is chosen from a floor that "starts near 60%" and accrues at the short rate. I also could not tell whether a 2033 outcome above the top is capped or simply given.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The reserve is concrete and well thought through: dated iBond funds for the early rungs, individual Treasuries for the later ones, the 2028 deposit filling any gap, and a clear definition of certainty as the market removed from the question. That is a good start. But it claims each rung pays its known value whatever happens to interest rates, and that is not true of a ladder that relies on coupons and fund distributions being reinvested. The hole goes unpriced. The 2028 deposit is "split in two" with no rule for the split. The equity stays fully in stocks right through 2031-2033, after the range has been named, so the top can be missed by a crash in the last two years. The flexibility share ("part of the VT holding") is never sized. Deferring the confidence level to the Final Report is allowed, but the reader is left without a framework for how it will be set.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: What share of VT goes into the top of the range, and how much of the deposit buys the floor rather than stocks.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This entry answers every one of Wharton's questions in order. The ladder is completed from the 2028 deposit. The growth sleeve is well diversified across US and international stocks with an intermediate Treasury buffer and a drift-band rebalancing rule. In 2031 it locks the bottom of the range in Treasuries before Laura speaks. In 2033 the gift is the floor plus at most half the growth, capped at the top of the range. The payments, the promise and the flexibility are ordered correctly, and the assumptions are stated. The flaw for me is internal. It says principal plus coupons covers each payment and that early coupons sit in short-term Treasuries, which is exactly where falling rates bite, and then it says rate swings never touch the payments. It does not say how the 2031 floor is sized relative to the sleeve, and the confidence on the top is only "stated, modelled". It is sound and safe, but I found no idea in it that another strong team would not also have.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none

## My overall view
Every entry here buys a Treasury ladder, so the ladder itself separates nobody. What separates them is whether the team understands that a ladder costing $300,000 for $500,000 of payments is funded partly by coupons that have not yet been reinvested. Four of the six call their reserve risk-free apart from default, and that is the hole I expected to find. Entries 1 and 3 (and, less carefully, 2) found it, decided who absorbs it and when, and put it below the payments and ahead of the promise, with the gap reserved before anything is said to co-sponsors. That is how a liability-driven investor thinks. Entry 4 has the best growth engineering but the weakest reserve logic. To win, the best entry needs to keep its claim order and its 2031 lock while adding three things: its return, inflation and currency assumptions in one plain line; a stated size, even as a share, for the flexibility Laura keeps once the reinvestment allowance is taken; and a clear sentence on what the range actually expresses once the top has been moved into bonds.
