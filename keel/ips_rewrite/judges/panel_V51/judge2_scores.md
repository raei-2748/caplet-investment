# Judge J2 scores

(Marcus, former equity portfolio manager. Scored only against Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 - the only plan that puts real stock exposure behind the facility gift, with an explicit rule, stated assumptions, and honest accounting of what the rule costs (cash-lock, gap risk).
2. Entry 5 - clean "ladder first, 80/20 growth engine on top" plan with assumptions, a glide path and a sensible 2031 floor; conventional but sound and growth-minded.
3. Entry 1 - correct, plain ladder-plus-floor-plus-world-stock plan with return and drawdown assumptions stated; stock sleeve is small.
4. Entry 2 - same floor-plus-interest structure as 1, the best-reasoned of the three near-identical "capital-protected note" entries, but no return assumptions and very little money left for growth.
5. Entry 4 - same structure; argues the trade-off against stocks openly but never prices it, and has no return assumptions.
6. Entry 3 - same structure; the least explicit about its own trade-offs and assumptions of the three.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- It buys the ten payments first (iBond ETFs for 2033-36, individual Treasuries for 2037-42), then splits the 2028 deposit into a Treasury floor for 2032 and one world stock fund held untouched. The logic hangs together and the definition of "high certainty" is clear and honest (removing the market from the question). It states return assumptions (6-7%) and admits a -30% year is possible, which most entries do not. What holds it back for me is the size of the growth sleeve: after the ladder and the floor, very little money is left in stocks for a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks, and the entry never says what that safety costs in expected facility gift. The 2031 range is described only loosely ("a share of VT's value"), and diversification beyond one stock fund and Treasuries is thin. Decent, safe, not distinctive.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the "share" of VT is that counts toward the top of the range, and how much of the 2028 deposit goes to the floor versus VT. Neither is given.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure is a ladder for the payments, a Treasury floor for the building gift bought with the 2028 deposit, and only the floor's expected interest put into world stocks. The client work is the best of the timid group: it notices that Laura's 2028 deposit comes from income that behaves like equity, so a bought ladder protects her where a forecast would not. It also names reinvestment risk and says how the gap is handled before the range is announced. But for a client with six to sixteen years and living costs covered elsewhere, the stock sleeve is tiny, so the "upside" is mostly a rounding error on the floor. There are no return or volatility assumptions anywhere, so a judge cannot check the range or the "honest upside" it promises. The capsule branding takes up a lot of space that could have gone on numbers. I mark the strategy as sound but timid.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "expected interest" comes to in dollars, and what the range actually looks like. The entry never gives a figure or odds.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It is the same strategy as Entry 2 with a few things taken out. It still has the sound ladder and the clear order of priorities (payments, then floor, then gift). But the explicit hit-by-hit handling of the reinvestment shortfall is thinner, and there is again no return assumption, no stress case and no stated probability on the range. The timid stock sleeve is the same, and the entry does not reckon what it gives up. Wharton says judges want the assumptions and trade-offs behind the decisions, and this one mostly asserts that a floor is better than a gamble.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how large the floor and the stock sleeve actually are, and why "only a long fall in interest rates could shrink the floor", given that the floor is a bought Treasury.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same ladder, same floor, same small stock sleeve. It deserves some credit for stating the trade-off openly: it admits it could chase a bigger gift with stocks and chooses not to, and says the reinvestment gap is "accepted". The argument it uses, that time makes a loss less likely without making it smaller, is fair but is used to wave away a growth choice instead of pricing it. The line about shares that "cannot expire worthless" is loose. Still no return assumptions, no stress case and no numbers on the range. A judge who wants to see the cost of safety counted will not find it here.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "any gap comes from Laura's kept half before the floor". It is unclear whether the floor or the kept half takes the loss first.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Ladder first, sized to cost about two-thirds of the contributions; everything above it goes into a diversified growth portfolio (about 80% global stocks, 20% intermediate Treasuries) with an annual drift-only rebalance. In 2031 the bottom of the announced range is moved into Treasuries, so the floor is set from what growth has actually delivered and is not capped by a 2028 guess. That is the better way to build the facility gift: it gives the money six years of equity exposure. It states assumptions (6-7% stocks, 4-5% Treasuries, 2.5% inflation), tests good, average and severe markets, ties stocks to Taiwan construction-cost inflation, and describes how the portfolio changes in 2031 and 2033. The weaknesses are that it is conventional, it does not count what the 20% bond sleeve costs, and the "modelled confidence" is promised, not shown. Still the most complete answer to Wharton's questions in plain language.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. (One small point: "at most half of the remaining growth portfolio" and "capped at the top of her announced range" leave it open which binds first.)

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one entry that treats the 2028 deposit as risk capital and says how it will be managed. The rule starts at about full equity exposure, pulls back as the cushion over the floor shrinks, and is bounded by the ban on margin (the multiplier of three is chosen with reasons). The reserve is separate and never touches stocks. The idea that the number told to co-sponsors is the number the portfolio is built to defend ties the strategy straight to Wharton's 2031 credibility test. It owns its costs: cash-lock if the sleeve falls to the floor, gap risk inside a month, the equity-like nature of the 2028 deposit, and currency risk from Taiwan. It gives volatility and return assumptions and a 95% confidence on the minimum with roughly even odds on the top, which is the "honest about the upside" I want. The weaknesses are real: it is dense and jargon-heavy (CPPI, cushion), it goes into mechanics Wharton says belong elsewhere (monthly rule, multiplier, 10,000 paths), and a rule-based approach that sells after a fall can whip-saw and lock in a loss. A lay judge may find it hard to follow. But on strategy and analysis it is clearly ahead.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor moves from "near 60% of the sleeve" to "the present value of the announced minimum" in 2031, and whether the 95% confidence applies to the minimum as built or as announced.

## My overall view
Four of six entries were the same safe answer: buy the ten payments, buy a Treasury floor for the building, and put whatever is left in a world stock fund. It is a sound answer and I would not call it wrong, but in three of those entries the stock sleeve is so small that the "growth" half of the plan barely exists, and none of them counts what the safety costs Laura in expected gift. The two entries that stood out do the opposite: they treat the first $300,000 as spoken for, then let the money that is actually risk capital take real equity risk, with stated assumptions and stated downsides. Entry 6 does this most thoughtfully and is the most honest about what it gives up, though it needs to be shorter and plainer to land with a generalist judge. Entry 5 is the safer way to the same place. To win, Entry 6 needs to cut the machinery to the idea (a floor that equals the promise, a sleeve that protects it), say the likely range in dollars, and show that the cash-lock case is rare. Entry 5 needs to count what its bond sleeve costs and show the range it implies.
