# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Read blind: Wharton's criteria and IPS questions, the Laura Gao case, the six entries. Nothing else.

My prior, stated up front: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes. All six entries reached it. So the question for each was "what did I learn here that I did not already know?", and then a pairwise tournament on Wharton's five criteria.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5 ... the capital-protected-note idea with the option stripped out, plus the career-as-a-stock reading of the client, and a pitch that admits its own weak point; the most new thinking per word, with an honest pitch.
2. Entry 1 ... the same strategy and the same two ideas, a touch more complete on diversification and the 2031 mechanics, but a pitch that spends its 50 words on the name rather than on the risk.
3. Entry 3 ... the only genuinely different mechanism (a pre-committed CPPI rule with the 2031 promise as its floor) and the best-argued trade-offs in the set; held back by complexity a lay judge must work for, and by an unargued starting floor.
4. Entry 6 ... the same strategy as 1 and 5 with a good argument about time and risk, but its pitch promises "never the floor" while its own text admits the floor can be hit; the inconsistency costs it.
5. Entry 2 ... a clean, honest ladder-plus-one-fund plan that buys the 2031 minimum early; competent, little new, the growth split left undecided.
6. Entry 4 ... "promise first, then grow" done tidily with an 80/20 growth engine and annual rebalancing; exactly my prior, well presented, nothing I had not already assumed a good team would do.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: two things. First, the growth sleeve is sized to the expected interest on the floor Treasuries, so the equity position is literally the yield that a bank would otherwise spend on an option; that is a structured product rebuilt without the derivative, and it answers "how much risk" without a percentage. Second, the client's 2028 deposit is itself an equity-like cash flow, correlated with the very downturns that make bonds dearer, which is the sharpest reading of Laura's circumstances in the panel. The strategy is disciplined, the ordering (cheques, floor, gift) is a real decision rule, and the reinvestment weakness is named rather than hidden. What holds it back on Portfolio Analysis is that nothing is quantified: no return assumption, no confidence attached to the top of the range, and "half her stocks" is asserted, not argued. The pitch uses most of its words on the capsule metaphor, which is charming but tells a co-sponsor less than it could.
- Confused on first reading: "roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn" made me pause; I had to work out that this means the sleeve is sized to five years of expected coupon income up front, not that coupons are invested as they arrive. I also had to re-read how the reinvestment set-aside and the move of half the stocks into Treasuries interact in 2031.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: not much that was new, but the plan is honest and complete. Buying a 2032 Treasury in 2028 so the minimum promise is already owned three years before Laura speaks to co-sponsors is a sound, under-rated choice, and the definition of certainty as "does not depend on any forecast" is clear. Return assumptions and a crash magnitude are stated, inflation is placed with the asset best able to carry it, and liquidity is handled. The strategy is table stakes executed well: one ladder, one fund, leave it alone. It is held back by leaving the central allocation undecided ("one part ... the rest" of the 2028 deposit), by deferring the confidence figure to the Final Report, and by a presentation that reads like a checklist of Wharton's questions rather than a single argument.
- Confused on first reading: how the 2028 deposit is split between the 2032 Treasury and the stock fund, which is the one number the whole facility range depends on.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: "Floor = Promise" is a real design principle: the number Laura announces in 2031 becomes the floor the rule mechanically protects, so the promise and the risk budget are the same object. The growth sleeve runs a pre-committed CPPI rule, and the entry does what I most want from a decision-making framework: it states the rule, names the two ways it fails (gap risk, cash-lock), says why it chose a multiplier of three and not five, and accepts the consequence. Assumptions are explicit, the 95% target for the minimum and even odds for the top are the clearest confidence statements in the panel, and currency and the variable 2028 deposit are both considered. It is held back by two things. The starting floor "near 60% of the sleeve" is not argued, and that single parameter decides how much equity Laura ever holds. And the mechanism costs the reader: a lay judge or co-sponsor meeting "three times the cushion" for the first time will not feel they understand where Laura's money is, which matters for earning her confidence. The rule also sells after falls and trades monthly, which is a cost the entry does not mention.
- Confused on first reading: the floor "from 2031 equals the present value of Laura's announced minimum", but the minimum is itself derived from the floor at that date; I had to read twice to see this is a hand-off, not a circle. Why 60%.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing I had not already assumed a competent team would do, which is my prior exactly. The two-jobs framing (promise and vision) is clear, the ladder is correctly built with coupons parked in short Treasuries, the 2031 step of moving the bottom of the range into Treasuries is sensible, and the operating reserve self-liquidates. The analysis section is the tidiest of the six: stated return, inflation and no-outside-money assumptions, three scenarios, a rebalancing band, an annual review. It is held back by the growth engine: 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries is asserted, not reasoned, and the 20% does not obviously earn its place when the payments are already secured elsewhere. The facility bottom is locked only in 2031, so for four years the promise is not owned. Well organised and credible, but it would be hard to pick out from a hundred good entries.
- Confused on first reading: none of substance; I only wondered why 20% intermediate Treasuries rather than nothing or more.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same two ideas as Entry 1, the option-free capital-protected note and the correlated 2028 deposit, so the strategy and client reading score the same. What lifts it above Entry 1 is the pitch, which uses its 50 words on the strategy and ends by disclosing the one thing that could shrink the floor (a long fall in rates). A co-sponsor who reads only the pitch learns both the promise and its limit; that is exactly what Wharton means by communicating uncertainty credibly, and it is consistent with the reinvestment paragraph in the body. The body is otherwise a very close copy of Entry 1 and shares its gaps: no stated return assumption, no confidence attached to the top of the range, "half" asserted rather than argued, and the diversification point is lighter here (one world fund, without saying what that means). For a top-10 finish rather than a top-50 one, it needs a number next to the top of the range and a sentence on why half.
- Confused on first reading: the same two points as Entry 1 (the meaning of "roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn", and the 2031 sequencing of the set-aside and the move of half the stocks into bonds).

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: again the same strategy, with one addition I valued as a decision scientist: the point that a long horizon makes a stock loss less likely without making it smaller, which is the correct answer to the usual "stocks are safe over six years" argument and the right reason to buy the floor rather than hope for it. "Laura carries more stock-market risk than she may realise" is also a stronger way to state the career-correlation insight. It is held back by consistency, which Wharton scores explicitly. The pitch ends "Never the floor", but the body says a reinvestment gap comes from Laura's kept half "before the floor", which concedes the floor can be reached; the pitch promises more than the policy delivers, and that is the error the case warns against. "Shares, which cannot expire worthless" is also loose: a world fund is very unlikely to go to zero, but the contrast with an option (which expires worthless if markets merely stand still) was made more precisely elsewhere. Portfolio analysis is the thinnest of the three variants: the reinvestment risk is accepted without saying how large it could be or how it is sized.
- Confused on first reading: whether the floor is truly never touched (pitch) or can be reduced after the kept half is exhausted (body).

## My overall view
Three entries (1, 5, 6) are close variants of one text and I scored their differences; the other three are independent. What separated the top from the rest was not the ladder, which everyone had, but whether the entry taught me something about the problem: that the growth sleeve can be sized to the floor's expected interest so the equity risk is literally the yield a bank would spend on an option; that Laura's own 2028 deposit is an equity-like cash flow correlated with bad markets; that a promise can be made the floor a rule defends and the rule's failure modes argued openly. Entries 2 and 4 are good, credible plans I would trust with the money, but they are what I expected to read before I opened the file. Entry 5 wins on the strength of its ideas and an honest pitch, but it is winning a Top-50 place, not yet the competition: to win it needs what Entry 3 has and it lacks, namely a stated return assumption, a confidence figure attached to the top of the 2031 range, and one sentence arguing why half of the stocks is the right share to promise. Entry 3, in turn, would need to make its rule feel as simple to Laura as it is to its authors, and to defend its 60% starting floor.
