# Judge J3 scores

Priya, pension actuary. My method: trace Laura's cash flows ($300,000 in 2027, $150,000 in 2028, the 2031 range, the 2033 contribution, ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042) through each plan and find where it breaks or where the reasoning is waved through.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6: every dollar traceable, the coupon reinvestment leak named and paid for, and the one real trade-off priced as mean cost against tail damage.
2. Entry 4: same traceable plan; the rival comparison is framed in the right units (how often the promise to co-sponsors collapses), but return assumptions are never stated.
3. Entry 2: a real liability-protection tool (CPPI) with its gap risk understood, but the ladder sizing is loose and the range confidence is close to a coin toss.
4. Entry 5: same sound plan as 4 and 6, but the comparison is against a straw man and its own failure figure contradicts its certainty claim.
5. Entry 1: a correct, standard liability-matching plan, but it overclaims rate immunity and leaves the 2031 floor exposed to four years of 80% equity.
6. Entry 3: an appealing staging idea that moves money past 2033 and conditions the gift on co-sponsors, with almost no analysis behind it.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The structure works: the 2027 money buys a cash-flow-matched Treasury ladder with "principal plus coupons" sized to the payments, the 2028 money tops up any gap first, and the surplus grows. It answers all of Wharton's questions, including how the reserve runs down and how the gift rule leaves flexibility. Two holes. First, it holds early coupons in short-term Treasuries and still says rate swings "never touch the payments". That is false: if yields fall, the reinvested coupons earn less and the ladder falls short. Second, the 2031 floor is just whatever an 80/20 sleeve is worth in 2031, so the number Laura gives co-sponsors carries the full 2028-2031 equity risk, and the entry never weighs that. Its "stated, modelled confidence level" is promised but never given, and its assumptions are listed rather than tested. It's a competent textbook plan that doesn't show it understands where it could fail.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry that uses a real dynamic floor rule. Its CPPI multiplier is chosen for a stated reason: a breach needs roughly a one-third monthly fall at multiplier three. It accepts cash-lock openly and commits not to override the rule when markets move, which is the discipline Wharton asks for. It covers the most ground: the 2028 deposit's dependence on her career income, Taiwan-dollar risk, inflation on facility costs and liquidity. The analysis is weaker than the breadth suggests, though. Saying each rung is "sized to deliver $50,000" skips how coupons are handled. Read literally, ten $50,000 face bonds near par would cost far more than $300,000. It never mentions coupon reinvestment risk. The 60% starting floor has no stated reason. With a 95% target on the minimum and the top at the median, Laura's 2033 gift lands inside the range only about half the time, which answers Wharton's confidence question weakly, and it is unclear whether a result above the top is capped.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the protected minimum carries only a 95% confidence target when a CPPI floor should fail only through gap risk. I also couldn't tell whether a contribution above the median-based top is paid in full or capped.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The operating ladder is sound in principle, and the founder framing of committed burn against growth capital is a reasonable way to explain priority. Past that, the plan comes apart. The facility money is staged into 2034-2035, while the case asks for a contribution decided in 2033. Gate 2 depends on co-sponsors committing first, at the same time as they are deciding on the strength of Laura's commitment. The QQQ tilt is justified by Laura's career being in technology and creative industries. For a client whose 2028 deposit depends on that same career income, that argues for less tech exposure, not more. There is no range confidence, no inflation or currency treatment, no sizing of the seed tranche and no check that $300,000 buys the ladder. I could not trace a single dollar from 2028 to 2033.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the seed tranche is and how the 2031 range is sized. It is also unclear how a Gate 3 tranche in 2034-2035 squares with a contribution announced for 2033.

### Entry 4

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- I can follow this plan from start to finish. The 2027 money buys the ladder "or nearly", and the 2028 money closes any gap. Most of the rest buys a five-year Treasury floor and the remainder world stocks. In 2031 the coupon-reinvestment shortfall is priced and set aside before any gift is named, the promised half of the stocks moves to Treasuries, and the reserve then runs itself down. It treats "high certainty" as a list of named failure modes, and it is the only entry that sees the risk that matters most to me: the 2028 deposit and the ladder's cost are both hit by the same recession. ### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- It has the same plan as Entry 4: a matched ladder, a Treasury floor bought from the 2028 money, the coupon shortfall priced and set aside from the gift side before any range is named, the promised half moved to Treasuries, a stated 97-in-100 chance of reaching the top, and a reserve that runs down. Its trade-off analysis is the best on the panel. It compares against the strongest rival (same ladder, floor bought later from stocks), matches the two at the same median bottom, and states both sides: about $19,000 lost on average by 2042, against about $90,000 instead of $140,000 named to co-sponsors in the worst twentieth of paths. That is how an actuary would justify paying for protection, and it ties back to the case's warning about credibility. What holds it back: the return and inflation assumptions are still not written down, and the flexibility Laura keeps after the gift is small and never sized. There is also a puzzle in the floor. It is described as "owned since 2028", yet it drops to $140,000 in bad paths. I can infer the reason (bad stock years tend to come with falling yields, which raises the coupon set-aside), but the entry should say so.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why an owned floor still moves from $145,000 to $140,000 across paths. I also didn't see what "Do not crush" refers to.

## My overall view
Every entry buys the ten payments with Treasuries, so the operating commitment did not separate them. What separated them was the next layer of cash flows: what happens to coupons received years before they are needed, what happens if the recession that makes the ladder dearer also shrinks the 2028 deposit, and how the 2031 number survives a bad 2028-2030. Entries 4 and 6 are the only ones that name all three and say who pays for each, in order: payments first, floor second, gift last. Entry 6 earns first place because it prices the protection the way a trustee would, as a stated average cost against a stated tail loss, against the rival a thoughtful risk-taker like Laura would actually consider. To win outright, it needs to state the return, rate and inflation assumptions behind its model, explain why the "owned" floor still moves in bad paths, and size the flexibility Laura keeps after the gift, since that is the part of the case the plan treats most lightly.
