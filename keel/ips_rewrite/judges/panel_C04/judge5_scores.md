## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: nothing new. Ladder for the ten payments, 80/20 growth on top, the bottom of the range moved into Treasuries in 2031, half the growth money given and half kept. It is well organised, defines high certainty sensibly (every payment backed by a maturity, residual risk is US default), states return and inflation assumptions, and sets a rebalancing band, which is more discipline than many teams show. Client fit is adequate but generic: "entrepreneur who takes thoughtful risks" and "credibility with co-sponsors" is read off the case rather than thought through. The range's top is "a stated, modelled confidence level" that is never stated, and the trade-off between locking the floor in 2031 and not locking it is never priced; the entry asserts its choices rather than arguing them. Under Wharton's own words, judges want the reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs behind the decisions; this gives the decisions.
- Confused on first reading: none.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the 2031 promise can be made the mechanical floor of a CPPI rule, and that a pre-committed rule is a defence against the team itself ("falls cut stock exposure before anyone is tempted to decide"). As a decision scientist I rate that pre-commitment device highly, and the entry is the most complete on Wharton's Portfolio Analysis criterion: return and volatility assumptions, a 95% target on the minimum, even odds on the top, liquidity, and a list of accepted risks with the reason each is accepted (multiplier three not five; cash-lock accepted because the promise still holds; the 2028 deposit claimed first; Taiwan dollar paid from the flexibility share). What holds it back: the rule protects a floor that is never put in dollars and that starts at an unargued 60% of the sleeve, so in the bad 2028-30 paths that matter most to co-sponsors the protected minimum is low and path-dependent; the entry buys considerable complexity (monthly rebalancing, gap risk, a cushion multiplier) for a floor a simpler design would buy outright. Laura's confidence, which Wharton asks for, is harder to earn with a rule she cannot see the result of than with a bond she owns.
- Confused on first reading: what the 60% starting floor means in dollars for Laura, and why 60%; whether the minimum "announced" in 2031 is simply whatever the floor has accrued to by then, so that a bad early market lowers her promise.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: four things. High certainty defined as a list of failure modes, one of which is new to me in this setting: a recession before purchase lowers yields, raising the ladder's cost, at the same moment it shrinks the entrepreneurial income behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap; that is a correlated risk most teams miss. The coupon-reinvestment leak, priced and set aside in 2031 before any gift is named. The 2028 deposit built as a capital-protected note without the option: Treasuries that grow back to the full deposit by 2032, the expected interest in a world fund, so the floor of the range is owned from 2028, not bought in 2031. And the asymmetry of a capped range once named, handled by moving the promised half into Treasuries on naming day. The client fit is strong: "ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill" is a sharper statement of her problem than the case gives. What holds it back is the trade-off it chooses to argue: a 60/40 portfolio that misses a cheque one time in three is not a serious rival under a case that demands high certainty, so the comparison proves what every ladder entry proves. Assumptions are thinner than Entry 2's (one return figure, no volatility, no Treasury return), and the reserve's composition over time gets one sentence.
- Confused on first reading: a line reading "Sell low, by appointment" sits on its own between paragraphs and I took a moment to see it as a heading for the paragraph after it; "just enough to grow back into all of it" needed a second read before I understood the floor is the whole 2028 deposit; the closing "Do not crush" I only understood after the capsule explanation two sentences before it.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution can be staged like venture funding, each tranche a real option Laura holds and need not exercise, with the announced range's bottom equal to a tranche already secured. That is a genuinely new way to see the client's problem, it answers Wharton's "preserving flexibility" question structurally rather than with a percentage, and it is tied to how co-sponsors themselves think. The portfolio behind it is where it falls down. The growth money is 75% world stocks and 25% a technology index, justified because Laura's own career is in technology: that doubles her exposure to the sector her 2028 deposit already depends on, which is the opposite of the diversification Wharton scores. No confidence level is attached to the range, no volatility or downside assumption beyond "deep drawdowns possible", and no definition of the "target" that opens Gate 2. The third gate pays in 2034-35, after the 2033 contribution the case describes, which is defensible as flexibility "as the project develops" but is never reconciled with the case's timing. A strong frame on a weak chassis.
- Confused on first reading: what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio at Gate 2; how the 2031 range's top is sized or how confident Laura should be in it; whether paying a tranche in 2034-35 is still a facility contribution in the case's sense.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: everything in Entry 3 (the same design), plus the right trade-off argued. The rival here keeps the ladder and fixes the floor only in 2031 out of a 2028 deposit held in stocks, which is exactly the design of Entries 1 and 4 and, I expect, of most competent teams. The entry then prices the marginal decision: to name the same typical minimum, the rival has to promise under $100,000 about one year in thirteen, this plan about one in 150, and owning the floor early costs Laura about $10,000 in a typical 2033, which the team says it would pay. That is a well-argued trade-off in Wharton's sense: a decision, the reasoning, the cost. The design itself keeps the correlated recession risk, the coupon leak, the owned floor, the capped-range asymmetry, and a clear priority order if anything changes. What holds it back: the rival and its "bottom" are introduced before the reader has been told what the floor is, so the argument lands before its premise; no return or volatility assumption is stated, only "our model"; and the reserve's later composition and the stock sleeve's diversification get a sentence each.
- Confused on first reading: "fixing the gift's bottom only in 2031" and "that floor" appear a paragraph before the floor is explained, so I had to read on and come back; "grow back into all of it" as in Entry 3; "Do not crush".

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same four ideas as Entries 3 and 5, and the fullest statement of the trade-off any entry makes. The rival is again the floor-in-2031 design, matched to the same median minimum, and the entry concedes it usually wins, by about $19,000 for Laura on average by 2042, before showing what it loses in the worst twentieth of paths: a promise of about $90,000 to co-sponsors against $140,000 here, and more than $40,000 less kept. Mean cost against tail outcome, on both the promise and her own money, with the team's choice stated and owned: that is the integration of quantitative and qualitative analysis Wharton's Portfolio Analysis criterion describes, and it is the one place in the six entries where I could see the shape of the decision rather than its conclusion. Placing the comparison after the range is explained means every term in it has already been defined, which Entry 5 does not manage. Client fit is the same as its siblings: the case's "credible range" and "damage her credibility" are turned into the design's reason for existing, and the Taiwan-dollar building cost is where the kept half goes. What holds it back: assumptions are still implicit ("our model"), the stock sleeve is one fund and the reserve's run-down is one sentence, and the density of the prose means a judge reading once will miss a step or two. It would not need to be more clever to win; it would need to be a little plainer.
- Confused on first reading: the rival is matched to "the same $145,000 bottom as ours at the median" but the bad-path comparison is "against our $140,000"; I inferred the difference is the coupon shortfall set aside in 2031 from the earlier paragraph, but the entry does not say so; "grow back into all of it"; "Do not crush".

## My overall view
Every entry bought the ten payments with Treasuries, so the ladder decided nothing; what separated them was whether the team had seen anything past it. Entry 1 had not. Entry 4 saw something real, staged commitment as options, and then undercut it with a portfolio that concentrates Laura's risk where her income already sits and attaches no confidence to anything. Entry 2 saw that the promise should be the floor and built a rule to defend it, with the honesty to list what the rule costs; it loses to the Caplet XR entries only because they buy the same floor outright, earlier, for less machinery, and then tell me what the floor costs. Among the three near-identical XR entries the design is the same and the ranking turns entirely on which rival is argued against and where: Entry 3 beats a rival the case already forbids, Entry 5 beats the real rival but introduces it before its own floor, Entry 6 beats the real rival after the reader knows the terms and shows both the expected cost and the tail. To win rather than reach the semifinal, Entry 6 would need to state its return and volatility assumptions in a line, give the reserve's run-down and the sleeve's diversification one sentence more each, and loosen the prose so that a tired judge reading once does not have to reconstruct "grow back into all of it" or the $145,000 versus $140,000 step; the ideas are already there, and they are the only ones in this panel I had not met before.
