# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6 - Safe core, but it prices the upside it gives away ($19,000 on average, far worse for the rival only in a bad-start tail) and defends the trade-off with numbers.
2. Entry 1 - The only plan that runs real equity exposure with a rule, and it is candid about its cash-lock and gap risks; complexity and a cash-lock that could waste the growth sleeve hold it back.
3. Entry 3 - Same machine as Entry 6; the giveaway is stated ($10,000 in a typical year), but the "rival" is a narrow comparison and the cost looks smaller than I believe it really is.
4. Entry 5 - Sensible, balanced and clear (80/20 growth sleeve, half-the-surplus cap), but it never counts what the safety costs.
5. Entry 4 - Same machine again; it does admit the near-$200,000 giveaway, but beats a 60/40 strawman and then calls that giveaway "selling low by appointment".
6. Entry 2 - Entrepreneur metaphor and a concentrated growth tilt, but thin on numbers, no stated confidence for the 2031 range, and the staged tranches run past 2033.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the only entry whose growth sleeve is allowed to be mostly stocks, and it shows the mechanics (floor, multiplier of three, cap at 100% because margin is banned). It is honest about what can go wrong: gap risk, cash-lock if the sleeve touches the floor, and a variable 2028 deposit. It also states its assumptions (7% stocks, 16% volatility, 4.5% Treasuries) and says where the range comes from (10,000 simulated paths, 95% confidence in the minimum, roughly even odds at the top). My reservations: a CPPI that locks into Treasuries after a drawdown is a bond-heavy plan by another route, and the entry accepts that cost without saying how much upside it forfeits. The half-the-gain cap on the 2033 gift is also a giveaway that is never sized. It is dense and rule-heavy for a 500-word document, and the opening "Floor = Promise" is a tidy slogan, but the substance underneath is real.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the sleeve floor can start at about 60% yet "accrue" and then become the present value of the announced minimum in 2031; I could not tell whether the reserve ladder and the sleeve floor are the same money. The pitch's "stock-or-Treasury rule" was clearer after the body.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The reserve is a standard held-to-maturity Treasury ladder and the growth side is aggressive (75% world stocks, 25% QQQ), which I like in principle for a client who takes thoughtful risks. But the QQQ tilt is justified by a story about Laura's career, not by analysis, and it concentrates the one pot that has to deliver the gift. There is no stated confidence for the 2031 range, no dollar range, no simulation result and no sizing of anything, so portfolio analysis is nearly absent. The staged tranches are an appealing founder idea, but they push part of the facility contribution into 2034-35, whereas the case frames the decision as made in 2033 from the portfolio at that date. The "real option" language also overstates what is really just holding back money. It reads well but does not tell a co-sponsor how much to expect.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the seed tranche is the whole announced minimum or a part of it, and how Gate 2's conditions ("above its target") are set when no target is given.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The structure is clear: buy the ten payments, buy a floor with most of the 2028 deposit, put only the expected interest in a world fund, and give a range whose top is reached about 97 times in 100. It names concrete failure modes for "high certainty", including coupon reinvestment, and sets that shortfall aside in 2031. It also compares itself with the obvious rival (ladder plus stocks), quantifies the failure frequency (under $100,000 about 1 in 13 versus 1 in 150) and states a cost of about $10,000 in a typical year. For a growth person this is still a very conservative portfolio: most of $450,000 sits in Treasuries and only about $40,000 or so is in stocks, so 97 times in 100 reaching the top mostly says the top is set low. The $10,000 price looks modest against the upside actually forgone.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Set to promise the same $145,000 in a typical year" took a re-read; I had to work out that both plans were calibrated to the same median bottom.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Same design as Entries 3 and 6. Its distinctive paragraph does state the cost of safety bluntly: a 60/40 usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead. That is the kind of honesty I want. But it then sets this against a 60/40 on everything, which is a rival nobody serious would run with ten fixed liabilities and a known floor. The claim that it misses a cheque or breaks its word about one time in three is hard to believe against a ladder-plus-stocks design, and it makes the comparison easy to win. "Selling low, by appointment" is a fair point about sequence risk, but it is used to justify giving away roughly $200,000 without asking whether a middle design captures most of it. The honesty is real; the test is rigged.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what exactly the "60/40" holds (does it still buy the ladder?) and why it would miss cheques one time in three at 7.5% stock returns.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, professional plan: Treasury ladder first, then an 80/20 growth sleeve, annual rebalancing with a 5-point band, the bottom of the 2031 range moved to Treasuries, and a 2033 gift of the floor plus at most half the remaining growth money. It gives good reasons for owning stocks (Taiwan construction costs) and tests good, average and severe markets. It is the most conventional entry and the easiest for a co-sponsor to understand. But it states no dollar range, no confidence level and no number for what the half-cap or the 20% bonds cost, so "stated, modelled confidence" is promised and never shown. The cost of its safety is never counted, which is my main complaint, though its equity weight is the more honest compromise.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: whether the "locked floor" in 2031 is bought out of the growth sleeve or from the ladder's headroom, and what size it is.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same core as Entries 3 and 4, but with the best treatment of the trade-off I read. The rival here is a serious one (ladder kept, 2028 deposit mostly in stocks, floor bought only in 2031, matched to the same $145,000 bottom), and the entry says plainly that the rival usually wins, by about $19,000 on average by 2042. It then shows where the rival fails: in the worst-twentieth of 2028-30 stock paths, it names about $90,000 to co-sponsors against $140,000 and leaves her over $40,000 behind. That is a stated price for safety, with the tail that justifies it, and I would pay that too. I would still prefer more of the 2028 deposit in stocks, and the dollars of upside given away by the half-cap are not sized. Still, this is the one where I can see the trade and judge it for myself.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "the twentieth of our paths where 2028-30 is worst" needed a second pass (the worst 5% of paths).

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether the team counted the price of its own caution. Entries 3, 4 and 6 share an architecture that is, by my lights, too bond-heavy for a client who takes thoughtful risks, and the equity sleeve is small. Entry 6 earns first place because it picks a serious rival, concedes that the rival wins on average, and shows the tail that makes the safety worth buying. Entry 1 is the only one that really runs equity risk with a rule, and its candour about cash-lock keeps it second, though the sleeve could give back most of its growth after one drawdown. To win, the best entry would need to size what the half-cap and the Treasury floor forgo in dollars, show a middle design that keeps more of the upside, and then explain why it still chooses the floor. Entry 2 is the boldest in asset mix but the thinnest in analysis.
