The 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back to the announced minimum by 2032, with stocks on top for the gift. The risks to "high certainty" are listed, including the one that is easy to miss: the 2028 deposit depends on Laura's variable income, and coupons reinvest worse if yields fall. ### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The central idea (the minimum told to co-sponsors is the number the portfolio is built to protect) is good and tied to the case. Unusually, it admits its own weak points: gap risk, being locked into cash, a variable 2028 deposit, and currency. Assumptions are stated. But Wharton asks for a framework, not mechanics, and a monthly rule with a multiplier of three, a cushion and a floor accruing at the short yield is a lot to hand a co-sponsor. It reads more as technique than as a team's own reasoning. A top-range figure that "about half of simulated paths would beat" is honest but sits oddly with Laura's need to avoid overpromising. It is thorough, not warm, and I could not tell whether students would defend it under questioning.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor is set at "near 60% of the sleeve" and then becomes the present value of the announced minimum in 2031. Why a rule that goes entirely to Treasuries when the sleeve hits the floor suits a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Again the same core plan, but this version best shows real thinking in plain words. It uses an everyday 60/40 as the rival and concedes it usually ends almost $200,000 ahead. Then it explains the point I would want a student to grasp: Laura owes ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill, and a crash in the wrong year forces selling low on a fixed timetable. The one-in-three against under one-in-sixty comparison makes the trade-off concrete, and it is honest about what is given up. The rest is the same careful design (certainty as a list of failures, floor and stocks, a range with a cap and its catch, the reserve running down). Weak points: the stray heading "Sell low, by appointment" is a slogan dropped into otherwise prose, and the end is more cute than the content needs. The 7.5% stock assumption is introduced abruptly.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the "60/40 misses a cheque" so often (I worked out it is about forced sales). The "capital-protected note" analogy needed a second read. "Do not crush" meant nothing until the capsule line.

### Entry 5

- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is a sensible, readable plan that a keen student could have written: ladder first, 80/20 growth sleeve, move the bottom of the range into Treasuries in 2031, ladder becomes the reserve, up to half of what is left is contributed and the rest kept. It follows the case closely (2028 deposit completes the ladder, no outside money, a modelled confidence level for the top) and states assumptions. What it lacks is any sign of thinking harder: no rival considered, no price of its choices, no talk of what could go wrong with the 2028 deposit or the currency, and the headings ("Commitment Ladder", "Growth Engine") sound like a brochure. Clear and safe, but nothing separates it from many teams who will land in the same place.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. "At most half" has no reason given.

## My overall view
Three entries share one skeleton and one pitch, so I treated them as three versions of the same team. What lifts that plan above the rest is that the writers argue against themselves, say what their first plan was, put numbers on the cost of their own choice, and still say "we'd pay it". That is rare. Polish is cheap now, and what I wanted was a reader who feels the team has been surprised by its own model. Entry 4 gets there most plainly because the rival is something a student would actually propose, and the concession (the 60/40 usually ends almost $200,000 ahead) is large and unflattering. Entry 1 digs deepest but is harder to follow. The CPPI entry has real depth but too much mechanism; the simple Treasuries-plus-stocks entry is sound but has no trade-off to remember; the milestone entry is clever and fits the client least. To win, the best entry would need to cut the cutesy ending and the stray slogan heading, move the "case against" to after the strategy has been explained, and keep one concession in plain words. It should also say in a sentence why the 2028 deposit is the weak point, since the case is explicit about where it comes from.
