## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thinking is real. It separates the promise from the hope, buys the promise with Treasuries and takes only the expected interest into stocks. It lists exactly what could break a payment, including the link between a recession, falling yields and a weaker 2028 deposit, which shows it read the client case closely. It argues against its own approach by comparing a 60/40 portfolio, and it gives both the upside it gives up and the failure rate it avoids. That is what I want from students. What holds it back is the tone. It is packed with catchphrases and section titles that sound like marketing, and a closing two-word line that I could not tie to anything. A teenager might write some of this, but the polish makes me wonder how much the team wrote and how much was edited in. The strategy is good enough that it survives this, but the voice costs it points with me.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "all ten cheques – or nearly" (does the first deposit fully fund the ladder or not?). The closing "Do not crush." means nothing until you connect it to the capsule joke. It is also unclear how a "half her stocks" cap works once the range is named.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, defensible plan: a ladder for the ten payments, 80/20 global stocks and Treasuries for growth, a floor locked in 2031, and a facility gift of the floor plus at most half the growth. It defines "high certainty", states return and inflation assumptions, and says what happens in a crash. Nothing here is wrong, but nothing shows the team wrestling with anything. There is no rejected alternative, no number from their own testing, and the client appears mostly through the tagline "entrepreneur who takes thoughtful risks". The headings ("Commitment Ladder", "Growth Engine") read like a brochure. It is competent, and I suspect many of the 2,300 will look much like it. It does not stand out, and it does not give me a reason to believe a real team worked through it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. The "80% stocks as a shock absorber" wording is odd, since the 20% in Treasuries is what does the absorbing.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The startup-funding framing is a fresh way in, and the Treasury ladder for the payments is sound. But the metaphor does most of the work and the numbers do very little. The 2031 range is never given a dollar figure or a confidence level, which the case explicitly asks for. The growth sleeve is 75% world stocks and 25% QQQ, justified by "innovation companies have led returns" and Laura's tech career. That is a backward-looking and concentrated argument, and it ties her investments to her job when the case says to avoid correlated risk. The later tranches depend on co-sponsors committing and the residency "operating on plan" in 2034-35. That pushes the contribution past 2033 and makes it depend on things the portfolio does not control, which the case does not ask for. The analysis never tests what happens if markets fall hard, beyond saying she can decline to invest more. Charming, but the substance is thin.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "real option" to give more later fits with Laura telling co-sponsors a range in 2031. It is also unclear who judges whether the project is "on plan".

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the one that feels most like a team that built a model and learned its limits. The central idea, that the minimum told to co-sponsors should be the number the portfolio is built to protect, is simple and fits the case well. The CPPI rule is sophisticated for a high-school team and is explained with its weak points. It says a one-month fall of about a third could breach the floor, why the multiplier is three and not five, that margin is banned so stocks are capped at 100%, and that the rule can lock into cash and miss a recovery. It also covers the Taiwan-dollar risk and the variable 2028 income. That honesty about what could go wrong is rare and is what I reward. Against it, the mechanism may be more than a 500-word IPS can carry. Wharton says to focus on the framework rather than detailed calculations, and monthly rebalancing and a multiplier read as mechanics. The jargon will lose some readers. The range logic is also a little unclear.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "the contribution that about half of 10,000 simulated paths would beat" next to "at least 95% confidence in the minimum" (are these two separate confidence levels?). Also what the floor "accruing at the short Treasury yield" means in practice for a non-specialist.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This has the same strategy as Entries 1 and 6, but it reads most like people who did the work and changed their minds. It says the team started at three-quarters in stocks, the model showed about one future in thirty missing a cheque, and they rebuilt the plan. That is real learning with a real number behind it. It ties the client's income risk to recessions more clearly than the others, saying her speaking and licensing income shrinks in exactly the markets where bonds get dear. It gives a specific cushion figure for a two-point yield fall and says plainly what the capped range costs, with the rare miss put at about $1,000. It also argues against a 60/40 portfolio fairly, "usually leaves her richer", then says what that costs. It still uses a few clever lines (the pill, the "crush" joke), but there are fewer than elsewhere and the reasoning keeps moving. This is my pick.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "about $125,000 still does" (does what, exactly: fill the gap?). "The rare miss is about $1,000" wasn't clear about which miss.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Almost the same text as Entry 1, with the same good reasoning: buy the promise, put the floor in Treasuries, take only the interest into stocks, and it makes the case against its own approach. It adds a clearer statement that the coupon shortfall is set aside from the gift in 2031, and puts a number on its own failure rate ("under one in sixty") beside the 60/40 portfolio's "one in three". The pitch says "the change buys world stocks", which is vaguer than Entry 1's pitch, and the pitch never says what the world-stock part is for. The slogans and headings are as heavy as Entry 1's, so I carry over the same reservation about voice.

## My overall view
Three entries share one plan: buy the ten payments, put a floor under the gift and put only the expected interest into stocks. That plan is the best idea in the pile, because it answers Wharton's question about funding reliability and flexibility in one move. What separated Entry 5 from the other two is that I believed a team had been through it. It tells me the first plan failed, shows its numbers, and says what the final plan gives up. Entries 1 and 6 have the same ideas but sound more like a polished pitch. Entry 4 is the other plan I would back, and the most original, but it needs a clearer, shorter account of its rule and how its two confidence figures fit together. To win, Entry 5 would need to cut the cute lines, say what the $125,000 and $1,000 figures refer to, and sound a little more like a student explaining their mistake and a little less like a consultant who has already fixed it.
