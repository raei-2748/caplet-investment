# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid (decision science). Method: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran all fifteen pairwise comparisons on Wharton's five criteria and the IPS questions, and read the ranking off the wins. My prior, stated up front: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes. Most competent teams will get there. Credit above the middle goes to a new idea, a sharper reading of Laura's problem, or a trade-off that is actually argued rather than asserted.

Note before scoring: Entries 2, 4 and 5 present the same plan under the same name, with most paragraphs in common. I do not know why, and it is not my job to guess. I scored each as written; the differences between them are small but real, and they decided the order at the top.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4 (5 wins of 5): the same plan as 2 and 5, but it shows the team's decision path, quantifies its one tail, and ties the range to how co-sponsors behave.
2. Entry 5 (4 wins): the cleanest statement of the "buy the promise, invest only the interest" plan, with the sleeve sized.
3. Entry 2 (3 wins): the same plan, slightly less quantified and slightly harder to follow in places.
4. Entry 1 (2 wins): a pre-committed rule that defends the 2031 promise; rigorous about its own failure modes, weaker on why this rule rather than simply owning the floor.
5. Entry 3 (1 win): the only genuinely different idea in the set (staged contribution as real options), let down by its growth allocation and thin analysis.
6. Entry 6 (0 wins): a complete, disciplined, error-free version of exactly my prior, and nothing more.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe (leaning yes)
- What I learned: the idea of treating the number Laura announces in 2031 as the floor the portfolio is mechanically engineered to defend, and the honest accounting of what a rule-based floor costs (gap risk inside the rebalancing interval, and the chance of being locked into Treasuries after a fall and missing the recovery). As a decision scientist I like that the rule is set in advance so nobody has to decide under stress, and that the team explains why it chose a cautious multiplier. What holds it back: the entry never argues why a dynamic rule serves Laura better than the simpler alternative of owning her floor outright from 2028, the pre-2031 floor level looks arbitrary, and the top of the range is pitched as roughly even odds, which is honest but means Laura would under-deliver on her stated top about half the time, a credibility cost the case explicitly warns about. The flexibility share is described clearly. It reads as a well-labelled list rather than a connected argument, and the team's own process is barely visible.
- Confusion on first reading: how the floor can both start at a fraction of the sleeve and later equal the present value of the announced minimum; I had to re-read to see those were two phases of one rule.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: three second-order risks that a table-stakes team misses, namely that the recession which makes the ladder dear is the same recession that shrinks the publishing and speaking income funding the 2028 deposit; that early coupons are a reinvestment leak that should be priced and reserved before any range is named; and that a capped range has an option-like asymmetry (falls hit the gift, rises past the cap go to Laura), which the team neutralises by moving the promised half into Treasuries on the day the range is announced. The trade-off against a conventional balanced portfolio is argued with numbers, in both directions. What holds it back: the growth sleeve is never sized, so a reader cannot tell how small the at-risk money is; the plan commits the whole 2028-built floor as the gift and keeps only half of a small stock sleeve as flexibility, and it asserts rather than argues that this is the responsible amount; and the section headings and closing flourish ask the reader to work out the metaphor before the point. Sequence-of-payments reasoning (ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill) is the sharpest reading of the client's problem in the set.
- Confusion on first reading: what "the change" in the pitch amounts to in dollars, and what "Treasury funds that mature like bonds" are.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution need not be one decision at one moment; staging it through gates tied to project and market evidence turns the contribution into a sequence of options Laura holds, and that is a genuinely new way of seeing the flexibility question, which none of the other five addresses as directly. The founder framing (runway versus growth capital) fits her biography and is coherent with the strategy. What holds it back is serious: the growth allocation tilts a quarter into a technology index on the grounds that such companies have led returns and that Laura's career sits in technology, which is both the hindsight reasoning Wharton warns against and the opposite of what her situation calls for, since her own income is already exposed to that sector. The gate conditions are not defined (what target, what share), no confidence level is attached to the range, the analysis is one return assumption and no scenario, and the later gates sit after the 2033 contribution the case asks the 2031 range to describe. A strong idea in a weak frame.
- Confusion on first reading: how the top of the 2031 range would be explained to a co-sponsor when part of it depends on gates that open in 2034-2035.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned beyond Entries 2 and 5, which share its plan: the team shows its decision path (a stock-heavy first design, a model that rejected it, a rebuild), states the correlation between Laura's income and bond prices as a plain fact about her rather than as a risk list, quantifies the rare miss on the gift in dollars so a co-sponsor can see how small it is, and gives a reason for the shape of the range in terms of how a lead gift behaves in fundraising. That last point is the best use of the case's co-sponsor material in the set. The strategy separates the promise from the hope, buys the promise, and lets only the interest on the second deposit take equity risk, with a clear order of priority if anything changes. What holds it back: two comparison portfolios (three-quarters stock, then 60/40) do overlapping work; one sentence on resilience to a two-point fall in yields is hard to parse; and, as with its siblings, the flexibility left after the gift is small and the entry does not argue why that is responsible rather than simply stating the range. The pill metaphor is used lightly here and does not get in the way.
- Confusion on first reading: the sentence about yields falling two points and a dollar figure "still" doing something; I think it means the deposit still covers the ladder gap, but I was not sure.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was not the ladder, which five of six entries built, but what each team saw after building it. The Caplet XR entries (4, 5, 2) saw that the risks left over are second-order and correlated: the deposit that must fill the ladder shrinks in exactly the market that makes the ladder expensive; coupons leak; a capped range is an asymmetric bet unless the promised part is de-risked the day it is named. They also argued the trade-off against the obvious alternative with numbers in both directions, which is what Wharton means by reasoning, assumptions and trade-offs. Entry 1 saw its own rule's failure modes clearly but not why its rule beats simply owning the floor. Entry 3 had the set's one truly new idea and spent its credibility on a sector tilt. Entry 6 did everything right and nothing new. The tournament was transitive: 4 beat all five, 5 beat the four below it, and so on; the closest pairs were 4 against 5 (decided by shown process and co-sponsor reasoning) and 3 against 6 (decided by my prior that an idea with a hole beats no idea at all). For Entry 4 to win outright it would need to confront the one question it dodges: having made the payments nearly certain, why is committing almost all of the remaining floor to the gift, with only half a small stock sleeve kept back, the responsible amount of flexibility for a project whose cost is unknown? Argue that as well as it argued the 60/40 case, and it is a finalist's IPS.
