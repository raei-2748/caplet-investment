# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - Treasury ladder for the payments, then a stock-heavy rule in the 2028 sleeve that actually pursues growth. It states its risks and its assumptions.
2. Entry 1 - Same safe-ladder foundation, with an honest 80/20 growth engine and a sensible 2031 and 2033 path. Clear and fits the client, but thin on numbers.
3. Entry 5 - The best argued of the "capital-protected note" plans, with the most complete client logic. It gives away almost all of the upside.
4. Entry 4 - Same plan as 5 with fewer reasons given for the client fit. It has a cleaner risk statement than Entry 2.
5. Entry 2 - Same plan again, a little less precise than 4, and it leans on turns of phrase.
6. Entry 6 - Growth-minded, but the staged "gates" misread the case, the range is never defined, and the analysis is thin.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The plan is easy to follow. Ladder of Treasuries for the ten payments, which costs about two-thirds of her money. The rest goes 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries, rebalanced yearly with a drift band. The floor of the range is moved into Treasuries in 2031. In 2033 she gives the floor plus at most half the remaining growth money. As a PM I like that the surplus is actually invested for growth and that the stocks are tied to a real client risk, construction costs rising in Taiwan. It does not say how much upside the ladder costs her. "A stated, modelled confidence level" appears with no number attached, so the analysis is more assertion than evidence. The assumptions (6-7% stocks, 4-5% bonds, 2.5% inflation) are reasonable. Good enough to be near the line, not distinctive.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none. I could not tell how large the growth sleeve is compared with the floor.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe, leaning no
- The ladder for the ten payments is sound. After that, almost the whole 2028 deposit goes into Treasuries that grow back to itself, and only about a fifteenth of the portfolio goes into stocks. That is a capital-protected note with a very small option on top. The entry does count the cost: it says a 60/40 portfolio ends nearly $200k ahead "usually". It then answers with a one-in-three failure rate for 60/40. But that comparison is not fair. The real alternative is a ladder plus a growth sleeve, which Entry 1 and Entry 3 have. A 60/40 of the whole pot is a weak opponent. The result is a client with a ten-year runway and risk tolerance whose gift is essentially a fixed floor of roughly $150k plus a sliver. That is timidity presented as prudence. Many of the lines are about the phrasing, and the explanation of the range is clear but long.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the "floor plus half her stocks" gift reaches its top 97 times in 100 when the stock part is so small. The "sell low, by appointment" heading also took me a moment to understand.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thesis is that the minimum told to co-sponsors is the number the portfolio is built to protect. The ladder covers the operating payments. The growth sleeve runs a CPPI rule with multiplier 3, capped at 100% stocks because margin is banned. It therefore starts fully invested in a world ETF and cuts risk only as the cushion shrinks. This is the only entry that takes real equity risk in a disciplined way and says what could go wrong: gap risk, cash-lock (missing a recovery), the 2028 income risk, and Taiwan dollar exposure with derivatives banned. The 2031 range is tied to a stated confidence level (95% for the minimum, roughly even odds for the top), and the assumptions are set out. Weaknesses: it gives away half the gain above the floor to flexibility without saying what that costs; it is more mechanical than Wharton asks for ("not detailed financial calculations"); and the monthly rule is a lot of machinery for a high-school IPS. A reader may find "Floor = Promise" confusing, since "the promise" usually means the ten payments.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Floor = Promise" was ambiguous. I could not tell whether "promise" meant the operating payments or the announced minimum gift. I also wondered how the floor is set before 2031, when no minimum has yet been announced.

### Entry 4

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 6.5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe, probably yes
- Same capital-protected-note design, but the most complete version. It tells the story of its own process (a 75% stocks start that failed in the model), argues that her income falls in the same recessions that make bonds expensive (a real client insight), says what the "rare miss" of the range costs (about $1,000), and states the priority order if things change. That is good reasoning and tradeoff talk. It still takes almost no growth risk on the facility money and rests on the same 60/40 comparison, which is not the real alternative. The "$125,000 still does" sentence about yields falling two points is hard to parse. On Wharton's scheme it is the best of the three twins, but it is the sort of plan I mark down on its central choice.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does." I could not tell what "does" what. Is it the gap-filling capacity of the 2028 deposit?

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It takes the growth side seriously (75% VT, 25% QQQ in the surplus) and the ladder is safe. But the Nasdaq tilt is justified only by "innovation led global returns" and "her own career sits in technology". That is a weak reason for concentrating a client's money. The staged "gates" are the main idea, and they misread the case. Laura decides her facility contribution in 2033, and gate 3 (2034-2035) is outside the case's structure. The gates also depend on co-sponsor commitments and project milestones that the portfolio cannot see or control. "Each gate is a real option" is loose language for a rule she may or may not follow. The 2031 range is never given a dollar basis or a confidence level, and there are no stress numbers. The startup metaphor is thoughtful but it is mostly packaging.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "range" announced in 2031 is consistent with tranches that are released conditionally in 2034-2035. If a gate fails, does she break the promise she made co-sponsors?

## My overall view
What separated the top entries is that they take risk on purpose with the money Laura can afford to risk. Entries 3 and 1 secure the ten payments with a ladder and then invest the surplus for growth. Entry 3 goes furthest, with disciplined real equity exposure and an honest risk list. The three "capital-protected note" entries (2, 4, 5) did careful work, and they do count what safety costs, which I credit. But they compare against a 60/40 straw man and end up giving her a gift that is almost a fixed floor. For a client who is willing to take thoughtful risks and has 6 to 16 years, that gives away too much. Entry 6 has the growth appetite but not the analysis or the reading of the case. To win, Entry 3 would need to say plainly what its half-gain cap and cash-lock cost in expected dollars, simplify the machinery, and clear up the "Floor = Promise" wording. Entry 1 would need real numbers behind the range and its confidence.
