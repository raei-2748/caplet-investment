# Judge J3 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5: the only entry that finds the ladder's hidden leak, says where the money to cover it comes from, and admits its own failure rate.
2. Entry 4: the same sound structure, with the trade-off priced in dollars and the correlated recession risk named precisely; the leak is priced but its funding source is left vague.
3. Entry 1: same structure and more internal sizing, but one key sentence on the ladder gap did not parse and the leak's funding source is implied, not stated.
4. Entry 6: the most technically ambitious growth sleeve (CPPI) with honest risk disclosures, but the reserve hole is unpriced, the floor asset is mismatched, and the range confidence is unclear.
5. Entry 2: a clean, textbook promise-first plan that answers every question, but it claims rates "never touch the payments" when its own coupons say otherwise, and certainty is never quantified.
6. Entry 3: a creative staging idea, but the growth sleeve doubles Laura's existing tech exposure, the gates are circular, and the range has no confidence attached.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7.0/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- When I trace the cash flows, they hold up. $300,000 buys a held-to-maturity ladder for the ten payments. Any shortfall is the first claim on the 2028 deposit. Most of the $150,000 buys a 2032 floor and the remainder (they size it at about a fifteenth of $450,000, which does reconcile with five years of Treasury growth) goes into one world fund. The range is the floor plus half the stocks, and that half is de-risked the day it is named. Unlike most student plans, this one finds the coupon-reinvestment leak on the ladder and prices it before the range is set. It also gives the size of the rare miss on the top of the range, which is the kind of number I want. The client reasoning is real, not decorative: Laura's 2028 income is correlated with exactly the recession that makes bonds expensive, so the plan does not stake the payments on it. The entry also shows the team testing and dropping its first idea, a 75% stock plan. What holds it back: the leak is set aside "in 2031" but it never says from which pot. The floor bonds bought in 2028 carry their own smaller coupon-reinvestment problem, which is never mentioned. Flexibility after the gift is thin, roughly half of a small stock sleeve, so Laura commits most of what is left beyond the reserve. Total equity of about 7% is very cautious for a client "willing to take thoughtful risks", though the team argues for it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the line saying that even if yields fall two points, "about $125,000 still does". I could not tell what the $125,000 is: the part of the 2028 deposit left after filling the ladder gap, or something else.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- This is a competent, well-organised promise-first plan, and it answers every IPS question in turn. It covers the ladder, an 80/20 growth sleeve, a floor locked in 2031, floor-plus-half capped at the top, and a self-liquidating reserve. The diversification is better than in the Caplet XR-style entries, and the assumptions (stock and bond returns, 2.5% inflation) are written down. But it contains the hole I expect to find. The bonds are sized so that "principal plus coupons" covers each payment, and early coupons are parked in short-term Treasuries. That is reinvestment-rate risk, and it directly contradicts the claim that rate swings never touch the payments. "High certainty" is defined only as "backed by a Treasury maturity", with no number, and the range's confidence is "a stated, modelled confidence level" that is never stated. It does not say how the bottom of the range is chosen, and "good, average and severe" scenarios are not a measure of funding reliability. It reads like a lot of entries I would expect in the pile: correct in outline, with the analysis left as a promise.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "rebalance once a year, only when weights drift more than five points" mixes a calendar rule and a threshold rule; I was not sure which one governs.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- Treating the facility gift as a staged real option is original, and staging does preserve flexibility. On the reserve side the plan is the same held-to-maturity ladder as the others, with the same unpriced coupon-reinvestment gap behind "payout is fixed whatever markets do". The growth sleeve is 100% equity with a quarter in QQQ, justified because Laura's career sits in technology and creative industries. From a liability-matching view that is backwards: her 2028 deposit and her future income already depend on that sector, so the portfolio should diversify away from it, not double it. Gate 2 makes Laura's tranche conditional on co-sponsors committing, but the case says co-sponsors commit because of her contribution, so the gates are circular. The top of the 2031 range includes tranches that are not secured, which conflicts with "every promise is sized to a tranche already secured". It gives no confidence for the range and states few assumptions (one return figure, nothing on bond yields, inflation or the Taiwan dollar). The idea is stronger than the analysis behind it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what "the growth portfolio is above its target" means at Gate 2. No target is defined.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The structure is the same as Entry 1 and just as sound when I trace it, and two things put it ahead. First, its definition of high certainty names the one correlated failure that matters. A recession before the purchase lowers yields, so the ladder costs more, and at the same moment it shrinks the 2028 deposit that has to fill the gap. That is a liability-side insight most teams miss. Second, it puts a price on the trade-off. Its modelled 60/40 usually ends almost $200,000 ahead, but misses a payment or its own range about a third of the time. That is exactly the "reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs" Wharton asks for. The capped-range asymmetry and the de-risking of the promised half on announcement day are well understood. What holds it back: the reinvestment leak is "priced and set aside in 2031" with no source named. The 2028 floor's own coupon leak is not mentioned. It gives no failure rate for its own plan, only for the alternative. As with Entry 1, the flexibility left after the gift is small, and the bottom of the range is not stated in dollars. It does not have to be, but an indication would help co-sponsors.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none of substance. The "$200,000 ahead" figure surprised me by its size, and I would want to see the model behind it in the Final Report.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.0/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the most complete version of the strongest strategy in my pile. The ladder buys the promise, and the 2028 deposit first closes any gap and then funds a floor plus a small world-equity sleeve. The range is the floor plus half the stocks, and the promised half is de-risked on the day it is named. Every cash flow from the case traces through without a gap. This is the only entry that closes the hole I went looking for. It names the coupon-reinvestment shortfall, prices it in 2031, and says the money comes "from the hope, before any range is named". That means the payments are protected by the gift, not by an assumption. It also gives its own failure rate (under one in sixty) next to the alternative's (about one in three), which is how a certainty claim should be made. Describing the plan as a protected note with "the bank's option swapped for shares" is technically accurate and shows the team knows what it built. What holds it back: the floor bonds' own reinvestment leak is not addressed, and the post-gift flexibility is modest. Equity is a small share of the whole, so the growth case rests on a fairly narrow sleeve. The range will almost always land at its top, which is credible but tells co-sponsors little that a single number would not.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Tying the growth sleeve's floor to the minimum Laura announces ("Floor = Promise") is a coherent central idea. CPPI is a real tool, used properly here: a multiplier of three, a cap at 100% because margin is banned, and gap risk and cash-lock disclosed with the right arithmetic (a one-third monthly fall). It lists more of Wharton's required assumptions than any other entry: returns, volatility, cash-flow timing, inflation, currency, and the variability of the 2028 deposit. The holes are in the liability matching. The reserve ladder is "sized to deliver $50,000" but has the same unpriced coupon-reinvestment gap as Entries 2 and 3. The CPPI floor accrues at the short rate and its safe asset is short Treasuries, while the liability (the 2033 minimum) is a two-to-five-year fixed amount, so falling short rates would leave the floor underfunded. If the rule protects the minimum, I could not see why its confidence is only "at least 95%". The top is set at even odds, and the plan does not say whether the gift is capped there, so the chance of landing inside the range, which is what the case asks for, is unclear.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: what the floor is before 2031 ("near 60% of the sleeve"), and how that number becomes the announced minimum. It is also unclear whether the 2033 gift is capped at the top of the range.

## My overall view
Every entry gets the first step right: the ten payments are fixed nominal liabilities, so you buy them with a Treasury ladder and hold it. What separated the entries was whether the team understood that a coupon-bond ladder is not a perfect match. Coupons arrive before the payments and have to be reinvested at rates nobody knows yet. It also mattered whether they understood that the 2028 deposit, which plugs any ladder gap, is most likely to shrink exactly when that gap is largest. The Caplet XR-style entries (5, 4, 1) price both and protect the payments with the gift. The others claim immunity from rates that their own bonds do not give them. Entry 6 brings the best tool but bolts it onto a reserve that has the same unpriced gap. To win, Entry 5 would need to extend its leak accounting to the 2028 floor bonds. It should also say roughly how much of what remains after the reserve Laura keeps as flexibility, and why that share is enough. Finally, it should show that keeping equity this low is a measured cost of certainty, not just caution. It already has most of that last point.
