## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: answers all five of Wharton's questions, with the most tested funding-reliability case and a precise range with a stated confidence.
2. Entry 3: the same thesis and structure as Entry 1, in clearer prose, with stronger client tailoring but a thinner stress test.
3. Entry 4: near-twin of Entry 3, with a stated failure rate for the plan but less sizing and less client colour.
4. Entry 5: the strongest portfolio analysis and liquidity treatment, but it reads as a specification with little voice.
5. Entry 6: well organised and mirrors the questions, but leaves out the confidence figure it promises for the 2031 range.
6. Entry 2: a creative milestone frame, but almost no numbers, no tested certainty, and a facility sequence that sits awkwardly with the case.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7.5/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: The central idea is stated in one line and is easy to hold onto: the ten cheques are bought first with Treasuries that mature in time, and the gift is a bond floor plus a share of world stocks. Funding reliability is the best tested of the six. It names the two things that break a cheque, gives a stress result (a two-point fall in yields still leaves the gap covered), prices the ladder's one leak before the range is named, and reports how often the 75%-stock plan would have missed a cheque. The 2031 range is defined exactly, with a confidence figure for the top, and the client insight (her income falls in the same recessions that make bonds expensive) is the sharpest in the set. What holds it back is the compressed prose, with several ideas packed into single sentences, and a thin argument for diversification in the surplus, which rests on one world index fund.
- Anything I did not understand on first reading: The sentence "Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does" is unclear. I could not tell whether the $125,000 is the part of the 2028 deposit left after the gap, or the size of the gap.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 3.5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- What it does well and what holds it back: The founder frame (fund milestones, not the full plan) is a real idea and speaks to the question of how much to commit in 2031. The portfolio analysis is almost absent, though. There is no projection, no test under bad markets (only "deep drawdowns possible"), and no evaluation of how certain the runway is beyond "held to maturity". "The seed tranche plus later tranches that depend on markets" could mean a market number or a milestone number, and the entry does not say which. I also could not tell how far the co-sponsors' share at Gate 2 is in Laura's control.

### Entry 3

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes

Certainty is defined as a list (a default, or a recession that cuts yields and shrinks the 2028 income behind the gap), and the one leak is priced in 2031. ### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 6.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the most rigorous entry on the portfolio side. ## My overall view
What separated the best entries from the rest was whether each one showed its work on funding reliability and the range, in a voice a co-sponsor could follow. Wharton asks what the team decided and also why, on which assumptions, and at what trade-off, and the strongest entries answered that with a stress result and a named confidence level. Entry 1 does both, and it does so in a clear thesis that answers all five questions. Entries 3 and 4 do most of the same with a clearer story, and Entry 5 does the portfolio analysis better than any other entry but reads as a specification. Entry 6 is well organised but leaves out the one confidence figure it promises. Entry 2 has a creative frame without the numbers to back it. To win, the best entry would need Entry 1's stress test, Entry 3's readability, and the stated confidence Entry 5 gives, plus a plain statement of what a two-point yield fall costs in dollars.
