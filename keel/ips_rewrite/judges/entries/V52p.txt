ELEVATOR PITCH (max 50 words):
Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are a promise, so we don't invest for them. We buy them. Her first $300,000 buys Treasuries that each mature before a January. Her 2028 deposit buys a Treasury floor for her building gift, and only the interest that floor should earn goes into world stocks.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT (max 500 words):
In January 2033, Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten cheques – $50,000 each, one every January, to fund her Taiwan residency. She also hopes to contribute to the building. But the cheques come first.

We call it Caplet XR. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a capsule that delivers its dose on schedule, labelled do not crush. Break it open and you ruin how it works. To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten holdings, one redeemed for each January.

In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds that mature like bonds, one rung falling due before each cheque. We don't invest toward these payments. We buy them. A Treasury held to maturity pays exactly what it promises, whatever markets do. That is what "a high degree of certainty" means to us – not a probability, but a purchase.

Laura's career already behaves a lot like a stock. Her 2028 deposit depends on publishing advances, speaking fees and licensing – income that dries up in exactly the kind of downturn that pushes bond prices up. If that deposit arrives smaller, the gift shrinks. A cheque funded by a Treasury already bought does not.

Her 2028 deposit first finishes any rung the first $300,000 couldn't buy. Most of what is left buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032. That is her floor: money she will own outright even if every share in the world fell to zero. The remainder, roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn, goes into one world index fund, spread across thousands of companies. A bank would call this a capital-protected note, except a bank would spend that interest on an option, which is worth nothing if the market just stands still. We assume about 7.5% a year from those stocks, but nothing Laura promises depends on it.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range for her facility contribution. The bottom is her floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her stocks, valued that day. Before she names it, that half moves into Treasuries too, so every dollar she promises is already in bonds. The other half stays with Laura, liquid any day, whether the building costs more in Taiwan dollars or she needs flexibility.

We could put the floor into stocks and chase a bigger gift. But a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn.

The weak point is reinvestment: if yields fall and stay low, the interest the bonds pay earns less when reinvested, and the ladder could fall short. Before she names the range, we set that shortfall aside from the stocks Laura keeps; whatever they can't cover comes off the bottom.

From 2033, the ladder becomes the operating reserve, paying out one bond at a time as each cheque is written. Whatever else changes, the order will not: the cheques, then the floor, then the gift.

Do not crush.
