# Judge J4 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1: the most honest account of what can go wrong and why the team chose its settings; a little engineered, but the thinking is visible.
2. Entry 3: the sharpest insight about Laura herself and a clear "buy the payments" idea; the packaging is a bit too neat for my taste.
3. Entry 5: plain, sensible, fully covers the questions; safe and somewhat generic, but reads like a team that did the work.
4. Entry 6: clear and honest about drawdowns, uses real WInS-type instruments; leaves the key numbers vague and defers the confidence to the Final Report.
5. Entry 4: same idea as Entry 3, cut down; loses the stated return assumption and the shortfall handling is thinner.
6. Entry 2: the safe half is sound, but the growth half is a thematic bet on tech and Taiwan, explained by conviction rather than reasoning.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The strategy is coherent: a bought ladder for the ten payments, then a rule-based growth sleeve that protects the minimum Laura will announce in 2031. The best thing is that the team says what the rule cannot do. It admits gap risk and cash-lock, and it explains why it picked a multiplier of three, not five. It also names the currency problem and the risk to the 2028 deposit, so a judge sees tradeoffs, which Wharton asks for. The assumptions (7%, 16%, 4.5%) are stated and the 2031 confidence is stated. What holds it back is that it leans on a technical mechanism a reader has to trust, and a few lines are heavy on jargon. It feels more like a fund memo than a student voice, so I would want to hear from the team in an interview. The tie to Laura personally is thinner than in Entries 3 and 4.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can be "60% of the sleeve" at the start and then the present value of the announced minimum from 2031. I took it as two phases but would have asked.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The lock-then-launch split is sensible and clearly explained, and the ladder section is solid. The growth half is where it weakens. It puts 30% into tech, semiconductors and Taiwan because the team believes AI will drive the decade. That is a view about what will perform well, which Wharton says is not the goal. It also stacks correlated holdings (QQQ, SMH and VOO overlap heavily), so the diversification claim is weak. Nothing is said about expected returns, volatility or how bad a bad market could be, so the reader cannot judge the 2031 range. The Taiwan holding is a cute link to the residency but does not do a job for Laura's funding. It is readable and sounds like enthusiastic students, which I like, but the reasoning behind the risky part is thin.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why holding Taiwan equities helps a client whose facility costs are in Taiwan dollars. I was not sure whether this was meant as a hedge or just a theme.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This entry understands Laura best. It notices that her 2028 deposit comes from income that behaves like a stock, so the safe money must already be bought. It makes the central idea plain: the ten payments are purchased, not invested for. The structure of Treasury floor plus only the expected interest in world stocks is a thoughtful, original answer to "how much can she promise." The team is honest that reinvestment is the weak point and says what happens to the range if yields fall. The returns assumption is stated. My hesitation is the delivery: the capsule metaphor, the "pharmacist" aside and the closing line feel branded, and a few sentences are very well turned. A teacher gets wary of that much polish. The substance carries it, but I would want to check that the students can explain the capital-protected-note comparison themselves.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the "X is ten and R is redemption" naming took a second read. I also wondered how large the stock portion actually is, since the entry only says "roughly the interest."

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- Essentially the same strategy as Entry 3, and the same strengths: bought payments, Laura's income behaving like a stock, a floor she owns outright, half the stocks moved to Treasuries before the 2031 announcement. It adds a useful sentence about what "crushing" the capsule would mean in practice, and "Nobody else can pay them" is a good reminder of the case rule. But it drops the stated stock return assumption, so a judge sees no number behind the upside. The reinvestment shortfall is handled in one short sentence. The capsule branding is even more prominent, in the pitch as well. It is a good entry, a notch below Entry 3 because there is less to evaluate under Portfolio Analysis.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how big the set-aside for the reinvestment gap would be and who decides it. It says "that gap is set aside" without saying how it is sized.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A well-organised, plainly written IPS that answers every question Wharton lists: the central idea, how certainty is defined, how the portfolio changes in 2031 and 2033, the flexibility share and the assumptions. The 80/20 stocks and Treasuries growth engine is reasonable and the reasoning about construction costs in Taiwan is sensible. I believe a real team wrote this. What holds it back is that it is not very distinctive: it is the standard "match the liabilities, then grow the rest" answer, and the cost of the ladder ("two-thirds") is stated loosely. The risk section is shorter than I would like and mostly says a crash shrinks the contribution, without saying by how much. Still, it is honest and consistent, and it would not embarrass its authors in an interview.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none really. I only wanted the stock-fund share of the 2028 money stated more clearly.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clean, honest plan. It matches payments with iBond ETFs for the earlier years and individual bonds for the later years, which is a practical detail about how a team would really do it. It states that years of -30% or worse are possible, which I value, and it is open about execution risk. It also says plainly that facility cost inflation sits with the growth fund. The weaknesses are that it leaves the important numbers vague ("a share of VT," "a Treasury maturing in late 2032" with no sizing) and puts off the confidence level to the Final Report, while the question asks for a stated level. "We are removing the market from the question" is slogan-ish. The reasoning is good enough, but Portfolio Analysis is thin for this stage.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how much of the 2028 deposit goes to the Treasury floor and how much to VT. The split is never given.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was whether I could see the team thinking, not just describing a sensible structure. Entry 1 shows its settings, its limits and what it is willing to lose, and Entry 3 shows a real insight about Laura's income that changes how the deposit is used. Those two earn their place. Entries 5 and 6 are honest and competent but cautious, and Entry 2 asks me to accept a tech theme on faith. I am wary of the polish in 3 and 4, and the single-fund-plus-slogan style is exactly what is easy to produce now, so my score leans on the reasoning rather than the writing. To win, the best entry would need to keep its depth but say it in plainer words, give a few more numbers (how big each part is, how confident the range is), and sound like students who did the work rather than a brochure.
