# Judge J2 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 1 - Treasury ladder plus a rule-based stock sleeve that keeps real equity exposure, with the most honest accounting of what the rule gives up and risks.
2. Entry 6 - Clean ladder-first plan with an 80/20 growth engine on everything above the ladder; sound and growth-aware, a little generic.
3. Entry 4 - Same ladder-first logic, a global stock fund held untouched, honest about drawdowns; the floor/stock split is never sized.
4. Entry 2 - Most growth-minded on paper, but the satellite sleeve is a concentrated tech/Taiwan bet and the assumptions are missing.
5. Entry 3 - Well argued and honest about what it gives up, but it hands most of the 2028 deposit to Treasuries; timid for this client.
6. Entry 5 - Same plan as Entry 3 with less of the risk and client reasoning.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The ladder protects the ten payments. The growth sleeve then runs a stated rule: stocks scale with the cushion above a floor, capped at 100% because margin is banned. That keeps a real equity position where a client "willing to take thoughtful risks" should have one. It also de-risks mechanically when markets fall. The 2031 range is tied to the protected minimum, and the high end is tied to simulated paths. Assumptions are stated (about 7% return, 16% volatility, 4.5% Treasuries), along with a confidence target for the minimum. The risks section is the most candid of the six: gap risk, being locked into cash, a variable 2028 deposit, and Taiwan dollar exposure. What holds it back is the cost of the rule. A floor that starts near 60% of the sleeve and accrues will drag stock exposure down within a few years in a flat market. That gives away upside, and the entry does not size how much. It is also heavier to run than a simple split, and the tone is more technical than a co-sponsor audience would like. The "at most half the gain above the minimum" cap on the gift is sensible and keeps flexibility.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why the floor starts near 60% and how that interacts with the 2031 announced minimum. I also could not tell how often the rule would actually cut stocks in a flat market.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The "Lock, then Launch" split is clear, and the ladder deals with certainty well. The growth side is aggressive, which I like in principle: it is fully in stocks for the early years and honest that a bad market lowers the top and never the floor. But the 30% satellite sleeve is QQQ, semiconductors and Taiwan. Those overlap heavily (the Taiwan fund is largely one chip company), so this is one thematic bet presented as diversification, and it rests on a forecast that AI will win the decade. Wharton asks for a strategy that works across market outcomes, not a view on what will perform. There are no return, volatility or confidence assumptions at all, and no stress case. In 2031 half the growth money goes to Treasuries with no reasoning for why half. The facility contribution mechanics are loose ("part of the equity half"). It reads as confident, but there is little analysis behind it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the 2031 range would be quantified, and how the Taiwan fund fits with the residency and exchange-rate risk beyond being thematically convenient.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The client reasoning is strong. Laura's 2028 deposit comes from income that behaves like a stock, so the ladder is bought first and the floor is protected. The entry is also honest that it could chase a bigger gift and chooses not to, and it names the reinvestment weak point and sets it aside before the range is named. As an equity person, my objection is size. After the ladder, most of the 2028 deposit goes into a Treasury floor, only its expected interest goes into stocks, and half of those stocks then move to bonds in 2031. That is a capital-protected note with the growth cut out. A client with six to sixteen years and outside income to live on is paying a large price for safety, and the entry never counts that price in dollars. The 2033 gift is likely to be modest, and the entry offers no numbers, assumptions or scenarios for it. The pharmacy analogy is memorable but does not add analysis. The "cheques, then floor, then gift" ordering is useful.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how large the stock position actually is relative to the floor, and what the gift would plausibly be in dollars.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A well-organised, plain plan. The ladder is matched to payments, "high certainty" is defined as not depending on any forecast, and the floor is a Treasury maturing in late 2032. The rest goes to one global stock fund held untouched, with assumptions (6-7% a year, drawdowns of -30% or worse) that are honest and specific. Inflation and flexibility each get a line. The weak spot is that the split between the floor and the stock fund is never sized, so I cannot tell whether this is a growth plan or a mostly bond plan. It also does not say how much upside the floor gives away. The 2031 range is described, with its likelihood deferred to the Final Report, which reads as thin on confidence. Naming specific funds and bond types is more product detail than the IPS asks for. There is no real creative angle, but nothing goes wrong either.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the proportion of the 2028 deposit that goes to the Treasury floor versus the stock fund.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- This is the same capital-protected-note design as Entry 3, with the same timid sizing, so my objection is the same. It also drops the risk section and the "chase a bigger gift" discussion that made Entry 3 the more candid of the two. It adds a 7.5% return assumption for the stocks and says nothing the promise depends on rests on it. That is a small plus. It still has no scenarios, no dollar sense of the 2033 gift, and no real treatment of downside beyond the reinvestment gap. I rank it last because it is Entry 3 with less in it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: why 7.5% was picked, and how large the stock slice is relative to the floor.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The logic is complete and runs in a sensible order: fund the promise, then let everything else grow. The growth engine is about 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries for six-plus years, which fits "willing to take thoughtful risks" and a client with outside income. The answer to rising Taiwan construction costs (stocks as a hedge) is a reasonable if loose point. It covers 2031 and 2033, a half-of-growth cap on the gift, a yearly review, stated return and inflation assumptions, and a good/average/severe test. It shows one limit clearly: a crash shrinks the facility contribution, never the payments. What holds it back is genericness. It is the textbook answer, and it does not quantify the range, the confidence, or how much upside the floor gives away. The 20% Treasury "shock absorber" costs return, and the entry does not argue for it beyond that phrase. Still, it fits the client and the questions well.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "roughly two-thirds of total contributions" as a description of the ladder's cost; I took it to mean about the whole first deposit.

## My overall view
What separated the top two or three from the rest is that they let real money work for the facility contribution while protecting the promise, and they said what they were doing in terms I can check. Entry 1 does that best, with a rule, stated assumptions, a confidence target and a candid risk list. Entry 6 does it more plainly, with a growth sleeve that is actually growth. Entries 3 and 5 are well written but turn the 2028 money into a bond holding with a small stock kicker, and then move half of that to bonds again in 2031; I think that short-changes the client and never prices the safety. Entry 2 has the right appetite but spends it on a narrow tech and Taiwan bet with no analysis behind it. To win, the best entry would need to show in dollars what its rule or its split gives away in an average year, show what the facility contribution looks like in good, middling and bad markets, and make its mechanism simpler to read in one pass. Entry 1 is closest to that, and Entry 6 would need a distinctive idea and some numbers on the range.
