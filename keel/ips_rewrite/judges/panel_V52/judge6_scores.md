## Ranking (1 = best)

2. Entry 1: a clear promise-first structure with the most convincing growth engine and diversification, and a good fit to Laura's risk appetite; thinner on liquidity and on how the 2031 top is confidence-rated.

4. Entry 4: a sharp central idea with a real insight (only the interest is at risk, so the stock sleeve cannot touch the gift floor), and it names the reinvestment shortfall and how it is set aside; the growth sleeve is so small that the strategy's growth role is hard to see.

6. Entry 3: the same architecture as Entry 4 with less quantitative backing, a 2031 range whose top I could not follow, and more of its space given to the name and metaphor than to the strategy.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the entry that most directly answers the question Wharton asked. It has a central idea, three named buckets (reserve, growth, flexibility), a section on reserve composition over time, a section on liquidity, and a list of risks it accepts (gap risk, cash-lock, the dependence on the 2028 deposit, currency), so the judge can see the trade-offs as Wharton wants them. ## My overall view
What separates the best from the rest is whether the IPS answers the five questions Wharton asked in a way a judge can check, with the trade-offs out in the open and the key numbers stated. Entry 2 does that: every question has a section, the risks it accepts are listed rather than implied, and the 2031 range comes with a confidence level and a basis. Entries 1 and 6 are both credible and well organised, and each has one gap that a judge would notice (a missing confidence figure in Entry 1, an unnamed share in Entry 6). Entries 3 and 4 have the most original thinking in the set, the idea that a promised gift should be bought from the floor and only the interest put at risk, but Entry 4 backs it with assumptions and an honest reinvestment risk where Entry 3 leaves the reader to guess. To win, the best entry would need to keep Entry 2's completeness, state its 2031 range and its contribution share in numbers, and make the pitch as plain as the body; it would also need to show liquidity and diversification as explicitly as it shows the ladder.
