# Pitch judge 3

Judge: Tom (English teacher, debating coach). Marked on Wharton's criteria for the pitch (summarise the strategy, communicate the central idea, show how it serves Laura's goals and funding needs) and on whether a real student is talking. Read 1 Nov 2026.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Pitch 2: the clearest voice in the set ("we don't invest for them. We buy them"), every sentence lands on first read, and it says exactly what the two deposits do.
2. Pitch 7: the only pitch that reaches Laura's 2031 moment with the co-sponsors, which is the thing she is actually worried about; held back by a last line that contradicts the one before it.
3. Pitch 4: plain, orderly and specific, nothing to trip over, but it opens by restating the case rather than saying something, so it is less of a hook.
4. Pitch 5: good opening, honest tone, but "guaranteed minimum" appears from nowhere and is aimed at the wrong people.
5. Pitch 3: the most original framing (defend the 2031 promise) and the best client fit on paper, but "runs a stock-or-Treasury rule" is a sentence I could not follow and the ending borrows Wharton's own words.
6. Pitch 6: the idea is there and the voice is natural, but "hopes for a gift" and two loose "it"s made me re-read half of it.
7. Pitch 1: telegraphic fragments and a slogan ending; the one pitch that reads like copy rather than a person.

## Per pitch

### Pitch 1
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 5/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 3/10
- The plan is in there (ten Treasuries for the ten payments, a floor plus stocks from the second deposit), so a patient reader can reconstruct it. But the first two words are a product name nobody has explained, "releases Laura's money on schedule" does not mean anything I can picture, and "buys world stocks with the expected interest" asks me to accept that you can spend interest before it exists without telling me why that is safe. "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor." is a tagline, and taglines are what people write when they have run out of words rather than things to say. Nothing about Laura herself, her residency or her co-sponsors.

### Pitch 2
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 9/10
- "Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are a promise, so we don't invest for them. We buy them." That is a student talking, and it is the central idea in twelve words. The next two sentences do the work: first deposit buys Treasuries maturing before each January, second deposit buys a floor for the building gift, and only the interest that floor should earn goes to stocks. The one phrase I had to read twice was "the interest that floor should earn", but it survives the second read. What is missing is Laura: no residency, no 2031 range, no word on flexibility. I would read the IPS to find out whether the writer has thought about those.

### Pitch 3
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 5/10
- The opening is the most interesting idea in the seven: make the number Laura tells co-sponsors in 2031 the thing the portfolio defends. That is squarely Laura's problem from the case and nobody else framed it that way. Then it goes soft. "Runs a stock-or-Treasury rule that protects her announced minimum" is a sentence I read three times and still cannot explain to a class: what is the rule, who runs it, when? "Near-certainty" and "preserving flexibility" are the examiner's words handed back to the examiner, which is what a nervous student does in an essay. I believe this team understands Laura; I am not sure they can say what they will do.

### Pitch 4
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- Nothing here misfires. The first sentence sets up the promise in Laura's own terms (every January, 2033 to 2042), the second buys the cheques outright, the third does the 2028 deposit. "Ten Treasury holdings that each mature before a January" is exactly the kind of concrete picture I want. My reservations: the opening sentence is the case restated, so the pitch does not say anything until sentence two, and "Caplet XR" is dropped in as though I already know it. It is the safest pitch, not the most alive.

### Pitch 5
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- "We buy the promise first" is a good line and "an honest upside" is a phrase a real person would use. The ladder sentence is clear. The trouble is the second half. "Only money left over" tells me nothing about what the 2028 deposit does or how much is left over. Then "giving co-sponsors a guaranteed minimum" stopped me: the co-sponsors are not given anything, Laura promises them something, and where does a guarantee come from if the leftover money is in a stock fund? The pitch names the right audience but the logic connecting the fund to the minimum is missing, and "guaranteed" is a word I would want to see defended.

### Pitch 6
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 6/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- "Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise. Her first $300,000 buys it" has the same good instinct as Pitch 2. But "hopes for a gift" reads on first pass as though Laura is hoping to receive one, when she is hoping to give one; I had to go back. Then "buys Treasuries that repay it in 2032": repay what? The deposit? The promise? I think the deposit, but the pitch should not make me guess. "What's left buys world stocks for the gift" is clear. A good idea delivered with its pronouns in a tangle, and the pitch forgot to say why 2032.

### Pitch 7
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 9/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- Same promise-and-buy opening as 2 and 5, done briskly. What lifts it is the last sentence: "When she names a range in 2031, every dollar is already in bonds." That is the one line in all seven that speaks to the thing Laura's case actually worries about, promising co-sponsors a number she might not be able to keep. It would make me open the IPS. But I have to be honest about the stumble: the sentence before says the floor's expected interest goes into stocks, so "every dollar" cannot be in bonds. I think they mean every dollar of the number she names, and a debater would be pulled up for leaving the audience to supply that. "Building gift" is odd English but I got it.

## Which one sounds most machine-written, and why
Pitch 1, without hesitation. It is the only one that opens with a brand name as the grammatical subject, as though the product were the author, and it never once uses "we" or a verb a person would say aloud ("releases Laura's money on schedule" is a feature list, not speech). The sentences get shorter as it goes until it ends on two fragments, "Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor.", which is the cadence of an advertisement that has been cut to fit a character limit, not of a student explaining a plan to a client. Every idea is compressed to the point where the reader has to unpack it ("buys world stocks with the expected interest"), and compression without connective tissue is what you get when words are optimised rather than spoken. Pitch 3 comes a distant second for echoing the marking scheme's vocabulary back at me, but Pitch 3 at least has a human idea behind it. Pitch 1 has a human idea too; it just does not sound like anyone had it.
