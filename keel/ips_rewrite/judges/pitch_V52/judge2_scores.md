# Pitch judge 2

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Pitch 2 - the clearest statement of the idea ("we don't invest for them. We buy them") and every clause earns its place.
2. Pitch 5 - plain, specific, tells me the whole structure; "hopes for a gift" is a little soft.
3. Pitch 1 - same engine as 2 but flatter; "Caplet XR buys those cheques outright" does the work, the ending is a touch mechanical.
4. Pitch 6 - easy to follow, but "guaranteed minimum" overclaims and "honest upside" is brochure copy; it also ignores the 2028 deposit.
5. Pitch 3 - good link to the 2031 range, but "every dollar is already in bonds" contradicts the stocks bought with interest and stopped me.
6. Pitch 7 - the idea is there, but the slogan close ("Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor.") and "releases ... on schedule" read like marketing.
7. Pitch 4 - abstract, jargon-led ("a stock-or-Treasury rule"), and I cannot tell what the portfolio actually buys.

## Per pitch
### Pitch 1
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- Clear strategy: buy the ten payments with Treasuries that mature before each January, then a Treasury floor for the building gift, with the floor's expected interest going into stocks. The idea of the interest funding the equity is the edge and it is stated plainly. "Treasury holdings" is a slightly vague phrase, and it never says why this is better than the usual approach. Competent, a bit even in tone.

### Pitch 2
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 9/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- The opening argument, a promise is something you buy not something you invest for, is the central idea in two short sentences, and I would read on to see how they price it. The floor plus only-the-interest-in-stocks rule is easy to restate. "Cheques" and the plain verbs sound like a student who has thought about it. Minor gap: nothing on the 2031 range or flexibility.

### Pitch 3
- Substance 7/10, Client fit 9/10, Makes me read on 7/10, Sounds like a real person 7/10
- This is the only one that ties the strategy to Laura's 2031 conversation with co-sponsors, which shows real client knowledge. But the last sentence, "every dollar is already in bonds," sits badly next to stocks bought with expected interest; I could not tell whether it is a claim about the whole portfolio or a slip. That one line costs it the top spot.

### Pitch 4
- Substance 5/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 4/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- Starts with the right insight (the 2031 promise should be a floor the portfolio defends) but then gets abstract. "Runs a stock-or-Treasury rule that protects her announced minimum" tells me nothing concrete, and "near-certainty" and "preserving flexibility" are the brochure words from the criteria handed back. I did not follow what the 2028 money actually does on first read.

### Pitch 5
- Substance 8/10, Client fit 8/10, Makes me read on 8/10, Sounds like a real person 8/10
- Names the product, states the principle (doesn't invest the promise, buys it), then says exactly what each deposit does: Treasuries maturing before each January, a 2028 Treasury repaying in 2032, the rest in world stocks for the gift. The structure is complete and concrete. "Hopes for a gift" undersells the facility contribution slightly, and the 2032 date raises a question I would want the IPS to answer.

### Pitch 6
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 6/10, Sounds like a real person 6/10
- Plain, readable, and the ladder is explained well. "Guaranteed minimum" is a word a careful manager avoids with Treasuries sold or held through rate moves, and "honest upside" is salesman copy. Calling the stock fund "only money left over" skips how the 2028 deposit is used, so the facility side is thin.

### Pitch 7
- Substance 6/10, Client fit 7/10, Makes me read on 5/10, Sounds like a real person 4/10
- The mechanics are right (ten Treasuries, a floor for the gift, interest into stocks) but it is compressed into slogans. "Releases Laura's money on schedule" is odd phrasing for what is a bond ladder, and the closing fragments feel written for a poster. I would read on only because the structure matches the better pitches.

## Which one sounds most machine-written, and why
Pitch 7. It is built from clipped, rhythmic lines and ends on a neat aphorism ("Stocks move the ceiling. Never the floor."), the pattern a language model reaches for to sound memorable. It also uses a verb ("releases ... on schedule") that does not describe what a bond ladder does and sounds like product copy. Pitch 4 is close behind for the opposite reason: it is stuffed with abstract policy language ("stock-or-Treasury rule", "near-certainty", "preserving flexibility") that echoes the marking criteria rather than telling me what the team would buy.
