# Judge J3 scores

(Priya, pension actuary. Method: trace the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the 2031 range, the 2033 set-aside and contribution, and the ten $50,000 payments 2033-2042 through each plan, and look for the unpriced hole.)

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 6: the only plan that names exactly where its payments could fail, prices its one leak before the range is quoted, and puts a number on what its safety costs.
2. Entry 1: the tightest liability logic on the panel (the leak comes off the range before it is quoted), but a co-sponsor or a tired judge would have to work hard to follow it.
3. Entry 4: the same sound structure, laid out clearly, but it never says what "high certainty" means or how big its cushion is.
4. Entry 2: sound ladder and an honest failure point, but its leak is settled after the range is quoted, from money that falls in the same scenario.
5. Entry 3: a clean, conventional plan that answers every question, but it claims the payments carry only default risk while leaving coupon reinvestment unpriced.
6. Entry 5: the reserve is not matched until 2031, "high certainty" is a 3% chance of failure, and its 2031 floor barely exists on the day it is announced.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The cash flows hold together from end to end. The first deposit buys the ladder. The second fills any gap and then buys a Treasury floor plus a small stock sleeve. In 2031 the range is built from money already owned, and the promised half is locked when it is named. It is also the only entry that prices the coupon-reinvestment gap in 2031 and takes anything uncovered off the range before Laura quotes it, which is exactly what the case's "the range must protect the operating commitment" demands. The diversification argument (ten bets on one government are one bet, not ten) and the rule that changes only happen in years the case already fixed show a team that knows why the structure works, not just what it holds. What holds it back is how hard it is to read. Several key mechanisms (how the floor is sized, what "topped up once" draws on) are only implied, it never states its definition of high certainty in so many words, and it gives no assumptions or confidence figures. Good logic is only half the job if a co-sponsor can't follow it.
- On first reading I did not follow how the floor is sized ("more of the first cheque's fund" with no target), what "each end as sure as its miss is costly" means in practice, or where the 2033 top-up comes from.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The core is right: the ladder is bought first, the 2028 deposit fills any gap before anything else, and the facility floor is sized to grow back to the whole remainder by 2032. The "ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill" answer to the sceptic is the correct actuarial argument, and naming the breaking point (a two-point fall in yields before purchase plus a halved 2028 deposit) is what a real definition of certainty looks like. The hole is the timing of the leak. The coupon gap is settled only in January 2033, two years after the range is quoted, and it is paid from Laura's half of the stocks. Falling yields and falling stocks tend to arrive together, so the money meant to fill the gap may be smallest exactly when the gap is largest. If it falls short, the plan says the floor shrinks, and that breaks the bottom of a range already promised. The gift is also left unlocked after 2031, which is a defensible choice but leaves the top at about three in four. The "three times worse" weighting is asserted, not derived.
- I could not tell where "about three times worse" comes from, or what happens to the promised bottom of the range if Laura's half cannot cover the 2033 coupon gap.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the most textbook plan: a matched Treasury ladder first, then an 80/20 growth sleeve with a stated rebalancing rule. In 2031 the bottom of the range is moved into Treasuries maturing before 2033, and the gift is the floor plus at most half the rest. It states its return and inflation assumptions and walks through every Wharton question in order. It also leaves Laura a real flexibility pot, since its growth sleeve is far larger than the other ladder plans'. This is the hole I came looking for, though. Coupons received before they are needed are "held in short-term Treasuries", which means they are reinvested at unknown rates. In the same document the team says rate swings never touch the payments and the only remaining risk is default. Both cannot be true, and the team does not seem to see it. "Good, average and severe" testing and a "stated, modelled confidence" that is never stated read as placeholders rather than analysis. It also shows nothing of how the team got here.
- I was confused that it says payments are untouched by rate swings after saying early coupons are rolled in short Treasuries. I also wondered what happens to the ladder in the year before the 2028 deposit if $300,000 does not cover it.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The structure is the same sound one as Entries 1 and 6, and it is the easiest of the three to follow. The headings track the cash flows in time order: the ladder in 2027, the 2028 floor and the stock sleeve, naming and locking in 2031, the reserve running down from 2033. It sees the asymmetry of a capped, named gift and fixes it by locking the promised half. It sets a cushion for reinvested coupons at the same moment, before the number is public. It explains why the 2028 deposit should never carry a cheque in terms of Laura's own income, and it handles inflation and the Taiwan dollar correctly (they threaten the building, not the fixed payments). What holds it back for me is that the numbers are absent where they matter. There is no definition of high certainty beyond "no cheque loses a cent", no size for the cushion, and no confidence figure for the range. The analysis is correct in shape but thinly evidenced.
- I was not sure whether "a fifteenth of everything she owns" means a fifteenth of $450,000. I also could not tell how big the coupon cushion is.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 3/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The fundraising framing (a committed lead gift plus a conditional match) is the most original idea on the panel and shows real thought about what co-sponsors respond to. The liability side does not hold up when traced, though. For four years the reserve is an intermediate Treasury fund that never matures and is shorter than the payments it backs. It is converted to matched notes only in 2031, at whatever prices then prevail, with a 5% margin that is never justified. Accepting a 3% chance of missing a fixed payment is a strange choice when an almost riskless match is available. Worse, the growth sleeve only starts moving into Treasuries in 2031, so the "committed lead gift" announced that year is close to nothing. A match conditional on other donors is not a range of Laura's own contribution with a stated confidence, which is what the case asks for. Finally, a 105%-of-present-value reserve may not even fit inside the first $300,000.
- I did not understand how large the committed lead gift can be in 2031 if the move into Treasuries only begins that year, or how the 5% margin and the 97% figure were chosen.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry where I could trace every dollar and find the failure point already written down. It defines high certainty by when it breaks (a US default, or a two-point fall in yields before purchase combined with a halved 2028 deposit). It prices the ladder's one leak in 2031, before Laura names anything. And it puts a number on the cost of its safety (a balanced portfolio usually leaves her richer but breaks a promise about one time in four), which is the trade-off judges are told to look for. The client reasoning is the best on the panel: Laura's "thoughtful risks" are already taken by the residency and by her income, so the portfolio should not add correlated risk. Locking the promised half on the day it is named closes the asymmetry other plans leave open. What holds it back is Portfolio Analysis. It states no return or inflation assumptions, does not mention inflation or the Taiwan dollar, and leaves the flexibility pot (half of a small stock sleeve) thin against an uncosted building.
- If the promised half moves into Treasuries the day it is named, I did not see why the gift reaches its top only about 97 times in 100. I was also unsure which promise the balanced portfolio "breaks one time in four".

## My overall view
Five of the six teams buy the payments with a matched Treasury ladder, so a ladder alone separates nobody. What separated them was what happens in the gaps between dates. That means coupons that must be reinvested before 2033, the year between the first and second deposits, and the two years between quoting a range and paying it. Entries 6, 1 and 4 understood that a named, capped gift is a promise in its own right, locked it, and settled the reinvestment leak before Laura speaks. Entry 2 saw the leak but settled it too late, from money that shrinks in the same scenario. Entry 3 did not see it at all, and Entry 5 left its reserve unmatched for four years. To win, the best entry needs Entry 6's honesty about where it breaks, Entry 1's rule that anything unpriced comes off the range before it is quoted, and Entry 4's clarity. It also needs the plain-stated return, inflation and confidence assumptions that only Entry 3 bothered to write down. The ladder plans are almost too safe to argue with. Their real exposure is a flexibility pot of perhaps half a small stock sleeve, and none of them says why that is enough for a building nobody has priced.
