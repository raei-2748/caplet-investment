## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that Laura's second deposit is itself an equity-like asset, because it is earned from advances, speaking and licensing that shrink in the same recessions that make bonds expensive, so the money she already has should buy the certainty and the money she expects should carry the risk. That is a real piece of client knowledge rather than a restatement of the case. I also learned something about this team's decision-making: it states how much worse it thinks over-promising is than under-promising, and the confidence it attaches to the top of the range is exactly the quantile that ratio implies, which tells me the range was derived rather than guessed. What holds it back: the planned changes after 2031 are thin (the promised half of the stocks stays in stocks until 2033, so one time in four co-sponsors will be told a top that does not arrive, and the entry accepts this rather than designing around it), the coupon-reinvestment repair happens after the range is announced, and the opening claim about what every other team will do is the one note that a judge who has read hundreds of entries will not enjoy. Nothing about teamwork or process beyond a brief reference to modelling.
- Not understood on first reading: how the 2033 repair of the coupon gap, taken from Laura's own half, interacts with the range she quoted two years earlier if stocks have fallen in between; and the pharmaceutical naming took two passes before I saw why it was there.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: three things, which is more than any other entry. First, a single governing rule (make each promise safe no later than the day it becomes one) from which the 2027 purchase, the 2031 lock and the 2033 reserve all follow, so the planned changes are consequences rather than a list. Second, that the usual instinct to diversify is wrong for a liability ladder: ten independent issuers each very safe would still miss a payment far more often than ten obligations of one government that stand or fall together, so the entry spreads the stocks and deliberately does not spread the payments. Third, the observation that a capped range once announced is an asymmetric position (falls come off the gift, rises go to Laura), and the fix of moving the promised half into Treasuries the day the range is named. The entry also reports the failed first plan and what it taught, which is the only real evidence of learning in the panel. What holds it back is the reading: it packs a rule, a reason and a consequence into nearly every sentence, so a judge reading once has to work hard, and the result feels more like a specification than a narrative. The client is well understood but the Taiwan and inflation material is brief, and the entry does not state how confident it is that the top of the range is reached.
- Not understood on first reading: the sentence contrasting money she has and money she expects; what "each end as sure as its miss is costly" means operationally; and whether anything remains a range at all once both ends have been locked in Treasuries, or whether it has become a single number with a small reinvestment uncertainty around it.

### Entry 3

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: one real idea, and it is the only one in the panel aimed squarely at the co-sponsor conversation: split the 2031 announcement into a lead gift already sitting in Treasuries and a matching pledge that is paid only as others give, so that uncertainty becomes a reason to participate rather than a reason to wait. That reads as a sharper view of what Laura's contribution is for, and it fits her as someone whose first job is to convince other people. What holds it back is everything underneath. The reserve sits in an intermediate Treasury fund for four years before it is matched to the payment dates, which means the one part of the portfolio that must not move is exposed to rate changes for the period when Laura has the least room to fix it; a five per cent margin and a simulated probability are offered but not reasoned. The growth sleeve lists six tickers and a dividend tilt, which Wharton told teams not to spend the IPS on, and the glide path into short Treasuries from 2031 is standard. Inflation, construction costs in Taiwan and the Taiwan dollar do not appear. Most importantly, the matching pledge is described as conditional only on donors, but it is funded by a sleeve that is conditional on markets, so the credibility risk the entry sets out to remove is still there in a bad market.
- Not understood on first reading: what happens if co-sponsors do give and the growth sleeve has fallen, since the match is then owed and not fundable; and what the five per cent margin is a margin against when the reserve is not yet duration-matched.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the reframing of Laura's risk appetite is the sharpest reading of the client in the panel. The case says she is willing to take thoughtful risks; this entry replies that she has already taken them, in the residency itself and in an income stream that moves with the market, so the portfolio's job is to stop adding correlated risk rather than to express courage. Everything else follows from that and is stated with the numbers Wharton wants: what a high degree of certainty means and the two conditions under which it fails; the cost of the choice, with a balanced portfolio usually leaving her richer but breaking a promise one time in four; the floor-and-interest construction of the gift; the lock of the promised half at the moment the range is named, with the stated confidence that the top is reached; the reinvestment leak priced and set aside before anything is said aloud; and a reserve that runs itself down. The planned changes fall on the dates the case fixes rather than on market views, which is exactly the discipline Wharton describes. What holds it back: the article does not stop to show its organisation, so a tired judge has to find the five answers inside continuous prose; the teamwork evidence is one parenthesis about a failed first plan; and the Taiwan currency and inflation point is reduced to a clause. It is also the fourth entry in this panel built on the same chassis, and I cannot tell whether that reflects a convergent good answer or a shared source.
- Not understood on first reading: what "a fifteenth of all she owns" is a fifteenth of (the whole portfolio in 2028, or the remainder after the ladder); and why, once the promised half is locked, the top is reached ninety-seven times in a hundred rather than always (I inferred the reinvestment leak, but had to infer it).

## My overall view
The field split on one question: did the team see Laura's problem differently from the textbook, or merely solve the textbook problem well? Entries 6 and, underneath its fundraising idea, 4 solved the textbook problem: ladder the payments, grow the rest, lock a floor in 2031. Entries 1, 2, 3 and 5 saw three things the textbook does not say: that fixed-dollar payments are a liability and not a spending rate, so the usual equity-over-a-decade argument does not apply; that Laura's own income is already an equity position, so the portfolio should not add correlated risk; and that a capped range once announced has an asymmetric payoff that must be designed around rather than hoped through. Among those four, Entry 5 wins because it does the most of Wharton's work with the fewest loose ends: it defines certainty, prices the trade-off it accepted, says how confident it is and locks what it says. Entry 2 has the deepest ideas but asks too much of a reader with one pass; Entry 1 has the best-derived range and the thinnest glide path; Entry 3 has the clearest shape and the least evidence. To win outright, the best entry would need Entry 3's signposting, Entry 2's argument for why the payments must not be diversified, Entry 1's explicit statement of the loss ratio that justifies the range's confidence, two or three more sentences on how the team worked and disagreed, and a shorter route through the pharmaceutical naming, which costs words that Wharton would rather see spent on the Taiwan dollar and on flexibility after 2033.
