### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- The thinking underneath is serious: promises made safe once they become promises, the admission that a three-quarters-stocks first plan missed cheques, the point that ten Treasury debts rise and fall together, and locking the promised half of the gift in 2031. But the writing is so compressed that I could not picture a team of teenagers producing it or defending it in a room. The central rule is an abstraction, the 2031 mechanics are described in a chain of "moves into the first cheque's fund" steps that are hard to follow, and the ten-bets-at-99% line is a clever aside that slows the argument. ### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A clear, well-organised plan: buy Treasuries for the ten payments, put the rest in 80/20 global stocks and intermediate Treasuries, move the bottom of the range into Treasuries in 2031, then let the ladder become the reserve in 2033. It defines high certainty in plain words, states return and inflation assumptions, and says a crash shrinks only the facility gift. It reads like something a capable team wrote. The weaknesses are that it is the standard answer many strong teams will give, uses brochure headings ("Commitment Ladder", "Growth Engine"), says little about what the team weighed or what it would cost Laura to be this safe, and its handling of the coupon-reinvestment problem and the 2028 income risk is absent. The 2031 range is explained but not tied to any stated confidence beyond "modelled".
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: none.

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This reads as the work of people who thought it through and were willing to say so: the first plan missed cheques in their model, so they changed it. ### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- It makes the same core argument as Entry 4 but I found it the most honest. It gives its own definition of a high degree of certainty, including exactly when a cheque could fail, and says where its numbers come from ("in our model"): a balanced portfolio usually leaves Laura richer but breaks a promise one time in four, and the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100. That is the trade-off Wharton says judges want to see, and it is stated plainly. It also names the ladder's one leak, ties the 2028 deposit to the client's income risk, and explains why the "thoughtful risks" line from the case has already been used up. Weaker points: Taiwan costs and inflation are barely touched, the final paragraph's priority order is brief, and the "pill" opening is still a conceit. The first plan missing cheques is mentioned only in brackets. But as a whole it feels like a team that tested its idea and can defend it.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how "yields fall two points before we buy and her 2028 deposit more than halves" combine into a failure; I had to read it twice.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- It has an original angle: Laura's gift as a signal to co-sponsors, with a committed lead gift and a conditional match. It is the one entry that reads like a different team thinking about a different part of the case, and the voice is plain. But the core requirement, funding ten payments with a high degree of certainty, is weaker: the reserve starts as an intermediate Treasury fund with a 5% margin, so it can be hit by rate moves for years before individual bonds are bought, and the 97% figure is described as "in our simulations" without any sign of what failure looks like. The claims about fundraising research are stated as fact with nothing behind them. The growth sleeve names individual funds and a 65/35 mix that is not tied to Laura's horizons, and the "match" is conditional on other people's gifts, which sits awkwardly with the case's wish for a credible range. It doesn't say what the range is in terms of floor and top, or what it protects. It feels like a good idea attached to a thin plan.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a matching pledge that is "capped by what the growth sleeve can provide" translates into a dollar range Laura can announce; why the reserve is converted in 2031 when it is formally set aside in 2033.

## My overall view
Four of these six are clearly the same family, and that is what separated the top from the rest: in that family, the best versions explain a choice in everyday words, say what the safe option costs, and say where the numbers came from. Entry 5 does that best, and Entry 4 is close behind with a more human admission of an earlier mistake. Entry 1 knows the most but sounds the most polished, and Entry 2 compresses its ideas until I stopped believing students could explain them. Entry 3 is honest but ordinary, and Entry 6 has an idea but not a secure plan. To win, the best entry would need to keep its plain, tested reasoning, add the missing points on inflation, the Taiwan costs and the flexibility reserve in simple language, and drop the decorative flourishes so that the team's own thinking, not a clever frame, is what the judge remembers. Putting its biggest uncertainty and what the team would change next up front would help too.
