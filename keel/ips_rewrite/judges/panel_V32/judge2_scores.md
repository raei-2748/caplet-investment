## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3 - the only plan that puts the whole surplus to work (80% global stocks above a funded ladder) and says plainly that a crash costs only the gift.
2. Entry 5 - same safe core as 1, 4 and 6, but it counts the price of that safety ("a balanced portfolio usually leaves her richer, but breaks a promise one time in four") and gives a modelled hit rate for the gift.
3. Entry 4 - honest about the stocks-versus-bonds objection, names where the plan breaks and gives the odds of reaching the top, but the growth engine is small.
4. Entry 2 - most real growth after Entry 3 (65% stocks) and a fresh co-sponsor idea, but it de-risks from 2031 without counting what that costs and leans on unsourced "research".
5. Entry 1 - sound and clear, but the stock sleeve is tiny and the cost of the floor is mentioned, never measured.
6. Entry 6 - careful, with the best treatment of the coupon-reinvestment gap, but heavy on abstraction, light on numbers about growth, and the least readable.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the plan I would want to see for this client. The ladder takes the first deposit, which is the only way to deliver "a high degree of certainty" cheaply. Then everything above it, including the whole 2028 deposit, goes into roughly 80% global stocks and 20% intermediate Treasuries. That is thoughtful risk on money that has no payment attached to it. The reasoning is clear and short: stocks are Laura's best defence against Taiwanese construction-cost inflation, a crash "shrinks only the facility contribution", and the ladder is held to maturity. The 2031 step (lock the bottom of the range in Treasuries, the top reflects remaining stocks at a stated confidence) and the 2033 rule (floor plus at most half the rest, with the other half kept as flexibility) answer Wharton's questions on how the portfolio changes and how flexibility is kept. It does include assumptions (6-7% stocks, 4-5% Treasuries, 2.5% inflation). It stops short of numbers: no confidence level or range is given, and it does not measure how much more the growth engine earns than a protected structure would, so the upside is asserted rather than shown. It also says "rate swings never touch the payments", which slightly overstates it, because coupon reinvestment is ignored. It is plainly written, which helps with co-sponsor credibility.
- Did not understand on first reading: what "a stated, modelled confidence level" is, since none is stated; how coupons held in short Treasuries can earn enough, given no mention of reinvestment risk.

## My overall view
Four of these six are the same plan: buy the ten cheques with a Treasury ladder on day one, rebuild the 2028 deposit as a Treasury floor and run a small stock sleeve on top. That is safe and defensible for the payments, and the case does ask for high certainty, so I do not mark it down for the ladder. I mark it down for the second step. Once the payments are funded, the rest of the money belongs to a client who takes thoughtful risks and has no other claims on it, and a floor that rebuilds almost all of it leaves a small stock fund doing the work. Entries 1, 4, 5 and 6 differ mostly in how honestly they admit this. Entry 5 comes closest to counting the cost, and Entry 4 argues the case most clearly. Entry 3 simply does the sensible thing: it protects the promise and lets the rest grow in stocks. What separated the best from the rest was whether the team showed that it had weighed safety against growth for this client rather than assuming safety is free. To win, Entry 3 would need numbers: a modelled range with its confidence, what the growth engine is expected to earn against a protected structure, and how it behaves in a bad decade. Entry 5 would need a real stock sleeve, or a clear argument for why Laura would give up that growth. None of these should rely on a pharmacy metaphor to carry the idea.
