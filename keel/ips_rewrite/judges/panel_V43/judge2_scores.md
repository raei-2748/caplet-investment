## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 6/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The ladder-first logic is sound, and this is the entry that defines "high degree of certainty" as a short list of failure modes, tests it (a two-point yield fall, the 2028 gap, the reinvestment leak) and sets money aside before the 2031 range. It is also honest about the price: it says a 60/40 usually ends ahead, and it names the catch in a capped range and the fix (move the promised half to Treasuries). My problem is the strategy itself. After the ladder is bought, the remaining money is put into Treasuries that grow back to par, so only about a fifteenth of the portfolio is ever in stocks. For a client described as willing to take thoughtful risks with six to sixteen years to go, that is a gift of about the floor plus a small slice, and the "one in three" case against 60/40 compares against a plan that puts the cheques themselves at risk, not against the obvious rival (ladder plus a real stock sleeve). That is the price of safety counted on one side only. The 97-in-100 hit rate on the top of the range is mostly because the top is so close to the floor.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: the pill/XR wordplay and the "capital-protected note minus the bank" line took a second read; the "about a fifteenth of everything she owns" figure needed to be worked out from the setup.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 4/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The ladder for the operating payments is right and the "no payment relies on a return assumption" standard is clean. The growth sleeve is 75% VT / 25% QQQ on the argument that innovation has led returns, which is a narrow bet with no testing, and it is not diversification in the sense Wharton asks for. The staged-gate facility idea is original, but it hurts the client: the case says Laura needs to state a range in 2031 and contribute at the start of 2033, yet tranches two and three depend on co-sponsor commitments and project progress into 2034-35, so the plan does not say how much she gives in 2033 or how sure anyone can be. ### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the only entry that treats the post-ladder money as something to grow while keeping the commitment safe. The idea (the 2031 minimum is the number the portfolio is mechanically built to protect) is simple and ties promise to portfolio. The CPPI sleeve starts close to fully invested, cuts stock exposure as the cushion shrinks and lets the upside run, and the entry says plainly what it costs: cash-lock if the sleeve hits the floor, gap risk, and why a multiplier of three and not five. Assumptions are stated (7%, 16% vol, 4.5%, start-of-year flows), the range is tied to 10,000 simulated paths with a 95% confidence on the minimum and roughly even odds at the top, and the currency and income-variability risks are named. Weaknesses: monthly rebalancing and "present value of the announced minimum" is more technical than a semifinal judge may want, and there is no dollar range, so I cannot tell how big the upside is. Still the best balance of growth and discipline here.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor can start at 60% of the sleeve and later equal the PV of the announced minimum (the jump from one definition to the other); whether "half the gain above it" is half the sleeve's gain or the portfolio's.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- This is the plan I would call the default sensible answer: match the ten payments with Treasuries, put everything above the ladder in diversified global stocks with a Treasury cushion, lock the bottom of the range in Treasuries in 2031, and let the rest grow as flexibility. It is the right shape for a client who takes thoughtful risks and the ladder-becomes-reserve mechanics are clear. What holds it back is that it asserts rather than tests: no probability of reaching the top of the range, no figure for the shortfall risk if yields fall before the ladder is bought, no sizing of what a severe market does to the facility contribution, and "stocks are her best defence against rising construction costs in Taiwan" is a weak claim for a dollar portfolio. The "half of the remaining growth portfolio" rule is the one place a thoughtful judge would ask why half. Competent, not distinctive.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "costs roughly two-thirds of Laura's total contributions" (it works out, but the entry does not show it); whether the locked floor is part of, or in addition to, the 80/20 sleeve.

## My overall view
What separated the top two from the rest was that they answered the question Wharton asks: what the strategy does to funding reliability and to the facility contribution across market outcomes. Entry 1 does it with the most evidence, but its answer is to turn the growth sleeve into a bond with a small stock kicker. That counts the cost of risk and not the cost of safety: for a client with a long horizon and no near-term withdrawals, it is the ladder that provides certainty, and the rest of the money should be working. Entry 3 is the only one that keeps both: the commitment is mechanically defended and the sleeve still takes risk where it is affordable, with the failure modes written down. To win, Entry 3 needs to give a dollar range and a probability for it, say what the rule typically does in good and bad paths, and put the CPPI in plainer language. Entry 1 would win if it tested its note against ladder-plus-stocks (Entry 5's design), not against a 60/40 that has no ladder.
