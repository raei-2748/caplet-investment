## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 3: the clearest answer to Wharton's questions in plain numbers (what breaks a cheque, how often the 2031 range is missed, why the range sits where it does), told as one story.
2. Entry 1: the most rigorous decision framework, with explicit risks accepted, a stated 95% confidence for the range floor and a liquidity section; less warm and harder to follow (CPPI mechanics are not explained).
3. Entry 2: the same skeleton as Entry 3 with a stronger voice and the honest 60/40 trade-off, but less quantified on the certainty test and the range miss.
4. Entry 4: the most polished prose and a sharp "calendar" argument, but it never states how confident the 2031 range is, which Wharton asks for directly.
5. Entry 6: well organised against the five IPS questions, with real assumptions and a sensible "promise first" logic, but thin on outcomes and vague on the range's confidence level.
6. Entry 5: a creative staged-gate idea, but it defers the facility decision past 2033, gives no sized range or confidence, and runs the growth money through a tech tilt with no numbers behind it.

## Per entry
### Entry 1

- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What it does well and what holds it back: This is the most complete walk through the five IPS questions: a reserve that holds no stocks and gets safer each January, a growth sleeve with a pre-committed rule, a flexibility share in 2033, and a 2031 range defined by a stated 95% confidence on the floor. ### Entry 3

### Entry 1 and Entry 3 comparison note
Entry 1 is the stronger framework and Entry 3 the stronger answer to Laura's situation. I placed Entry 3 first because Wharton asks for a clear thesis, client fit and confidence in plain terms, and Entry 3 gives all three without asking the reader to work out a rule.

## My overall view
What separated the best entries from the rest was whether the team stated its numbers for the things Wharton asks about: how certain the 2033-42 cheques are, how confident the 2031 range is, and what it costs to be safe. Entries 1 and 3 do this clearly; Entries 2 and 4 do most of it but leave one figure unstated. Entries 2, 3 and 4 share a skeleton and several figures, and I scored each on how well it was executed, which is where they separate. Entry 5 has the best idea and the least evidence; Entry 6 has the clearest structure and too little outcome. To win, the best entry would need to state in one sentence the confidence level behind the 2031 range and the inflation assumption for the facility cost, and to explain in plain words how the $125,000 buffer works.
