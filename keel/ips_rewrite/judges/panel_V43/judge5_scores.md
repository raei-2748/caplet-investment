# Judge J5 scores

Judge: Ingrid, professor of decision science. Method as declared: for each entry I first wrote down what I learned that I did not already know, then ran a full pairwise tournament (15 pairs) on Wharton's five criteria and derived the ranking from wins. My prior, stated up front: a Treasury ladder for the ten payments plus stocks for the rest is table stakes. Four of the six entries reach it; three of them reach almost the same second layer on top of it. So the ranking below is mostly about what each entry adds beyond the ladder, and how well it argues the trade-off it chose.

Tournament wins: Entry 5 (5 wins), Entry 6 (4), Entry 1 (3), Entry 2 (2), Entry 3 (1), Entry 4 (0).

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 5 ... the ladder plus a second, explicit capital-protection layer for the 2028 money, a counterfactual the team actually modelled and argued against itself, and a decision-theoretic fix (lock the promised half the day the range is named). The most complete version of this design.
2. Entry 6 ... a pre-committed rule (CPPI) that ties the announced minimum to a mechanical floor, with its own failure modes named and priced; the most honest quantitative treatment of the range, and the only entry to handle currency.
3. Entry 1 ... the same design as Entry 5 with one or two extra stress numbers, but compressed to the point where two of its key figures confused me on first reading.
4. Entry 2 ... the same design again, the clearest to read of the three, but it drops the coupon-reinvestment leak and the confidence on the top of the range, so it leans more on assertion than the other two.
5. Entry 3 ... a genuinely different way of seeing the facility contribution (staged, option-like tranches), undermined by thin analysis, an undefined range, and a tech tilt justified by exactly the correlation that should argue against it.
6. Entry 4 ... a competent, complete, generic version of the table-stakes design; nothing wrong, nothing I had not already assumed a good team would do.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes (just)
- What I learned: that Laura's 2028 deposit and the ladder's price move against her at the same time, because the recession that shrinks advances and speaking fees is the one that drives yields down and bond prices up; and that an announced capped range is a short option position for Laura, since falls come off the gift and rises past the cap go to her, which is why the promised half should be de-risked the day the range is named. Both are sharp, client-specific observations that go well beyond the ladder. The structure itself (ladder for the payments; the 2028 deposit split into Treasuries that grow back into the whole sum plus the interest in one world fund) is a home-made capital-protected note, and the team says so, which is exactly the kind of "why this and not that" reasoning Wharton asks for. The trade-off against a 60/40 portfolio is modelled, not asserted, and the team is candid that the alternative usually leaves her richer. What holds it back is density: the entry packs more numbers per sentence than a reader can absorb once, and the growth sleeve is tiny, so I wanted one more sentence on why a fifteenth of the portfolio in stocks counts as the "appropriate balance" the case asks for rather than a near-total retreat from growth. Articulation of process is present (the team tells us what it tried first and why it abandoned it) but brief.
- Did not understand on first reading: a figure of roughly $125,000 that "still does" something after a two-point fall in yields; I worked out it meant the 2028 deposit still covers the ladder's shortfall, but only on a second pass. I also stumbled on two adjacent failure rates (about one in thirty for a 75%-stock plan, about one in three for a 60/40 plan): the second includes landing below an announced range, the first does not, so the numbers are not comparable and the entry does not say so.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 6/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: the same two ideas as Entry 1, stated more cleanly, plus an explicit liquidity statement (every rung could be sold, none will be) and the point that a building priced in Taiwan dollars is a reason to keep half the stocks unpromised. The reframing of the client's liability as ten one-year bills rather than one ten-year bill is a genuinely useful way to see the problem, and the entry builds its whole argument on it coherently. The strategy is clear, consistent across the 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2033 dates, and the voice is authentic. What holds it back is that it carries less evidence than its siblings: it names the 60/40 counterfactual and its one-in-three failure rate, but drops the coupon-reinvestment leak and gives no confidence figure for the top of the range, so the Portfolio Analysis criterion ("reasonable assumptions and projections ... under varying market outcomes") is served mostly by one comparison. For a decision scientist, a well-argued trade-off needs the numbers on both sides; this entry has them on one.
- Did not understand on first reading: none of substance. I did want to know how much of the 2028 deposit the ladder is expected to need before the floor and the fund get anything; the entry says "nearly" and leaves it there.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 8/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- What I learned: that the facility contribution need not be one decision at one date, and that staging it behind market and project gates is how a founder (and a co-sponsor) would naturally think about committing capital. That is the one entry in the set that reframes the second half of the client's problem rather than the first, and I give it real credit for it. What holds it back is nearly everything underneath the idea. The runway is a plain held-to-maturity ladder with no treatment of coupon timing or of the 2028 deposit's variability. The growth capital adds a 25% tech-index tilt and justifies it by Laura's own career being in technology and creative industries, which is the opposite of the right inference: her human capital already carries that risk, so the portfolio should not double it. The range communicated in 2031 is described as a seed tranche plus later tranches, some of which are paid in 2034-35, yet the case asks for the range of her 2033 contribution; the entry never says what number she actually tells co-sponsors. There is no probability, no confidence level, no stress case, and no assumption beyond "about 7% with deep drawdowns possible", so the Portfolio Analysis criterion is barely addressed. Making Gate 2 conditional on co-sponsors committing first also sits awkwardly with the case's point that her contribution is the signal that brings co-sponsors in.
- Did not understand on first reading: what "above its target" means for the growth portfolio at Gate 2 (no target is defined), and whether the announced range's top is a 2033 figure or a 2033-35 total.

### Entry 4

The entry describes a plan; it does not show me the team wrestling with a trade-off.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 9/10, Client Knowledge 9/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 9/10, Overall 9/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: the same core as Entries 1 and 2 (the recession that shrinks the 2028 deposit is the one that makes the ladder dear; a named capped range is an asymmetric bet, so de-risk the promised half on naming day; a home-made capital-protected note for the 2028 money), and this version carries the most complete chain of evidence for it. It makes the case against its own strategy first, with a modelled figure for how much richer a 60/40 plan usually ends and a modelled failure rate for how often it breaks a payment or its own range; it names the ladder's one leak (coupons reinvested at lower yields) and says when and how it is covered; it gives a probability that the gift reaches the top of the range; and it sets an explicit priority order for when things change. Client fit is strong: the definition of a high degree of certainty as a short list of ways to fail, rather than an adjective, is precisely what the case asks teams to define and evaluate, and the two entries on that list are both Laura-specific. The planned changes across 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2033 are all present and consistent. What holds it back is the same question I put to Entry 1: a fifteenth of the portfolio in stocks is a very small growth allocation for a client the case describes as willing to take thoughtful risks, and the entry answers it by argument (the big risk is the residency itself; one in three is too often) rather than by showing what the gift is likely to be; I also note that the one-in-three failure rate for the alternative includes falling below a range that the alternative was never designed to name carefully, which tilts the comparison. The naming device at the end costs words that could have carried another number.
- Did not understand on first reading: how large the floor bought in 2028 is relative to the whole deposit, given that an unknown slice of that deposit goes to complete the ladder first; the entry says "most of it", which is enough for an IPS but left me estimating.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 9/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- What I learned: that the announced minimum can be made the thing the growth sleeve is mechanically built to protect, through a pre-committed rule that cuts stock exposure as the cushion shrinks, so no one has to decide anything in a falling market. For a decision scientist this is the cleanest idea in the set: a commitment device aimed squarely at the behavioural failure (selling late, or not at all) that a discretionary plan invites, and it echoes Wharton's own instruction not to rewrite the strategy because markets moved. The entry is also the most honest about what its idea costs: gap risk is sized (what one-month fall would breach the floor, and why a multiplier of three rather than five), cash-lock is named and accepted, the 2028 deposit's variability is acknowledged and the ladder given first claim, and currency risk is the only treatment of the Taiwan dollar in the six entries that says who pays for it. Assumptions are stated with volatilities, the range has a confidence at the bottom (95%) and an honest description of the top (even odds), and liquidity is covered. What holds it back: the rule is more machinery than Laura needs to understand to trust it, and the entry explains the mechanics more fully than it explains why 60% of the sleeve is the right starting floor or what the minimum is likely to be in dollars; client knowledge is correct but thinner than Entries 1, 2 and 5, which saw something specific about how her income and her bond prices move together. Monthly rebalancing on a sleeve of this size also has a cost in commissions that goes unmentioned.
- Did not understand on first reading: the relationship between the floor and the announced minimum. The floor starts at about 60% of the sleeve and accrues at the short yield, and from 2031 it "equals the present value of Laura's announced minimum"; I had to decide for myself that the minimum is set from the floor at that date rather than the floor being reset to a minimum chosen some other way. It is consistent either way, but the entry should say which.

## My overall view
What separated the top from the rest was not the ladder; everyone sensible has the ladder, and Entry 4 shows that doing it properly earns a passing mark and no more. The separation came from whether a team had seen something about this client's problem that a generic planner would not, and whether it then argued the trade-off with numbers on both sides. Entries 1, 2 and 5 saw two such things: that Laura's 2028 money and her bond prices are hostage to the same recession, and that a capped range is a one-sided bet she should hedge the day she names it. Entry 6 saw a third: that the minimum she promises can be the number a pre-committed rule defends, and it was the only entry to price its own idea's failure modes and to say who pays for the Taiwan dollar. Entry 3 saw something real about staging the contribution and then did almost no analysis to support it. I put Entry 5 first because it carries the fullest chain of evidence for the strongest client insight, and it argues against itself before it argues for itself, which is what I want from a team that will later have to defend a very conservative growth allocation to a client who describes herself as a risk-taker. To win outright, Entry 5 would need to close exactly that gap: show, in one or two sentences, what the facility gift is likely to be under this plan and why a client who has already bet her career on the residency is better served by a near-certain modest gift than by a probable larger one, and make the comparison with the 60/40 alternative fair by giving the alternative a range designed as carefully as its own. Entry 6, had it matched the client texture of the Caplet entries, would have been first.
