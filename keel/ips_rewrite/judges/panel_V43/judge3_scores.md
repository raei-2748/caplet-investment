# Judge J3 scores

Judge: Priya (pension actuary, asset-liability matching). Method: traced the $300,000 (2027), the $150,000 (2028), the ten $50,000 payments (2033-2042), the 2031 range and the 2033 contribution through each plan, looking for the place it fails or the reasoning is hand-waved.

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4: liabilities matched, and it prices the hole most plans skip (coupon reinvestment), the 2028-deposit risk and the capped-range asymmetry; flexibility is thin.
2. Entry 6: almost the same machine and the same holes priced, and it states what the safety costs. It is a little lighter on why this fits Laura.
3. Entry 1: a real rule (CPPI) with the multiplier, gap risk and cash-lock reasoned through, but the reinvestment risk is unpriced and its range confidence doesn't answer the case's question.
4. Entry 3: a clean, complete textbook liability match with more growth, but it says rate swings "never touch the payments" while its own parked coupons are exposed, and it gives almost no evidence.
5. Entry 2: the same design as 4 and 6 but missing the parts that make it hold up: no return assumption, no range confidence, the reinvestment leak not mentioned, and numbers with nothing under them.
6. Entry 5: creative staging, but the 2034-35 tranche sits outside the 2033 contribution the case asks about, the QQQ tilt adds risk tied to Laura's own income, and there is almost no analysis.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 7/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe (leaning yes)
- This is the only entry that turns the surplus into a mechanical, pre-committed rule, and the team clearly understands it. The multiplier of three is tied to the size of a one-month crash that would break it, the cash-lock is named and accepted, and liquidity, currency, inflation on the facility and the 2028 income risk are all addressed. The sleeve stays heavily in stocks early on, so Laura gets real growth and a real flexibility share, which suits "thoughtful risks" better than the bond-heavy entries. When I traced the cash flows, two things came loose. First, a ladder "sized to deliver $50,000" from coupon Treasuries bought in 2027 throws off years of coupons before 2033 that must be reinvested at unknown rates, and the entry never prices that. Second, the 2031 range: the top is set at the median, and the contribution is "the minimum plus at most half the gain", with no cap at the top. So roughly half of outcomes land above the range, and the entry's "95% in the minimum, even odds at the top" is not a confidence that the contribution falls inside the range, which is what the case asks for. Why the floor starts at 60% of the sleeve is never explained.
- Confusing on first reading: how the floor switches from "60% of the sleeve accruing at the short yield" to "the present value of the announced minimum" in 2031 (what if the two differ?), and whether the 2033 gift is capped at the top of the range.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no (maybe at a stretch)
- The structure is sound: a cash-flow-matched ladder for the ten payments, the 2028 deposit filling any gap first, a principal-protected floor with only the expected interest in stocks, and the promised half moved into Treasuries once the range is named. It defines high certainty as a short list of ways the plan fails, which I like, and it links the 2028 deposit to the same recession that makes the ladder dearer. It shows learning by rejecting its first, stock-heavy plan. But much of the analysis is asserted, not shown: no return or inflation assumption is stated, no confidence is given for the range, and the coupon-reinvestment exposure inside the ladder isn't mentioned. The comparison with a 60/40 lumps "misses a cheque" together with "lands below its own range", which hides how often it actually misses a payment. Flexibility is thin and never defended: the whole floor is promised, and Laura keeps only half of a stock sleeve that is about a fifteenth of the portfolio. The entry doesn't say why committing ~90% of what remains is still "responsible".
- Confusing on first reading: what "about $125,000 of it still does" refers to; how a recession "before we buy" matters when the ladder is bought in January 2027; what "a floor that grows back into itself" means in the pitch.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 5/10, Overall 6/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe (leaning no)
- This answers every IPS question in order: a ladder held to maturity, an 80/20 growth engine with a stated rebalancing band, the bottom of the range locked into Treasuries in 2031, the reserve running itself down, and a clear 2033 rule (the floor plus at most half the growth, capped at the top) that leaves real flexibility. It states its assumptions, and it gives more growth than the bond-heavy designs, which suits the client. The hole is in the ladder. The entry says coupons received early sit in short-term Treasuries, then says rate swings "never touch the payments". Those parked coupons are exactly what rate swings touch, and no buffer is priced for it. The 2028 deposit is treated as certain: nothing on what happens if it is smaller or later, though the case ties it to volatile income. The analysis is mostly a promise ("we test good, average and severe markets") with no result, so I can't check whether the range or the growth engine's sizing holds. Many teams will submit something close to this, and it gives a judge no reason to remember it.
- Confusing on first reading: none, apart from the clash between parking coupons in short Treasuries and saying rates never affect the payments.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 8/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- This is the entry whose holes I went looking for and found already priced. The ten payments are cash-flow matched with Treasuries. The one leak in a coupon ladder (early cash reinvested at lower rates) is named, priced and set aside in 2031 before the range is announced. The ladder's sensitivity to the 2028 deposit is tested against a two-point fall in yields. The team also sees the asymmetry of a capped range (falls come off the gift, rises go to Laura) and fixes it by moving the promised half into Treasuries, then states how often the top is reached and how big the rare miss is. Its client reasoning is the strongest of the six: her 2028 income from advances and speaking shrinks in the same recessions that make the ladder dearer, so the payments should rely on money she already has. What holds it back: putting only the floor's expected interest into stocks leaves Laura very little growth and a thin flexibility share. Nearly all of the 2028 deposit is promised as the floor, and the entry doesn't defend why that much commitment is "responsible" given the case's warning. And with the top reached 97 times in 100, the "range" works almost like a fixed number.
- Confusing on first reading: "about $125,000 still does" (does what, out of what?); the first plan missing "one future in thirty" next to the 60/40 failing "one time in three" read as inconsistent until I saw the 60/40 figure includes missing its own range; "the rare miss is about $1,000" (a miss of the top, I assume).

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 5/10, Portfolio Analysis 3/10, Articulation 4/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 4/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The ladder for the payments is right, and treating later contributions as options Laura may decline is a genuinely original way to preserve flexibility. But the plan doesn't fit the case's timeline: the case asks for a range in 2031 for the 2033 contribution, and Gate 3 moves money to 2034-35, so it is unclear what the 2031 range covers and how confident Laura can be in it. The gates are vague ("above its target", "recovered from any downturn"), and no confidence level is given. The 25% QQQ tilt is justified by Laura's own career in technology and creative industries. To me that is the wrong way round: it adds risk that moves with her income and with the 2028 deposit, and it reads as chasing past returns. Apart from "about 7%", nothing is quantified, the ladder's reinvestment risk is ignored, and there is no reasoning about how big the surplus is or how it behaves.
- Confusing on first reading: whether the Gate 3 tranche is part of the "2033 contribution" in the 2031 range; where the gated money sits between gates (stocks or Treasuries); what "co-sponsors have committed their share" means for a decision that must protect Laura's flexibility.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 6/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Mechanically this is Entry 4: a matched ladder, the reinvestment leak priced and set aside in 2031, a floor plus interest-in-stocks, and the promised half moved into Treasuries once the range is named, with a stated chance of reaching the top. It is the most honest of the six about the trade-off. It makes the case against itself first, states a return assumption, and admits a 60/40 usually ends far ahead, which is exactly the "reasoning, assumptions and tradeoffs" Wharton asks for. Its certainty definition is a list of failure modes, including the link between a recession and the 2028 deposit. It loses a little to Entry 4 on client fit: it doesn't make the argument that Laura has already taken her big risk, and it gives no size for the rare miss. It shares the same weakness: little growth, a thin and undefended flexibility share, and a range so tight it is close to a single promised number. Its 60/40 failure rate again lumps missing a payment with missing a range.
- Confusing on first reading: how large the 2031 set-aside for the reinvestment leak is and which bucket pays for it; otherwise none.

## My overall view
Every entry here gets the operating commitment basically right: Treasuries matched to the ten January payments, with the 2028 deposit as the backstop. What separated them was what happened once I traced the money past the obvious part. Entries 4 and 6 found the holes I expected nobody to have priced: early coupons reinvested at lower rates, a 2028 deposit that shrinks in the same recession that makes the ladder dearer, and a capped range that loses on falls and gives away rises. They priced or fixed each one and said where the money comes from. Entry 1 understands its own risk rule better than anyone, but it left the ladder's reinvestment risk open and answered a different question about the range than the case asks. Entries 3 and 2 have the right shape with less underneath it, and Entry 5 is an idea in search of analysis. To win, the best entry (4) has to deal with its own weak point, which is flexibility, not safety. It should say how much of the post-2033 money Laura keeps and why that is enough, and either allow a little more growth or argue plainly why she should accept so little. It also needs to make its headline comparison count missed payments separately from missed ranges, so a judge can see how often the alternative actually fails Laura.
