# Judge J4 scores

## Ranking (1 = best)
1. Entry 4 - owns a real mistake (the 75% stock start), explains the "why" in plain steps, numbers sit beside what they mean.
2. Entry 1 - the most honestly argued risk section of the six; a rule-based strategy with its failure modes named, a little heavy on mechanics.
3. Entry 5 - same bones as 4 with the argument intact, but more performed prose and slightly less plain explanation.
4. Entry 3 - same strategy again, with the best-argued "case against us", but tidier and more slogan-driven; the close is oddly clipped.
5. Entry 6 - sound, plausible, readable, but generic; the thinking stops at "match the bonds, grow the rest".
6. Entry 2 - an appealing idea, but built on a startup metaphor, with a facility timeline that drifts from the case and little quantitative backing.

## Per entry

### Entry 1
- Investment Strategy 7/10, Client Knowledge 7/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- A Treasury ladder covers the ten payments, and the 2028 money runs a CPPI rule (multiplier 3, capped at 100% because margin is banned) that protects the minimum Laura will announce in 2031. This is the entry that thinks hardest about how its own plan fails. It names gap risk and explains why the multiplier is 3 rather than 5. It names the cash-lock trap and accepts it on purpose. It flags that the 2028 income is uncertain, and it flags the Taiwan-dollar exposure. It defines "high certainty" in terms of what the payments do not depend on, and says where assumptions come from ("we chose"). It reads like a team that tried to break its own plan. Against it, CPPI is a lot of machinery for teenagers to run and defend, and the "Floor = Promise" label is a slogan. The 95% confidence and "roughly even odds" language is a bit loose, and the section headings give it a report feel. Still, the reasoning is visible, which is what I want.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor "starts near 60% of the sleeve" and why that number. I also could not tell how the 2031 low-end promise differs from the CPPI floor in the years before 2031.

### Entry 2
- Investment Strategy 5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 4/10, Articulation 5/10, Creativity & Presentation 6/10, Overall 5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? no
- The Treasury ladder for the payments is sensible. The staged "tranche" idea for the facility contribution is original and speaks to the case's concern about flexibility. But it leans on a founder/startup analogy throughout (runway, burn, seed, real options), which sounds like a pitch deck rather than a team that worked through the numbers. Gate 3 releases money in 2034-35, outside the case's 2033 contribution framing, and Gates 2 and 3 depend on co-sponsors committing, which the case says shouldn't be assumed. The growth sleeve is 75% VT / 25% QQQ justified by "innovation-driven companies have led returns", which is thin reasoning and weak diversification. There is no stated confidence for the range, and no downside figures or trade-offs. A fresh idea, but not tested or quantified.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how a "seed tranche" is fixed in 2031 if the range top depends on later market-linked tranches; what confidence the team actually has in the range.

### Entry 3
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7.5/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thesis is a capital-protected note built by hand. A Treasury ladder pays the ten payments, a second Treasury position grows back into the whole 2028 deposit, and only the interest buys world stocks. It is a clear, well-fitted answer to a client who must keep her word, and it deals with the 2031 range, the capped-range catch (moving the promised half into Treasuries on the day the range is named) and the order of priority if things change. It makes the case against itself fairly, citing a roughly $200k median advantage for 60/40 and a one-in-three failure rate, and it names the coupon-reinvestment leak. My discomfort is the voice. The headings and one-liners ("Sell low, by appointment", "Have your cake and invest the icing", and the closing "Do not crush.") are very neat and read as crafted. A teenager could write this, but I would be unsure the thinking came first. The ending adds nothing.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "Do not crush" only makes sense once you have read the extended-release capsule joke, and the coupon-leak sentence is hard to follow.

### Entry 4
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8.5/10, Portfolio Analysis 8/10, Articulation 8/10, Creativity & Presentation 7.5/10, Overall 8.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- Same underlying strategy as Entries 3 and 5, and it makes the case best, mostly because it admits where the team started. They began with three-quarters in stocks, their model showed about one future in thirty missing a cheque, and they changed course. That is the honest, learning-based reasoning I most want to see. It breaks "high certainty" into two failure routes and gives a figure ($125,000 of the 2028 deposit still fills the gap after a two-point yield fall). It ties Laura's income risk to the recession problem. It says why a range should be "low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones", which shows client understanding. It also gives the cost of being wrong ("the rare miss is about $1,000"). It still has a few cute lines (the pill joke), but the argument comes first and the sentences are plain enough for students to own. It would be stronger with a sentence on which assets are actually held and a little more on diversification.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "about a fifteenth of everything she owns" as a size for the stock position, and why the upside is split in half between Laura and the co-sponsors' range.

### Entry 5
- Investment Strategy 8/10, Client Knowledge 8/10, Portfolio Analysis 7/10, Articulation 7/10, Creativity & Presentation 7/10, Overall 7.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? yes
- The thinking matches Entries 3 and 4 and it also shows the abandoned three-quarters-stocks start. It argues well that the flaw was "the calendar, not the return" and that a patient investor can wait out a bad decade but "a January cannot". That is real insight into the client's cash-flow structure. It is the most written of the three, with line after line built to land ("one in three is not a risk. It is a forecast."), and I trust it a little less for that. It gives one number for the cost of failure ($125,000) but no confidence figure for the range top, and it does not mention the 97-in-100 reach. The "icing/cake" image is used without explaining what the floor actually holds.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: "A named top can only be missed" took two reads; I was unsure what the "promised half moves into Treasuries" meant for the stock position.

### Entry 6
- Investment Strategy 6.5/10, Client Knowledge 6/10, Portfolio Analysis 5/10, Articulation 5.5/10, Creativity & Presentation 4.5/10, Overall 5.5/10
- Top-50-of-2,300? maybe
- A plain, competent, believable plan: Treasury ladder first, 80/20 growth sleeve, lock the bottom of the range into Treasuries in 2031, contribute at most half the remaining growth portfolio, review each January. It covers most of the Wharton questions and is the entry most likely to be genuinely student-written. It does not argue against itself, never tests its stock/bond mix, and gives no confidence number, no probabilities and no comparison with alternatives. "Roughly two-thirds of Laura's contributions" for the ladder is a rough figure with no source. Bold headings and phrases like "Growth Engine" lean toward brochure language. Safe but unremarkable.
- Anything you did not understand on first reading: how the floor in 2031 is sized if the sleeve has already grown; what "stated, modelled confidence level" the team actually has.

## My overall view
What separated the best from the rest was visible thinking. Entries 3, 4 and 5 and Entry 1 show a team that tried something, saw it fail in their own model, and changed the plan, and they say what they gave up (the median gain over 60/40). Entry 4 does this most plainly, which is why I rank it first. Entry 1 is the other entry that argues honestly about risk, but its CPPI rule is a lot to claim ownership of. Entry 6 is believable but stops thinking early, and Entry 2 relies on a metaphor in place of numbers. I should say that three entries share nearly the same strategy and phrasing, so my ranking among them rests on voice and clarity rather than on different ideas. To win, the best entry would need to loosen its clever lines, state what it actually buys (names the ETF types and the Treasury maturities), give the confidence in its range plainly, and say which of its choices came from a teammate's argument rather than from the model.
