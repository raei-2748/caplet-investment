## What the essays do (Keel's reading of them; check it against your own)
- **A thesis with a twist, said with total confidence**, often landed by a one-word or one-line paragraph.
- **Full, rhythmic sentences** that build clause on clause with spaced en-dash asides, then a blunt short one. Not
  terse. Not compressed. The long sentences carry the reasoning; the short ones carry the punch.
- **Pun and idiom-twist headings** (a well-known phrase bent to the point of the section). Short, wry, never cute for
  its own sake.
- **Steelman, then dismantle:** the opposing case stated at full strength ("There is a notable objection..."), then
  "But this proves less than it appears to" kind of turn.
- **Homely, sardonic images inside formal sentences**: an everyday object or gesture that makes an abstract point
  physical and a bit funny. One per section at most, and each one argues.
- **Anaphora and lists of concrete things** (the same opening repeated over three or four short sentences) when
  building indignation or stakes.
- **Numbers as digits, woven into the argument**, each one doing work (a share, a multiple, a scale).
- **Named ideas used as tools** (a thinker's concept applied, not dropped). In the IPS: no names, no citations
  (Wharton); use the idea itself.
- **A callback ending**: the opening image or claim returns, changed, in the last lines. The last line is short.
- **Wry brackets** that talk back to the sentence they sit in.
- British spelling; en dashes; "we".

## What the finance writers with personality add (shapes only, never their famous lines)
- Levine: explain the mechanism like a toy, step by step, whose money goes where; deadpan about absurdity.
- Marks: one idea per piece, an opponent, aphorism earned by the argument, ends on what we will do.
- Buffett: folksy plain analogies; admits the dull part plainly; "we buy to keep".
- Schwed / Asness / Cembalest: wry, self-mocking, rude about bad ideas only after being rude about their own.
