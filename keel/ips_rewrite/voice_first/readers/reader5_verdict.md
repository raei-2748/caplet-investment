(private file, never quoted here) crossed with a dry, opinionated finance writer. Not judged: investment merit, whether
a machine wrote it, compliance with Wharton's form.

What separates first from last: Entry 2 argues with someone (it builds the case against itself, lets it stand for two

## How the essays move (described, not quoted)

Read for movement, the three essays do the same things every time:

- **Openings.** A formal, almost textbook statement of the orthodox view, then two or three very short sentences that
  mock it in the second person; or a cold, enormous number in digits followed by a physical image. The reader knows
  within four lines whose side the author is on.
- **Headings every 250-400 words, all jokes.** Reworked proverbs, film and advert lines, retail slogans, often with a
  deflating parenthesis attached. The heading carries the section's argument; you could read the headings alone and
  get the essay.
- **Rhythm.** One long sentence stacking clauses with spaced en dashes (a definition, a list of three concrete nouns, an
  aside), then one short sentence that shuts the door. One-word and two-word paragraphs are used as landings, and the
  essays end on two or three plain words after a swelling paragraph. He builds; he does not compress.
- **Concessions are real and formal.** The opponent gets a whole paragraph in courteous language (critics "not without
  ammunition"-type phrasing, "a notable objection"-type openers), then a blunt "But this proves less than it appears"
  pivot, usually through a homely analogy (a carcinogen, a broken car, a chained boat, house insurance).
- **Jokes go at the end of the paragraph, after the data.** A statistic or a mechanism, then a single cruel image
  (a legal costume on revenge, a bar-room dare, a teaspoon against a sinking ship). Never two in a row; never at the
  victim's expense.
- **Mock-formal politeness toward the opponent** ("Very well", "to put it gently", "Let us test the theory") is the
  tonal signature. Numerals as digits. British spelling. Rhetorical questions asked and answered at once.
- **Triads of concrete nouns** are common in his prose (he is not shy of lists of three), but they are lists of things,
  never of abstractions, and never used as a close.
- **Anaphora at the climax** (four sentences starting with the same subject) once per essay, then the short ending.

So the voice we are matching is: long build, short land; a fair opponent; one image that argues; one joke per section
placed after the evidence; a two-word ending; headings that are jokes. The finance-writer cross adds: mechanism before
label, numbers said flat, a quoted document, and a dry aside in brackets.

## Entry by entry

### Entry 2 (first)

**What sounds like him.** The first sentence is a long one with an opinion in its second half (most of the ways the
money could go wrong begin with confusing the two): that is his opening move, a claim the reader wants to argue with.
The case document is quoted in inverted commas twice, the way a columnist quotes the filing. Three pun headings, all
reworked proverbs or sales lines, each carrying the section's point. The concession is done his way: "There is a
serious case against all this, and we made it first" is the formal, fair introduction of the opponent, with a
self-deprecating twist, followed two sentences later by "But 'usually' is doing a lot of work there", a dry columnist's
idiom that turns the paragraph. The mechanism paragraph on what breaks a cheque runs as one chain of "which" clauses
(a recession pushes yields down, which makes the ladder dearer, and shrinks the income behind the deposit, which has to
fill the gap): the long build. "Ours is the same note, minus the bank (and its fee)" is the joke in brackets at the end
of a technical paragraph, exactly where he puts them. "As for her taste for risk, she has already spent it on the big
one: the residency" is a real argument said in one breath. The close is an instruction off a pill bottle, two words,
after a sentence that explains it once: the earned short ending, and the only encore of the one image.

**What sounds generic.** The pitch is compressed the way a model compresses: "buys" four times, a verb-less noun phrase
("a floor that grows back into itself") that is a riddle at 50 words, and a slogan to close. The ladder sentence relies
on a label ("Treasury funds that mature like bonds") rather than a mechanism. "The hope is a gift to her residency's
facility in Taiwan" uses the case's word, not a person's. The range paragraph is the one paragraph with no opponent, no
turn and no joke: inventory. The $125,000 sentence stacks three qualifiers before its subject.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. Pitch: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for her gift." The
   image is private: a floor growing into itself means nothing to a judge who has not read the model. A pitch is said
   across a table; this would be met with "sorry?".
2. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it." Read
   aloud, the reader trips at "a two-point fall a deposit". The honest hedge ("in our model") is buried in the middle
   where it reads as throat-clearing instead of as the source.
3. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they
   should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." The second riddle
   ("grow back into all of it") and the one place where the entry names a result before it has shown whose dollars go
   where. This is the paragraph a bank would write; the sentence after it (icing, never cake) is the one a person would.

### Entry 3 (second)

**What sounds like him.** The opening is a story told against the writer: four short sentences that build the case for
the plan the team then abandoned, ending "on paper it looked like the obvious answer". That is the dinosaur move:
praise the thing before you kill it. The one odd, specific detail ("Every holding can be sold any afternoon") is the
proof a person wrote it. "Every cheque therefore rests on one borrower, on purpose" has his mock-formal connective and
a dry two-word landing. The capital-protected-note paragraph is the best mechanism writing in the four entries
(whose money goes where, step by step, then "it arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours arrives with neither"). The
bracketed aside about the Taiwan dollar ("which nobody can translate for her yet") is his habit of a deflating
parenthesis. The ending is an object, not a lesson.

**What sounds generic.** No headings at all, and the headings are the single most recognisable thing about his prose.
The second paragraph fires two mirrored pairs in four sentences (return/calendar, patient investor/January): a drum the
reader hears coming. The opening also has two flat declaratives of identical length in a row, the staccato a model
produces when it is summarising. Plainer and more Buffett than him: fewer jokes, less arch politeness toward the
opponent, no "Very well, let us test it" tone. The pill returns in the last line almost word for word from the pitch,
so the image rides along rather than paying off once.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "She has taken thoughtful risks all her working life. Stocks beat bonds over six years more often than not." Two
   equal-length declaratives with no connective; and "all her working life" edges past what the case says into
   biography. Join them with the "and so" he would use, and keep the claim to what the case supports.
2. "The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar." followed three sentences later by "A patient investor can wait
   out a bad decade. A January cannot." Either pair alone is his; both together is a rhythm, not a thought. Keep the
   January one (it is the argument) and say the other plainly.
3. "Whatever the ladder does not need of the 2028 deposit is for the gift." A syntax knot ("does not need of") that no
   one says aloud. Put the deposit first and the verb second.

### Entry 1 (third)

**What sounds like him.** Two pun headings. "A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails,
not a soothing adjective" is an opinion a finance columnist would own, said flat. "Hence Caplet XR" has his mock-formal
connective. "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, and charge for it" is the dry tail on a technical paragraph.
"For money that keeps a residency's doors open, one in three is not a risk. It is a forecast" is a real turn of thought
with stakes in it.

**What sounds generic.** This is the squeezed sibling of Entry 2, and the squeezing shows. It spends five or six
kicker pairs in 480 words (risk/forecast, return/calendar, investor/January, sold/none will be, icing/cake,
promise/hope), so the quotable endings stop being quotable. The promise/hope frame appears in the pitch, the first
paragraph, the second, and the last line: the frame rides along instead of being set up once and paid off once. The
opponent's case is stated as a number, not voiced; there is no "serious case against all this" sentence, so the
concession reads as a stat the team already beat. Three sentences are riddles created by cutting the small words. The
last line is a chiasmus, which is a slogan bolted on, not an ending earned.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. Pitch: "Her 2028 deposit finishes it, then a floor that grows back into itself." The verb has been cut and a noun
   phrase left standing in for a sentence; "finishes it, then a floor" is not something a seventeen-year-old would say
   out loud.
2. "A named top can only be missed, so the promised half moves into Treasuries the day she names it." The whole
   mechanism (once a cap is announced, gains above it are Laura's and losses below it are the gift's) has been
   compressed to five words that have to be decoded. Entry 4 says the same thing in two plain sentences; borrow them.
3. "The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar that it does." A mirrored close. The author's
   endings are two or three plain words after the build, never a balanced antithesis; this one sounds like a tagline.
   (Also: "about $125,000 of it still does" has no clear antecedent and should be fixed.)

### Entry 4 (last)

**What sounds like him.** One paragraph: the one on her income ("shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds
dear"), which argues from a cause and lands on a decision ("So the cheques use money she already has"). The rhetorical
question answered at once ("What does a high degree of certainty mean? Only two things break a cheque") is a move he
uses. "A capped range has a catch" is a plain, spoken opening to a mechanism, and the two sentences that follow explain
it better than any other entry.

**What sounds generic.** Almost every sentence is the same middling length, so the prose has no build and no landing;
it reads like a form filled in well. Eight or nine numbers in 500 words, said in a row, none felt. No headings. The
acronym is explained ("X for ten, R for redemption") the way a brochure glossary would, where the author would let the
joke stand or put it in a bracket. The opponent's case (the 60/40 comparison) is dropped into the middle of the floor
paragraph with no introduction and no turn, so it is inventory, not argument. The one joke ("you don't crush an XR
pill") is tucked inside a dash in the last paragraph, where it apologises for itself. "To sell any day for a dearer
building" is a compression knot. The last line is a list.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "We started with three-quarters in stocks. Our model sank it: about one future in thirty missed a cheque. Laura
   doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the
   bottom." The team's one real confession, reduced to a note. Four sentences of the same length, no "and so", no fair
   hearing for the plan before it is sunk.
2. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten,
   R for redemption." Explaining the name kills it; Caplet asked for subtle. Say what the pill does once, and let the
   reader get the X.
3. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5%, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her almost $200,000 richer by 2042. But
   about one time in three, that portfolio lands below its own range or misses a cheque." The opponent's best argument
   arrives after the plan it argues against, with no sentence introducing it as the case against. Move it under the
   confession and give it a "There is a case for the other plan, and it is ours from October" kind of entrance.

## Rewrite plan for Entry 2 (first place)

Seven changes. Each keeps the facts and numbers, stays within a few words of the original length, and invents nothing
about Laura or the team. Current words quoted exactly; replacement follows.

**1. Pitch, sentence 3 (49 words stays 49).**
Current: "Her first $300,000 buys the ten $50,000 cheques, or nearly: Treasury bonds, each maturing before its January.
Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for her gift."
Replace with: "Her $300,000 buys ten $50,000 cheques, or nearly: Treasury bonds, each maturing before its January. Her
2028 deposit buys Treasuries worth all of it again by 2032; only their interest buys world stocks for her gift."
Why: "a floor that grows back into itself" is a private image; "worth all of it again by 2032" is the same fact said as
a thing that happens on a date. The repeated "buys" is fine: the pitch is about buying, and he repeats a verb on purpose
when it is the argument.

**2. IPS paragraph 1, last sentence.**
Current: "The hope is a gift to her residency's facility in Taiwan."
Replace with: "The hope is a gift towards the residency's building in Taiwan."
Why: "facility" is the case's word; "building" is a person's. The essays always choose the concrete noun (the cell, the
cage, the vending machine) over the administrative one.

**3. IPS paragraph 3, the ladder sentence.**
Current: "as a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each paying out before its own
January. Any rung could be sold any day. None will be."
Replace with: "as Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that close and pay out on a fixed date as a bond does, one lot
falling due before each January. Any of them could be sold any day. None will be."
Why: "mature like bonds" is a label that assumes the reader already knows what the fund does; the replacement is the
toy explanation (mechanism before label). Dropping "ladder/rung" also leaves the pill as the only image in the piece.

**4. IPS paragraph 4, last sentence.**
Current: "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
Replace with: "Even if yields fell two full points, a deposit of about $125,000 – not the $150,000 she plans – would
still fill it, in our model."
Why: the condition goes first, the number in the middle, the source last. Read aloud it no longer trips at "fall a
deposit", and "in our model" at the end is where he puts the qualifying phrase (after the claim, as a shrug).

**5. IPS, under "Sell low, by appointment", sentence 2.**
Current: "Laura has been "willing to take thoughtful risks", and in our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40
portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."
Replace with: "Laura is, in the case's words, "willing to take thoughtful risks", and in our model, with world stocks
earning 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."
Why: "has been" turns a present description into a past habit we are reporting on, which sounds like a model
paraphrasing a document. "In the case's words" is the columnist's attribution and says where the phrase came from.

**6. IPS, under "Have your cake and invest the icing", sentence 1.**
Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the
interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund."
Replace with: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 – fewer dollars than the whole sum today,
the whole sum when they repay – and the difference, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, buys one world
index fund."
Why: whose dollars go where, in order, before the result. The reader now sees the discount is the interest, so the
icing/cake sentence that follows lands on something they have already understood instead of explaining it after the
fact. If the team is attached to "grows back into all of it", keep it as a second clause after the mechanism, never
instead of it.

**7. IPS, under "A bird in the hand, half the bush", sentence 1.**
Current: "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at the
floor plus half her stocks."
Replace with: "In 2031 she names co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028, and no forecast
can move it. The top adds half of whatever the fund is worth that day."
Why: the only paragraph in the entry with no turn. Splitting it gives it one short landing ("no forecast can move it",
which is a true claim already implied by the floor) and makes the top explicitly a thing that moves and the bottom a
thing that does not, which is the argument of the heading. Same facts, same numbers.

Leave alone (these are the lines that make it first): the opening sentence; "So we don't invest the promise. We buy
it."; the "which" chain on what breaks a cheque; "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first";
"But 'usually' is doing a lot of work there"; "she has already spent it on the big one: the residency"; "Ours is the
same note, minus the bank (and its fee)"; the three headings; "Do not crush."

Kicker count after the plan: "We buy it", "None will be", "icing, never cake", "Do not crush": four, one over the
guide's three. If one must go, cut "None will be" (the pill line already says the same thing at the end).
