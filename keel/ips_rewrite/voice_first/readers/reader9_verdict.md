# Reader 9 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Brief: judge how much each of four anonymous entries sounds like the author of the three essays (crossed with a personable finance writer) arguing about money, and how much it sounds generic, compressed or machine-made. Investment merit not judged. AI-origin not guessed. The essays are not quoted here; their moves are described.

## Ranking

1. **Entry 1**
2. **Entry 3**
3. **Entry 4**
4. **Entry 2**

What separates first from last: Entry 1 lets a sentence run while it carries a mechanism and then stops it dead, and it jokes in the headings and in asides, the way the essays do; Entry 2 cuts every sentence to the same middling length, explains its own pun, and closes on a triad, so it reads like minutes of the same meeting.

## How the essays move (the yardstick)

Read twice, once for argument, once for motion. What a reader hears:

- **The opponent speaks first, in short second-person sentences.** Each essay opens by stating the view it will attack at full strength, often as a three-beat creed, and concedes that the view is comforting before the "But" arrives. The concession is sincere and takes a whole paragraph, sometimes a whole section with its own heading.
- **Build long, land short.** The mechanism sentences run to forty or fifty words with nested en-dash asides; the landing sentence is often two to five words, sometimes one. Paragraph length varies from a single line to a dozen sentences. Nothing is a uniform fifteen words.
- **Homely image inside a formal sentence.** A broken machine nobody beats; a vending machine that is at least honest; a sinking ship and a teaspoon; boats chained to the sea floor; a furniture argument on a raft. One image per section, set up once, paid off once, and one of them returns in the last paragraph.
- **Headings are jokes that also state the thesis.** Bent proverbs, retail slogans with a tail, a sitcom line, a prospector's drawl, a legal-philosophy pun. Roughly one every 300-400 words.
- **Statistics are said flat, then reacted to in a short sentence.** The reaction is the voice; the number is scaffolding. He uses numerals, British spelling, spaced en dashes, and parenthetical asides that mock his own list ("a non-exhaustive list", "at least at face value").
- **He is willing to be slightly unkind to the other side,** and the unkindness is where the best jokes live. He is never unkind to the victim of the argument.
- **Endings:** a reversal of a cliché, or a literary callback, then a last line of two words.

Against that yardstick, every entry is quieter and more polite than he is, and every entry carries more numbers per hundred words than he does. The ranking is about which one still moves like him.

## Entry 1

**What sounds like him.** The first sentence makes a claim, not a statement of fact ("most of the ways her money could go wrong begin with confusing the two"), which is how his introductions work. "So we don't invest the promise. We buy it." is his short-sentence landing after a set-up. "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be." is the same move, done better. The concession paragraph is the most essay-like passage in all four entries: it gives the opposing plan its strongest number, admits "we made it first", and only then turns, with "'usually' is doing a lot of work there", which is a columnist's idiom and a human one. "As for her taste for risk, she has already spent it on the big one: the residency." is the kind of aside he writes. "Ours is the same note, minus the bank (and its fee)." has his parenthetical sting. Three headings pun and argue at once; "Sell low, by appointment" is the best heading in the set. The ending, a two-word instruction lifted off a pill label, matches his two-word closes.

**What sounds generic.** "Grows back into itself" (pitch) and "grow back into all of it" (body) is an invented idiom, repeated, that no one says across a table. The recession paragraph is a mechanism with no landing, and its final sentence stacks three numbers. "A crash costs her icing, never cake." is a mirrored slogan: rhythm, not thought. "A bird in the hand, half the bush" is a pun that does not resolve; the reader has to do the joke's work. "The ten holdings are the operating reserve" is form language.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it." Three numbers, two commas-as-brackets, and the verb arrives last. He never ends a mechanism on a number pile; he ends it on a reaction.
2. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." One 40-word sentence with no short sentence after it, carrying an invented phrase. Long is fine; long without a landing is a form.
3. "A crash costs her icing, never cake." The one place the entry reaches for a slogan. Delete it and the argument still stands, which is the test for a line that is only rhythm.

## Entry 3

**What sounds like him.** "A high degree of certainty is a list of ways to fail, not an adjective." is the single most Ahaan-like sentence in the four entries: it takes a phrase everyone nods at and turns it into a concrete object, then lists the failures. "Selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" is the odd specific detail that proves a person wrote it. The concession keeps "our first plan was three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it", so the admission has content. "A capped range has a catch" is the toy-explanation opening he uses before a mechanism. "Ours swaps the bank's option for shares, which can't expire worthless." argues rather than decorates. "Do not crush." lands.

**What sounds generic.** "Ours, under one in sixty" is a verbless fragment: the compression a machine does when it keeps every idea and shortens each. "The hope" is used as a noun for a sum of money three times ("set it aside from the hope", "then the floor, then the hope"); he names ideas, but he does not make an abstraction hold a bank balance. The pitch runs two parallel sentences ("her first $300,000 buys it... Her 2028 deposit buys...") that read as a table. Only one heading in 500 words, so the prose runs flatter than Entry 1 across the middle. The close explains the name with a colon-list ("ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole") at the moment it should just land.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word to co-sponsors; ours, under one in sixty." The semicolon fragment is the tell. He would finish the sentence with a verb and a reaction.
2. "In 2031 we price that shortfall and set it aside from the hope, before she names any gift." "The hope" as a pot of money; also the third sentence in a row built as "we do X, before Y".
3. "We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole." The colon-triad ending. The essays end on a reversal or a callback and then two words; this one ends on an explanation and then two words.

## Entry 4

**What sounds like him.** "The change buys world stocks" in the pitch is the one odd human word in all four pitches: "change" as in loose change, said by a person. "Leaves out the bank (and its fee)" keeps the parenthetical sting. Two pun headings. "Do not crush." lands.

**What sounds generic.** "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective." has been compressed one step past sense: a list of what? The confession has lost its content: "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first." is followed immediately by the 60/40 numbers, so "we made it first" points at nothing. "Selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" has been cut, which is exactly the odd detail a human would have kept. "With nothing sold early and nothing to rebalance" is a mirrored pair. "(X for ten, R for redemption)" decodes the acronym in brackets, which is the glossary move. The heading "Have your cake and invest the icing" sets up an image that never appears in the body below it: an orphan joke.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective." Put back the three words that made it a thought (Entry 3 has them).
2. "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first. Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and in our model..." Restore what "it" was (the three-quarters-stocks plan); a confession without its content reads as a formula.
3. "each turns to cash before its cheque, with nothing sold early and nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule (X for ten, R for redemption): one cheque a year, and it only works whole." A mirrored pair, a triad, a bracketed decode and a colon-list in four lines: the ending is where the entry sounds most assembled.

## Entry 2

**What sounds like him.** "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills" is a true turn of thought, said plainly. "She has already taken the big one: the residency." "Minus the bank and its fee." The one rhetorical question ("What does a high degree of certainty mean?") is answered at once, which he does.

**What sounds generic.** Almost every sentence is ten to twenty words; nothing builds and nothing lands, so the ear hears a form. No headings. "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart." is a sentence any team could write. The Caplet XR paragraph explains the joke twice ("On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption."), which is a glossary, not a voice. The concession arrives after its own answer (the 60/40 comparison is tucked into the middle of the capital-protected-note paragraph), so there is no "But" to turn on. Thirteen or more numbers in 500 words, none reacted to. The best line ("you don't crush an XR pill") is buried inside dashes mid-sentence, and the piece closes on a triad ("the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope") rather than on a thing. The pitch is a summary of the body, not a hook.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption." A pun that is explained is a pun that has died. Entry 1 shows how to say it once and let the last line carry it.
2. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer. But about one time in three, that portfolio misses a cheque or lands below its own range." The opponent's case appears after the answer and inside the wrong paragraph; it needs its own paragraph, stated first, as the essays always do.
3. "Nothing is sold before it pays – you don't crush an XR pill – and each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope." The kicker is thrown away in a dash-aside and the piece ends on a list. Pull the pill line out, make it the last line, delete the triad.

## One note for all four

The essays react to their numbers. A statistic lands, then a short sentence says what to feel about it. In the four entries only Entry 1 does this once ("'usually' is doing a lot of work there"). Whichever entry goes forward, the fastest way to make it sound like the author is not to add jokes but to add one short reaction after the one number that matters, and to cut the numbers that get no reaction.
