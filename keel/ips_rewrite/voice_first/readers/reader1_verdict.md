# Reader 1 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Judged only on voice: how much each entry sounds like the author of the three essays arguing about investing.
Investment merit and whether a text is AI-written were not judged. The essays are not quoted here; their moves are
described.

## The essays' moves I scored against

From two readings, one for the argument and one for how the prose moves:

1. **The opposing view goes first, stated fairly.** Each essay opens on the comfortable or popular belief, gives it
   real weight, and only then turns. The strongest objection gets its own section and its best number before the
   author takes it apart.
2. **Long build, short drop.** Most sentences are long, with stacked clauses, dash asides and parentheses. Then a
   paragraph lands on one very short sentence, or a one-line paragraph. The short line hits because it comes after
   the long ones. Without the long ones it loses its force.
3. **The jokes go in two places: headings and paragraph endings.** The headings are bent idioms or famous lines,
   each twisted so that it carries the section's argument. The body prose stays serious and formal. Humour shows
   up as a dry closing line after a serious build, never as a run of gags.
4. **Homely images for abstract claims.** A broken car, a vending machine, house insurance, furniture on a raft, a
   teaspoon on a sinking ship. Each image is ordinary and physical, and it does the arguing.
5. **Antithesis and reversal.** "Not X but Y" sentences, mirrored pairs, a phrase turned back on itself.
6. **Holding a phrase at arm's length.** A loaded word goes in single quotes and gets asked what it really means.
7. **Honest concessions that shift the burden.** The author admits the evidence is not
   conclusive, then argues it is enough to make the other side prove its case. Limits are admitted openly.
8. **A slightly lofty register.** Words like ought, alas, a dry understatement, the odd rhetorical question, an
   imperative "let us" when the argument speeds up.
9. **Endings call back to the opening image** and close on a very short line.

**Weakness shared by all five:** every entry is written almost entirely in short declaratives. The essays' long,
clause-stacked sentence, the one that builds and qualifies before the drop, appears at most once or twice in any
entry. So the short punchlines arrive with no long sentences before them to land against. The "one ten-year bill /
ten one-year bills" line and the "capital-protected note, minus the bank" idea appear in nearly every entry, so they
don't separate one entry from another.

## Entry 1: 6/10

**Why:** It has the essay author's structure: an aphoristic thesis, a steelman of the stock-heavy plan ("strong
enough that we made it ourselves"), bent-idiom headings, and a short closing line. But four pun headings in about
420 words is denser than the essays ever get, two of them are cheque puns, and the last third is spec-sheet prose.
The closing line calls back to a pill introduced halfway through. It does not return to the opening image.

**Most alive**
- "Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and she has already taken the big one. It is called the residency."
  This is a concession turned into a reversal, landed with a deadpan short sentence. It is exactly the essays' move.
- "Too careful, and the gift comes out smaller than it might have been. Too brave, and a residency built on her word
  opens one January without the money to run it." The mirrored pair carries an asymmetry argument, and the second
  half is concrete and has a human stake.
- "Ours is the flat-pack version, minus the bank and its fee." A homely, physical image that pays off its heading
  (Some assembly required). The heading and the line work together, as they do in the essays.

**Deadest**
- "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, each turning to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs
  itself down with nothing to rebalance." This is a fact sheet. Nothing is argued and nothing turns.
- "The US government defaults, or yields fall before we buy, the ladder costs more than $300,000, and her 2028
  deposit ... cannot fill the gap." A comma run-on. The essays' long sentences are controlled, and this one stumbles.
- "Most of that deposit buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032, which by then grow back into the whole of it; the rest
  ... goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies." Pure mechanism, with no image and no edge.

**False / forced:** "Cheques and balances" is a pun with nothing under it: the section isn't about balance. Four
headings make the piece read like a pun sampler. "Like any extended-release pill it works only if nobody crushes
it" asks the reader to get a pill joke before the name has been explained.

## Entry 2: 4/10

**Why:** This is the least like the essays. There are no headings. Nearly every sentence is a short declarative of
about the same length, so nothing builds and nothing lands. The strongest essay move, the generous steelman,
appears only as a stray statistic two-thirds of the way down, after the plan has already been laid out. It reads
as a well-organised briefing, not an argument.

**Most alive**
- "low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones." A balanced antithesis with a
  stake in it.
- "A capped range has a catch." A plain, conversational turn that promises a problem and then delivers it.
- "Her income from advances, speaking and licensing shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear." This
  is a real irony, and the essays like causal traps like this one.

**Deadest**
- "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart." Flat. The idea is good, but there is no image and no
  turn.
- "The ladder has one leak – early cash earns less if yields fall – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031,
  before she names her range." A technical sentence with nothing for a reader to feel.
- "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5%, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her almost $200,000 richer by 2042."
  This should be the steelman's big moment, but it arrives late and unframed, so it reads as a data point rather than
  an opponent being given its due.

**False / forced:** "Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption" spells out the joke, which kills it. The essays
never explain their own wordplay. "What does a high degree of certainty mean?" raises the essays' kind of question
but then answers with a list instead of interrogating the phrase.

## Entry 3: 6.5/10

**Why:** It has some of the most essay-like individual moves. The opening uses a long-then-short landing with a
one-line paragraph. One sentence interrogates the case's own phrase, as the essays do with loaded words. And the
ending is a mirrored sentence that returns to the opening's promise/hope frame, the essays' callback ending. It is
let down by a glossary paragraph for the name, stiff connectives ("Hence", "So ... So"), and a middle that turns
mechanical.

**Most alive**
- "A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective." This is the
  best line in all five entries for this author: it takes a loaded phrase, holds it at arm's length and redefines it
  with an antithesis.
- "Keep them in one pot and, sooner or later, the hope eats the promise. / So we didn't." The long-short drop, done
  properly. The image (hope eating promise) is physical and a little menacing.
- "The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar it does." It closes the circle on the
  opening frame with a reversal. That is how the essays end.

**Deadest**
- "Hence Caplet XR. On a pill, XR is extended release: the same dose, on schedule. On ours, X is ten and R is
  redemption." A dictionary entry, and "Hence" is a register the essays don't use for transitions.
- "In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds
  that mature like bonds, each due before its January." Accurate and inert.
- "A named top has a catch: a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past it goes to Laura. So the day she
  names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries." Correct mechanism with no voice in it.

**False / forced:** The XR acronym explained in full (same problem as Entry 2). The pill line tacked onto the
operating-reserve paragraph ("you don't crush an XR pill") feels pasted in for continuity, not earned.

## Entry 4: 7/10

**Why:** This entry argues best, and its rhythm is closest to the essays' long-build, short-drop. It opens with the
essays' signature move: it lays out the stock-heavy case fairly, in her terms and with its own logic, before
turning on it. Its paragraphs are longer and connected ("So", "Then"), so the short sentences land against
something. It has no headings, though, which is where the essays put half their humour. The register is calm
financial plain-speaking rather than the essays' more lofty, polemical edge.

**Most alive**
- "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot." The single best landing in the five entries:
  personification, antithesis and a short drop after a long paragraph.
- "The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar." A reversal that moves the whole argument in eight words.
- "A bank sells exactly this and calls it a capital-protected note; it arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours
  arrives with neither." Wry, physical detail (the brochure), with the closer a beat behind. This is how the essays'
  humour works.

**Deadest**
- "Whatever the ladder does not need of the 2028 deposit is for the gift." Awkward syntax, and it reads like a
  clause from a spec.
- "Or, in our model, yields fall before we finish buying, the ladder costs more than she has, and her 2028 deposit
  cannot fill the gap – though even after a two-point fall, about $125,000 of that $150,000 still fills it." The
  dash qualifier is in the essays' manner, but the logic gets lost and "fills" appears twice.
- "So in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys the ten cheques, or very nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds, and
  Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one falling due just before each January it is for." Functional and
  heavy-footed.

**False / forced:** The parenthetical "(it will be priced in Taiwan dollars, which nobody can translate for her yet)"
reaches for a joke that doesn't quite connect. "Ten doses, one a year. Nobody crushes the pill." closes on an image
the IPS body never set up (only the pitch did), so the callback partly misses.

## Entry 5: 8/10

**Why:** This entry uses the most of the essays' actual moves, and uses them where the essays use them. The
headings are bent idioms that each carry the section's argument ("Sell low, by appointment" is the case against
stocks in four words). The thesis is a mirrored pair, given its own one-line paragraph. The steelman is generous
and comes with the opponent's best number. "usually" is quoted and cross-examined, the way the essays treat a
loaded word. Each section closes on a dry line after a plain build. It loses points for a gag ending and for
clipped sentences in the mechanical passages.

**Most alive**
- "But "usually" is doing a lot of work there: about one time in three, by the same model, the 60/40 lands below its
  own range or misses a cheque." This holds a word at arm's length and makes it confess, which is the essays' move
  exactly, and it follows a fair concession of the rival plan's $200,000.
- "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" A dry, concrete image that turns the abstract risk of
  dated payments into something absurd and visible.
- "A crash costs her icing, never cake." A homely image that pays off its heading and states the whole floor-plus-
  stocks idea in six words.

**Deadest**
- "The hope is a gift to her residency's facility in Taiwan." Flat. The opening sentence before it promises an
  argument, and this one drops back to a fact sheet.
- "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
  Clunky: three qualifiers stacked in front of the verb.
- "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs
  itself down with nothing to rebalance." Spec prose. (The same sentence appears in three entries.)

**False / forced:** "Do not crush." as the last line is a pharmacy-label gag. The essays' final short lines carry the
stakes of the argument, and this one carries a pun on the product name, explained only one sentence earlier. Three
bent-idiom headings in a row starts to feel like a formula. "A bird in the hand, half the bush" is the strained one:
clever, but it takes a second to decode. "Doing a lot of work" is a stock internet phrase. The move behind it is
right, but the words are borrowed.

## What Entry 5 needs to sound more like the essays' author

1. **Let two or three sentences run long.** The recession paragraph (yields fall, the ladder gets dearer, and the
   same recession shrinks her advances and speaking fees) is the natural place for one controlled, dash-laced
   sentence that builds the trap before a short drop. At the moment every paragraph has the same clipped beat, so the
   short lines have nothing to land against.
2. **Cross-examine "high degree of certainty", not just "usually".** Entry 5 quotes the case's phrase and leaves it
   alone. The essays always ask what the loaded phrase really means. A line that turns certainty into a short, named
   list of ways it fails (as Entry 3 does) would be the author's move.
3. **End on the argument, not the label.** Swap "Do not crush." for a closing line that returns to the opening
   promise/hope pair with a reversal, as the essays' endings return to their opening image. Or plant the capsule
   early and lightly, so the last line becomes a callback and not a punchline with its own explanation attached.
4. **Two idiom headings, not three.** Keep "Sell low, by appointment" and "Have your cake and invest the icing", and
   give the range section a plain heading or a sharper one. The essays bend about one idiom per section of several
   hundred words, so the piece stays an argument and doesn't become a game.
5. **Borrow nothing.** Replace "doing a lot of work" with the author's own dry understatement. The essays' asides
   are the writer's own phrasing, never stock internet idiom.
6. **Admit a limit, as the essays do.** Entry 5 concedes the rival plan's upside well. One more honest sentence on
   what this plan gives up, followed by the turn that makes the other side prove its case, would carry the essays'
   habit of shifting the burden of proof.
7. **Fix the clunky sentences** ("after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it";
   "a gift to her residency's facility") so the plain passages read as easily as the clever ones.
