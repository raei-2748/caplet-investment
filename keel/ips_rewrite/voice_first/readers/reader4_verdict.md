# Reader 4 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Blind read. I judged voice only: how far each entry sounds like the essays' author arguing about money, crossed with a
dry, opinionated money columnist, and how far it sounds generic or assembled. Not investment merit, and not a guess at
authorship. The essays are described below, never quoted.

## What I listened for (the essays' moves, described)

- **An argument, not a report.** Each essay states the comfortable belief its reader holds, then says plainly it will
  argue against it. The reader is being talked out of something from the first paragraph.
- **The opponent gets a real hearing.** He gives the other side its best case, sometimes in its own short, blunt
  sentences, sometimes as a whole section given over to the case against him, and only then turns, with a plain
  conjunction and a claim that the objection proves less than it seems.
- **Build long, land short.** Clause-stacked sentences with spaced en dashes carry the mechanism or the evidence; then
  a one-line paragraph or a two-to-five-word sentence closes the door. Paragraphs vary wildly in length.
- **Headings are bent idioms.** Proverbs, film lines, adverts and nursery phrases twisted so the pun is also the
  section's thesis.
- **Homely images inside formal sentences.** A household object or a cheap everyday scene stands in for a big
  abstraction, usually as a verdict, once, and it argues rather than decorates.
- **Asides and manners.** Parenthetical jabs, understatement used as an insult, and a mild moral edge aimed at a named
  target (in money terms: banks and fees, not the reader).
- **Numbers as evidence.** Numerals sit inside sentences with verbs, as proof, not stacked as spec.
- **Endings that call back.** The last lines return to the opening image and turn it; the very last line is often
  absurdly short.

## Entry 2 (ranked 1)

**What sounds like him.** It has the most of the essays' structure, not just their sentences. The opening states a
thesis that is also a warning (confusing the promise with the hope is where things go wrong). The one-line paragraph
"So we don't invest the promise. We buy it." is his land-short move. The three headings are bent idioms that are also
the argument of their section ("Sell low, by appointment" is the plan's whole objection to stocks in four words). Above
all, it concedes properly: "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first" is the essays' habit of
voicing the strongest opponent before answering. The columnist is here too: "'usually' is doing a lot of work there"
is a dry money-writer's aside, "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" is the best image in all four
entries, and the parenthetical "(and its fee)" is a jab with manners at the right target. The two-word close echoes his
habit of a final line that is almost nothing.

**What sounds generic.** The mechanism paragraphs go flat between the jokes. Two sentences chain "which ... which"
clauses until they read like a term sheet. The "bird in the hand" section has a clever heading and then one long,
level sentence that never pays the heading off. The XR image appears only in the last paragraph, explained in a
subordinate clause, so the ending leans on the pitch to have set it up.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "The other is a recession that arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, which makes the ladder dearer, and it
   shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind her 2028 deposit, which has to fill the gap. Even then, in our
   model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it." The most important
   paragraph in the piece (what "high certainty" means) is its stiffest. The relative clauses pile up, and the last
   sentence puts three qualifiers before its verb. He would build this as one long, clean causal sentence and land it.
2. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they
   should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." Two ideas jammed into
   one sentence with commas inside commas. This is the toy explanation (whose money goes where) and needs to be told
   in steps.
3. "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at the floor
   plus half her stocks." Level, single-speed, no turn. It sits under the wittiest heading in the piece and does nothing
   with it.

## Entry 3 (ranked 2)

**What sounds like him.** Sentence for sentence, this is the sharpest entry. "One in three is not a risk. It is a
forecast." has exactly his not-X-it-is-Y verdict after a long sentence. "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A
January cannot." makes a month the opponent, which is his kind of everyday image used as proof. "A high degree of
certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective" is pure opinionated columnist, and it
recalls his taste for listing how things fail. "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, and charge for it" is
understatement aimed at the right target. The close returns to the opening's promise and hope and turns them, which is
his callback ending. "Ten redemptions, one a year" slips the R in quietly.

**What sounds generic.** The texture is too even. Nearly every paragraph ends on an aphorism, and most of the
aphorisms are mirrored pairs (promise/buy, return/calendar, investor/January, list/adjective, icing/cake, need/dollar).
His essays do this too, but they space the pairs between long stretches of evidence; here the pairs are the evidence,
so it starts to sound like a set of lines rather than a person thinking. The opponent is shot in the same sentence that
introduces it, so the concession never breathes. The opening paragraph is a definition, not a claim.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "In our model a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 richer than ours – and about one time in three it
   lands below its own range or misses a cheque." The other side's best number and its refutation share one sentence,
   with "our model" in the sentence before as well. He would let the opponent finish speaking first.
2. "Hence Caplet XR: extended release, with an X for ten." "Hence" is a textbook word, and spelling out the X explains
   the joke after the reader has already got it.
3. "A named top can only be missed, so the promised half moves into Treasuries the day she names it." A riddle followed
   by a rule. The aphorism half is cryptic and the mechanism half is clipped; the essays never ask the reader to decode
   a line.

## Entry 4 (ranked 3)

**What sounds like him.** It is the most humanly paced of the four: it opens as a story in plain past tense (we
believed X, we tested it, we were wrong), which is honest and readable. "Every cheque therefore rests on one borrower,
on purpose" is a good dry line. "It arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours arrives with neither" is a real money
columnist's joke. "Every holding can be sold any afternoon" has a nice specific word.

**What sounds generic.** It sounds more like a careful finance writer than like him. No headings, so his structural
humour is gone. The opening paragraph is set-up rather than a claim anyone would argue with, and some connectors are
stock narrative ("on paper it looked like the obvious answer", "Then we ran it through our model"). The toy explanation
of the fund ("If the fund goes to nothing ... If it does well ...") is clear but sing-song in its symmetry. The pill
ending repeats the pitch instead of turning something the body built.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "She has taken thoughtful risks all her working life. Stocks beat bonds over six years more often than not." The
   first sentence stretches the case's wording into biography; the second is a generic market maxim with no source.
   Together they read like a template's "establish the counter-view" step.
2. "So our first plan put three-quarters of her money in the stock market, and on paper it looked like the obvious
   answer. Then we ran it through our model..." Stock storytelling phrases. The confession is real; the words carrying
   it are anybody's.
3. "(it will be priced in Taiwan dollars, which nobody can translate for her yet)". The aside is the essays' kind of
   move, but "translate" muddles currency with language, so the joke lands as confusion.

## Entry 1 (ranked 4)

**What sounds like him.** The ideas and a few lines are right: "Our model sank it", the ten one-year bills reframe,
"minus the bank and its fee", and the pill-crushing aside in the last paragraph. "She has already taken the big one:
the residency" is a good turn.

**What sounds generic.** Almost every sentence is a compressed fact. There is no heading, no long building sentence,
no paragraph that lets an opponent speak; the 60/40 case is dropped into the middle of the floor paragraph and refuted
in the next sentence. It reads like a summary of an argument, not the argument, and the mechanism language (a leak, a
catch, a ladder, a floor, a pill) is several images competing rather than one that argues.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten,
   R for redemption." A glossary entry. It explains the joke twice and makes the name the subject of a paragraph.
2. "The ladder has one leak – early cash earns less if yields fall – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031,
   before she names her range." Jargon-compressed: three steps of mechanism in one sentence that the reader must
   unpack, with no voice in it.
3. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5%, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her almost $200,000 richer by 2042. But
   about one time in three, that portfolio lands below its own range or misses a cheque." The piece's central objection
   and its answer, crammed into the wrong paragraph with no room, so the strongest moment of argument goes by like a
   footnote.

## Rewrite plan for Entry 2

Seven changes plus trims to pay for them. Same facts and numbers, no new biography, no new claims. With all of them
applied, a plain word count of the IPS (headings included) moves from 487 to 500; the team should re-run its strict
count, and dropping "Her first cheque is six years off." (change 2) is the cheapest way to buy room if needed.

1. **Make "high certainty" a list, and build the recession sentence cleanly.**
   Current: "Only two things can break a cheque. One is a US government default. The other is a recession that arrives
   before we buy: it pushes yields down, which makes the ladder dearer, and it shrinks the advances, speaking and
   licensing behind her 2028 deposit, which has to fill the gap. Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a
   deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
   Replace: "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective. Ours is short. The US government defaults. Or a
   recession arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, so the ladder costs more, and it shrinks the advances,
   speaking and licensing behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap. Even after a two-point fall, our model says
   a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, covers it."
   Why: it calls back the quoted criterion from the first paragraph and takes a position on it; the long sentence now
   carries cause and effect without "which ... which"; the number lands at the end of its sentence.

2. **Let the opponent speak in its own short sentences before the turn.**
   Current: "Laura has been "willing to take thoughtful risks", and in our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a
   60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."
   Replace: "Laura is "willing to take thoughtful risks". Her first cheque is six years off. In our model, with world
   stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."
   Why: the essays give the other side its case in blunt, separate sentences, so the "But" that follows has something
   to hit. "Six years" is just 2027 to 2033, already in the piece.

3. **Give the reframe its own two sentences, and cut a repeated qualifier.**
   Current: "about one time in three, by the same model, the 60/40 lands below its own range or misses a cheque. Laura
   doesn't owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills, and a crash..."
   Replace: "about one time in three the 60/40 lands below its own range or misses a cheque. Laura doesn't owe one
   ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash..."
   Why: the model is already named twice in this section; the reframe is the idea a judge will repeat, and a full stop
   lets it land before the long sentence that ends on "printed years in advance".

4. **Tell the floor as a toy, in steps.**
   Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the
   interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund."
   Replace: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, just enough to grow back into all of it.
   What's left – the interest those bonds should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums – buys one world
   index fund."
   Why: one step per sentence, whose money goes where, with the spaced dashes the essays use for the aside; then
   "A crash costs her icing, never cake" lands as the short line it is.

5. **Make the range paragraph turn, under its heading.**
   Current: "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at
   the floor plus half her stocks."
   Replace: "In 2031 she names co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028: a fact, not a
   forecast. The top is the floor plus half her stocks."
   Why: short declaratives say the floor flat, which is the confidence the section needs, and "half her stocks" now
   sits alone as the half bush the heading promised.

6. **Thread the pill into the reserve, and keep the name subtle.**
   Current: "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve
   runs itself down with nothing to rebalance. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule that
   works only when swallowed whole."
   Replace: "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before
   its cheque, with nothing to rebalance. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten doses,
   one a year, and it only works whole."
   Why: "ten doses, one a year" gives the X without a glossary and ties the image to the ten cheques, so the two-word
   close that follows answers something the paragraph built rather than something only the pitch said.

7. **Pitch: stop saying "buys" four times.**
   Current: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for her gift."
   Replace: "Her 2028 deposit sets a floor that grows back into itself; only its interest chases the gift, in world
   stocks."
   Why: "We buy it" is the pitch's hook; repeating "buys" three more times blunts it. "Only" carries the
   risk-limit idea in one word. The pitch stays at 50 words.

8. **Trims to pay for the above.** "most of the ways her money could go wrong" becomes "most ways her money could go
   wrong"; "And in our model" (in change 2) loses its "And". Everything else in Entry 2 stays as written, including the
   headings, the opening one-liner, "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be.", "selling low, on a timetable
   printed years in advance", "minus the bank (and its fee)", and the two-word close.
