# Reader 8 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Brief: judge how much each of four anonymous entries sounds like the author of the three essays at his best, crossed
with a personable finance writer, arguing about money; and how much it sounds generic, compressed or machine-made.
Investment merit is not judged, and nothing here guesses at who or what wrote an entry. The essays are not quoted
anywhere below. Their moves are described instead.

## Ranking

1. **Entry 1**
2. **Entry 3**
3. **Entry 4**
4. **Entry 2**

What separates first from last: Entry 1 tells a story with a real opponent and a real confession, and lets its long
sentences build before a short one lands. Entry 2 reads as a run of short, equal-length statements, with its one image
explained like a label and its concession buried in the middle of a paragraph about something else.

The margin between 1 and 3 is narrow. Entry 3 has the cleaner sentences. Entry 1 has more of the author in it: a
confession that backs up its "we made it first", a dry put-down of the opponent's best word, a line about Laura's appetite
for risk that only someone arguing could write, and a close that sounds like a warning on a pill packet. Entry 3 keeps the
claim of a confession but drops the confession itself, so its best section rests on a gesture.

## How the essays move (the yardstick)

- **A thesis that argues from the first sentence.** Each essay opens formally, states a position someone would fight,
  and soon snaps into two or three very short sentences.
- **Concede in full, then "But".** The opponent gets a fair paragraph with their best evidence before the turn.
- **Build long, land short.** A long sentence that carries a mechanism, clause on clause, closed by a blunt line, often
  set as its own paragraph.
- **Jokes at the end, made of homely objects.** A household thing dropped into a formal sentence, usually as the last
  clause of an analytic paragraph. The author never explains his own joke.
- **Pun headings that the prose pays off.** A proverb or catchphrase bent to the topic, sometimes with a bracketed
  afterthought, and the section beneath it often echoes the heading near its end.
- **Arch asides.** A dry concession, a bracket, a mock-polite phrase, rationed to once or twice a page.
- **Numbers said flat, then scaled** to something the reader knows.
- **Endings that come back to the opening object** and finish on a very short line.

## Entry 1 (ranked first)

**Sounds like him.** The opening sentence argues instead of describing: it names the two things and says that mixing
them up is where the danger starts. Then it snaps ("So we don't invest the promise. We buy it."). "Any rung could be sold
any day. None will be." is the long-then-short landing at its smallest. The concession is real and dated: the team's own
first plan, three-quarters in stocks, sunk by its own model, which earns "we made it first". "'Usually' is doing a lot of
work there" is the dry columnist turning the opponent's own word against them, which is the arch aside he rations.
"As for her taste for risk, she has already spent it on the big one" is the strongest sentence in the four entries: an
opinion, with a sting, that comes from the client's own case. Three pun headings, the bracketed "(and its fee)", and a
close that returns to the capsule and ends on a two-word line, the way his essays end.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** Two sentences are clotted with commas, putting several facts in one place: the
$125,000 sentence and the sentence about the 2028 deposit. The pitch's "a floor that grows back into itself" is
puzzling, not witty. The third heading strains the proverb, and the paragraph under it is one definitional sentence that
never pays off the pun.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
   Four commas in a short sentence, and the one number that should reassure the reader is blurred. He says numbers
   flat and then scales them. This one is lost in punctuation.
2. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they
   should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." This is the mechanism
   at the centre of the plan, and it needs a step-by-step explanation a child could follow. Here it is one breathless
   sentence with an unclear "all of it". It should build in two steps.
3. "A bird in the hand, half the bush" and the sentence under it. The heading is the weakest pun of the three and nothing
   below echoes it, unlike his headings, which the section pays off. The paragraph is a flat definition with no turn.

## Entry 3 (ranked second)

**Sounds like him.** The same strong opening and snap. "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective" is a
real columnist's line: an opinion about a word, said with confidence. "'Usually' is the whole trick" and "selling low,
on a timetable printed years in advance" make the opponent's case and then turn it. "A crash can cost her the icing,
never the cake" pays off its heading. "A capped range has a catch" is a plain-spoken set-up for a mechanism, the way a
good money columnist warns the reader. The ending returns to the capsule and closes short.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** It says "we made it first" and then never says what it made, so the confession is
missing from the one section built around it. The list of what could go wrong begins with a bare fragment. The end
piles up three closing lines in a row (the order of priorities, the explained pun, the warning label), which is more
punchlines than one paragraph can carry.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first." On its own this is a gesture. His concessions
   are real and come with their evidence. Either the 75%-stocks plan goes back in, or the line should say less.
2. "The ladder's one leak – coupons that arrive early, reinvested at lower rates if yields fall – we price and set
   aside in 2031." Putting the object first and holding back the verb reads as squeezed. A dry writer would make the
   leak the subject and let it do something.
3. "If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the
   extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole." Two endings in a row, and the
   second spells out its own joke ("ten redemptions, one a year"). He lets the object speak and stops.

## Entry 4 (ranked third)

**Sounds like him.** It shares Entry 3's strong frame (the opening, the "list, not an adjective" line, "a capped range
has a catch"). "And the change buys world stocks" in the pitch is the best small joke in any of the pitches: a homely
word doing real work. The leak is clearer than in Entry 3 because it gets its own plain sentence.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** It cuts the lines that paid off both headings ("selling low, on a timetable" and the
icing and the cake), so "Sell low, by appointment" and "Have your cake and invest the icing" now sit over paragraphs
that never come back to them. Headings with no payoff read as decoration. "Ours swaps the bank's option for shares" puts
the jargon before the explanation. The bracket "(X for ten, R for redemption)" glosses the pun, which he never does.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word to co-sponsors; ours, under one in sixty."
   The comparison is a good number, but the semicolon and the dropped verb make it read like a table cell. With the
   timetable line gone, the heading above has nothing to answer it.
2. "What's left – the interest those bonds should earn – buys one world index fund. Banks sell this as a capital-protected
   note. Ours swaps the bank's option for shares and leaves out the bank (and its fee)." The icing has gone, so the
   heading's joke is orphaned, and "option" comes before any explanation of what an option is.
3. "We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule (X for ten, R for redemption): one cheque a year, and
   it only works whole." The bracket explains the image while it is being introduced, which is how a product label reads.

## Entry 2 (ranked fourth)

**Sounds like him.** The confession comes early and with a number ("about one future in thirty missed a cheque"),
which is honest and specific. "The rare miss is about $1,000" is a number said flat and small, which works. The
rhetorical question about "a high degree of certainty" is answered at once, which is allowed. The last line, cheques
then floor then hope, is a decent close.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** Almost every sentence is between five and fifteen words. Nothing builds, so nothing
lands, and the piece reads like a form. There are no headings. The capsule image is introduced as a definition two
paragraphs in and then explained letter by letter. The opponent's best case (the 60/40 usually leaves her richer) is
tucked into the end of the floor paragraph, after the bank-note joke, when he would give it its own paragraph before the
turn. "Low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones" is a mirrored pair that sounds
neat but adds little. The capsule joke ("you don't crush an XR pill") is placed between dashes in the middle of a
sentence, not at the end where his jokes go.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten,
   R for redemption." The image is explained before it has done anything, letter by letter. He drops the object in and
   lets the reader get it.
2. "What does a high degree of certainty mean? In our model, only two things break a cheque. The US government defaults.
   Or her 2028 deposit can't fill the gap. Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does." Five clipped
   sentences in a row, and "about $125,000 still does" doesn't say what does. This is where a long sentence should carry
   the mechanism (recession, lower yields, a dearer ladder, a smaller deposit).
3. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer. But about one time in
   three, that portfolio misses a cheque or lands below its own range." The essay's central concession is squeezed into
   two sentences at the bottom of a paragraph about the floor. It needs its own paragraph, ahead of the plan, voiced
   fairly before the "But".

## Line-level changes for Entry 1 (ranked first)

Same facts and numbers, roughly the same length, no invented biography.

1. **Pitch.** Current: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for
   her gift." Replace with: "Her 2028 deposit buys bonds that grow back into the whole deposit; their interest buys world
   stocks for her gift." (This keeps the idea and drops the puzzle.)
2. **Sell low, by appointment.** Current: "and we made it first: our first plan put three-quarters of her money in
   stocks". Replace with: "and we made it first: our original plan put three-quarters of her money in stocks". (This
   removes the repeated "first".)
3. **Only two things.** Current: "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not
   $150,000, still fills it." Replace with: "Even after a two-point fall, our model says the deposit could shrink from
   $150,000 to about $125,000 and still fill it." (The number now reads flat, then in scale.)
4. **The "usually" line.** Current: "about one time in three, by the same model, the 60/40 lands below its own range or
   misses a cheque." Replace with: "about one time in three, the same model has the 60/40 landing below its own range,
   or missing a cheque." (One comma, and the worse outcome now lands last.)
5. **Ten one-year bills.** Current: "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in
   the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom – selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance." Replace with:
   "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the
   bottom: selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance." (A short sentence, then the long one builds to the
   heading's payoff.)
6. **Have your cake.** Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all
   of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index
   fund." Replace with: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that mature late in 2032 and grow back into the whole
   deposit. The interest they should earn – about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums – goes into one world index
   fund." (Two steps, in his spaced dashes.)
7. **The icing line.** Current: "A crash costs her icing, never cake." Replace with: "A crash can cost her the icing,
   never the cake." (The articles make it sound spoken, not like a headline.)
8. **A bird in the hand.** Current: "The other half stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars
   comes in dearer." Replace with: "The other half of the bush stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in
   Taiwan dollars comes in dearer." (The section now echoes its heading quietly, as his sections do, without explaining
   it.)
