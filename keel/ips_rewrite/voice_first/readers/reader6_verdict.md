# Reader 6 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Brief: judge the four anonymous entries in `reader6_entries.txt` on voice alone, against the author's three essays
(a private file, described here, never quoted) crossed with a dry, opinionated market-letter writer. Not judged:
investment merit, whether a machine wrote it, compliance with Wharton's form.

## Ranking

1. **Entry 3**
2. **Entry 2**
3. **Entry 1**
4. **Entry 4**

What separates first from last: Entry 3 lets its sentences run when they carry an argument and then shuts the door with a
short one, and it gives the other side a real hearing before turning on it. Entry 4 puts the same facts in sentences of
one length, one after another, with no headings and no opponent until it is too late to matter.

## How the essays move (read twice; described, not quoted)

- **Openings.** A formal statement of the orthodox view, then a few very short sentences that mock it from inside, or a
  huge cold number followed by a physical picture. Within four lines you know whose side he is on.
- **Headings are jokes that argue.** Twisted proverbs, slogans, film lines. Read only the headings and you get the essay.
- **Build long, land short.** One long sentence stacking clauses and spaced en dashes (a definition, three concrete
  nouns, an aside), then a blunt sentence of a few words. Single-sentence paragraphs end most sections.
- **He is not compressed.** He spends words on connective tissue (after all, in other words, indeed, it bears
  emphasising, to put it gently). His sentences breathe. The punch comes from contrast with that looseness, not from
  every line being a punch.
- **Concessions are real.** He names the opponents, says they are armed, gives their argument a full paragraph, then
  turns with "But" and a homely comparison that makes the objection look small.
- **Jokes go at the ends of serious paragraphs**, usually a domestic image dropped into formal prose, or a dry
  parenthesis. Sarcasm is aimed at the other side, never at the reader.
- **Scare quotes** around the opponent's own vocabulary, to hold it at arm's length.
- **Endings call back the opening image** and close on a line of one to three words.

## Entry 1

**Sounds like him.** The two pun headings. "one in three is not a risk. It is a forecast" is exactly his short reversal
after a build. "a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective. Ours has two entries" has a finance
columnist's dryness. "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, and charge for it" is a good deadpan jab. The last
line calls back the opening promise and hope, which is his ending move.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** Almost every paragraph is a string of punches with nothing loose between them, so the
punches stop landing; it reads like a stand-up set, not an essay. There is no fair opponent: the 60/40 case is
reported as a number, never argued for, so the "But" that gives his prose its swing never happens. "Hence" is a
textbook word he would not put there. Several sentences are squeezed until they stop parsing.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "Or a recession arrives before we buy, raising the ladder's price just as it shrinks the advances, speaking and
   licensing behind her 2028 deposit, and that deposit can't fill the gap. In our model, even after a two-point fall in
   yields, about $125,000 of it still does." One sentence carries three mechanisms, and "of it still does" leaves the
   reader guessing what "it" is. This is compression, not voice: the idea needs two sentences and a plain subject.
2. "Our first plan was three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it. In our model a 60/40 portfolio usually ends
   2042 almost $200,000 richer than ours" The confession is good, but it jumps from a 75% plan to a 60/40 portfolio
   with no bridge and no case for the other side. He would build the opponent's argument first and let it stand.
3. "A named top can only be missed, so the promised half moves into Treasuries the day she names it." A real idea
   (the capped-range catch) crushed into an aphorism. It sounds clever and explains nothing; the reader needs the
   step-by-step version (falls come off the gift, rises go to Laura) before the conclusion.

## Entry 2

**Sounds like him.** "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first" is his concession move, and
"But 'usually' is the whole trick" is his turn. "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" is an image that
argues. "minus the bank (and its fee)" is his dry parenthesis. The capped-range paragraph explains the mechanism like a
toy before deciding, and "Do not crush." is a short close that calls back the capsule.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** The first section front-loads the risk list before any argument, so the piece opens
like a disclosure. The pitch is plain reporting with no turn. Caplet XR is explained in a closing clause that reads like
a glossary entry. Headings stop after two, so the second half loses his shape.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective. The US government defaults. Or a recession arrives" The
   list items arrive as bare sentences with no lead-in, which reads as clipped notes rather than a person talking.
2. "The ladder's one leak – coupons that arrive early, reinvested at lower rates if yields fall – we price and set
   aside in 2031." Inverted syntax around a jargon aside; it sounds like a footnote pulled up into the text.
3. "Laura promised her residency $50,000 a year for ten years, and hopes to add a gift. ... only their interest buys
   world stocks." (pitch) Accurate and flat: no hook, no turn, nothing a judge would repeat.

## Entry 3

**Sounds like him.** The opening sentence runs on to carry an idea (most ways her money could go wrong begin with
confusing the two), then lands with the two short sentences. That is his rhythm exactly. The concession is the best of
the four: it owns the first plan, gives the opponent the case's own words in quote marks (his scare-quote habit turned
on the brief), and turns with "'usually' is doing a lot of work there", which has his conversational irony. "she has
already spent it on the big one: the residency" is a dry, opinionated line of the kind a money columnist would kill
for. Three pun headings, the third a twisted proverb in his manner. The close is a callback and a two-word line.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** The range paragraph is flat and uses none of its heading. A few short lines are
punch-for-the-sake-of-punch ("A crash costs her icing, never cake."). The Caplet XR image appears only at the very end,
so it closes the piece without having argued in it. The risk sentence with "$125,000, not $150,000" is knotted.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it." Four
   qualifiers stacked before the verb; the comma-not-comma tangle makes a reader stop. This is where it sounds
   compressed.
2. "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at the floor
   plus half her stocks." The heading promised a bird and a bush; the paragraph delivers a specification. He always
   pays off his heading inside the section.
3. "One is a US government default. The other is a recession" The first failure mode is stated like a form field. He
   would put a dry aside on the absurd one and save the attention for the real one.

## Entry 4

**Sounds like him.** "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart" is plain and good. "low enough to keep
her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones" has a real idea in its symmetry. "you don't crush an XR
pill" as a dashed aside is a nice touch.

**Sounds generic or compressed.** No headings at all, which removes the most recognisable thing about his essays.
Sentences of nearly the same length march in order, each one a fact; there is no long build anywhere. The opponent
(60/40 usually leaves her richer) appears in the middle of the bond paragraph, after the plan is already built, so it is
never answered, only mentioned. The name is explained letter by letter, which kills the joke. The ending is a summary.
It also carries the most numbers, and they are reported, not felt.

**Three passages that most need rewriting**
1. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten,
   R for redemption." Explaining your own pun is the surest way to make it sound written by committee.
2. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer. But about one time in
   three, that portfolio misses a cheque or lands below its own range." The piece's best argument, tucked at the end of
   the wrong paragraph; it needs its own section and a fair hearing.
3. "In our model the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100. The rare miss is about $1,000." Two numbers read off a
   table in a row; nothing tells the reader how to feel about them.

## Entry 3: line-level changes (same facts and numbers, roughly the same length)

1. **Risk sentence (untangle).**
   Current: "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
   Replace: "Even after a two-point fall in yields, our model says a deposit of about $125,000 – not the full $150,000 –
   would still fill it."

2. **First failure mode (dry aside, attention saved for the real one).**
   Current: "One is a US government default. The other is a recession that arrives before we buy:"
   Replace: "One is a US government default, in which case these cheques are not the world's biggest problem. The other,
   the one we actually plan for, is a recession that arrives before we buy:"

3. **Range paragraph (pay off the heading).**
   Current: "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at the
   floor plus half her stocks."
   Replace: "In 2031 she names co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028: the bird in the
   hand. The top adds half her stocks: half the bush."

4. **Opponent given a voice before the turn.**
   Current: "Laura has been \"willing to take thoughtful risks\", and in our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a
   60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."
   Replace: "Laura has been \"willing to take thoughtful risks\", so why not take one here? In our model, with world
   stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours."

5. **Floor sentence (one long build that the reader can follow, then the short land stays).**
   Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the
   interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund."
   Replace: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced so that by then they have grown back
   into the whole deposit; the interest they should earn – about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums – goes into
   one world index fund."

6. **Icing line (less of a slogan, still short).**
   Current: "A crash costs her icing, never cake."
   Replace: "A crash, however bad, can only eat the icing."

7. **Let the image argue earlier (one early touch, so the close is a callback).**
   Current: "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be."
   Replace: "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be. Like a slow-release pill, it works only if nobody crushes it."

8. **Pitch ending (the IPS's last line as a warning label; same word count, so the pitch stays at 50).**
   Current: "Caplet XR sells nothing early."
   Replace: "Caplet XR: do not crush."
