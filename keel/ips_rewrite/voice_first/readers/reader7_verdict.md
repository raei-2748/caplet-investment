# Reader 7 verdict: voice only (31 Oct 2026)

Brief: judge how much each of four anonymous entries sounds like the author of the three essays, crossed with a
personable finance writer, arguing about money; and how much it sounds generic, compressed or machine-made.
Investment merit not judged. AI-authorship not guessed. The essays are not quoted anywhere below; their moves are
described.

## Ranking

1. **Entry 3**
2. **Entry 1**
3. **Entry 4**
4. **Entry 2**

What separates first from last: Entry 3 argues in long sentences and lands in short ones, puts its jokes where he
puts his (the end of a paragraph, a pun in a heading, an aside in brackets), and cuts by dropping whole ideas. Entry 2
is a tidy run of same-length declaratives with the one joke buried mid-sentence and the acronym spelled out like a
product label.

## How the essays move (the yardstick)

Read for movement rather than content, the author does a small number of things again and again:

- **Opens formal, then snaps.** A definitional, multi-clause sentence or two, then a burst of two or three very short
  sentences, often in the opponent's voice. The snap is where the reader sits up.
- **Concedes for real, at length, then turns on "But".** The opponent is given a full paragraph in fair terms, including
  his best evidence, before the turn. The concession is never a token clause.
- **Builds long, lands short.** A paragraph of eight to twelve sentences that lays out a mechanism, closed by one blunt
  line, frequently set as its own one-line paragraph.
- **Jokes go at the end.** The joke is almost always the last clause of a long analytic paragraph, or a one-liner
  paragraph directly after it. It is a homely object dropped into a formal sentence: a courtroom costume, a kitchen
  utensil used against the ocean, a bar-room dare, a piece of domestic furniture on a raft, a vending machine. Never
  an abstract antithesis.
- **Pun headings, with brackets.** Every section has a heading that is a proverb or a catchphrase bent to the topic,
  sometimes with a parenthetical afterthought. The headings are sly; the prose under them is not.
- **Arch courtroom register, once or twice a page.** A dry "very well, let us test it", a "to put it gently", an "alas",
  an "it bears emphasising". Willing to be rude about the opponent's hangers-on, fair to the opponent's argument.
- **Numbers said flat, then scaled.** A statistic, then a comparison to something the reader knows (ten times the
  general rate). Numerals, not words. British spelling, spaced en dashes, scare quotes around the opponent's key word.
- **Triads for rhythm, used deliberately.** Three parallel sentences starting the same way, once per essay, at the
  climax. Also the "not X in any naive sense; more Y; more Z" repair.
- **Endings are short and physical.** A callback to the opening image (the extinct species, the farm animals), then a
  two-word or one-word final paragraph. He stops; he does not sum up.
- **One domestic analogy carries each essay** (the broken car, the house insurance, the vending machine) and is paid
  off once.

A personable finance writer adds: the toy explanation before the jargon; whose money goes where; a willingness to say
"this is the whole trick" or "that word is doing a lot of work"; one admitted mistake, in the past tense.

## Entry 1

**What sounds like him.** The open ("one promise and one hope") followed by the short turn ("So we don't invest the
promise. We buy it.") is his formal-then-snap. "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first" is the
real concession. The two pun headings are his habit exactly. The aside "minus the bank (and its fee)" is his bracketed
afterthought. "'usually' is the whole trick" is the finance columnist's idiom. "selling low, on a timetable printed
years in advance" is a mechanism made into an image. "Do not crush." is a short physical ending with a callback.

**What sounds generic or machine-made.** The pitch's second and third sentences run on colons and semicolons with the
verbs thinned out ("buys it, in Treasuries maturing before each January", "Treasuries worth all of it by 2032; only
their interest buys world stocks"): the compression the guide calls AI squeezing. The certainty paragraph is an
aphorism followed by three mechanisms in one breath. The final paragraph stacks three separate ideas (the reserve, the
priority rule, the name) before the kicker. Overall it reads like Entry 3's skeleton with less air in it.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective." then the two failure modes, then "The ladder's one leak
   – coupons that arrive early, reinvested at lower rates if yields fall – we price and set aside in 2031." The
   opening is an "X, not Y" slogan where he would put a plain claim; the leak sentence is object-first inversion
   (subject arrives after the dash), which no one speaks aloud; and "advances, speaking and licensing" arrives as an
   unexplained noun list.
2. The pitch, sentences two and three (see above). Say it across a table and the verbs are missing.
3. "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before its
   cheque, with nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope. We
   call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole."
   Three colons in three sentences, three ideas, then the kicker. He would give the name its own breath and drop one
   of the three.

## Entry 2

**What sounds like him.** "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart" is plain and true. The
confession in flat past tense ("We started with three-quarters in stocks. Our model sank it") is the F3 move done
right. "she has already taken the big one: the residency" is a real turn. "The rare miss is about $1,000" is the odd,
specific detail that proves a person was there. The closing priority rule is a decision, not a slogan.

**What sounds generic or machine-made.** No headings, so none of his puns. Almost every sentence is eight to fifteen
words; nothing builds, so nothing lands. The acronym is decoded letter by letter ("X for ten, R for redemption"),
which is a product insert, not a writer: he never explains his own puns. The one joke ("you don't crush an XR pill")
is parked mid-sentence between dashes, where he would never put it. Paragraph eight scrambles the order of ideas:
floor, interest, index fund, bank's note, and only then the 60/40 comparison, which belongs with the confession four
paragraphs earlier. The result is clear, honest and flat: a well-organised summary of someone else's argument.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "So we built Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule. Ours also means X for ten,
   R for redemption." Explaining the name kills it. The guide wants XR subtle; he would let the pun sit and move on.
2. The paragraph beginning "Most of it buys a floor" and ending "lands below its own range." Five facts in a row with
   the opponent's case tacked on at the end as a sixth; the concession arrives after the plan it argues against.
3. "Nothing is sold before it pays – you don't crush an XR pill – and each turns to cash before its cheque, so the
   reserve runs itself down." The joke is in the middle; the sentence then explains past it. Also a sample of the
   uniform rhythm: "Her 2028 deposit plugs any gap in the ladder, then builds her gift." Read ten of these aloud and
   it is a form.

## Entry 3

**What sounds like him.** The first sentence is the most him of anything in the four entries: a plain statement that
grows a second clause which is itself an opinion ("most of the ways her money could go wrong begin with confusing the
two"). The case's phrase in inverted commas ("with a high degree of certainty", "willing to take thoughtful risks") is
his scare-quote habit, used to hold a phrase up to the light rather than to sneer. "Any rung could be sold any day.
None will be." is the two-beat landing. The concession and the confession are fused into one honest sentence ("we made
it first: our first plan put three-quarters of her money in stocks, until our own model sank it"), which is the
strongest F3 in the set. "'usually' is doing a lot of work there" is the finance writer's voice. "As for her taste for
risk, she has already spent it on the big one: the residency" is a turn he would be pleased with. Three pun headings
and a bracketed aside. The icing line and "Do not crush." land where his jokes land. It also cuts the way he cuts: the
capped-range mechanism and the priority rule are dropped whole, and what remains is at full size.

**What sounds generic or machine-made.** The pitch's "a floor that grows back into itself" is cryptic shorthand that
only makes sense after reading the IPS; "Caplet XR sells nothing early" is a tagline. "Treasury funds that mature like
bonds" is a label without its toy. The $125,000 sentence stacks three qualifiers before its subject. The floor
paragraph, the one mechanism that most needs the whose-money-goes-where explanation, is delivered as inventory. And
three proverb puns plus the capsule is one image more than the ear wants in 500 words.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. Pitch: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for her gift.
   Caplet XR sells nothing early." A reflexive metaphor and a slogan in the fifty words where the verbs matter most.
2. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
   Three adverbial qualifiers, then a parenthetical "not $150,000", then the verb. Nobody says this.
3. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest
   they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." "All of it" has
   no referent; the mechanism is squeezed into one relative clause; the sentence lists where he would explain.

## Entry 4

**What sounds like him.** "one in three is not a risk. It is a forecast." has the exact shape of his best courtroom
line (a strong noun denied, then a stronger one supplied in a four-word sentence). "A January cannot." is a
personification he would enjoy. "a soothing adjective" has his dryness. "one world index fund of thousands of
companies" is the concrete detail. "Any rung can be sold any day. None will be." again.

**What sounds generic or machine-made.** Too many of those lines, too close together: "not a risk. It is a forecast",
"never the return. It was the calendar", "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot" are three
antitheses in nine lines, and the ear hears the rhythm coming. His antitheses land on an object (a costume, a
teaspoon); these land on abstractions (forecast, calendar). "It sits, it matures, it becomes the cheque" is a triad
used as a connector, then "Hence Caplet XR", which is a textbook's word. "Ours has two entries" is list-speak. "A
named top can only be missed" keeps the conclusion and throws away the toy explanation that Entries 1 and 2 give
(every fall comes off the gift, every rise past the top goes to Laura): that is machine compression. The closing
line is a mirrored slogan bolted on after the piece has already ended on the priority rule. The pitch's "Only the
interest chases the hope" is a tagline, and "finishes it, then a floor that grows back into itself" has lost its verb.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. The run from "For money that keeps a residency's doors open" to "A January cannot." Keep one of the three
   antitheses (the first is the best), and let the other two ideas be said plainly.
2. "It sits, it matures, it becomes the cheque – ten redemptions, one a year. Hence Caplet XR: extended release, with
   an X for ten." The triad, the dash, the "Hence", the colon: four connectors doing the work of one "so".
3. "A named top can only be missed, so the promised half moves into Treasuries the day she names it." plus the last
   line "The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar that it does." The first is a conclusion
   without its mechanism; the second is a slogan where he would stop on a thing.

## Entry 3: eight line-level changes

Same facts and numbers; roughly the same length; nothing added about Laura or the team that the entry does not
already say.

1. **Pitch.** Current: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for
   her gift. Caplet XR sells nothing early."
   Replace: "Her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that grow back to its full size by 2032, and only the interest they
   earn buys world stocks for her gift. Nothing is sold early."
   Why: puts the verbs back; the reflexive metaphor becomes a thing a listener can picture; the brand-name tagline
   goes, and the name is left for the IPS where it has room.

2. **The ladder sentence.** Current: "as a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each
   paying out before its own January."
   Replace: "as a ladder of Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that wind up and pay out on a fixed date the way a bond
   does, each falling due before its own January."
   Why: the toy before the label. "Wind up" is the plain word, and it is the kind of small specific he keeps.

3. **The two failure modes.** Current: "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000,
   not $150,000, still fills it."
   Replace: "Even then, our model says, if yields fall two whole points, about $125,000 of her $150,000 deposit still
   fills the gap."
   Why: subject, verb, object, in that order; the two numbers sit side by side where a judge can compare them.

4. **The concession's turn.** Current: "about one time in three, by the same model, the 60/40 lands below its own range
   or misses a cheque."
   Replace: "about one time in three, by the same model, the 60/40 lands below the range it named, or misses a cheque
   outright."
   Why: ends on the worse outcome, said flat, so the sentence lands instead of trailing; "it named" gives the range a
   human act behind it.

5. **The floor paragraph.** Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back
   into all of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one
   world index fund."
   Replace: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, just enough of them that by then they
   have grown back into the whole deposit. The interest they would have paid along the way, about a fifteenth of the
   portfolio by our sums, buys one world index fund instead."
   Why: whose money goes where, in two sentences, with "the whole deposit" replacing "all of it". This is the one
   mechanism the piece must make a smart fourteen-year-old follow.

6. **The icing line.** Current: "A crash costs her icing, never cake."
   Replace: "A crash can cost her the icing. It cannot touch the cake."
   Why: the small words back in, and the two-beat landing he uses; the squeezed version reads as a slogan.

7. **The third heading.** Current: "A bird in the hand, half the bush" as a heading over a two-sentence paragraph.
   Replace: delete the heading and let the range paragraph sit under "Have your cake and invest the icing", opening
   "In 2031 the range she names to co-sponsors starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 ..."
   Why: his headings head sections, not single paragraphs, and four images (cake, bird, capsule, appointment) in 500
   words is one more than the ear wants. The cake image already covers the half she keeps.

8. **The reserve sentence.** Current: "Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with
   nothing to rebalance."
   Replace: "Each turns to cash just before its cheque falls due, so the reserve runs itself down, and there is
   nothing left to rebalance, trade or second-guess."
   Why: a long sentence that builds, right before the short last two ("We call the plan Caplet XR ..." and "Do not
   crush."), so the ending is earned by contrast rather than arriving after another clipped line.

Leave alone: the first sentence, "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be.", the fused confession in the "Sell
low" section, "'usually' is doing a lot of work there", "spent it on the big one: the residency", "minus the bank
(and its fee)", and "Do not crush." Those are the lines that sound like him.
