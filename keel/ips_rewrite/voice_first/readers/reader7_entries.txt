===== Entry 1 =====
PITCH:
Laura promised her residency $50,000 a year for ten years, and hopes to add a gift. Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise: her first $300,000 buys it, in Treasuries maturing before each January. Her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries worth all of it by 2032; only their interest buys world stocks.

IPS:
Laura Gao has one promise and one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone with a high degree of certainty. The hope is a gift towards the residency's building in Taiwan.

So we don't invest the promise. We buy it.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques – or nearly – as Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one falling due before each January.

A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective. The US government defaults. Or a recession arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, so the ladder costs more, and it shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap. The ladder's one leak – coupons that arrive early, reinvested at lower rates if yields fall – we price and set aside in 2031.

Sell low, by appointment

There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first. Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and in our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours.

But "usually" is the whole trick: about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or falls short of the range it named. Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom – selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance.

Have your cake and invest the icing

Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, just enough to grow back into all of it: her floor. What's left – the interest those bonds should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums – buys one world index fund. A crash can cost her the icing, never the cake. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours is the same note, minus the bank (and its fee).

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028. The top is the floor plus half her stocks. The other half stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer.

A capped range has a catch: once it is named, every fall in stocks comes off the gift while every rise past the top goes to Laura. So on the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries, and in our model the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before its cheque, with nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole.

Do not crush.

===== Entry 4 =====
PITCH:
Laura has one promise and one hope. Caplet XR doesn't invest the promise; it buys it. Her first $300,000 all but buys ten $50,000 cheques, in Treasuries due before each January. Her 2028 deposit finishes it, then a floor that grows back into itself. Only the interest chases the hope.

Laura Gao has one promise and one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone, with a high degree of certainty. The hope is a gift to her residency's building in Taiwan.

So we don't invest the promise. We buy it.

Sell low, by appointment

Our first plan was three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it. In our model a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 richer than ours – and about one time in three it lands below its own range or misses a cheque. For money that keeps a residency's doors open, one in three is not a risk. It is a forecast.

The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar. Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills, and a portfolio that meets a crash in the wrong year must sell at the bottom to pay the one that is due. A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each falling due before the January it pays. Any rung can be sold any day. None will be. It sits, it matures, it becomes the cheque – ten redemptions, one a year. Hence Caplet XR: extended release, with an X for ten.

A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective. Ours has two entries. The US government defaults. Or a recession arrives before we buy, raising the ladder's price just as it shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind her 2028 deposit, and that deposit can't fill the gap. In our model, even after a two-point fall in yields, about $125,000 of it still does.

Have your cake and invest the icing

The rest of the deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced to grow back into the whole sum; the interest they would have earned – about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums – goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies. A crash can cost her icing, never cake. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, and charge for it.

In 2031 Laura names co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028; the top adds half her stocks. The other half stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer. A named top can only be missed, so the promised half moves into Treasuries the day she names it.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, turning to cash one January at a time, with nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope.

The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar that it does.
