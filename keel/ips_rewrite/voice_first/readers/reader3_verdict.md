# Reader 3 verdict: voice only

Judged against the essays' actual moves, as I heard them on a second, sentence-by-sentence read:

- **Argues, doesn't report.** Each essay states a thesis early, then picks up the strongest objection and answers it ("this proves less than it appears to"). The reader is being talked out of something.
- **Rhythm.** A long, many-clause sentence carrying a dash or two, then a short flat verdict. Paragraphs often land on a fragment or a one-line turn, not a summary.
- **Humour is deadpan and structural.** Section headings are twisted idioms that turn out to be on-topic. Inside paragraphs the jokes are one flat absurd image delivered as a verdict, or a parenthetical aside. Never a run of gags, never a wink.
- **Analogy as proof.** One everyday comparison (a car, an insurance policy) carries a whole argument. It is not decoration.
- **Contempt with manners.** Mild moral edge against a named target (here, banks and their fees), understatement, "to put it gently."
- **Callbacks.** The ending reuses an image from the opening and turns it.
- **Numbers sit in the argument.** They are used as evidence in a sentence with a verb, not stacked as spec.

---

## Entry 1

**(a) 5/10.** The ideas are right, but almost every paragraph is two or three claims squeezed into one. There is no sustained objection-and-answer. The stocks case gets one sentence and a stat. There are no headings, so none of the essays' structural humour is available. The jokes that survive (the pill, the bank) are carried by content, not by timing. It reads like a good summary of an argument, not the argument.

**(b) Most alive:**
1. "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom." The best single move in the entry: a reframe in two short sentences, then the consequence. (Entries 2 to 5 have it too.)
2. "It's a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee." Compact, and the target is named.
3. "Nothing is sold before it pays – you don't crush an XR pill". Deadpan, and it earns its dash.

**Deadest:**
1. "The ladder has one leak – early cash earns less if yields fall – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031, before she names her range." Jargon-compressed. A reader has to unpack it, and it carries no voice.
2. "A capped range has a catch. Once it's named, a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past the top goes to Laura." Term-sheet prose. The "catch" tease is promised and then paid off in dry mechanics.
3. "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5%, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her almost $200,000 richer by 2042. But about one time in three, that portfolio lands below its own range or misses a cheque." The central objection and its answer are crammed into two sentences with no room to breathe.

**(c) Forced or off:** The "X for ten, R for redemption" gloss is bolted straight after the pill line, so it explains the joke. The closing "the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope" ends on a rule, not a turn. Nothing is cutesy, but only because little is attempted.

## Entry 2

**(a) 8/10.** This is the only entry that uses the pun-heading move, and it uses it well. "Bull in a cheque shop" is a heading that is also a fact about the plan. The asymmetry paragraph (too careful, too brave) argues instead of describing. The first plan is steelmanned honestly. The opening gives a thesis in a pair of tidy sentences. What holds it back is a garbled central sentence, a thin middle, and a heading or two that are only decoration.

**(b) Most alive:**
1. "Bull in a cheque shop" (heading). On-topic, quick, and it sets up the steelman.
2. "Too careful, and the gift comes out smaller than it might have been. Too brave, and a residency built on her word opens one January without the money to run it." Balanced and concrete, and the second half has a real picture in it.
3. "She has already taken the big one. It is called the residency." A flat, understated landing after a long sentence, which is exactly the right shape.

**Deadest:**
1. "The US government defaults, or yields fall before we buy, the ladder costs more than $300,000, and her 2028 deposit – earned from advances, speaking and licensing, which shrink in the very recessions that make bonds dearer – cannot fill the gap." It is syntactically muddled (a list that is secretly a chain), with an aside buried inside an aside. It is the most important sentence in the piece and the least clear.
2. "Most of that deposit buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032, which by then grow back into the whole of it; the rest – about what those Treasuries will earn – goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies." Fact-sheet cadence with a semicolon and a dash holding it together.
3. "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, each turning to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance." Procedure, with no stance.

**(c) Forced or off:** "Cheques and balances" is a pun for its own sake: the heading does not say what the section argues. "Money where her mouth is" has the same problem, and it is a stock idiom. Four punning headings in about 450 words is one too many, and the repeated one-line ending ("Nobody crushes the pill.") is a little twee.

## Entry 3

**(a) 8/10.** The most authorial *stance* in the set. It argues about the client's own phrase: "a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective" treats a word as a suspect, which is exactly what the essays do to terms like "choice" and "justice." It has a fragment turn, a good last line, and decent headings. It loses points because the middle paragraphs are compressed and the range paragraph is a tangle.

**(b) Most alive:**
1. "Keep them in one pot and, sooner or later, the hope eats the promise. / So we didn't." A claim, then a two-beat dry turn. The most essay-like shape in the batch.
2. "A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective." Interrogates a loaded term and takes a position.
3. "The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar it does." A clean aphoristic ending with a real edge on it.

**Deadest:**
1. "A named top has a catch: a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past it goes to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries." Spec language dropped into an otherwise talky paragraph, so the voice goes away exactly when the content gets hard.
2. "The same dose, on schedule, never crushed." (pitch, last line). A fragment that is cryptic for a cold reader. It is a joke before the setup.
3. "Most of her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced to grow back into all of it." Plain brochure.

**(c) Forced or off:** "Money where her mouth is" is the stock idiom again and doesn't say anything. "Hence Caplet XR." is fine, but the pill gag appears three times in short order (pitch, gloss, end of the reserve paragraph) and is spent by the last line. "Two ways to bounce a cheque" is good, but following it with "then nothing anywhere is safe" is a slight rhetorical flourish.

## Entry 4

**(a) 9/10.** The only entry that reads like a person building an argument from start to finish. Concession first (stocks win over six years, so we did it), then the turn, then the diagnosis in two short sentences, then the consequence. Sentence lengths alternate the way the essays' do. The bank joke is handed to the reader as a deadpan comparison, not explained. No headings, which costs it the essays' structural wit, but it has the strongest paragraphs.

**(b) Most alive:**
1. "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot." The best line in the whole set: the idea in eleven words, a long thing set against a short thing.
2. "The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar." A reframe in the essay author's exact shape: deny the expected culprit, name the real one.
3. "A bank sells exactly this and calls it a capital-protected note; it arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours arrives with neither." Dry, specific and aimed at someone.

**Deadest:**
1. "Whatever the ladder does not need of the 2028 deposit is for the gift." Pure plumbing, and nobody would say it aloud.
2. "...buys one world index fund: thousands of companies, dozens of countries." A reflex diversification list, with a dead colon.
3. "Or, in our model, yields fall before we finish buying, the ladder costs more than she has, and her 2028 deposit cannot fill the gap – though even after a two-point fall, about $125,000 of that $150,000 still fills it." Too many clauses with no resting place. A dense sentence is fine for this writer, but this one has no landing.

**(c) Forced or off:** "She has taken thoughtful risks all her working life" asserts a biography the case doesn't give, and it reads as filler. The Taiwan-dollar parenthesis ("which nobody can translate for her yet") is a muddled joke: "translate" is doing double duty. Ending on the pill echo from the pitch is slightly repetitive, though the callback itself is the right instinct.

## Entry 5

**(a) 7.5/10.** It has the best opening move in the set ("we don't invest the promise, we buy it": a thesis in two short sentences) and the most explicit steelman ("a serious case against all this, and we made it first"). The cost-of-selling-low line is very good. But the headings overreach: two of three are proverb mashups, and one means nothing. The risk paragraph is a long relative-clause chain, and "doing a lot of work" is a stock phrase.

**(b) Most alive:**
1. "So we don't invest the promise. We buy it." Thesis, delivered flat.
2. "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance" A vivid, original image for the real risk.
3. "A crash costs her icing, never cake." Lands the whole structure in a domestic image (it is the best of the three food lines, and it is only good because it is short).

**Deadest:**
1. "But 'usually' is doing a lot of work there" A worn phrase of the kind commentators use as a lever, with no new thought in it.
2. "The other is a recession that arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, which makes the ladder dearer, and it shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind her 2028 deposit, which has to fill the gap." Relative clauses stacked to the point of fog.
3. "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance." Procedure again, and nearly word for word the same as in the others.

**(c) Forced or off:** "A bird in the hand, half the bush" is a mangled idiom that the section never earns. "Have your cake and invest the icing" is cutesy, and two stacked proverb-twists in three headings reads as a joke machine. The "XR" explanation sits in the last paragraph as an afterthought.

## Ranking, best first

1. **Entry 4**
2. **Entry 3**
3. **Entry 5**
4. **Entry 2**
5. **Entry 1**

Entry 4 is #1 because it is the only one that actually argues like the essays' author: it concedes the opposing case, turns on a two-sentence reframe, and spends its jokes (the brochure and the fee) as deadpan comparisons inside paragraphs of real length, not as headings or tags.

(Entry 2 has the best single heading and the best structural instinct, but it is held back by a garbled key sentence and weaker paragraphs; it could easily swap with Entry 5.)

## What Entry 4 needs to sound even more like the essays' author

- **Two or three on-topic pun headings**, not four. The heading should say what the section argues. The strongest candidates are one over the stocks-then-turn paragraph and one over the bank-note paragraph. Entry 2's "Bull in a cheque shop" is the model for how a heading should work.
- **One bigger, flatter, more absurd image** for the three-quarters-in-stocks plan or for the bank's fee, delivered as a verdict ("to put it gently" energy). Entry 4 is wry throughout but never commits to a single ridiculous picture.
- **Interrogate one loaded word** the way Entry 3 does with "a high degree of certainty": take the client's phrase, say what it ought to mean, and give a short list.
- **Fix or cut the dead plumbing**: delete "Whatever the ladder does not need..." and the "thousands of companies, dozens of countries" list, break up the two-point-fall sentence so it can land, and drop "all her working life."
- **Let the ending turn the opening**, not just repeat the pitch's pill line. The opening's "the first is six years away" and the "A January cannot" idea are ready to be called back to, with the wrong-year crash as the closing image.
