# Reader 2 verdict: voice only

Read: the three essays (twice), then `reader2_entries.txt`. Nothing else. Investment merit and authorship (human or machine) are not judged here; only whether each text sounds like the essays' author arguing about money.

## What the essays' author actually does (the yardstick, described, never quoted)

1. Opens a section with a general claim about how people think, then tests it on the case. The essays rarely open with a fact; they open with a belief and why it is comforting or wrong.
2. States the opponent's best case generously, often with the opponent's own number, and only then turns on a hinge ("but", "and yet", "this proves less than it appears to" in spirit). Concession-then-reversal is the engine of every essay.
3. Section headings are twisted idioms or puns, and each one carries the section's argument. They are never decoration, and there is about one per 400 words, not one per 100.
4. A long, clause-stacked sentence (dashes for asides) followed by a blunt sentence of two to five words. Paragraphs land on the short one.
5. Analogies are physical and faintly absurd (vehicles, insurance, kitchens, furniture, teaspoons) and are pushed exactly one beat further than expected, then dropped.
6. The reframe "X is not Y. It is Z."; triads; anaphora across three sentences.
7. Parentheses that deflate; single quotes around a word the author distrusts.
8. Sarcasm aimed upward (at the powerful, the comfortable, the people selling the fantasy), never at the reader and never cute. The humour is in the logic, not in winks or exclamation marks.
9. A register drop: formal, essayistic prose that lets in one colloquial phrase and then goes straight back to formal.
10. Endings reverse an earlier phrase and are very short; the final sentence is often two words.
11. Always a moral stake for a real person, stated plainly, before the policy.

## Shared material

All five entries share a spine ("one promise and one hope"; ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill; the model sinking the first plan; one future in thirty; the note minus the bank; you don't crush an XR pill; money she already has). Those lines are scored once, as common property, and the entries are separated by what each does on its own.

## Entry 1

**(a) Likeness: 7/10.** This is the only entry that runs the essays' engine in full: it gives the opposing plan a whole paragraph and its own best number ("almost $200,000 ahead of ours") before turning, which is exactly how the essays treat an opponent. The opening paragraph starts with a general claim about how money goes wrong (confusing two things) rather than with a fact, which is the essays' habitual way in. The headings are twisted idioms that carry the argument. The cake/icing analogy is pushed one beat further ("A crash costs her icing, never cake") and then dropped, which is the essays' exact handling of an image. The deflating parenthesis "(and its fee)" and the two-word close "Do not crush." are both in the author's grammar. Deductions: three pun headings in 500 words is roughly three times the essays' density; the middle paragraphs compress into lists; and one idiom is borrowed rather than made.

**(b) Three most alive**
- "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first: our first plan put three-quarters of her money in stocks, until our own model sank it." The concession stated as a confession, before the turn. The essays do this every time.
- "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom – selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance." Long clause-stacked sentence with a dash and a sardonic coda; the heading above it ("Sell low, by appointment") is the idiom-twist that carries the argument.
- "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours is the same note, minus the bank (and its fee)." Sarcasm aimed upward, parenthesis deflates, short sentence lands.

**(b) Three deadest**
- "In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques – or nearly – as a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each paying out before its own January." Pure mechanism, no stance; the dashes are doing bookkeeping, not thinking.
- "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." One sentence carrying four facts and no attitude; reads like a term sheet.
- "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance." Fact-sheet cadence; the essays never end a section on procedure.

**(c) False, forced, cutesy, show-offy**
- "'usually' is doing a lot of work there" is an internet-forum idiom, not this author's; he makes his own turns rather than borrowing a stock one.
- "A bird in the hand, half the bush" does not parse on its own; the reader has to read the paragraph to decode the heading, which is backwards. The essays' headings land first and the paragraph confirms.
- "Have your cake and invest the icing" is on the edge of cute, but it earns its keep because the next paragraph uses the image to say something true. Keep it, cut the bird.
- Pitch: "a floor that grows back into itself" is slightly mystical for a writer who prefers concrete images.

## Entry 3

**(a) Likeness: 6.5/10.** Has the essays' antithesis ("Too careful... Too brave..."), the kicker after a long sentence ("It is called the residency."), the physical-absurd analogy ("the flat-pack version"), and headings that mostly carry argument. The sentence about the two ways a cheque breaks is the most essay-shaped long sentence in the whole set: a stack of clauses with a dash aside, then a short verb to close. Deductions: four headings in 500 words, two of them on the same cheque pun, which starts to read as a run of jokes rather than one argument; the ending is a sitcom button rather than a reversal; and the plainest mechanism paragraphs are as flat as everyone else's.

**(b) Three most alive**
- "Too careful, and the gift comes out smaller than it might have been. Too brave, and a residency built on her word opens one January without the money to run it." Balanced antithesis with a real person's stake at the end, which is the essays' moral move.
- "Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and she has already taken the big one. It is called the residency." Long then short; the short one reframes.
- "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours is the flat-pack version, minus the bank and its fee." Domestic-absurd analogy, upward sarcasm, and the heading above it ("Some assembly required") sets it up properly.

**(b) Three deadest**
- "In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each one coming due before the January it pays." Same term-sheet sentence as the others.
- "Most of that deposit buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032, which by then grow back into the whole of it; the rest – about what those Treasuries will earn – goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies." The dash aside here is a footnote, not a thought.
- "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, each turning to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance." Procedure with a participle; no voice.

**(c) False, forced, cutesy, show-offy**
- "Bull in a cheque shop" then "Cheques and balances" two headings apart: the second pun on the same word reads as the writer enjoying himself rather than arguing. The essays never repeat a pun's root.
- "Nobody crushes the pill." as a standalone final line is a button, not a reversal; it closes the joke rather than the argument.
- "Money where her mouth is" is a fine heading but is shared verbatim with Entry 2, so it is not this entry's own.

## Entry 4

**(a) Likeness: 6/10.** The best sentence rhythm of the five and the least compressed; it has the essays' long-then-short pattern throughout and the deflating parenthesis ("which nobody can translate for her yet"). "The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar." is the essays' "X is not Y. It is Z." reframe exactly, and "A January cannot." is the kind of personified three-word blow the essays end paragraphs on. But it is a calmer, kinder writer: no headings, no puns, no register drop, and the only sarcasm (the brochure) is gentle. It sounds like a good human essayist; it sounds about half like this one, who is combative, arch and plays with idiom.

**(b) Three most alive**
- "The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar." Reframe in two sentences; the second is four words.
- "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot." Personification plus a three-word sentence; this is the most essay-like line in the entry.
- "A bank sells exactly this and calls it a capital-protected note; it arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours arrives with neither." Upward sarcasm done by parallel, no adjective needed.

**(b) Three deadest**
- "She has taken thoughtful risks all her working life. Stocks beat bonds over six years more often than not." Two assertions laid side by side with no attitude between them; sounds like a briefing.
- "Whatever the ladder does not need of the 2028 deposit is for the gift. Most of it buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032 – just enough of them to grow back into the whole sum by then." Allocation prose.
- "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before its cheque, nothing is rebalanced, nothing is decided." The anaphora is a nice touch but the sentence is still a procedure.

**(c) False, forced, cutesy, show-offy**
- "Ten doses, one a year. Nobody crushes the pill." reads as a tagline, closer to ad copy than to an argument's last word.
- "Every cheque therefore rests on one borrower, on purpose." The shrug of "on purpose" is a little pleased with itself.
- Nothing cutesy otherwise; the fault is the opposite one, too even-tempered to be this author.

## Entry 2

**(a) Likeness: 5.5/10.** Has the single most essay-like sentence in the whole set (the "soothing adjective" line: an attack on a comforting word, which is how all three essays begin), the three-word kicker "So we didn't.", and the right sarcastic aim ("keep a fee for the privilege"). But the prose is clipped into short declaratives throughout, where the essays stack clauses and let a sentence run before cutting it off; the effect is staccato and a little breathless. It also explains its own pun (what X and R stand for), which the essays never do: a pun is trusted to land or it is cut. The closing chiasmus is polished to a shine that reads as writerly rather than argued.

**(b) Three most alive**
- "A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective." The reframe of a comfortable phrase into a demand; this is the essays' opening move applied to a client brief.
- "Keep them in one pot and, sooner or later, the hope eats the promise. / So we didn't." Aphorism then a three-word paragraph.
- "A bank would sell her this exact structure, call it a capital-protected note and keep a fee for the privilege." Triad, upward sarcasm, "for the privilege" is the right dryness.

**(b) Three deadest**
- "In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each due before its January." Term sheet.
- "Most of her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced to grow back into all of it." "Priced to" is product-brochure diction.
- "The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028. The top adds half her stocks; the other half stays hers, to sell any day, for a building priced in Taiwan dollars." Three facts in a row with no connective thought; compression showing.

**(c) False, forced, cutesy, show-offy**
- "Hence Caplet XR. On a pill, XR is extended release: the same dose, on schedule. On ours, X is ten and R is redemption." Explaining the acronym is show-offy; it tells the reader the joke was clever.
- "nothing is sold early – you don't crush an XR pill" is bolted on with a dash; the image does no work in that sentence.
- "The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar it does." Too neat; the essays' last lines are blunt, not balanced.

## Entry 5

**(a) Likeness: 3.5/10.** This is the fact sheet. It opens with "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart", which is an explanation, not a claim about how people think. It asks a FAQ question ("What does a high degree of certainty mean?") and answers it. It has no headings, no idiom-twists, no analogy of its own, and it explains the acronym. Numbers arrive in a cluster (two points, $125,000, 7.5%, 60/40, $200,000, one in three, 97 in 100) with no rhetorical frame around any of them, where the essays set every statistic up and then draw the blood from it. The ending is a procedure. The alive moments are almost all shared property.

**(b) Three most alive**
- "Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, but she has already taken the big one: the residency." The one kicker with a colon.
- "It's a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee." Shared joke, but delivered cleanly.
- "A capped range has a catch." Five words, a real turn; the paragraph after it, unfortunately, goes straight back to mechanism.

**(b) Three deadest**
- "They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart." Explanatory opener; the essays never open by explaining.
- "What does a high degree of certainty mean? Only two things break a cheque." FAQ format.
- "In our model, with world stocks at 7.5%, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her almost $200,000 richer by 2042. But about one time in three, that portfolio lands below its own range or misses a cheque." The concession is here, but as a bare pair of statistics dropped mid-paragraph with no setup and no consequence drawn; the essays would spend a paragraph on it.

**(c) False, forced, cutesy, show-offy**
- "Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption." Explaining the pun.
- "The ladder has one leak – early cash earns less if yields fall – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031, before she names her range." A technical insertion that interrupts the only argumentative paragraph; it reads as a committee's addition.
- "to sell any day for a dearer building" is compressed to the point of sounding like notes.

## Ranking (best first)

1. **Entry 1** (7)
2. **Entry 3** (6.5)
3. **Entry 4** (6)
4. **Entry 2** (5.5)
5. **Entry 5** (3.5)

Why 1 over 3: both have the headings and the analogies, but Entry 1 is the only text that gives the opposing plan a full paragraph and its best number before turning, and that concession-then-reversal is the essays' engine, not an ornament. Why 3 over 4: Entry 4 has the better sentence rhythm, but it is a different temperament; the essays' author is arch and plays with idiom, and Entry 3 does both. Why 4 over 2: Entry 2 has the best single sentence but is clipped throughout, and the essays breathe. Entry 5 is last by a distance.

## What Entry 1 would need to sound even more like the essays' author

1. **Fewer headings, each longer and sharper.** Cut "A bird in the hand, half the bush". Keep two headings at most in 500 words, and let each be the kind of twisted idiom that lands before the paragraph is read. The essays run one per section of 400 words or so.
2. **Let the sentences breathe before the blow.** The author's signature is a long, clause-stacked sentence with a dash aside, then a short one. The ladder and 2028-deposit paragraphs are compressed into lists; unpack one of them into a sentence that has a subordinate clause and an aside, then cut to three words.
3. **Make the sarcasm its own, and aim it at a target.** Replace "'usually' is doing a lot of work there" with a turn built from the essay's own material. The bank is the right target; push "minus the bank (and its fee)" one more beat, the way the essays give an image one extra step and stop.
4. **Say the stake for a person, plainly, once.** Entry 3 has the residency opening a January with no money to run it. Entry 1 never says what a missed cheque means for anyone. The essays always put the human cost before the policy.
5. **One register drop.** The essays are formal prose that lets in a single colloquial phrase and then straightens its tie. Entry 1 has none; one would do.
6. **Close on a reversal, not a label.** "Do not crush." is good and short, but it is a pharmacy warning rather than a turn on something said earlier. The essays' final lines pick up a phrase from the opening and invert it. The opening here ("most of the ways her money could go wrong begin with confusing the two") offers the hook.
7. **Trust the reader with the name.** Entry 1 already does this better than 2 and 5 (it does not spell out X and R). Keep it that way.
