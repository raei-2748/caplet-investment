===== Entry 1 =====
PITCH:
Laura has one promise and one hope; Caplet XR never makes them share. Her first $300,000 all but buys the promise: ten $50,000 cheques, each held in Treasuries due before its January. Her second deposit finishes it, then builds the hope on a floor she owns, topped with world stocks.

IPS:
Laura Gao has made one promise and holds one hope.

The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone, with a high degree of certainty, to keep a creative residency in Taiwan running. The hope is a gift towards the building, a figure she must name to co-sponsors in 2031. So we buy the promise from the US Treasury and leave only the hope to the stock market.

Bull in a cheque shop

Our first plan was three-quarters stocks, and the argument for it was strong enough that we made it ourselves: the gift needs growth, and over ten years stocks usually win. Then our own model sank it. In about one future in thirty, a cheque went unpaid, because Laura does not owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a portfolio that meets a crash in the wrong January must sell at the bottom to pay the one that is due.

The arithmetic is lopsided. Too careful, and the gift comes out smaller than it might have been. Too brave, and a residency built on her word opens one January without the money to run it. Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and she has already taken the big one. It is called the residency.

Cheques and balances

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each one coming due before the January it pays. Any rung can be sold on any trading day; none will be. That is Caplet XR, and like any extended-release pill it works only if nobody crushes it.

Only two things break a cheque. The US government defaults, or yields fall before we buy, the ladder costs more than $300,000, and her 2028 deposit – earned from advances, speaking and licensing, which shrink in the very recessions that make bonds dearer – cannot fill the gap.

Some assembly required

Most of that deposit buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032, which by then grow back into the whole of it; the rest – about what those Treasuries will earn – goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours is the flat-pack version, minus the bank and its fee.

Money where her mouth is

In 2031 Laura names a range. Its bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028, so the least she promises is money she already has. Its top is the floor plus half her stocks; the other half stays hers, to sell on any day, in case a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, each turning to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope.

Nobody crushes the pill.

===== Entry 4 =====
PITCH:
Laura owes ten dated cheques and hopes for one gift. Her first deposit buys the cheques outright – ten Treasuries, one maturing before each January – and only her second deposit chases the gift. We call it Caplet XR: ten doses, one a year. You don't crush an XR pill.

IPS:
Laura owes ten cheques of $50,000, one every January from 2033 to 2042, and the first is six years away. She has taken thoughtful risks all her working life. Stocks beat bonds over six years more often than not. So our first plan put three-quarters of her money in the stock market, and on paper it looked like the obvious answer.

Then we ran it through our model, and about one future in thirty missed a cheque. The flaw was never the return. It was the calendar. Laura does not owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom. A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot.

So in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys the ten cheques, or very nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one falling due just before each January it is for. Every holding can be sold any afternoon – but nothing is sold before it pays. It sits, it matures, it becomes the cheque. We call it Caplet XR, as in extended release.

Only two things break a cheque. The US government defaults. Or, in our model, yields fall before we finish buying, the ladder costs more than she has, and her 2028 deposit cannot fill the gap – though even after a two-point fall, about $125,000 of that $150,000 still fills it. Every cheque therefore rests on one borrower, on purpose.

Whatever the ladder does not need of the 2028 deposit is for the gift. Most of it buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032 – just enough of them to grow back into the whole sum by then. The rest, which is the interest they would otherwise have earned (about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums), buys one world index fund: thousands of companies, dozens of countries. If the fund goes to nothing, she still has the whole sum in 2033. If it does well, she has more. A bank sells exactly this and calls it a capital-protected note; it arrives with a brochure and a fee. Ours arrives with neither.

In 2031 she names co-sponsors a range for that gift. The bottom is the floor she already owns. The top is the floor plus half of whatever the fund is worth that day. The other half stays hers, to sell if the building turns out dearer than planned (it will be priced in Taiwan dollars, which nobody can translate for her yet). Low enough to keep her word; high enough that a lead gift pulls in bigger ones.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before its cheque, nothing is rebalanced, nothing is decided. If her life changes, the order is fixed – the cheques, then the floor, then the gift.

Ten doses, one a year. Nobody crushes the pill.
