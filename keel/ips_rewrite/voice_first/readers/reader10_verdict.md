# Reader 10 verdict: voice only (1 Nov 2026)

Brief: judge how much each of four anonymous entries sounds like the author of the three essays (crossed with a personable finance writer) arguing about money, and how much it sounds generic, compressed or machine-made. Investment merit is not judged, and I don't guess whether anything is AI-written. The essays are not quoted here; I describe their moves instead.

## Ranking

1. **Entry 3**
2. **Entry 2**
3. **Entry 4**
4. **Entry 1**

What separates first from last: Entry 3 moves like the essays. It has pun headings that also argue, asides with a sting, and a long build that ends in a short drop. Entry 1 has no headings at all, and its case against itself is one paragraph of model-speak with five numbers in two sentences, so it reads like a results table.

## The yardstick: how the essays move

- **The opponent gets the floor first, at full strength.** Each essay states the view it will attack, grants that it is comforting or reasonable, and only then turns on a "But". The concession is real and often gets its own section.
- **Long, then short.** The sentences that carry a mechanism run long, with en-dash asides inside them. Then a sentence of a few words lands the point. Paragraphs range from one line to a dozen sentences.
- **Homely images in formal sentences.** A broken machine nobody would punish, a vending machine, a teaspoon against a sinking ship, an argument about furniture on a raft. Each one is set up once and paid off once.
- **Headings are jokes that also state the thesis.** Bent proverbs, a shop slogan, a sitcom plea, a prospector's drawl. There is one every few hundred words, and they make the piece feel run by a person.
- **Numbers are said flat and then reacted to.** The reaction is where the voice is. Numbers are sparse: a few per section, never piled up.
- **Endings** turn a cliché around or call back to the opening image, and the very last line is two or three words.

Entries 1, 2 and 4 share most of their text: the pitch, the promise-and-hope opening, the ladder, the "list of ways to fail" paragraph, the floor-and-note paragraph, the range and the capsule close. So their ranking turns on the passage where the plan argues with its rival, and on headings. Entry 3 is a separate draft.

## Entry 3 (first)

**What sounds like him.** The opening line makes a claim rather than stating a fact: most of what can go wrong starts with mixing up the promise and the hope. That is how his introductions work. "Any rung could be sold any day. None will be." is his long-then-blunt landing in its purest form. The concession section is the most essay-like passage in the set. It gives the rival its best number, admits "we made it first", then turns with "'usually' is doing a lot of work there", which is a columnist's phrase and a human one. "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill; she owes ten one-year bills" is the best image in any entry, and it argues: delete it and the reason for the ladder goes with it. "As for her taste for risk, she has already spent it on the big one: the residency." is a dry aside of the kind he drops at the end of a paragraph. "minus the bank (and its fee)" has his parenthetical sting. It is the only entry with several headings, and they pun the way his do. "Sell low, by appointment" is the best heading in the set. The two-word pill-label close matches his two-word last lines.

**What sounds generic.** The pitch says the floor "grows back into itself", and the body later says the Treasuries "grow back into all of it". Nobody says that out loud, and the repeat makes it noticeable. The recession paragraph explains a mechanism and then stops on a pile of numbers instead of a reaction. "A crash costs her icing, never cake." is a mirrored slogan that adds rhythm but no thought. "A bird in the hand, half the bush" never resolves, so the reader has to finish the joke. "The ten holdings are the operating reserve" is form language. The pitch ends flat on "sells nothing early", and the body repeats its "We buy it" turn word for word, so a judge reading both fields hears the best line twice.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it." Three numbers and two bracketing commas, with the verb at the very end. He never ends a mechanism on a pile of numbers; he ends it on a short reaction.
2. "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund." Forty words with no short sentence after them, built around a made-up idiom. A long sentence is fine. A long sentence that never lands reads like a form.
3. "A crash costs her icing, never cake." The one place the entry reaches for a slogan. The heading has already made the cake joke, so this line explains it a second time.

## Entry 2 (second)

**What sounds like him.** The concession section ("Sell low, by appointment") is almost as good as Entry 3's: the rival's best number comes first, then "'usually' is the whole trick", then the ten one-year bills. The turn ends on a long clause that carries the point, "selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance", which is a properly dry line. "A high degree of certainty is a list of ways to fail, not an adjective" is an opinion with an edge, a definition only this team would write. "One leak remains" is a small, homely image. The capsule close, with "ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole", ends on a short line rather than a summary.

**What sounds generic.** The concession arrives before the plan is finished: the reader hears "ours" before learning what the second half of "ours" is, and then the entry goes back to explaining. His essays put the opponent early too, but against a thesis already stated in full. Apart from that one heading, the rest is unbroken paragraphs of about the same length. The three listed income sources behind the 2028 deposit are inventory, not voice. "Ours swaps the bank's option for shares, which can't expire worthless" uses the label before the mechanism; it assumes the judge already knows how a structured note works. "The cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope" is a neat triad standing in for a closing thought.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word to co-sponsors; ours, under one in sixty." The semicolon comparison with the verb dropped is how a table reads. The human half ("breaks its word") is buried mid-sentence.
2. The placement of the whole "Sell low, by appointment" section, between the risk paragraph and the floor paragraph. The argument stutters: it concedes, turns, and then goes back to explaining.
3. "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours swaps the bank's option for shares, which can't expire worthless." It needs the toy explanation (whose money goes where) before the jargon. As written, it is two labels in a row.

## Entry 4 (third)

**What sounds like him.** It has the most generous line about Laura in the set: she has taken thoughtful risks all her career, "and the strongest rival takes one for her". That is personable and a little wry. The rival comes after the plan is fully laid out, so the argument runs plan, opponent, verdict, which is the order of a market letter. "We'd pay the $15,000." is the best short landing in the four entries: a cost accepted in public, in five words, with no hedging. "But 'usually' is the whole trick." works here too.

**What sounds generic.** It has one heading, and it arrives about 70% of the way through, so it looks orphaned rather than like a structure. The rival's set-up is technical. The capped-range paragraph names a catch and then answers it with a procedure ("moves into Treasuries"), with no reaction after it. It shares the stubby failure list and the jargon-first note sentence with Entries 1 and 2.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "Set in our model to promise the same $145,000 as us in a typical year, it usually wins: counting the gift and what she keeps, it leaves her about $15,000 more by 2042." It opens on a dangling participle, has two set-up clauses before the claim, and reads like the caption of a chart.
2. "In the worst twentieth of our futures for stocks in 2028-30, the rival can promise co-sponsors only about $90,000, where we promise $140,000 – selling low, on a timetable printed years in advance." "Worst twentieth of our futures" is model-speak, and the dry tail is attached to the wrong clause. Grammatically it trails "we promise", when it describes the rival.
3. "A capped range has a catch: once it's named, every fall in stocks comes off the gift while every rise past the top goes to Laura. On the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries." It is a mirrored pair followed by an instruction, with no turn after it. This is where his essays would have put a short sentence reacting to the catch.

## Entry 1 (fourth)

**What sounds like him.** It shares the good common core: "So we don't invest the promise. We buy it.", the list-of-ways-to-fail definition and the capsule close. "We'd pay the $15,000." is just as strong here as in Entry 4. "Our own model sank it" is a plain-past-tense confession, the way he would admit it.

**What sounds generic.** There is no heading anywhere: 500 words of paragraphs of about the same length, which none of his essays look like. The rival paragraph is the most compressed passage in the four entries, with jargon ("name our ... median bottom", "the twentieth of our paths", "after the 2031 set-aside") and five numbers in two sentences. A judge hears a model report, not a person arguing. The capped-range paragraph adds a sixth number right before it ("about 97 times in 100"). It shares the stubby failure list, the jargon-first note and the closing triad.

**Three passages that most need rewriting.**
1. "Matched in our model to name our $145,000 median bottom, it usually wins: counting the gift and what she keeps, it leaves Laura about $15,000 more on average by 2042." Every phrase is jargon, and the possessive "our ... our" makes it clumsy. No one talks like this across a table.
2. "But in the twentieth of our paths where 2028-30 is worst for stocks, it names about $90,000 to co-sponsors against our $140,000 after the 2031 set-aside." Three numbers, a date range and a technical qualifier come before the reader feels anything. The $50,000 gap is the point and should be felt, not added up.
3. "A high degree of certainty is a list of ways to fail, not an adjective. The US government defaults. Or a recession arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, so the ladder costs more, and it shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap." A good opening line is followed by a stub, then a forty-word sentence that lists income sources. The list has no build and no landing.

## Entry 3: line-level changes (same facts and numbers, about the same length)

1. **Pitch.** Current: "Her 2028 deposit buys a floor that grows back into itself; its interest buys world stocks for her gift. Caplet XR sells nothing early."
   Replace: "Her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that repay it whole in 2032; the interest they'd earn buys world stocks for her gift. Nothing is ever sold early."
   Why: it removes the made-up idiom and ends the pitch on a plain promise instead of a product name.

2. **Recession paragraph.** Current: "Even then, in our model, after a two-point fall a deposit of about $125,000, not $150,000, still fills it."
   Replace: "We modelled the bad case: yields two points lower, a deposit of about $125,000 instead of $150,000. The gap still closes."
   Why: the numbers are said flat, then a short reaction lands the point, and the source is stated ("we modelled").

3. **Opening the risk paragraph.** Current: "One is a US government default."
   Replace: "One is a US government default, in which case the residency will have plenty of company."
   Why: one dry aside of his kind, aimed at the risk rather than at Laura. Optional, but it is the joke this paragraph lacks.

4. **The floor sentence.** Current: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032 that grow back into all of it, and the interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of the portfolio by our sums, goes into one world index fund."
   Replace: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced to pay the whole deposit back. The interest they should earn – about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums – goes into one world index fund."
   Why: the long build now has a full stop in the middle, the idiom is gone, and the aside sits inside en dashes the way his do.

5. **The icing line.** Current: "A crash costs her icing, never cake."
   Replace: "If stocks crash, she loses icing; the cake was paid for in 2028."
   Why: it keeps the callback to the heading but swaps the mirrored slogan for a fact with a date.

6. **Third heading.** Current: "A bird in the hand, half the bush"
   Replace: "One bird in the hand, half the bush on offer"
   Why: the joke now resolves. The floor is the bird she holds, and half the stocks are what she offers co-sponsors.

7. **The range sentence.** Current: "The range she names to co-sponsors in 2031 starts at the floor she has owned since 2028 and tops out at the floor plus half her stocks."
   Replace: "In 2031 she names co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028; the top adds half her stocks."
   Why: a short sentence leads into the mechanism, so the paragraph reads aloud instead of reading like a definition.

8. **Reserve paragraph.** Current: "From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down with nothing to rebalance."
   Replace: "From 2033 the ladder is her operating reserve. Each rung turns to cash just before its cheque, and nobody has to rebalance anything, which is rather the point."
   Why: "ladder" and "rung" pick up the image from earlier instead of switching to form language, and the paragraph ends with a dry columnist's tag instead of a procedure.
