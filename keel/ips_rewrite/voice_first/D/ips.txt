Laura has made one promise and holds one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone, with a high degree of certainty. The hope is a gift for the building. Keep them in one pot and, sooner or later, the hope eats the promise.

So we didn't.

Our first plan put three-quarters of her money in stocks, and our own model sank it: in about one future in thirty, a cheque went unpaid. Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill that a decade of average returns can cover; she owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year makes her sell at the bottom.

Hence Caplet XR. On a pill, XR is extended release: the same dose, on schedule. On ours, X is ten and R is redemption.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques, or nearly: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each due before its January.

Two ways to bounce a cheque

A high degree of certainty should be a short list of the ways it fails, not a soothing adjective. Ours has two. The US government defaults – and then nothing anywhere is safe. Or, in our model, her 2028 deposit can't plug the gap a fall in yields would open; even after a two-point fall, about $125,000 of her $150,000 does.

Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks. But she has already taken the big one: the residency. Her income from advances, speaking and licensing shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear, so the money she adds later is weakest when the ladder needs it most. So the cheques use money she already has.

The note, minus the bank

Most of her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, priced to grow back into all of it. The interest they should earn – about a fifteenth of the portfolio, by our sums – goes into one world index fund. A bank would sell her this exact structure, call it a capital-protected note and keep a fee for the privilege.

Money where her mouth is

In 2031 Laura names a range to co-sponsors: low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028. The top adds half her stocks; the other half stays hers, to sell any day, for a building priced in Taiwan dollars. A named top has a catch: a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past it goes to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries.

From 2033 the ten holdings are her operating reserve. Each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down; nothing is sold early – you don't crush an XR pill.

The hope gets everything the promise doesn't need – and not a dollar it does.
