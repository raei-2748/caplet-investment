# H10
ARCHETYPE: Dividend-income team; dividend growth ETFs and Dividend Aristocrats generate "income" to help fund the payments, Treasuries for the rest
COUNTRY / LEVEL: US (public high school, New Jersey); investing-club team, confident, polished with AI help and then edited
STRATEGY NAME: The Paycheck Portfolio
## PITCH
Laura built a career on ideas that keep paying. Her portfolio should too. A Treasury core secures the ten $50,000 payments, while dividend growers (SCHD, VIG and the Dividend Aristocrats) produce rising cash income that cushions those payments and grows her facility contribution, without ever forcing her to sell.
## IPS
**What we are trying to do**
Laura needs ten $50,000 payments from 2033 to 2042 with high certainty, a meaningful facility contribution in 2033, and enough flexibility to adapt as the residency develops. Our answer is a portfolio that pays her: a Treasury core for certainty and a dividend-growth sleeve for growth and income.

**Why dividends fit Laura**
Laura's income has always come from many creative streams. We want her portfolio to work the same way. Companies that have raised dividends for 25+ years, like Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola and Johnson & Johnson, tend to be profitable, low-debt businesses. That quality matters in bad markets: in 2022 the dividend-growth funds we use fell far less than the broader market. Most importantly, dividends arrive as cash. Once payments begin, Laura can draw on income instead of selling shares after a crash, which avoids locking in losses.

**Portfolio construction (2027-2032)**
- Treasury core (about 45%): individual US Treasury notes and bonds maturing between 2033 and 2042, plus IEF. This core is sized to cover the payments without any help from stocks.
- Dividend-growth sleeve (about 55%): SCHD 25%, VIG 20%, NOBL (S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats) 10%. Dividends are reinvested until 2033.
- The 2028 $150,000 contribution first fills any gap in the Treasury core, then goes to the dividend sleeve.
- We rebalance once a year back to target.

**Operating reserve (2033)**
In 2033 Laura sets aside the Treasury core as her operating reserve, holding bonds that mature as each payment falls due. The reserve stays in Treasuries and shrinks as payments are made. From 2033 we also switch the dividend sleeve from reinvesting to paying out. Using a ~3% yield, we haircut that income by 25%, roughly the drop in S&P 500 dividends in 2008-2009, and count the rest as a second layer of protection. That lets us hold a slightly smaller reserve and release more money for the facility.

**High certainty**
We define high certainty as every payment covered by Treasury maturities alone, before any dividend. We tested historical crashes (2000-2002, 2008, 2020, 2022) and in every case the payments were still met because the Treasury core never depended on stock prices.

**Facility contribution and flexibility**
After the reserve, the dividend sleeve is the source of the facility contribution. We recommend Laura contribute about 70% of the sleeve and keep 30% invested. That retained portion keeps producing income she can use for overruns or new ventures, which is real flexibility instead of idle cash.

**Communicating in 2031**
In 2031 we will give co-sponsors a range. The bottom assumes the dividend sleeve falls 35% before 2033; the top assumes it grows in line with long-run averages. Because dividends kept flowing even in 2008, Laura can promise the lower end with high confidence.

**Assumptions**
Treasuries earn their yield at purchase; dividend stocks return about 7% a year including a ~3% dividend yield; inflation does not affect the fixed payments but may raise facility costs, which dividend growth helps offset.
## SIMULATOR NOTES
Does well: warm client fit (income streams like Laura's career), concrete tickers, an honest dividend haircut and a Treasury core that protects the payments.
Blind spot: the 2033 section quietly relies on uncertain dividends to shrink the reserve, contradicting its own "Treasuries alone" definition; dividend "safety" and "no selling" ignore that total return is what matters, and the 2031 range rests on loose scenarios.
