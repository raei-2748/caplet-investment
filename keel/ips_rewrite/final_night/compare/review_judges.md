# Blind comparison: three Wharton judges

Strict counts: A 50 + 495; B 50 + 500 (zero margin). Both drafts follow the same plan: a ladder bought with the $300,000; a 2028 stake setting the floor, with roughly its interest in world shares; and in 2031 half the shares moved into Treasuries for the top and half held as reinvestment cover. They differ in argument and clarity, not in design.

## Scores (Strategy / Client / Analysis / Experience / Creativity; Top 50)

- **Fixed-income PM:** A 8/7/8/5/7, yes. B 8/8/6/5/7, yes.
- **Growth investor:** A 6/6/6/4/5, no. B 6/8/6/5/7, yes.
- **Finance professor:** A 8/7/8/4/6, yes. B 8/8/7/5/8, yes.

Neither draft says anything about the team's process; the Final Report has to carry that.

**Fixed-income PM prefers A.** It thinks like a liability manager: the payments are a debt, the public range is a second debt, and both are bought before anyone relies on them. It tests the waiting money at zero interest and names the one remaining risk, a US default.
- Remembers A: "no market can put a payment or a named figure out of reach, barring a US default."
- Remembers B: "The ladder's weakness is money that waits."
- Doubts A: "its interest makes up the difference by December 2032". A fund's yield isn't locked, and "the difference" doesn't yet refer to anything.
- Doubts B: "dollar Treasuries match them exactly". Coupons and fund payouts don't, and B's fifth paragraph admits it.

**Growth investor prefers B.** In both drafts only the stake's interest goes into shares, a token amount for a client of "thoughtful risks". A's case against more equity attacks an all-balanced portfolio and never considers anything in between. B gives a reason he can't dispute: the residency's other funding shrinks in the same recessions. He loses A's thread in its third paragraph.
- Remembers A: "A balanced mix would fit her record of thoughtful risks and probably leave her richer by 2042."
- Remembers B: "her part is most useful as the one source that won't."
- Doubts A: "levered like the shares of a heavily borrowed company". Leverage is the price of growth, and A uses it against a portfolio nobody proposed.
- Doubts B: "Pricing a fixed sum at what shares usually earn makes it look cheaper." A pension lecture that never mentions Laura.

**Finance professor prefers B, narrowly.** A has the richer concepts, but Wharton asks the IPS to "make it clear". A's 2028 mechanism and pitch both need a second read, and "hence the X and R" never says that X = 10. B states one rule and walks through the years in order.
- Remembers A: "inflation can raise what the building costs, never what she owes."
- Remembers B: "American public pensions are allowed to price their promises that way, and they have taken on more risk to keep up."
- Doubts A: "Half her shares are held back". Neither draft says why half.
- Doubts B: "as sure of landing inside it as of Treasuries". With both ends in Treasuries she almost surely lands at the top, so the range is really a floor plus a near-certain figure. A co-sponsor can't check that claim. A has the same gap.

## Brilliance

Because the design is shared, originality comes down to the arguments. **A holds the more original idea.** The payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, so any swing in a balanced mix lands on the remaining third: surplus risk, worked out from her own numbers. A also treats the spoken range as a liability, funded like the payments. B's co-sponsor correlation is the best client link in either draft. Its pension point is accurate but borrowed. **B explains far more clearly:** its first sentence states the whole rule, while A's pitch and third paragraph don't land on a first read.

## Panel pick

**B, 2-1, with the fixed-income PM dissenting. Strength: narrow.** B wins on clarity, not ideas, so A's best ideas should move into it. B must still fix:
1. "Exactly": say the Treasuries match the payments' currency and amount, with the waiting-money gap handled later.
2. The 2033 gift: say what she actually gives (what the range's Treasuries pay, up to the top) and where any surplus goes.
3. Inflation: say it can raise the facility's cost but not the payments.
4. The range: say what can still move after she names it, and give a confidence level a co-sponsor can check.
5. The half split: give a reason for it.
6. Equity: add A's point that losses fall on the remaining third.
7. Pitch: say what the structure gives her, not only how it works.
8. Words: there is zero margin on both fields. Shorten the pension passage first, and tie its point to Laura.
