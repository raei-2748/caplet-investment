Caplet XR rests on one rule: Laura should never promise a dollar she doesn't already own. She makes two promises, ten $50,000 operating payments from 2033 and, in 2031, a range for her facility contribution, and each is bought before she says it. The name counts what holds up the first: ten Treasury holdings, X for ten and R for redemption, the day the government repays each one before the January it pays for.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys iBonds funds for the 2033 to 2037 payments and Treasury bonds for 2038 to 2042, each held until it is repaid. At today's yields the ten payments need only the return Treasuries already pay, so shares could add nothing to them but risk. To us, then, a high degree of certainty means that no payment depends on a share price, a forecast or a sale, and one could fail only if the US government missed a payment of its own.

The usual case for shares is that over a decade they nearly always win. Time makes a loss rarer, though, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for the recovery. Worse, because the payments are fixed they take their money first, so every loss lands on what is left for the building, and a portfolio that looks moderately risky becomes, for the building money, riskier than owning nothing but shares. Laura also owns plenty of share-market risk already, in book advances, speaking and licensing, which can dry up in the same recessions.

So the 2028 deposit does two jobs, in order. First it tops up the ten holdings if falling yields made them cost more than $300,000. Then most of it buys an iBonds fund maturing in late 2032, sized to repay roughly what she put in, and only the rest, about the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund. Banks sell this shape as a capital-protected note and spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless; built by hand, Laura owns the shares and pays no bank. The world fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, because no payment waits on it.

In 2031 the range's bottom is what the 2032 fund will pay, rounded down. The top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud can't be quietly halved after a bad year. Her strongest fact for co-sponsors is that the operating payments are already paid for, so every dollar they give goes to the building, where funders would much rather put it than into running costs.

The plan's weak point is the bonds' interest, which must be reinvested until the payment it was counted for. Before Laura names her range, we price that gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her shares into Treasuries, so a fall in rates shrinks the range, never a payment. The other half of her shares stays unpromised, because money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent, and it is her cushion against Taiwan's building costs.

Nothing changes because markets move, only because a date arrives. From 2033 the ten holdings become her operating reserve, and most portfolios would now be selling shares as the deadlines near; here the reserve shrinks instead, one holding a year, while her kept shares stay invested. In January 2042 the tenth redemption pays the last operating payment.
