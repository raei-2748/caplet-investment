Caplet XR rests on one rule: Laura should never promise a dollar she doesn't already own. She makes two promises, ten $50,000 operating payments from 2033 and, in 2031, a range for her facility contribution, and the plan buys each one before she says it. The name counts what holds up the first: ten Treasury holdings, X for ten and R for redemption, the day the government repays each one before the January it pays for.

The tempting way to fund fixed payments is to assume shares will earn their long-run average and set aside less. Many American public pension funds valued their promises that way, at the return they hoped for, and found the hole when markets fell. We price Laura's payments at what Treasuries charge instead. In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys iBonds funds for the 2033 to 2037 payments and individual Treasury bonds for 2038 to 2042, each held until it is repaid. Cash would look safer, but a dollar waiting in cash for a 2040 payment is a bet that interest rates won't fall for thirteen years, while a bond maturing in 2039 has already locked its rate in. So, to us, a high degree of certainty means no payment depends on a share price, a forecast or a sale, and one could fail only if the US government missed a payment of its own.

The usual case for shares is that over a decade they nearly always win. Time makes a loss rarer, though, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for the recovery. Worse, because the payments are fixed, they take their money first and every market loss lands on what is left for the building, so a portfolio that looks moderately risky becomes, for the building money, riskier than owning nothing but shares. And her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking and licensing, which can dry up in the same recessions that cut share prices and the grants the residency will apply for. The one part of the residency's money we control should be the part a recession can't reach.

The 2028 deposit does two jobs, in order. First it tops up the ten holdings if falling yields made them cost more than $300,000. Then most of it buys an iBonds fund maturing in late 2032, sized to repay roughly what she put in, and only the rest, about the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund of thousands of companies. Banks sell this shape as a capital-protected note and spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless; built by hand, Laura owns the shares and pays no bank. The world fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, because no payment waits on it.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a credible range, and both ends are hers before she says them. The bottom is what the 2032 fund will pay, rounded down. The top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries first, because a number said aloud can't be quietly halved after a bad year. The range then measures a choice, not a market, and gives her something to offer: the top can be promised as a match for what co-sponsors pledge. Her strongest fact for them is simpler. The operating payments are already paid for, so every dollar they give goes into the building, where funders would much rather put it than into running costs.

The plan's weak point is the bonds' interest, which arrives twice a year and must be reinvested until the payment it was counted for; if rates fall and stay low, it comes up short. Before Laura names her range, we price that gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her shares into Treasuries, so the range shrinks and no payment ever waits on shares. The other half of her shares stays unpromised and liquid, because money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent. It is her flexibility, for Taiwan's building costs, which will likely rise faster than any figure she names, and for whatever the residency needs once it opens.

Nothing in the plan changes because markets move, only because a date arrives. From 2033 the ten holdings and the money set aside become her operating reserve, and each January one holding is redeemed and pays that year's $50,000. Most portfolios sell shares as a deadline nears; here the reserve shrinks instead, one holding a year, while the shares she kept stay invested, because her risk was always set by what she had promised. In January 2042 the tenth redemption pays the last operating payment.
