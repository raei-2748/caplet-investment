The building Laura plans can look to co-sponsors for help, but its ten operating payments of $50,000 cannot, so Caplet XR pays for those first. In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings, five iBonds funds and five Treasury bonds, each redeemed, or repaid by the US government, in the year before the January it pays for. The plan is named for those ten redemptions (X is the Roman ten), and it holds to one rule from then on: Laura never names a number she does not already own.

At this autumn's yields, $300,000 is about what the ten holdings cost, so the payments need no return beyond what Treasuries already pay. We take a high degree of certainty to mean that no payment depends on a share price, a forecast or an early sale, and a payment could fail only if the US government missed one of its own.

Shares nearly always beat Treasuries over ten years, and the plan still keeps them away from the payments. Because the payments are fixed, they take their $50,000 first whatever markets do, so every loss lands on the money left for the building. With shares mixed in beside the payments, the building money would swing harder than the share market itself.

When the 2028 deposit arrives, it first tops up the ten holdings if falling yields made them cost more than $300,000. Most of the rest buys an iBonds fund maturing in late 2032, priced to grow back to roughly the whole deposit by then, and the slice left over, about the interest that fund will earn, goes into a world index fund. Treasuries alone would leave her a larger sure sum, but shares are the only part of her money that can grow with building costs in Taiwan, and the worst they can do is hand back her deposit.

In 2031 the bottom of her range is what the 2032 fund will pay, rounded down because a fund's payout is not fixed. For the top, she moves half of her shares into Treasuries before naming the range, so a crash in 2032 can't leave her short of a figure co-sponsors have heard. With both ends owned on the day she says them, she can give the range with near-complete confidence. Her best argument to co-sponsors is the payments: ten years of running costs are already bought, so the building they help pay for won't stand empty.

The weak point is the interest the holdings pay along the way, which must be reinvested until its payment is due and earns less if rates fall and stay low. So before she names her range, we work out that gap as if the waiting money earned nothing, and move that much from her shares into Treasuries. If her shares can't cover it, the range is smaller.

The other half of her shares is never mentioned to co-sponsors, since every overrun would become a claim on it, and it stays her flexibility if the building costs more than she named. From 2033 nothing changes because markets move. Each January one holding is redeemed and pays that year's $50,000, and the operating reserve shrinks by one holding a year until, in January 2042, the tenth redemption pays the last operating payment.
