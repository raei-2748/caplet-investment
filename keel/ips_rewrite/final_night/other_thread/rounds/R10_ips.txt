Co-sponsors can help Laura pay for the facility, but her ten operating payments of $50,000 must come from this portfolio alone, so Caplet XR buys them first. In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings: five iBonds funds, which hold Treasuries that all mature in one December, and five Treasury bonds. Each matures in the year before the January it pays for, and the plan is named for those ten redemptions (X for ten, R for redemption). From then on it keeps one rule: Laura never states an amount she doesn't already hold the money for.

Shares are expected to earn more than Treasuries, but the payments have no use for the extra, because at late 2026's interest rates $300,000 is about what the ten holdings cost. Shares would also put the facility at risk. Fixed payments work like a debt the portfolio owes: they are paid in full whatever markets do, so every loss falls on what is left over, and a leftover held beside the payments in a mix of shares and bonds swings harder than the share market itself. We therefore read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment depends on a share price, a forecast or selling anything before it matures. Each payment's cash arrives from its own holding, and the funds can still be sold on any trading day.

The 2028 deposit first buys any holding the first $300,000 couldn't afford, which happens if bond prices rise before January 2027. Most of the rest buys an iBonds fund maturing in late 2032, priced to grow back to about what she put in, and this sets the bottom of the range she will name in 2031. Leaving the deposit in shares until then would probably mean a bigger range, but in a bad market the bottom would shrink in the same years that co-sponsors give less. Only the slice left over, about the interest that fund will earn, goes into a world index fund. Treasuries alone would leave her a larger sure sum, but shares are the only part of her money that can grow with building costs in Taiwan, and this is the thoughtful risk the case says she will take. Even if the shares lost everything, the fund would still return about her deposit.

The holdings pay interest along the way, and that interest, with the cash from the two bonds that mature months early, waits in a short-term Treasury fund until its January. If rates fall and stay low, that waiting money earns less than the bonds' prices assumed. So before she names her range, Laura moves enough of her shares into Treasuries to cover the shortfall, worked out as if the waiting money earned nothing at all, which is about what American cash paid from 2009 to 2015.

Then she moves half of the remaining shares into Treasuries, and the top of her range is the bottom plus that half. A good market before 2031 raises the top and a bad one lowers it, but once named, the range can't move. Left in shares, a named top would take every fall while every rise above it went to Laura. With both ends held in Treasuries, she can state the range with near-complete confidence, and where she lands in 2033 is her choice, once the facility's cost and the co-sponsors' pledges are known.

The other half of her shares stays outside the range. If it were offered, every cost overrun would become a claim on it; kept back, it is her financial flexibility as the project develops. From 2033 the ten holdings and the money set aside are her operating reserve, nothing in it is sold, and each January's $50,000 comes from the holding redeemed before it. The tenth redemption, a Treasury bond maturing in November 2041, pays the last operating payment in January 2042.
