# A new name and a new central idea: Fixed Point

Claude, Thu 5 Nov 2026. Keel's research, for you to rewrite in your own words. No trade changes. Every choice is yours.

**Roots is out.** Ahaan gave the case to other AIs and they also suggested "Roots", after Laura's memoir *Messy Roots*, so
it can't be what sets us apart. It's gone from every draft, and so is the tablet image: both were pictures laid over the
plan, not ideas inside it.

**The idea.** It's natural to plan Laura's portfolio as if it were the residency's only money, but the case says the rest
may come from co-sponsors, grants, program fees and her own business income (case p. 3). Each of those can shrink in the
years share prices fall. Her portfolio is the one source whose amount can be settled before anyone is asked, and then
held there. So we judge every dollar by one question: would the residency miss it in a recession? It would miss the
operating payments and her minimum facility contribution, so each goes into Treasuries the moment its deposit arrives
(2027 and 2028). It would miss the top of her range once co-sponsors have heard it, so that moves into Treasuries before
she names it. Shares hold only money nobody is counting on.

**What it settles.** The case says two things that seem to pull apart. Laura's living costs are covered outside the
portfolio, so she could take a lot of risk, yet the payments need a high degree of certainty. A judge will ask why so few
shares. Our answer is that she could afford more risk, but the residency couldn't, because a bad year would cut its other
money at the same time. The idea also says why her minimum is bought in 2028 and not in 2031. We modelled a plan that
holds shares until 2031: on average it leaves Laura about $19,000 more by 2042. But in its worst twentieth of 2028-30
markets, it names about $90,000 to co-sponsors against our $140,000, in the same years co-sponsors give less.

**The name.** Fixed Point: in a residency whose other money moves with the economy, her part is settled first and stays
put. It needs no more explaining than that.

**Where it comes from** (for the Final Report; read at the source on the date shown):
- Robert Merton, "Optimal Investment Strategies for University Endowment Funds" (NBER Working Paper 3820, 1991; read 5
  Nov). He says the usual approach treats an endowment "as if it were the only asset of the university". Once the other
  income is counted, the best portfolio is "not efficient in the sense of the risk-return tradeoff". So a plan can look
  too safe for Laura alone and still be right for the residency.
- Stephen Dimmock, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 94(3), 2012 (abstract read 5 Nov): universities whose other
  income swings more hold more bonds. Institutions really invest this way.
- *Giving USA 2010*, executive summary (read 5 Nov). After inflation, US giving to the arts fell 11.9% from 2007 to 2009,
  and all giving fell 5.5%. Foundation grants fell 8.6% in 2009, and after past recessions giving took "three to five
  years" to recover. US private foundations must pay out at least 5% of their assets each year, so their grants shrink
  when their assets do.
- List and Lucking-Reiley, *Journal of Political Economy* 2002 (read 30 Oct): when the money already in hand at a
  campaign's start rose from 10% to 67% of its goal, gifts rose nearly sixfold.

**Where it's weak.**
- Giving fell far less than shares did, so this is a reason to lean towards bonds, not the whole case for them. The fixed
  payments and the cost of over-promising still do most of the work.
- Corporate giving rose 5.9% in 2009, and the case doesn't say who the co-sponsors are.
- A fall in yields, common in recessions, widens the ladder's reinvestment gap. We price that gap before Laura names her
  range, so it can lower the range before she names it, never after.
- The half of her shares she keeps would be smallest in exactly the downturn this idea is about. That's why it's her
  flexibility, and nobody counts on it.
