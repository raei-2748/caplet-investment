# R3 review: fixed-income and pension finance professor (blind to change notes)

Read: WHARTON_WORD, BIBLE (Books 1-5, 9-12, Ten Commandments), INSTRUCTIONS I82-I88, the case, the 5 Nov October check, R3_pack.txt.
Counts: pitch 50 words, IPS 574 (74 over).

## One-read test
**Strategy in three sentences.** (1) Price every commitment at what Treasuries charge to deliver it, and buy it in Treasuries the day it is made, so no operating payment ever depends on a share price. (2) The $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings, one maturing before each January 2033-2042; the 2028 deposit mostly buys a late-2032 Treasury fund, whose payout is the bottom of the range given to co-sponsors in 2031. (3) Only the interest on that fund sits in world shares; half of it is moved into Treasuries before the range is named, so both ends of the range are already owned.

**Biggest strength.** The range owned at both ends before it is said, and the discipline behind it (a promise is only made out of money already bought). It answers the case's own warning about damaging credibility, and it is the one idea a judge can retell. Second: the honest pricing argument with the pension example.

**Top 50 of 2,300?** Yes, narrowly. It is among the 15 or 20 I would flag in a pile of 300 for logic and for one idea nobody else has. It is not safe: a judge who wants "thoughtful risk" will find about 7% of the money in shares, and the flexibility story has a hole (below). Fix those two and it is comfortable.

## (a) Book by book
Met:
- No case summary up front; opens on the thesis ("money leaves shares on the day somebody starts counting on it"). Book 12 met.
- No "our model", no team history, no subheadings, no rhetorical question, no "if a judge asks", no "every team", no medicine imagery, no GPTZero/AI-draft talk. Books 11, 12, I82, I85, I88 met.
- Case words mostly used: operating commitment, credible range, co-sponsors, high degree of certainty, facility contribution, operating reserve.
- Contractions present ("doesn't", "won't", "can't"). One concession-then-"But" ("looks cheaper, but the difference..."). A short sentence lands in paragraph 1 ("The reason is pricing.").
- Name Caplet XR explained with the real second meaning of R (redemption, the bond market's word). Good, and it is not medicine.

Missed or shaky:
1. **Triad and slogan, Commandment III / Book 12:** "the payments come first, the range second, her flexibility last." This is exactly the tricolon slogan the Bible bans. It is also the end of the best paragraph and sounds like a brochure.
2. **Compressed, two-read sentences (Commandment IV, Books 9-11).** Worst offenders are in the pitch (below). The IPS reads mostly plainly.
3. **Coined labels (I83):** "even for the building" (the case word is facility); "that set-aside" (the case defines "set-aside" as the whole operating reserve, here it means the gap money, which collides with the case); "her kept shares" / "the shares she moved" (labels for pots the reader has to track). "Ladder" is ordinary finance and I would let it stand.
4. **Full-circle XR (Book 11 item 4).** XR is introduced in paragraph 3 and never returns. The last sentence is mechanical ("until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042"). Book 12 and I84/I85 loosened the motif, so this is a minor miss, but the ending does not land.
5. **"Every number says where it came from" (V).** "two decades" has no source; "half her shares" is a design choice with no reason given; "roughly the interest that fund will earn" has no figure or origin. No "we modelled" line anywhere.
6. **Human voice:** long runs of declarative "Her ... Its ... The ..." with little wry texture, and paragraph 4 and 5 each carry three clauses joined by commas and "and".

## (b) Wharton criteria
- **Investment strategy (clear thesis, disciplined planning across time horizons, diversification, consistency):** thesis is clear and creative; horizons well handled (2027 ladder, 2028 deposit, 2031 range, 2033 hand-over). **Diversification is the weak spot:** roughly 90%+ in US Treasuries and about 7% in one stock fund. A judge will ask where the "growth" in "balance growth and protecting capital" is. The IPS says the stock fund is "the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong" but never says what growth Laura is buying, or why she has any shares at all if her own argument is that her income already moves with the economy.
- **Client knowledge:** very good on income risk ("book advances, speaking fees and licensing" can fall with shares) and on the residency's co-funding. The case never says this; it is inference, and "can" is the right strength. It leans on "thoughtful risks" not at all.
- **Portfolio analysis:** concepts used correctly (reinvestment risk, liability-matching, risk-free discounting). No assumptions needed in an IPS, fine.
- **Flexibility (case and IPS question 5):** weakest point of the whole draft, see Problem 1.
- **Composition change as payments approach:** answered in the last paragraph ("the reserve gets a year shorter"). Adequate.
- **Creativity and presentation / co-sponsor communication:** the range owned at both ends is the best answer in the draft.

## (c) Brilliance
Ideas a judge retells tomorrow: (1) the range is only said once both ends are already owned; (2) cash as a bet that rates won't fall; (3) the reinvestment gap is priced at zero before the range is named; (4) her contribution is worth most as the part of the residency's money that doesn't shrink with the rest. None of the four is the field's default. The headline (ladder plus a stock sliver) is the field's default shape, but the reasoning on top is not. Pitch headline "money leaves shares" is slightly off: the $300,000 never sits in shares, so it is the 2031 half-move that "leaves"; the stronger and truer headline is "buy it the day you promise it."

## Three biggest problems (exact words)
1. **Financial flexibility is spent, not preserved.** "the facility contribution is paid from the 2032 fund and the shares she moved" together with "The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and promised to no one." The "other half" is half of an interest-sized sliver (my arithmetic: a few tens of thousands at most, perhaps 3 to 5% of the surplus). The case warns that "committing all remaining assets could limit her financial flexibility." Under this plan the whole surplus except that sliver is the contribution, and then "her flexibility last" lets even the sliver be spent on the coupon gap. A judge will see a plan that commits nearly everything. Nothing in the text says Laura may contribute less than the range, or says what she keeps.
2. **The bottom of the range is not firm, contradicting the headline.** "Its bottom is what the late-2032 fund will pay, rounded down, which she has owned since 2028" versus "If they can't cover it, she names a lower range." If the coupon gap exceeds the kept shares, the shortfall must come out of the 2032 fund, so the bottom is not "owned." The draft needs one consistent rule, and the order "payments, range, flexibility" admits the range is not safe from the ladder.
3. **Ladder claims run ahead of the instruments.** "A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do, so we define a high degree of certainty as..." but the ladder is "Treasury bonds and Treasury funds," and a fund pays its net asset value at termination, not a promise of par (the October notes say so: "the final payout is not fixed"). The sentence is true of bonds and untrue of funds, and the reader cannot tell which holdings are which. The definition is also by exclusion ("no operating payment depending on a share price"); coupon reinvestment, fund NAV payout and the state of the Treasury are other ways a payment could fail, and the draft deals with only one of them.

## Sentences I read twice
- Pitch: "Her 2028 deposit buys the least she could give the facility; the most she'll name in 2031 is bought before co-sponsors hear it." (Two clauses, one semicolon, and "buys the least she could give" is not natural English.)
- Pitch: "money leaves shares the day someone starts counting on it" (shares she never held, until you read the IPS).
- "Her 2028 deposit tops up the ladder if rates have moved against it." (Rates moving after a ladder is bought cannot hurt a ladder held to maturity. The only time a top-up helps is when $300,000 will not buy all ten at January 2027 yields. As written it is wrong or at least unclear.)
- "Its top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud gets planned around." (Three ideas in one line.)
- "Companies raise dividends only to levels they expect to keep for the same reason: a cut costs more trust than the rise ever earned." ("the same reason" points at a reason about planning; the dividend reason is about signalling.)
- "So before Laura names her range, we price the gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries." (Which gap? "waiting dollar" is a coined image.)
- "the facility contribution is paid from the 2032 fund and the shares she moved." (Reader must recall which funds and which shares.)
- "none is sold before its redemption, even for the building." ("none" refers back across a dash.)

## Doubtful finance claims, one by one
- **Public-pension discount rate.** True. US public plans may discount liabilities at the expected return on assets (GASB 67/68, when the plan is projected to be funded), and expected returns have drifted down while allocations to private and alternative assets rose. The causal "to keep up with it" is the draft's inference and is unsourced; acceptable as a sentence, risky as a fact. The "one honest price" is the financial-economics view (Novy-Marx and Rauh, Bodie), which actuaries dispute; "honest" is stronger than the field's own consensus, though defensible for a payment that is fixed in dollars and not pre-funded for any other purpose. I accept it.
- **"A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do."** True for a Treasury bond's principal and coupons in nominal dollars; the coupons then have to be reinvested, which the draft concedes later. False for the funds in the same ladder (see Problem 3).
- **"Cash only looks safer... a bet that interest rates won't fall."** Right: for fixed dollars on fixed future dates, rolling bills carries reinvestment risk. The honest completion is that the ladder pays for this by giving up the right to profit if rates rise.
- **Dividends.** Broadly right (Lintner; later CFO surveys show managers will do almost anything to avoid cutting). "Cost more trust" is loose; the evidence is a larger market reaction to cuts than to raises. The analogy to a stated range is fair but decorative; it does not carry the argument.
- **The cash/reinvestment claim and the zero-return stress.** Correct that coupons earn less if rates fall and stay low. "As if every waiting dollar earned nothing" is a sensible worst case, but it is priced once, in 2031, for coupons through 2042; the draft does not say what happens if the gap is bigger than the money in kept shares (Problem 2).
- **$300,000 buying all ten payments.** It buys them only if long yields on the purchase day are roughly 5% (rule of thumb: ten annual $50,000 payments from 2033 cost about $327k at 4% and about $294k at 5.25%). The pitch states it as a fact. The IPS partly protects itself with the top-up, but the top-up trigger is worded wrongly.
- **Range mechanics.** Width looks tiny: bottom is about the 2028 deposit and top is that plus half of an interest-sized slice. That is a range of perhaps 10% from top to bottom. It is "credible," but the case says the contribution is uncertain "in 2031" and wants confidence plus favourable and unfavourable cases. A range this narrow, owned entirely in Treasuries, will make a judge ask why the portfolio carries the shares at all.
- **2033 coherence.** The ladder's 2033 holding matures "before January" and so is cash in December 2032; the same late-2032 fund also sets the range bottom. These are two lots of the same fund and the text lets a reader think they are one. Also the case says the operating reserve is set aside "at the beginning of 2033, before making the first payment"; the draft's "From January 2033 the ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve" is consistent with that but uses the case's word "set-aside" in a different sense (see problem 3 in Books).
- **Pitch vs IPS.** Pitch says "the most she'll name in 2031 is bought" but the IPS says the top is the bottom plus half the shares, so the top is bought but is also less than the full surplus; consistent but the pitch hides the half.

## What I would cut whole to lose about 100 words (574 to about 475)
1. The dividend sentence, 24 words ("Companies raise dividends... ever earned."). The range argument stands without it.
2. "Each January after that, one holding is redeemed... in 2042.", 25 words. The reserve getting shorter is already implied; the fund-and-set-aside sentence before it does the work.
3. "So when co-sponsors hear the range, both ends are already bought.", 11 words. The pitch and the previous sentence already say it.
4. "The deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up... co-sponsors." and "Her contribution is worth most as the part of the residency's money that doesn't shrink with the rest.", 48 words together. This is the weakest-sourced argument and it undercuts having shares at all. Cut as a pair; then "Laura's fortunes already ride on the economy" needs a clause of support or goes.
Total about 108. If the author will not cut item 4, cut the pension clause's tail ("and have spent two decades... keep up with it", about 18 words) and the "We call the plan" sentence's gloss on the bond market (13 words) instead, and accept being about 70 over: not allowed, so item 4 should go.

## What to fix beyond words
- Say what Laura keeps after 2033 and that the contribution may be below the range; one clause.
- State which holdings are bonds and which are funds in the certainty sentence, or restrict the claim to "bonds."
- Make the top-up trigger the January 2027 purchase day.
- Make the order "payments, range, flexibility" a plain sentence, not a triad.
