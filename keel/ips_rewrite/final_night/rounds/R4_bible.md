## The Writer's four questions
1. **Opening rule and the pensions claim: accurate and clear.** "A fixed payment has one honest cost: the price of
   Treasuries that will pay it on its date" is right (discount a liability at the rate that matches its risk). The
   pensions clause is sourced off-page (GASB 67/68; Andonov, Bauer & Cremers 2017, sample about 1990-2012: R4_facts
   row 6), so "two decades" stands. One clarity snag: "Laura's money **leaves** shares" implies it starts in shares;
   her $300,000 never touches them. A cold reader may stumble once. Consider "stays out of shares from the day ...".
2. **"Both ends of her range are bought before anyone hears it": holds, if you don't add more to it.** The bottom is
   the 2032 fund's payout "rounded down", and the top is moved into Treasuries before she names the range, so both
   are held in Treasuries when she speaks. The payout isn't fixed (IBTM pays its net asset value, not par: TNA note
   2), and that is exactly what "rounded down" covers. The reinvestment gap is pre-funded by the zero-reinvestment
   rule. So "bought" is true. What would make it false: any wording that says the amount is fixed, or "markets can't
   touch it" (FACTS never-say). "A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do" is true of
   the bonds; the funds promise nothing, so keep that sentence about bonds only (it is now). **Hole:** "rounded down"
   to what? One plain word ("to a round figure below it", or "by enough to cover the fund's costs") closes it.
3. **Wharton questions: two are thin.** *Diversification* (W9): only "thousands of companies". The residency-
   diversification idea is safe in the bank (B4), but the IPS now has nothing on the Treasury side either (ten dates
   spread across ten years is diversification by date, against one issuer; a single clause would do). *Liquidity*
   (W3): one word, "liquid". Nothing says that she needs no cash before 2033, which is *why* the money can be locked
   into dates. One clause fixes both.
4. **Caplet XR full circle without medicine: lands at three-quarters.** No medicine anywhere (B4 PASS). The ending
   ("until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042") is a true pay-off. But the name is set up in
   paragraph 2's middle, after a two-sentence aside, so the circle doesn't open in the introduction (Book 11). Moving
   the XR sentence to close paragraph 1 (it follows the rule naturally: ten holdings, each bought on the day) would
   complete it at no word cost.

## The three failures that matter most
1. **Why half (W13/B48, ★).** Two drafts running now. Give one plain reason in a clause. Something the plan believes,
   not an arbitrary split: for instance, the half kept is what remains her financial flexibility, and the case warns
   that committing everything limits it. Or replace "half" with the rule that decides it.
2. **The erudition went with the cut (B28/B30).** The dividend comparison was the cleverest piece of logic in R2. It
   proved why a range must be bought before it is said, using corporate finance, not a metaphor. Spend the six spare
   words and the "This is the plan's real strength:" frame on bringing it back as one sentence. The capital-
   protected note must at least go to the bank.
3. **The strength comes last (B2) and the circle opens late (B23).** Both are fixed by the same move: name the strength
   and Caplet XR in paragraph 1, then let the last paragraph pay off the tenth redemption.
