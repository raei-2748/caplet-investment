In our plan, money leaves shares on the day somebody starts counting on it. That can sound timid for a client who is happy to take thoughtful risks, so it is worth saying why we think it is the opposite. A fixed payment has one honest price, which is what Treasuries charge to deliver it on its date. Price it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, but the difference is a return nobody has earned yet – and American public pensions, whose rules let them do exactly this, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it. So each sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries when she commits to it, and her risk sits only on money no one else is relying on.

The first is the operating commitment. In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, each maturing shortly before one of the ten Januaries from 2033 to 2042. A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do, so we define a high degree of certainty as no operating payment depending on a share price. Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it is a bet that interest rates won't fall. We call the plan Caplet XR – X for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the bond market's word for the day a bond is repaid – and none of them is sold before its redemption, even for the building.

Her 2028 deposit tops up the ladder if rates have moved against it. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032, which becomes the smallest facility contribution she could make. What remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. A bank would sell her this shape as a capital-protected note and spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless; we would rather she owned the shares. The share fund stays small because her career is already a bet on the economy. The deposit itself comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which dry up in the recessions that hit share prices, and so do the residency's co-sponsors, grants and program fees. Her contribution is most useful as the part of the residency's money that doesn't shrink when the rest does.

The second commitment is the credible range she gives co-sponsors in 2031. Its bottom is what that late-2032 fund will pay, rounded down, which she has owned since 2028. Its top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names the range, because a number said aloud gets planned around, and cutting it later costs more trust than it ever raised. Companies treat dividends the same way, raising them only to levels they expect to keep. So by the time co-sponsors hear the range, both ends are bought. They also hear that her ten operating payments are already paid for, so their money can go to the building, which is where donors much prefer to give. The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and never promised to the building, since money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent.

The ladder has one real weakness. Its bonds pay interest along the way, and if rates fall and stay low, that interest earns less while it waits for its January. Before Laura names her range, we price the gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. If they can't cover it, she names a lower range. The payments come first, the range second, her flexibility last.

From January 2033 the ladder and the money set aside for that gap become her operating reserve, and the facility contribution is paid from the 2032 fund and the shares she moved. After that the reserve needs no decisions, only dates: each January one holding is redeemed and the reserve gets a year shorter, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
