Caplet XR keeps every sum Laura commits to in Treasuries before anyone counts on it, starting with her ten $50,000 operating payments from 2033. They're fixed in dollars and can come only from her portfolio, so we treat them as a debt. Her first $300,000 goes into ten Treasury holdings that repay it, with the 2028 deposit buying whatever $300,000 couldn't if prices have risen. The X and R in the name stand for those ten and their redemption.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But at current Treasury prices the payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and in such a mix the facility would get what was left over. Like the shares of a heavily borrowed company, that leftover would swing far harder than the share market.

Five of the ten are funds that hold one calendar year's Treasuries, pay out that December and sell more easily than a small lot of bonds. With the rest of the 2028 deposit, Laura buys an extra stake in the fund behind the 2033 payment, big enough to get that money back in December 2032. The bottom of the facility range she names in 2031 starts from that sum, rounded down. The stake costs less than it pays back, so shares get the difference, through a world index fund of thousands of companies, and next to an all-Treasury plan they can only cost her that difference.

Interest arrives years before the payment it's meant for, and each holding repays weeks or months before its January. That money waits at short-term rates, funds included, and years of near-zero rates would leave the payments short. The cover for that is bought in 2031, before she names a range, sized as if the waiting money earned nothing and paid for with up to half her shares. If that half isn't enough, the shortfall comes out of the bottom, moving the whole range down.

Selling the other half the same day sets the top at the bottom plus what that half fetched. A named top left in shares could only disappoint, and promising more than she can give could damage her credibility. Barring a US default, no payment ever waits on shares, interest rates or an early sale, which is what we mean by a high degree of certainty. Leaning on that one borrower is deliberate, because the residency's other money, from co-sponsors and her own work, is already a bet on good times.

In 2033 she gives the top, or less if the building, priced in Taiwan dollars, needs less, but never less than the bottom. She keeps, unpromised, whatever she doesn't give and any shares the cover didn't use, since new buildings throw up surprises. Her operating reserve is what's still waiting to be redeemed, the cover included, so setting it aside takes no trade, and by January 2042 redemptions have repaid the debt in full.
