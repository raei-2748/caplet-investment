Caplet XR makes sure every amount Laura commits to is held in Treasuries by the time anyone relies on it. Her ten operating payments of $50,000 come first, and only her own money can fund them. In January 2027 her first $300,000, topped up from 2028 if bond prices rise first, goes into ten Treasury holdings, one redeemed in the year before each payment: five bonds, and five funds that each hold Treasuries maturing in a single year. The X in Caplet XR is those ten holdings, and the R is their redemption.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But the payments would take about two thirds of her $450,000 whatever markets did, so every loss in shares would fall on the third left for the facility. That third would swing far more than the market, as a company's shares do when it borrows heavily. Matching each payment with Treasuries takes that leverage away. We read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment depends on a share price or on selling a bond before it matures. All ten rest on the US government alone, because other borrowers would add ways to miss a payment without removing that one.

Laura has been willing to take thoughtful risks, yet most of her 2028 deposit also goes into Treasuries, because a meaningful contribution she already owns can signal to co-sponsors that the residency is viable. The deposit buys an extra stake in the fund that pays the 2033 payment, and the payout of that stake alone, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, is the bottom of the range she names in 2031. A world index fund, spread across thousands of companies, gets only about what the stake will earn by 2032. If every share were lost, the stake would still pay back about all she put into it and the shares.

The top is set in 2031, the day she names the range: the bottom plus half of her shares, sold for Treasuries, because a top left in shares could fall out of reach. The other half covers the plan's weak point. Interest and bond repayments that arrive early wait for their payment at short-term rates, which may fall and stay low. So she first buys enough Treasuries to fill that gap as if the waiting money earned nothing, and if the other half can't pay for them, she names a lower range, cutting the top before the bottom.

Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, and where it lands depends on what the building needs in 2033, after inflation, not on markets. What is left of the other half, which in bad years may be nothing, stays in shares as her financial flexibility, sellable any day.

The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 needs no trade, because it already holds everything still waiting for a payment. The last payment, in January 2042, is met by the tenth holding and the interest saved for it.
