Whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys the Treasuries that will pay it that same day. A fixed payment has one honest cost, which is the price of Treasuries that will pay it on its date. Cost it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, but the saving is a return nobody has earned yet – and American public pensions, whose accounting rules let them do exactly that, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it. So her shares only ever hold money nobody else is counting on.

The first sum is the operating commitment. In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042; if rates leave it short, her 2028 deposit finishes it. Each holding pays out at its end date whatever markets do, so for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price. A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the day a bond is repaid. None is sold early, even for the facility.

Most of the 2028 deposit buys more of the late-2032 fund that pays her first January, and that extra sets her minimum facility contribution. Only what's left, roughly the interest it will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. That fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, and it stays small because Laura's own income can rise and fall with the economy. Her deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same recessions that hit shares.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is that extra in the 2032 fund, rounded down. Its top adds half of what her shares are then worth, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because co-sponsors will plan around any number she says. So both ends of Laura's range are bought before anyone hears it. The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and promised to no one.

The ladder's weakness is that its bonds pay interest before their January arrives, and if rates fall and stay low, that waiting money earns less than we planned. Before Laura names her range, we work out the gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. If they can't cover it, she names a lower range.

The plan's real strength is that by 2033 nothing a resident or a co-sponsor is relying on depends on a share price. The ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve. After that, one holding is redeemed before each January and the reserve gets a year shorter, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
