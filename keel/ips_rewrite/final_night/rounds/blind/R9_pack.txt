Laura buys Treasuries to repay each sum as she commits to it. Her $300,000, topped up if needed, covers ten $50,000 operating payments. Her 2028 deposit secures a minimum facility contribution; roughly its interest buys world shares. Both ends of her 2031 range are owned before she names it.
---
Whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys Treasuries that will repay it in time. Pricing a fixed sum at what shares usually earn makes it look cheaper, because it counts growth that hasn't happened yet. American public pensions are allowed to price their promises that way, and they have taken on more risk to keep up. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for its ten Treasury holdings, R for redemption.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and fixed-date Treasury funds, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. Each holding, kept to its end, pays out what its Treasuries repay, whatever its price does meanwhile. The payments aren't adjusted for inflation, so dollar Treasuries match them exactly, and for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price.

Her 2028 deposit first covers any shortfall in the ladder. Most of the rest buys more of the late-2032 fund that pays her first January, and those extra units set her minimum facility contribution. Roughly the interest they will earn goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies, the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong. Buying the minimum in 2028, not 2031, gives up some growth. Laura's living costs are paid from outside the portfolio, so she could afford more risk, but the residency couldn't: its co-sponsors, grants and program fees can shrink in the same recessions that hit shares, so her part is most useful as the one source that won't.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is what those extra units will pay, rounded down because a fund's final payout isn't fixed. Its top adds half of what her shares are then worth, moved into Treasuries before she names it. With both ends bought before anyone hears the range, she can be as sure of landing inside it as of Treasuries. Falling shares before 2031 can lower only the top, and rising shares lift it.

The ladder's weakness is money that waits: interest and early repayments sit for months before their January, earning less than planned if rates fall and stay low. Before she names her range, we assume that money earns nothing and move enough of the half of her shares she kept into Treasuries to cover it. If that falls short, the 2032 fund covers the rest and her range starts lower. Whatever that half isn't needed for stays hers, liquid and promised to no one: the financial flexibility she would lose by committing everything.

The plan's strength is that markets can change how large a range Laura names, but never whether she meets a payment or a number already named. From 2033 the ladder and the Treasuries moved to cover its shortfall become her operating reserve, and no holding is sold early. One holding is redeemed before each January, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
