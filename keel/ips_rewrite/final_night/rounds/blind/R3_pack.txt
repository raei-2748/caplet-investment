In our plan, money leaves shares the day someone starts counting on it. Laura's first $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one repaying $50,000 before each January from 2033. Her 2028 deposit buys the least she could give the facility; the most she'll name in 2031 is bought before co-sponsors hear it.

---
In our plan, money leaves shares on the day somebody starts counting on it. The reason is pricing. A fixed payment has one honest price, which is what Treasuries charge to deliver it on its date. Price it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, but the difference is a return nobody has earned yet – and American public pensions, whose rules let them do exactly this, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it. So each sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries on the day she commits to it.

The first is the operating commitment. In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do, so we define a high degree of certainty as no operating payment depending on a share price. Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it is a bet that interest rates won't fall. We call the plan Caplet XR – X for those ten holdings, R for redemption, the bond market's word for the day a bond is repaid – and none is sold before its redemption, even for the building.

Her 2028 deposit tops up the ladder if rates have moved against it. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, the smallest facility contribution she could make, and what remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. That fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, and we keep it to the interest because Laura's fortunes already ride on the economy. The deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the recessions that hit shares, and so can the residency's grants, program fees and co-sponsors. Her contribution is worth most as the part of the residency's money that doesn't shrink with the rest.

The second commitment is the credible range she gives co-sponsors in 2031. Its bottom is what the late-2032 fund will pay, rounded down, which she has owned since 2028. Its top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud gets planned around. Companies raise dividends only to levels they expect to keep for the same reason: a cut costs more trust than the rise ever earned. So when co-sponsors hear the range, both ends are already bought. The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and promised to no one.

The ladder's real weakness is that its bonds pay interest along the way, and if rates fall and stay low, that interest earns less while it waits for its January. So before Laura names her range, we price the gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. If they can't cover it, she names a lower range: the payments come first, the range second, her flexibility last.

From January 2033 the ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve, and the facility contribution is paid from the 2032 fund and the shares she moved. Each January after that, one holding is redeemed and the reserve gets a year shorter, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
