Laura buys Treasuries to repay each sum as she commits to it. Her $300,000, topped up if needed, covers ten $50,000 operating payments. Her 2028 deposit secures a minimum facility contribution, with about its interest in world shares. Both ends of her 2031 range are owned before she names it.
---
Whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys Treasuries that will repay it in time. Pricing a fixed sum at what shares usually earn makes it look cheaper by counting a return nobody has earned yet, and American public pensions, which are allowed to do that, have taken on more risk to keep up with it. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for its ten Treasury holdings, R for redemption.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and fixed-date Treasury funds, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. Held to its end, each pays out what its Treasuries repay, however its price moves before then. The payments aren't adjusted for inflation, so dollar Treasuries match them exactly, and for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price.

Her 2028 deposit first covers any shortfall in the ladder. Most of the rest buys more of the late-2032 fund that pays her first January, and those extra units set her minimum facility contribution. Only about the interest they will earn goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies, the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong. Buying the minimum in 2028, not 2031, gives up some growth. Laura's living costs are paid from outside the portfolio, so she could afford more risk, but the residency couldn't: its co-sponsors, grants and program fees can shrink in the same recessions that hit shares, so her part is most useful as the one source that won't.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is what those extra units will pay, rounded down because a fund's final payout isn't fixed. Its top adds half of what her shares are then worth, moved into Treasuries before she names it. With both ends bought before anyone hears the range, she can be as sure of landing inside it as of Treasuries. A crash on its own lowers only the top; a boom raises it.

The ladder's weakness is that some of its money, its interest and its early repayments, waits months for its January and earns less than planned if rates fall and stay low. Before she names her range, we assume that money earns nothing and move enough of her other half into Treasuries to cover it. If it can't, the rest comes out of the 2032 fund, and her range starts lower. Whatever that half isn't needed for stays hers, liquid and promised to no one: the financial flexibility she would lose by committing everything.

The plan's strength is that markets can change how large a range Laura names, but never whether she meets a payment or a number already named. From 2033 the ladder and the Treasuries moved to cover its shortfall become her operating reserve, and no holding is sold early. One holding is redeemed before each January, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
