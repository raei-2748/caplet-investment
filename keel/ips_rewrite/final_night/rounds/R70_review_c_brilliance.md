# R70 review (c): is it as brilliant as possible?

**Verdict: close, but not yet.** Real thinking, almost no decoration; the reinvestment paragraph is the best. Held back by three things: it argues against one alternative only, two of three locks get a purpose but no reason, and its densest sentences hide its best idea.

## 1. Ideas a judge would retell
1. **The payments are a debt she buys, and the range is treated the same way.** The payments are fully argued; the range only partly.
2. **A "balanced" mix is a geared bet on the building.** The strongest idea, but argued by analogy alone. Missing: every dollar the mix gains or loses lands on the third left after the payments; geared equity can be wiped out, and then the lender (the January payments) is hit; and Laura's own leftover swings less than the market.
3. **A Treasury held to maturity still waits on its interest.** Fully argued, but it leaves out that unneeded set-aside money returns to her.
4. **She gets the 2028 money back and risks only its interest.** Buried in paragraph 3 and never said outright.
5. **The bottom is bought in 2028 because a slump would also cut co-sponsors' giving.** One clause; not argued as diversifying the residency; not priced.
6. **The 2033 reserve "only on paper".** Good.

## 2. The biggest strength
Everything anyone relies on (the payments in 2027, the bottom in 2028, the top in 2031) is in Treasuries before they rely on it. So certainty comes from ownership, not odds, and market risk sits only in money nobody has been promised.
- **Stated:** yes, in paragraph 1 and the pitch.
- **Argued:** fully for the payments. For the bottom, one clause. For the top, only "can't cost her credibility", which is the commonest reason given for any lock.
- **Priced:** only against a balanced mix, and loosely ("richer by 2042"). Never against keeping the whole 2028 deposit in Treasuries, a bond-literate judge's first comparison; "still get that sum back" invites it. It never says what growth the 2028 and 2031 locks give up, or that the range is narrow by design.

## 3. Used versus mentioned
**Used and tested:** matching (reinvestment risk named, priced at zero); risk-free as a date; a floor from owned holdings (variable payout named). **Used, untested:** leverage. **Only mentioned:** diversification (a count, not a reason), liquidity (the funds' trade-off unstated), credibility, flexibility (the case's words), inflation (the building's side missing), the share-return assumption, and high certainty (implied, never given as the definition the case asks for).

## 4. Missing ideas, ranked
1. **Why hold shares at all.** Convey: the case asks for growth, and she takes thoughtful risks. Against all-Treasuries, the shares cost at most the deposit's interest. They are the only money that can grow with a building's costs. The locks trade some growth for a range nobody has to forecast. About 22 words. Displaces the two doublings: "no payment depends on shares" (paragraphs 4 and 5) and "the payout isn't fixed" (paragraphs 1 and 3).
2. **Why it is still a range when both ends are owned.** Convey: markets no longer decide where she lands; the building's needs do. The bottom is what co-sponsors plan on. About 12 words. Displaces "which buildings seldom do".
3. **Prepaid running costs as her best co-sponsor fact** (Ray endorsed this). Convey: her share of operating costs is bought before co-sponsors are asked, so their money can go to the building, where funders prefer to give. About 15 words. Home: the pitch, replacing its mechanism clause (the IPS repeats it).
4. **The lopsided promise.** Convey: missing a named top breaks her word, and beating it earns nothing, so shares behind it carry only the bad half. About 10 words net, replacing the credibility clause.
5. **The run-off.** Convey: each January one holding becomes one payment, nothing is sold, and unneeded set-aside money returns to her. This answers Wharton's question 4. About 12 words net, merged into the 2042 close.
6. **Residency diversification, named.** Convey: her share is residency money that won't fall with everyone else's. About 6 words net.
7. **Final Report** unless words appear: the one storm (the rate fall that opens the gap tends to come with the slump that shrinks the deposit); ability versus willingness; dates, not triggers.

## 5. Packaging to cut
- **"which buildings seldom do"** undercuts the argument. If buildings seldom need less, then "or less" is an empty hedge and the bottom is decoration.
- **The name works.** Once, in a working sentence; R returns as the mechanism in paragraphs 4 and 6. X is only a label. R earns its place because redemption is why a held Treasury ignores price swings, and the gloss should carry that idea, not just "the R".

## 6. Cleverness that costs clarity
- **"funds of one year's Treasuries"** reads as one-year bills.
- **Paragraph 3's opening sentence** stacks three ideas. Its payoff (the deposit comes back; only its interest is at risk) needs its own plain sentence, and "that sum" is unclear.
- **Paragraphs 4 and 5 juggle three halves.** The reader loses track of what she keeps, and nothing says why the set-aside waits for 2031.
- **The pitch** introduces shares the reader hasn't met, and gives two instances of the rule instead of the rule.
