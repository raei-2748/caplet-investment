Caplet XR keeps every sum Laura commits to in Treasuries before anyone counts on it, starting with her ten $50,000 operating payments from 2033. They're fixed in dollars and can come only from her portfolio, so we treat them as a debt. Her first $300,000 goes into ten Treasury holdings that repay it, with the 2028 deposit buying whatever that can't. The X and R in the name stand for those ten and their redemption.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But at current Treasury prices the payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and in such a mix the facility would get what was left over. Like the shares of a heavily borrowed company, that leftover would swing far harder than the share market. Ours can't, because the debt is matched first.

With what's left of the 2028 deposit, Laura buys an extra stake in the Treasury fund behind the 2033 payment, sized to pay about that amount back in December 2032. Rounding that payout down, since a fund promises no fixed sum, gives the bottom of the credible range she'll name in 2031. The stake costs less than it pays back, and the difference buys a world index fund of thousands of companies. She's willing to take risks but can't afford many, with the payments taking nearly all of her first $300,000.

Interest arrives years before the payment it's meant for, and each holding repays weeks or months before its January. That money waits at short-term rates, so years of near-zero rates would leave the payments short. Rather than guess at rates, Laura assumes the waiting money will earn nothing. Up to half her shares are sold in 2031, before she names a range, for extra Treasuries that would make up the gap even then. When that half isn't enough, the remaining gap comes out of the bottom, so the whole range moves down.

Selling the other half for Treasuries the same day sets the top at the bottom plus what it fetched. Left in shares once named, the top could be missed but never beaten, and missing it could damage her credibility. Barring a US default, markets can change what she offers co-sponsors but never what a payment gets, which is what we mean by a high degree of certainty. Every payment leans on the US government alone because co-sponsors' giving and her own earnings are already bets on good times.

In 2033 she gives the top, or less if the building, priced in Taiwan dollars, needs less, never less than the bottom. Whatever she doesn't give, any shares the 2031 sale didn't need and whatever the zero-rate assumption over-saved stay hers as flexibility, unpromised and within reach. The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 is everything still held for the payments, and each redemption pays down the debt until the last payment clears it in January 2042.
