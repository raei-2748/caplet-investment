Laura's ten $50,000 operating payments work like a debt: fixed in dollars and payable only from her portfolio. Caplet XR meets them with ten Treasury holdings, each redeemed in the year before its payment, which is where the name comes from: X for ten, R for redemption. It also lets her tell co-sponsors only figures she already holds in Treasuries.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But at today's Treasury prices the payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, so in that mix her facility contribution would be whatever is left after a fixed claim. Like the shares of a company that has borrowed heavily, it would swing far harder than the market beneath it, and a figure she named that later fell short would cost her co-sponsors' confidence.

So in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys the ten holdings, and if they cost more than that, the 2028 deposit buys what is missing before anything else. Five are Treasury bonds; five are funds that each hold only Treasuries maturing in one year and pay out when they do. All ten lend only to the US government, since a US default would shake every other dollar borrower too, and adding them would only add ways to miss a payment. That is what we mean by a high degree of certainty: barring a US default, no payment can be made to wait on a share price or an early sale.

Laura's thoughtful risks have been in her career, and her earnings can fall in the same slump that sinks shares and thins co-sponsors' giving. A portfolio heavy in shares would fall at the same moment, so hers holds few. Most of the 2028 deposit buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, and only that extra part's payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed to the dollar, counts as the bottom of the range she names in 2031. Her shares are a world index fund sized at about what that extra part will earn by 2032, so losing them all would cost only that interest.

Some of the money for each payment arrives early, as interest and as holdings repaid months before January, and it waits at short-term rates. If those rates fall and stay low, the payments could come up short. So before naming a range in 2031, Laura sells half of her shares for Treasuries, and the top is the bottom plus what that half fetched. Strong markets until then raise the top and weak ones lower it, but once it is named, shares behind it would carry every fall while any rise past it only came back to her. From the other half she sells just enough to buy Treasuries that would cover the shortfall even if the waiting money earned nothing, and should that cost more than the other half, the range she names is lower.

From then on, share markets can't move the range. She will give at least the bottom and at most the top, and where between is her call in 2033, once she sees what inflation has done to building costs in Taiwan. What she doesn't give stays hers, with the shares she kept, which she can sell on any day. None of it is promised, because the project will need things nobody can name in 2031.

The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 is what the holdings still hold for the payments, so it needs no trade. It shrinks by one redemption a year, and the tenth, a bond repaid in November 2041, meets the last payment with the interest saved for it.
