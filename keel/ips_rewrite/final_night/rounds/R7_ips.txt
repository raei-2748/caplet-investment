Whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys the Treasuries that will pay it that day. A fixed payment has one honest cost: the price of Treasuries that pay it on its date. Cost it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, because future growth is expected to cover part of it. But that growth is a return nobody has earned yet, and American public pensions, which are allowed to do exactly this, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys the operating commitment: a ladder of Treasury bonds and fixed-date Treasury funds, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. Held to its end, each pays out what its Treasuries repay, whatever markets do, so for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the day a bond is repaid.

Her 2028 deposit first fills any gap falling rates have left in the ladder – at today's rates, a small one. Most of the rest buys more of the late-2032 fund that pays her first January, and that extra sets her minimum facility contribution. Only what's left, roughly the interest it will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies, the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong. Buying the minimum in 2028, not 2031, gives up some growth. But it is the last money Laura adds, so a crash in 2029 or 2030 can shrink the top of her range but never its bottom.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is that extra in the 2032 fund, rounded down. Its top adds half of what her shares are worth by then, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because co-sponsors will plan around whatever she says. With both ends bought before anyone hears the range, she can be as sure of landing inside it as of Treasuries.

The ladder's weakness is that its bonds pay interest, and two repay months early, so some money waits for its January, and if rates fall and stay low it earns less than planned. Before she names her range, we assume that money earns nothing and move enough of the half she kept into Treasuries to cover the shortfall. If it can't, the rest comes out of the 2032 fund, and her range starts lower. Whatever that half isn't needed for stays hers, liquid and promised to no one.

The plan's strength is that markets can change how large a range Laura names, but never whether she meets a payment or a number already named. From 2033 the ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve, and nothing in it is ever sold. One holding is redeemed before each January, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
