## Paragraph by paragraph, after one read, in my words

- **Pitch.** Once someone is relying on a sum, it comes out of shares. $300k buys ten Treasuries, one paying $50k before
  each January from 2033. The 2028 money buys the minimum facility gift, and whatever top number she quotes in 2031 is
  locked in before she says it. *Got it, but the first and last sentences each made me stop.*
- **P1.** Their central rule: value a fixed payment at the Treasury rate, not at the return shares might earn, because
  that return isn't earned yet. US public pensions did the opposite and took on more risk. So each commitment is bought
  in Treasuries when she makes it, and only money nobody relies on sits in shares. *Correct paraphrase, but only after
  the second sentence. The first sentence is a riddle until then.*
- **P2.** The $300k (Jan 2027) buys a Treasury ladder, one bond or fixed-date fund maturing before each payment 2033-2042.
  Held to maturity, so "high certainty" = no payment depends on shares. Cash isn't safer because of rates. The plan's
  name is Caplet XR: X = ten holdings, R = redemption. Nothing is sold early. *Correct, except the cash sentence: I can say
  "cash isn't safer" but not why.*
- **P3.** The 2028 $150k first fixes any shortfall in the ladder, then mostly buys a Treasury fund maturing late 2032 (the
  minimum facility contribution), and the leftover, about that fund's interest, goes into a world index fund. It's like a
  bank's capital-protected note but she owns the shares, not an option. Shares are kept small because her income and the
  residency's other funding already fall in recessions, so her money should be the stable part. *Correct; two stops.*
- **P4.** The 2031 range: bottom = what the 2032 fund will pay, owned since 2028. Top = bottom plus half her shares,
  switched into Treasuries before she announces it, like companies only raising dividends they can keep. The other half
  stays hers, unpromised: that's the flexibility. *Clear. Best paragraph for me. The dividend comparison landed at once.*
- **P5.** Weakness: the ladder's bond interest has to be reinvested, and if rates stay low that earns less. So before
  2031 they assume zero reinvestment, cover the gap from her kept shares, and if that's not enough she names a lower
  range. Priority: payments, then range, then flexibility. *Correct, with a small pause.*
- **P6.** From 2033 the ladder (plus the extra moved in) is the operating reserve. The facility gift is paid from the 2032
  fund plus the moved shares. Then nothing to decide: one holding pays out each January until the tenth in 2042. *Correct
  apart from "that set-aside".*

## Sentences I had to stop on (8 of 31: pitch 3, IPS 28)

1. **Pitch, s1 / IPS, s1** – "money leaves shares the day someone starts counting on it." Who is "someone"? Laura?
   The co-sponsors? Money "leaves" shares how? I only understood it after the pension sentences. As an opening it makes
   me guess before it tells me. (Counted once in the pitch and once in the IPS.)
2. **Pitch, s3** – "the most she'll name in 2031 is bought before co-sponsors hear it." You can't buy a number. I took it
   to mean "the money behind it is put into Treasuries", but I had to work it out.
3. **P1, s4** – "...have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it." "It" = the assumed return? the
   difference? I re-read the clause in my head. The point (pricing promises at share returns made pensions take more risk)
   is good but the grammar hides it.
4. **P2, s4** – "Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it is a bet that interest rates won't fall."
   I didn't follow why holding cash is a bet on rates. Needs one more step (cash earns whatever rates are later; a bond
   locks today's rate in until the payment date).
5. **P3, s1** – "tops up the ladder if rates have moved against it." Which way is "against"? (Lower rates make the bonds
   cost more? Higher rates? The ladder is already bought by then, so what is being topped up?) I moved on without knowing.
6. **P3, s2** – "Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, the smallest facility contribution she could
   make..." The appositive reads as if the fund *is* the contribution. I think it means "which sets the smallest
   contribution she could make". Stopped.
7. **P6, s1** – "the ladder and that set-aside" – which set-aside? I had to think back to P5's "move that much ... into
   Treasuries". The word wasn't used there, so it doesn't connect.

## The strategy in three sentences, as I'd retell it
Every sum Laura commits to is put into Treasuries the moment she commits to it, so markets can't break any number she has
said. The $300k buys a ladder that pays each of the ten $50k operating payments; the 2028 $150k buys a Treasury fund for
the minimum facility contribution, with just its expected interest in a world share fund. In 2031 half the shares go into
Treasuries before she names the range, so both ends are owned, and the other half stays hers as real flexibility.
