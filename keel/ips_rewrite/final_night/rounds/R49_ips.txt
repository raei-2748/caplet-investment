Laura's ten operating payments are a debt: $50,000 each January from 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars and payable only from her portfolio. Caplet XR meets that debt with Treasuries bought years ahead, and lets her give co-sponsors only figures she already holds in Treasuries.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But at today's Treasury prices the payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, so in that mix the facility money is whatever is left after a fixed claim. Like the shares of a company that has borrowed heavily, it would swing far harder than the market beneath it. Matching each payment with a Treasury that repays in time takes that leverage away.

So in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings, each repaid in the year before the payment it meets, and if the ten cost more, the 2028 deposit finishes the job. The X in Caplet XR is those ten; the R is their redemption. The last five are bonds, and the first five are funds that each own Treasuries maturing in one year, which can be sold any day but don't promise an exact payout. Every holding lends only to the US government, since other borrowers would add ways to miss a payment without removing that one. That is our high degree of certainty: barring a US default, no payment depends on a share price or on selling a bond early.

Laura has been willing to take thoughtful risks, and her 2028 deposit comes from publishing, speaking and licensing, earnings that can dry up in the same slump that sinks shares. So most of it buys an extra stake in the fund behind the 2033 payment, and that stake's payout, rounded down, sets the bottom of the range she names in 2031. Her risk is the rest: a world index fund spread across thousands of companies, about the size of what the stake will earn by 2032. If every share were lost, the stake would still return about all she put into both.

One gap is left in the payments. Interest, and bonds repaid months before their January, wait at short-term rates until they are paid out, and if those rates fall and stay low, that money could come up short. So before she names a range in 2031, half of her shares buy enough Treasuries to cover the gap even if the waiting money earned nothing, and the other half is sold for Treasuries to make the top, the bottom plus that half, because a top left in shares could fall out of reach. If the gap costs more than half, she names a lower range, cutting the top before the bottom.

After that, no market can move her outside the range. She will give at least the bottom, and how far above it depends on what the building in Taiwan costs by 2033. What she doesn't give, with any shares the gap didn't need, is her financial flexibility, which in a bad decade can shrink to little, because the payments and her promise come first.

The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 needs no trade: it is the Treasuries still waiting for a payment. It shrinks by one redemption a year until the tenth, a bond repaid in November 2041, meets the last payment with the interest saved for it.
