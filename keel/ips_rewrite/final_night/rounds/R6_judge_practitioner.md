# R6 — Judge V5 (Practitioner): fixed-income PM at an insurer, 20 years matching liabilities

Read blind: `blind/R6_pack.txt` and `WHARTON_WORD.md` only. Fri 6 Nov 2026, Sydney.

## After one read
**The strategy:** every sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries the day she commits to it. In 2027 the ten
operating payments go into a ladder of Treasury bonds and fixed-end Treasury funds, topped up from the 2028 deposit if
needed. Most of the 2028 deposit sets a minimum facility contribution in the late-2032 Treasury fund, and only about
its interest goes into a world share fund. In 2031 half the shares move to Treasuries, so both ends of the credible
range are owned before it is named. The other half stays hers, unpromised.

**Its biggest strength:** nothing a resident or co-sponsor relies on depends on a share price, so her confidence in
the range equals her confidence in Treasuries. It is a pension buyout's logic applied to a fundraising pledge.

**Length:** pitch 50 words, IPS 495 (wc). Inside the limits.

**Top 50?** **Yes, narrowly.** It is the most rigorous of the drafts I've seen: every risk the plan carries now has a
named cover, and the confidence claim is one I could defend to a client. What keeps it narrow is that creativity and
diversification are thin next to its rigour.

## Strongest technical claim
> "With both ends bought before anyone hears the range, she can be as confident of landing inside it as she is of
> Treasuries."

This answers the case's call for a range with a stated confidence without reading it off model percentiles, which is
the usual weak spot. Runner-up: the shortfall order of cover ("move enough of her remaining shares ... If they can't,
the rest comes out of the 2032 fund, and her range starts lower").

## Weakest technical claims
1. > "If falling rates make it cost more than $300,000, as they do today"

   This is a factual claim about today's ladder cost, with no support on the page. Correct in direction (lower rates
   mean a dearer ladder), but "as they do today" must match the team's own current pricing, or it is the one number a
   checker will test. Fact-checker to verify against the latest curve.
2. > "for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price"

   Still narrower than the risk the plan carries (reinvestment rates and the funds' payouts). The weakness paragraph
   now covers that risk fully, so the definition could simply claim it.
3. > "American public pensions ... have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it"

   Still stated as settled cause and effect, with nothing on the page to support it.
4. > "and two repay months early, so some money waits for its January"

   Precise, but cryptic for a reader who doesn't know the maturities. Still not addressed: the whole reserve rests on
   one credit, the US Treasury.

## What I'd retell to a colleague tomorrow
"They never let the client say a number she doesn't already own in Treasuries: the ten payments in 2027, the minimum
contribution in 2028, the whole pledge range in 2031. So their confidence in the range equals their confidence in
Treasuries."

## The one change that would move my score most
Spend the words the repeated opening wastes on one diversification clause: the reserve is diversified across ten
dates but rests on one credit, the US Treasury, and that is the single concentration the plan accepts on purpose. It
lifts the weakest criterion and closes the last gap a fixed-income reader would circle. Second: verify or drop "as
they do today".
