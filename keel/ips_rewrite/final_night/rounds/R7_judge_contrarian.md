# R7, blind judge: the contrarian (V7)

Read only `rounds/blind/R7_pack.txt` (544 words in the pack) and `keel/WHARTON_WORD.md`. Same persona: an
equity-minded industry judge who suspects a Treasury-heavy plan is too timid for a client "willing to take
thoughtful risks". Scored only on Wharton's five criteria.

## After one read

- **The strategy:** every sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries on the day she commits to it. That covers the
  payments in 2027, the minimum facility contribution in 2028 and both ends of the range in 2031. Shares get roughly the
  interest on the minimum and are "the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong".
- **Its biggest strength:** the new closing sentence says it better than any earlier round: "markets can change how
  large a range Laura names, but never whether she meets a payment or a number already named." I could repeat that
  over dinner, which is the test.

## Is my why-2028 attack answered? Yes, mostly.

"Buying the minimum in 2028, not 2031, gives up some growth. But it is the last money Laura adds, so a crash in 2029 or
2030 can shrink the top of her range but never its bottom." That is what I asked for: the alternative named, its cost
admitted, and a reason grounded in the client's timeline. It turns early certainty from a reflex into a choice.

Two things keep it from fully closing:
1. **"Some growth" is vague.** I'd believe the trade more if the page said roughly how much is given up, or at least
   that at today's Treasury yields the expected premium over five years is small. (No number needed; the logic will do.)
2. **"Never its bottom" sits a little uneasily with paragraph 5**, where low rates can force "the rest" out of the 2032
   fund and "her range starts lower". The two are consistent, because one is about a crash and the other about rates
   *before* the range is named, but a sharp reader may stop for a second. "Never its bottom" is about crashes; the
   page could make that unmistakable.
