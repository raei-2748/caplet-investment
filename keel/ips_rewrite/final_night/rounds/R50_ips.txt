Laura's ten operating payments are a debt: $50,000 each January from 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars and payable only from her portfolio. Caplet XR meets that debt with Treasuries bought years ahead, and lets her give co-sponsors only figures she already holds in Treasuries.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But at today's Treasury prices the payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, so in that mix the facility money is whatever is left after a fixed claim. Like the shares of a company that has borrowed heavily, it would swing far harder than the market beneath it. Matching each payment with a Treasury that repays in time takes that leverage away.

So in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury bonds and funds, each repaid in the year before the payment it meets, and if the ten cost more, the 2028 deposit finishes the job. The X in Caplet XR is those ten; the R is their redemption. Every holding lends only to the US government, since other borrowers would add ways to miss a payment without removing that one. That is our high degree of certainty: barring a US default, no payment depends on a share price or on selling a bond early.

Her 2028 deposit comes from publishing, speaking and licensing, earnings that can dry up in the same slump that sinks shares. So most of it buys an extra stake in the fund behind the 2033 payment, and that stake's payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, sets the bottom of the range she names in 2031. Her thoughtful risk is the rest: a world index fund about the size of what the stake will earn by 2032. If every share were lost, the stake would still return about all she put into both.

One gap is left. Interest, and bonds repaid months before their January, wait at short-term rates, and if those rates fall and stay low, that money could come up short. So before she names a range in 2031, she sells her shares. Half buy enough Treasuries to cover the gap even if the waiting money earned nothing. The other half buy Treasuries for the top, the bottom plus that half, because a top left in shares could fall out of reach. If the gap costs more than half, she names a lower range, cutting the top before the bottom.

After that, no market can move her outside the range. She will give at least the bottom, and how far above it depends on what the building in Taiwan costs by 2033. What she doesn't give, with any shares the gap didn't need, is her financial flexibility, small in a bad decade because the payments and her promise come first.

The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 needs no trade: it is the Treasuries still waiting for a payment. It shrinks by one redemption a year until the tenth, with the interest saved for it, meets the last payment.
