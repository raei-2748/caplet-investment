Under Caplet XR, before anyone relies on a sum Laura commits to, she owns the Treasuries that will repay it. A balanced mix would suit her record of thoughtful risks. But at current Treasury prices her ten $50,000 operating payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and they are fixed, so in a balanced mix every gain or loss would fall on the last third. That third would swing like the shares of a heavily borrowed company, and her 2031 range would rest on it.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of ten Treasury holdings, one repaying before each January from 2033 to 2042, and the 2028 deposit buys any it can't afford. Caplet XR's X is the ten, and its R is redemption, the day each repays; none is sold before then. Five are funds of Treasuries that all mature in one year, sellable any day but paying out what they hold, not a fixed sum. The payments are fixed in dollars, so inflation affects only what the facility will cost.

In 2028, what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, enough to pay back about all of that money by December 2032, which sets the bottom of her range. Because it costs less than it pays back, the difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund, so the shares risk only that interest. It is bought in 2028, not 2031, because the creative residency in Taiwan can't depend on shares: its co-sponsors, grants and fees can shrink in the same recessions that hit shares, and her facility contribution, resting on the US government instead, must hold when they do.

The ladder also carries reinvestment risk: interest arrives years before the payment it serves, so money waits, earning less if rates fall and stay low. So in 2031, before she names a range, she sells up to half her shares for extra Treasuries, enough to meet every payment even if waiting money earned nothing. If that half falls short, part of the 2028 holding's December 2032 payout makes up the rest.

The bottom is what that holding will pay after this, less a margin because a fund's payout isn't fixed. Then she sells the other half and puts the money in Treasury funds repaying before 2033; the top is the bottom plus that money. Both ends are owned before anyone hears them, and in 2033 she gives what those Treasuries pay, up to the top.

Whatever of the first half isn't sold, and anything paid beyond the top, stays hers, liquid and promised to no one, so she keeps room as the project develops. In 2033 her operating reserve is already in place: the ladder, the extra Treasuries and the money waiting for them. The plan's biggest strength, and what we mean by a high degree of certainty, is that, barring a US default, no payment, and no figure once named, depends on a share price. The tenth redemption, with what was set aside for it, meets the last $50,000 in 2042.
