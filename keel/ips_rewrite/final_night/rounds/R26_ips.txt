Before Laura commits to any amount, Caplet XR makes sure she already holds it in Treasuries, and only money she has promised to no one goes into shares. Her ten operating payments of $50,000 come first, because co-sponsors may help with the facility but cannot be relied on for those. In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings, each paying out in the year before the January it pays for, and their interest makes up the rest of the $500,000. The plan is named for those ten redemptions (X for ten, R for redemption).

Most plans for a client like Laura would mix shares and bonds. Because the payments must be made in full whatever markets do, every loss in such a mix would fall on the money left for the facility, and that money would swing harder than the share market itself, just as a company's shares grow riskier the more it borrows. Caplet XR keeps shares only where a loss costs no payment, so we read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment rests on a share price, a forecast or a sale before maturity.

If bond prices rise before January 2027 and the first $300,000 falls a little short, the 2028 deposit first buys the rest of the ten. Most of what remains buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, which holds Treasuries maturing during 2032. That extra holding's payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, becomes the bottom of the range she names in 2031. The rest, about the interest that extra holding will earn by 2032, goes into a world index fund spread across thousands of companies. Laura has taken thoughtful risks all her career, and this one is sized so that losing every share would cost her only interest, never her deposit.

The plan's weak point is the money that arrives before the January it is meant for, mostly interest. It waits in a short-term Treasury fund, and if rates fall and stay low, it may earn too little to fill every payment. In 2031, before naming her range, Laura moves half of her shares into Treasuries, and the top is the bottom plus that half. She uses the half she keeps to buy enough Treasuries to fill that gap even if the waiting money earned nothing at all. If the kept half can't pay for it, she names a lower range, so no payment ever waits on shares. A good market before 2031 raises the top and a bad one lowers it, and the bottom moves only if years of low rates need more than her shares can pay for.

Owning so few shares will probably leave her less to give than a bolder plan would. But promising more than she can deliver would cost her the co-sponsors' confidence, and if the top were left in shares, a fall would cut what she had told them, while a rise would only add to what she keeps. Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, at a point she chooses in 2033 once the facility's cost is known.

The payments are fixed in dollars, so Treasuries match them and inflation matters only for the facility. Whatever is left of the half she kept stays outside the range as her financial flexibility, sellable on any trading day and the part of her money with a chance to keep pace with building costs in Taiwan. From 2033 her operating reserve is everything still waiting for a payment, and it shrinks by one holding each January until the tenth redemption makes the last payment in January 2042.
