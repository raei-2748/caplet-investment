Under Caplet XR, Laura owns the Treasuries that will repay each sum she commits to before anyone relies on it. A balanced mix would suit her record of thoughtful risks. But at current Treasury prices her ten $50,000 operating payments cost about two thirds of her $450,000, and they are fixed, so in a balanced mix every gain or loss would fall on the last third. That third would swing like the shares of a heavily borrowed company, and her 2031 range would rest on it.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of ten Treasury holdings, one repaying before each January from 2033 to 2042, and the 2028 deposit buys any it can't afford. X is for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the day each repays. Five are funds of one year's Treasuries, which can be sold any day but pay out what they hold rather than a fixed sum. Each holding is kept to the end. Inflation can raise what the facility costs, but never what she owes.

In 2028, what the ladder doesn't need buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment, enough to pay back about all of that money by late 2032. It costs less than that, so the difference, roughly its interest, goes into one world index fund, and the shares risk only that interest. This holding sets the bottom of her range. Laura, whose living costs are met elsewhere, could have left the money in shares until 2031. The creative residency in Taiwan couldn't: its co-sponsors, grants and fees can shrink in the same recessions that hit shares, and her part, resting on the US government instead, must hold when they do.

The ladder also carries reinvestment risk: interest arrives years before the payment it serves and some holdings repay months early, so money waits in a short-term Treasury fund, earning less if rates fall and stay low. So in 2031, before she names a range, she sells half her shares to buy extra Treasuries. They are enough to meet every payment even if every waiting dollar earned nothing.

The bottom is what the 2028 holding will pay, less a margin because a fund's payout isn't fixed, and less whatever those extra Treasuries cost beyond that half. Then she sells the other half of her shares and puts the money in Treasury funds repaying before 2033; the top is the bottom plus that money. Both ends are owned before anyone hears them, and in 2033 she gives what those Treasuries pay, up to the top.

Any shares the extra Treasuries didn't need, and anything paid beyond the top, stay hers, liquid and promised to no one, so she keeps room as the project develops. In 2033 her operating reserve is already in place: the ladder, the extra Treasuries and the money waiting for them. The plan's biggest strength, and what we mean by a high degree of certainty, is that, barring a US default, no payment or named figure depends on a share price. The tenth redemption, with what was set aside for it, meets the last $50,000 in 2042.
