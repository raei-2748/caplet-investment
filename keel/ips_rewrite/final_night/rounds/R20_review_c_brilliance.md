## 1. Three ideas a judge would retell
1. **60/40 behind fixed payments is leverage** (P2). The best line, and a real surprise, but half explained. "What is left over" is never named as the facility money, and the cash-out (shares sit only where a loss lands one-for-one on unpromised money) is missing. Instead the "So" jumps to the definition of certainty. A judge can retell the warning but not the fix.
2. **One borrower, on purpose** (P2). Catchy, but missing its premise: no dollar borrower is safer than the issuer of dollars, and its failure takes the others with it. Without that, a finance judge says "concentration".
3. **Buy the bottom in 2028, because shares and co-sponsors' giving fall in the same years** (P3). Argued, case-specific, retellable as it stands.

A latent fourth: both ends are owned, so the range's width reflects the building's cost, not market luck ("her choice in 2033"). Saying it outright answers the readers' "why a range at all?"

## 4. Missing ideas, ranked (cost; what they displace)

1. **Why a named number must be owned.** Said aloud, the top turns the shares behind it into a fixed promise held in a volatile asset. Once the gift is capped, a fall comes off the gift and a rise above the cap only returns to her: all downside. The case's own asymmetry backs this (overpromising costs credibility; underpromising only fails to signal). This is Damodaran's quasi-bond plus Caplet's capped-range diagnosis, in plain words. **About 30 words**, replacing the naming clause and the closing sentence.

3. **Risk only the interest, drawn out.** P3's "about that fund's interest" is a mechanic. The so-what: lose every share and the 2028 deposit still comes back whole, so the growth costs at most one fund's interest. **About 15 words**, replacing "whose Treasuries all mature in late 2032" (7) and folding P4 into P5.
