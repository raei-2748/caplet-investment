Before Laura commits to any amount, Caplet XR makes sure she already holds it in Treasuries. Her first commitment is the ten operating payments of $50,000, the one cost she cannot count on co-sponsors to share, so in January 2027 her first $300,000 buys one Treasury holding for each, redeemed in the year before its January. Their interest makes up the rest of the $500,000. Those ten redemptions give the plan its name, X for ten and R for redemption.

The usual answer would be a balanced mix of shares and bonds, which would probably leave more for the facility. But the payments, owed whatever markets do, would take most of her money, so every loss would fall on the thin slice left over, and that slice would swing harder than the share market, as a company's shares do when it borrows. Caplet XR keeps shares only where a loss costs no payment, and we read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment rests on a share price or on selling a bond before it matures. Every payment rests on the US government alone, since no dollar borrower is safer and a riskier one would only add a way to fail.

Her 2028 deposit has three jobs, in order. If bond prices rose before January 2027 and the first $300,000 fell short, it buys the missing holdings. Most of the rest buys more of the fund that pays the 2033 payment, and that extra payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, becomes the bottom of the range she names in 2031. What is left, about the interest the extra holding will earn by 2032, goes into a world index fund spread across thousands of companies. Because the extra holding alone grows back to about her whole deposit, losing every share would cost her only that interest.

Only one part of the plan can fall short. Money that arrives before its payment, mostly interest, waits in a short-term Treasury fund, and if rates fall and stay low it may earn less than the payments need. So in 2031, before she names a range, Laura moves half of her shares into Treasuries maturing before 2033, and the top is the bottom plus that half. From the half she keeps, she first buys enough Treasuries to cover that shortfall, priced as if the waiting money earned nothing. If that half is not enough, she names a lower range, so no payment ever waits on shares.

A good market before 2031 raises the top and a bad one lowers it, and once she names the range, shares move neither end. A top left in shares could fall below what she had told co-sponsors and cost her their trust, while a rise would only add to what she keeps. Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, and where it lands is her choice in 2033.

Inflation can't touch payments fixed in dollars, but it can raise building costs in Taiwan, which is why half her shares stay out of the range. Whatever is left of them is her financial flexibility, sellable on any trading day. From 2033 her operating reserve is everything still waiting for a payment, and its make-up changes only as payments leave it, until the tenth redemption and the interest that waited for it make the last one in January 2042.
