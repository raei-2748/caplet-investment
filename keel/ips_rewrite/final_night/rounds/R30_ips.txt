Before Laura commits to any amount, Caplet XR makes sure she already holds it in Treasuries, and only money she has promised to no one goes into shares. Her ten operating payments of $50,000 come first, because co-sponsors may help with the facility but cannot be relied on for those. In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys ten Treasury holdings, each redeemed in the year before the January it pays for, and their interest makes up the rest of the $500,000. The plan is named for those ten redemptions (X for ten, R for redemption).

In the usual mix of shares and bonds, payments owed whatever markets do would take most of her money, so every loss would fall on the thin slice left for the facility, which would swing harder than the share market, as a company's shares do when it borrows. Caplet XR keeps shares only where a loss costs no payment, and we read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment rests on a share price or on selling a bond before it matures.

The 2028 deposit first buys any of the ten the first $300,000 couldn't afford, and most of what remains buys more of the fund behind the 2033 payment. That extra payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, is the bottom of the range she names in 2031. The rest, about the interest the extra holding will earn by 2032, goes into a world index fund spread across thousands of companies. Since the extra holding alone pays back about her whole deposit, losing every share would cost her only that interest.

The plan's weak point is money that arrives early, mostly interest, which waits in a short-term Treasury fund and may earn less than the payments need if rates fall and stay low. In 2031, before naming her range, Laura moves half of her shares into Treasuries maturing before 2033, and the top is the bottom plus that half. With the half she keeps, she buys enough Treasuries to cover that shortfall even if the waiting money earned nothing. If that half can't pay for them, the difference comes off the range, top first, so no payment ever waits on shares. A good market before 2031 raises the top and a bad one lowers it.

Owning so few shares will probably leave her less to give. But a top left in shares could fall below what she had told co-sponsors, while a rise would only add to what she keeps. Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, at a point she chooses in 2033.

Inflation can't touch payments fixed in dollars, but it can raise building costs in Taiwan, so whatever is left of the half she kept stays outside the range as her financial flexibility, sellable on any trading day. From 2033 her operating reserve is everything still waiting for a payment, and it changes only by making one each January, the last in January 2042 from the tenth redemption.
