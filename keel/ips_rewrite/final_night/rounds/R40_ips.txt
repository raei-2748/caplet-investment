Laura's residency will count on her for ten operating payments of $50,000, which no co-sponsor can be relied on to cover, and later its co-sponsors will count on the range she names for the facility. Caplet XR makes sure each of these is held in Treasuries by the time anyone relies on it. The payments come first, so in January 2027 her whole first $300,000 goes into ten Treasury holdings, each redeemed in the year before the payment it meets. Five are bonds and five are funds holding only Treasuries that mature in a single year. The plan takes its name from them: X for the ten, R for their redemption.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But the payments are fixed in dollars and owed whatever markets do, and they would take most of her money. Every loss would then fall on the thin slice left for the facility, which would swing harder than the share market, as a company's shares do when it borrows. Matching each payment with Treasuries removes that leverage. We read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment rests on a share price or on selling a bond before it matures, and every one rests on the US government, since no dollar borrower is safer.

Laura has been willing to take thoughtful risks all her career, but her first $300,000 is spoken for, so her room for risk arrives with the 2028 deposit. If bond prices rise before January 2027, the first $300,000 may fall a little short, and the deposit's first job is the missing holding. Its second is an extra holding in the fund behind the 2033 payment, whose payout, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, is the bottom of the range she names in 2031. Its third is growth: about what that extra holding will earn by 2032 goes into a world index fund, spread over thousands of companies so that no single firm can hurt her. If every share were lost, the extra holding alone would still repay about what she put into both, so what she risks is interest, not her deposit.

In 2031, before she names a range, her shares do two jobs. Half move into Treasuries, and the top is the bottom plus that half, because a top left in shares could prove out of reach while a rise above it would only add to what she keeps. The other half buys Treasuries to cover the plan's weak point: money that arrives before its payment, mostly interest, earns short-term rates while it waits and may earn less than needed if those rates fall and stay low. The cover is priced as if the waiting money earned nothing, and whatever the other half can't pay for comes off the range before she names it, top first, so no payment ever waits on shares and a named range is never cut.

Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, and where in it is her decision in 2033, once she sees what inflation has done to building costs in Taiwan. What remains of the other half is her financial flexibility, promised to no one and sellable on any trading day.

Setting aside the operating reserve in 2033 needs no trade, because it already exists: it is everything still waiting for a payment. Each January one payment leaves it, and in January 2042 the last is paid by the tenth redemption.
