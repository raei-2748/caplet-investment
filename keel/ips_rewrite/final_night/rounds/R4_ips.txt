Laura's money leaves shares on the day somebody starts counting on it. A fixed payment has one honest cost: the price of Treasuries that will pay it on its date. Cost it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, but the saving is a return nobody has earned yet – and American public pensions, whose accounting rules let them do exactly that, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it. So each sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries on the day she commits to it.

The first is the operating commitment. In January 2027 her $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042; if rates leave it short, her 2028 deposit finishes it. A Treasury held to maturity repays what it promised whatever markets do, so for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price. A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the bond market's word for the day a bond is repaid. None is sold early, even for the facility.

Most of the 2028 deposit buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, which sets the smallest facility contribution she could make. Only what's left, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. That fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, and it stays small because Laura's fortunes already ride on the economy. Her deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same recessions that hit shares.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is that 2032 fund's payout, rounded down. Its top adds half of what her shares are then worth, which she moves into Treasuries before she names the range, because co-sponsors will plan around any number she says. So both ends of Laura's range are bought before anyone hears it. The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and promised to no one.

The ladder's weakness is that its bonds pay interest before their January arrives, and if rates fall and stay low, that waiting money earns less than we planned. Before Laura names her range, we work out the gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. If they can't cover it, she names a lower range.

This is the plan's real strength: by 2033, nothing a resident or a co-sponsor is relying on depends on a share price. The ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve. After that, each January one holding is redeemed and the reserve gets a year shorter, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
