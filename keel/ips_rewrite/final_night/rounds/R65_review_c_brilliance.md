- **"Willing to take risks but can't afford many."** Worth retelling, but it trails P3's mechanics instead of justifying them.
- **Not worth retelling: the one-borrower sentence.** Its because-clause argues for safe assets, not one borrower. "Bets on good times" also goes past the case, which never ties her income to markets.

## 2. The biggest strength
It is stated in P1's first sentence: every sum is owned before anyone counts on it, so certainty rests on holdings, not forecasts. It is argued for the payments and the top, **not for the bottom**. The thesis allows buying the bottom from shares in 2031, but the plan buys it in 2028 and gives up expected gift to do so. Its largest choice has no reason, yet the reason is in the draft, misplaced: before 2031, co-sponsors' giving and her earnings can shrink together, so the bottom is fixed while the money is certain. Moved into P3, it makes the endorsed "diversify the residency" idea Wharton's diversification answer.

**Priced:** once, against a balanced mix. Three costs of its own choices are missing:
- The bottom sits below an all-Treasury plan by the interest that buys shares.
- Buying the bottom in 2028 gives up expected gift.
- The 2031 sale gives up about two years of share growth on that half.

One clause naming the trade answers the growth-minded judge.

One step further makes it a single rule: the named range is a second debt, created when she names it and matched like the first. P1, P5 and P6 then become one idea on three dates, "a debt, not a forecast", which the draft circles but never says.
