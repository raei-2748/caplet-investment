Caplet XR makes sure every amount Laura commits to is held in Treasuries by the time anyone relies on it. Her ten operating payments of $50,000 come first, because co-sponsors' money cannot be counted on for them. In January 2027 her first $300,000 goes into ten Treasury holdings, timed so that one is redeemed in the year before each payment: five bonds, and five funds that each hold Treasuries maturing in a single year. If bond prices rise first and the money falls a little short, her 2028 deposit buys whatever is missing. The X in Caplet XR is those ten holdings, and the R is their redemption.

A balanced mix of shares and bonds would probably leave her richer by 2042. But the payments would take about two thirds of her $450,000 whatever markets did, so every loss in shares would fall on the third left for the facility. That third would swing far more than the market, as a company's shares do when most of the company is owed to lenders. Matching each payment with Treasuries takes that leverage away. We read a high degree of certainty as near-ironclad: no payment depends on a share price or on selling a bond before it matures. All ten rest on one borrower, the US government, by choice, because spreading them across other borrowers would add ways to miss a payment without removing that one.

Laura has been willing to take thoughtful risks, yet most of her 2028 deposit goes into Treasuries too, because a meaningful contribution she already owns is what can signal to co-sponsors that the residency is viable. It buys an extra stake in the fund that pays the 2033 payment, and the payout of that stake alone, rounded down because a fund's payout isn't fixed, is the bottom of the range she names in 2031. A world index fund, spread across thousands of companies, gets only about what the stake will earn by 2032. If every share were lost, the stake would still pay back about all she put into it and the shares.

The top is set in 2031, just before she names the range: it is the bottom plus half of her shares, sold that day for Treasuries, because a top left in shares could fall out of reach while any rise above it would only add to what she keeps. The other half pays for the plan's weak point. Some money arrives early, interest along the way and bonds repaid months before their January, and earns short-term rates while it waits, which may be too little if those rates fall and stay low. So she buys enough Treasuries to fill that gap as if the waiting money earned nothing. If the other half can't pay for them, she names a lower range, cutting the top before the bottom.

Barring a US default, her contribution will land in the range, and where it lands depends on what the building needs in 2033, once she sees what inflation has done to costs in Taiwan, not on markets. What is left of the other half stays in shares as her financial flexibility, sellable any day.

The operating reserve she sets aside in 2033 needs no trade, because it already holds everything still waiting for a payment. Each January one payment leaves it, until the last, in January 2042, is paid by the tenth holding and the interest saved for it.
