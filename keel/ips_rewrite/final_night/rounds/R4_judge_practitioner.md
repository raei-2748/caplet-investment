**Top 50?** **Maybe, leaning yes.** It is compliant, technically clean and rests on one correct principle that most
teams get wrong. What keeps it from a firm yes is that one well-executed idea is less than the creativity criterion
asks for, and diversification is answered weakly.

## Strongest technical claim
> "A fixed payment has one honest cost: the price of Treasuries that will pay it on its date. Cost it at what shares
> usually earn and it looks cheaper, but the saving is a return nobody has earned yet"

This is the right liability-pricing principle, in plain English. Close second: "A patient investor can wait out a bad
decade. A January cannot." It answers the time-diversification argument in two sentences.

## Weakest technical claims
1. > "for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price"

   It is literally true, but it is the wrong test. The risk this plan actually carries is reinvestment rate risk on
   coupons (the IPS admits it two paragraphs later) plus the NAV payout of the Treasury funds. A definition of
   certainty that leaves out the risk the plan carries invites the obvious objection. The closing line ("nothing a
   resident or a co-sponsor is relying on depends on a share price") repeats the same narrow test.
2. > "sets the smallest facility contribution she could make" (and in the pitch: "buys her smallest facility contribution")

   The smallest she *could* make is zero. It means the minimum the plan commits to. A careful reader will trip on it.
3. > "American public pensions ... have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it"

   Directionally right, but stated as settled cause and effect with no support on the page.
4. Not addressed: the entire reserve rests on one credit, the US Treasury. One clause saying it is the single
   concentration the plan accepts on purpose would satisfy a fixed-income reader and would also strengthen the
   diversification answer.

## What I'd retell to a colleague tomorrow
"They priced the client's ten payments at the Treasury curve and then applied the same rule to her fundraising range:
both ends are bought before she says the number. It's a pension buyout's logic used on a pledge."

## The one change that would move my score most
Widen the certainty definition to cover rates as well as shares: no operating payment should depend on a share price,
or on where rates go once the bonds are bought, beyond a gap that is already costed and covered. The zero-reinvestment
rule already does that work, so the definition only has to claim it. It costs about 15 words, which could come from
the pension clause, and it turns the one claim I'd circle into the plan's most rigorous one.
