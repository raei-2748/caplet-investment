## Three biggest problems

**1. The sentence that carries the mechanism is too compressed to follow (Books 10, 11, 12; Commandment IV).** The plan's cleverest
move is that the 2028 deposit can't lose money: the late-2032 fund grows back to roughly the deposit, and only the
growth goes into shares. The draft packs all of that into one sentence with two appositives:
"Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, the smallest facility contribution she could make, and
what remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies."
It makes a fund and a contribution the same thing, and it leaves the reader to work out that the shares only risk
the interest. The pitch does the same: "Her 2028 deposit buys the least she could give the facility; the most she'll
name in 2031 is bought before co-sponsors hear it." Book 12 says "I swear I literally can't understand half of the
sentences". This is the kind of sentence that comment was aimed at.

**2. The centrepiece, "both ends are already bought", doesn't hold up on the page (I82, "every claim must hold on the page").**
- If the bottom (the 2032 fund) and the top (fund plus half the shares, moved into Treasuries) are both bought in 2031, the
  2033 contribution is known in 2031. P6 confirms it: "the facility contribution is paid from the 2032 fund and the
  shares she moved", which means she pays the top. So the "range" is really one number with a bought minimum under it. The
  page never says why she would ever give the bottom, or what the gap between the ends is for. The case's ask
  ("how favorable and unfavorable market outcomes could affect the amount") gets the answer "after 2031, nothing".
  That could be a brilliant answer, but only if it is said outright. As written, it reads as a hole.
- "Its top adds half her shares": why half? That is a policy number with no reason given (Commandment V; Book 10's
  "random, arbitrary number plucked out of thin air").
- "The second commitment is the credible range" relabels a case word. The case's whole point is "Rather than promising one exact
  amount". The idea behind the label ("a number said aloud gets planned around") is good. The label overclaims.

**3. The plan breaks its own design rule, and October's (C2).** P3 says the share fund "is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong."
P5 then covers a shortfall in the operating payments with shares: "we price the gap ... and move that much from her
kept shares into Treasuries." The cover for that gap depends on what the shares are worth in 2031. That strains the definition
"no operating payment depending on a share price". It also brushes against the Archive's C2, which contradicts the locked
record: reflection 3 says VT is "the only investment not standing between Laura and a payment deadline". The backstop
("she names a lower range") saves the logic, but only on a second reading. The same paragraph also contradicts P4:
the kept half is "promised to no one", and then part of it is promised to the payments.

Close behind: **the growth side is tiny, and the page never says so.** On my own rough arithmetic, not the draft's: if
the share fund ≈ the interest a 2028-to-late-2032 Treasury fund earns, that is about $25k of shares out of $450k,
roughly 5%. Laura is "willing to take thoughtful risks" and wants "an appropriate balance between pursuing growth and
protecting the capital". The draft argues well for keeping shares small ("Laura's fortunes already ride on the
economy") but never quotes her willingness or admits how small "small" is. A judge who does the sum will feel the
size was hidden from them. Flexibility then rests on half of that sleeve.

## Wharton's criteria and questions (lens b)

- **Central idea:** answered in sentence 1 and P1's last line ("each sum Laura commits to is bought in Treasuries on the
  day she commits to it"). Strong.
- **Principles:** pricing at Treasury cost; buy on the day of commitment; shares only where nobody is counting; payments
  before the range before flexibility. Answered.
- **Balance of growth, risk, liquidity, reliability, flexibility:** reliability and risk are excellent; liquidity gets one good
  line ("liquid and promised to no one"); **growth is under-argued and its size is never stated**; flexibility is half
  of a small sleeve.
- **How allocation changes as needs approach and payments are made:** answered by the timeline (2027, 2028, 2031, 2033,
  then "one holding is redeemed and the reserve gets a year shorter"). Clean.
- **Protect the operating commitment while preserving flexibility for a facility contribution:** answered, apart from the
  P5 strain (problem 3).
- **Definition of high degree of certainty:** "no operating payment depending on a share price". It's a definition
  in the case's own words, and a good one.
- **Diversification:** "one world index fund holding thousands of companies", and the clever extension to diversifying the
  residency's funding. Strong.
- **Not covered:** inflation's effect on facility costs (the case asks teams to state assumptions on it). Optional for the
  IPS, but it costs nothing to say the payments are fixed nominal.
- **Pitch:** gives the central idea but not the "why" (pricing), and never names Caplet XR. Its "Her 2028 deposit buys
  the least she could give the facility" contradicts the IPS ("Most of the rest", plus the top-up and the shares).

## Brilliance (lens c): what a judge retells tomorrow

1. **Price a fixed payment at what Treasuries charge, and buy it the day someone starts counting on it**, with American
   public pensions as the cautionary tale. Erudite, and a principle rather than a product.
2. **Her contribution is worth most as the part of the residency's money that doesn't shrink in a recession.** The
   share sleeve is sized by the residency's other funders' cyclicality. Genuinely distinctive.
3. **Both ends of the 2031 range are bought before she says it**, with the dividend-smoothing analogy. Strong, if
   problem 2 is fixed.

Field-default check: the portfolio skeleton (Treasury ladder, a Treasury floor fund, a small world equity fund, a 2031
lock-in) is the default. The originality is entirely in the reasons. That's what I84 asked for, but it means every
reason has to land on first reading, and right now three of them don't.

## Factual claims I doubt

- **"Laura's first $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one repaying $50,000 before each January from 2033" (pitch; P2
  likewise).** On my arithmetic, $500k of payouts spread over years 6-15 costs about $325k at 4.25% and about $302k at 5%.
  Maturities before each January push the cost higher. So it holds only around October's yields, with coupons reinvested.
  The IPS hedges this through the top-up; the pitch states it flat.
- **"one repaying $50,000" / "repays what it promised"** for the Treasury funds: they pay NAV, which the locked October notes call
  "not fixed". Book 5 allows "near-ironclad", but the pitch's to-the-dollar claim sits closer to C10 than it needs to.
- **Pensions "two decades taking on more risk"**: broadly right (the US public-plan discount-rate literature says exactly
  this). I'd keep it, but it has no "says who".
- **Lintner**: accurate.
- **"Cash … is a bet that interest rates won't fall"**: accurate for fixed nominal payments on fixed dates, and it matches October (C3).

## What to cut whole (the IPS is 73-74 over; to lose ~100)

- **Cut P5 entirely** (77 words: "The ladder's real weakness … her flexibility last."). It removes problem 3's
  contradiction, the triad, a read-twice sentence and the "kept shares" coinage in one go. The coupon-reinvestment honesty moves to the Final
  Report. In P6 this also means dropping the dangling "and that set-aside" (a three-word deletion, not a rewrite).
- **Cut the dividend sentence** (22 words: "Companies raise dividends … ever earned."). "a number said aloud gets planned around" already makes
  the point in plain English. Lintner goes to the Final Report, where it can be cited and critiqued.
- Total: about 99 words, which leaves the IPS at ~475.
- If P5's honesty must stay, the alternative is to cut the dividend sentence (22) plus P3's "The deposit comes from … co-sponsors." (32) plus
  the pension clause "– and American public pensions … keep up with it" (24), about 78 words in all. I'd advise against it,
  because it strips two of the draft's three most erudite moves.

Do not cut "Cash only looks safer …" (October's own C3 argument) or "Her contribution is worth most …" (the draft's most
distinctive idea).
