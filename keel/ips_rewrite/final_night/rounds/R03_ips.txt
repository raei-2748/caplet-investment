Laura will stake her word on this portfolio twice: first on ten operating payments of $50,000, one each January from 2033, and then, in 2031, on a credible range for her facility contribution. Caplet XR is built so that each time she already owns the money before she gives her word. For the operating payments, that means ten Treasury holdings, each redeemed – repaid by the US government – in the year before the January it pays for, and the plan is named after those ten redemptions, X being the Roman ten.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys the ten holdings: iBonds funds, which hold Treasuries that all mature in the same December, for the 2033 to 2037 payments, and individual Treasury bonds for 2038 to 2042. At this autumn's yields, $300,000 is about what all ten cost, so the payments ask for no more than Treasuries already pay, and shares could add nothing to them except the chance of falling short. We take a high degree of certainty to mean that no payment depends on a share price, a forecast or an early sale, so each could fail only if the US government missed a payment of its own. And because every payment's cash comes from a holding that matures before it, Laura never has to sell anything to pay a January.

The obvious objection is that shares nearly always beat Treasuries over ten years, so a plan this careful leaves money on the table. It does, and we accept that for two reasons. The payments are fixed, so whatever markets do they take their $50,000 first, and every loss lands on what is left for the building; mixing shares in with the payments would make the building money riskier than owning nothing but shares. And her 2028 deposit comes from her work as an author and speaker, which can shrink in the same recession that cuts share prices.

When that deposit arrives, it first tops up the ten holdings if falling yields made them cost more than $300,000. Most of what remains buys an iBonds fund maturing in late 2032, at a price that will grow back to roughly the whole deposit by then. That leaves only a slice, about the interest the fund will earn, which goes into a world index fund. A bank would sell Laura this shape as a capital-protected note, spending the interest on an option and charging a fee; built by hand, she owns the shares outright. The world fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong, because no payment waits on it.

In 2031 the bottom of her range is what the 2032 fund will pay, rounded down because a fund's payout is not fixed. To set the top, she moves half of her shares into Treasuries before she names the range, so a crash in 2032 cannot leave her short of a figure co-sponsors have already heard. With both ends owned on the day she says them, she can give the range with near-complete confidence. Her strongest argument for co-sponsors, though, is the operating payments: a new residency's worst risk is a building that opens and then can't pay to run, and Laura's ten payments are already bought.

The plan's weak point is the interest the holdings pay along the way, which has to be reinvested until the payment it was counted for; if rates fall and stay low, it comes up short. Before Laura names her range, we work out that gap on the gloomiest assumption, that the waiting money earns nothing at all, and move that much from her shares into Treasuries first. If her shares can't cover it, she names a smaller range.

The other half of her shares is never mentioned to co-sponsors. If they knew it was there, every overrun would become a claim on it; kept quiet, it is the financial flexibility she would lose by committing everything, ready if building costs in Taiwan rise faster than the figure she named.

From 2033 the ten holdings, with the money set aside for their interest, become her operating reserve, and nothing in the plan changes again because markets move. Each January one holding is redeemed and pays that year's $50,000, so the reserve shrinks by one holding a year while her kept shares stay invested. In January 2042 the tenth redemption pays the last operating payment.
