Read only `rounds/blind/R9_pack.txt` (547 words in the pack) and `keel/WHARTON_WORD.md`. Same persona: an
equity-minded industry judge who suspects a Treasury-heavy plan is too timid for a client "willing to take

## After one read

- **The strategy:** whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys the Treasuries that will repay it. That covers the ten
  payments in 2027, a minimum facility contribution in 2028 and both ends of the 2031 range. Roughly the interest on
  the minimum goes into world shares; half of those shares are kept back, promised to no one.
- **Its biggest strength:** "markets can change how large a range Laura names, but never whether she meets a payment
  or a number already named."

## Is the why-2028 timing reason back? Half back.

R9 replaces R8's "A crash on its own lowers only the top" with "Falling shares before 2031 can lower only the top, and
rising shares lift it." That is clearer and states the timing consequence: once the minimum is bought in 2028, a
fall in 2029-30 cannot touch the bottom. But it sits in the 2031 paragraph, a paragraph after "Buying the minimum in
2028, not 2031, gives up some growth", and nothing joins the two. A reader has to make the link: *the growth she
gives up buys a bottom that falls in 2029-30 can't reach.* R7 made that link in one sentence ("it is the last money
Laura adds, so a crash in 2029 or 2030 can shrink the top of her range but never its bottom"). R9 has the fact and
leaves the reasoning to the reader.

The residency argument, which follows the cost, answers *why Treasuries*. The timing answer is still the reader's job.
