Whenever Laura commits to a sum, she buys the Treasuries that will pay it that day. A fixed payment has one honest cost: the price of Treasuries that pay it on its date. Cost it at what shares usually earn and it looks cheaper, because future growth is expected to cover part of it. But that growth is a return nobody has earned yet, and American public pensions, whose accounting rules let them do exactly this, have spent two decades taking on more risk to keep up with it.

In January 2027 her $300,000 buys the operating commitment: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042. If falling rates make it cost more than $300,000, as they do today, her 2028 deposit makes up the difference. Held to its end, each one pays out what its Treasuries repay, whatever markets do, so for us a high degree of certainty means that no operating payment depends on a share price. We call the plan Caplet XR: X for the ten holdings, R for redemption, the day a bond is repaid.

Most of the remaining 2028 deposit buys more of the late-2032 fund that pays her first January, and that extra sets her minimum facility contribution. Only what's left, roughly the interest it will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. That fund is the one place the plan is allowed to be wrong. It stays small because her income can rise and fall with the economy too: her deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same recessions that hit shares.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a credible range. Its bottom is that extra in the 2032 fund, rounded down. Its top adds half of what her shares are worth by then, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because co-sponsors will plan around whatever she says. With both ends bought before anyone hears the range, she can be as confident of landing inside it as she is of Treasuries. The other half of her shares stays hers, liquid and promised to no one.

The ladder's weakness is that its bonds pay interest, and two repay months early, so some money waits for its January, and if rates fall and stay low it earns less than planned. Before Laura names her range, we assume that money earns nothing and move enough of her remaining shares into Treasuries to cover the shortfall. If they can't, the rest comes out of the 2032 fund, and her range starts lower.

The plan's real strength is that by 2033 nothing a resident or a co-sponsor is relying on depends on a share price. The ladder and the money set aside for that shortfall become her operating reserve. After that, one holding is redeemed before each January, until the tenth redemption pays the last $50,000 in 2042.
