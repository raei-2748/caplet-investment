| B13 | (Head Librarian) Why we own the shares rather than a bank's capital-protected note: the note puts most of the money in a zero and spends the interest on a call option that can expire worthless. At today's rates the pieces cost about the face value (5-year zero 77.4%, at-the-money call about 22.9%), so "full protection with full upside" is roughly break-even before the bank's fee. Our 2028 structure, Treasuries for the minimum plus the interest in a world index fund, is the same shape without the option or the fee. | Shows we know the product our plan resembles and why ours is cheaper and simpler. It cut as "packaging" in the IPS (Ray, I84), but in the FR it is analysis. Options are banned in WInS, so it is a benchmark only. | Strategy rationale: the 2028 deposit | V57 P5 (`../drafts/FINAL/ips.txt`); maths re-run in `IDEA_LIST_MATHS.md` #16 and `IDEA_LIST_VERDICTS.md` #13; Black-Scholes (1973) |
| B14 | (Head Librarian) Why we never sell shares as they fall: portfolio insurance (CPPI-style, sell as prices drop) helped deepen the 19 Oct 1987 crash, when insurers sold futures into a falling market. Ours changes only on fixed dates (2028, 2031), so it carries no gap risk from a fast fall. | Contrasts our static plan with a famous dynamic failure. Accurate only if we keep saying ours is NOT CPPI (`FACTS.md` s4). | Strategy rationale; risks | Brady Commission report (1988); `HARVEST_ROOMS.md` #166 and idea 30; Black & Perold (1992) for the CPPI mechanics |
| B15 | (Head Librarian) The two ways pension funds cover fixed payments: match the bonds' average timing to the payments' and rebalance as rates move (Redington immunisation), or buy one bond per payment and hold it (cash-flow matching or dedication). We do the second, which needs no rebalancing. Say only that each payment has its own bond. Never "duration-matched" (our average life is 7.6 against 10.0 years) and never "we don't duration-match", because TNA reflection 1 uses the word. | Names the textbook choice behind the ladder and why it fits a plan that must not trade. | Operating reserve: design | V57 P3; Redington (1952); Fisher & Weil (1971); Leibowitz on dedicated portfolios; `FACTS.md` N10 and s4; Archive A34 |
| B16 | (Head Librarian) Bodie's point against "stocks are safe in the long run": time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and insuring against a shortfall costs more the longer the horizon. A fixed January payment can't wait for a recovery. Show Siegel's side fairly. Supporting number (re-run): shares beat a 5-year Treasury held to maturity about 55% of the time on today's assumptions (7.0% return, 16% volatility, 5.07% yield), or 61% on the pasted list's assumptions, not the 64% it claimed. | The best academic answer to "why not more shares for a 7-year horizon?". | Risk and return; the case against the "too timid" reader | Bodie, "On the risk of stocks in the long run", *FAJ* (1995); Siegel, *Stocks for the Long Run*; `IDEA_LIST_MATHS.md` row 6 (MODEL) |
