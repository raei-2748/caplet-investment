## The strategy these were tested against
A Treasury ladder (T-bonds plus iBond Treasury ETFs held to their close) is bought in 2027 for the ten fixed nominal
$50k operating payments (2033-42). The 2028 deposit buys a Treasury fund maturing late 2032, which sets the minimum
facility contribution, and roughly its interest goes into one world share fund. Before the 2031 range is named, half
the shares move into Treasuries, so both ends of the range are owned before it's said. The rest stays Laura's and is
unpromised. The plan changes on dates, never on market moves. WInS-only, with no derivatives.

## The ten strongest concepts, ranked

### 1. A promise made only when the asset is already bought ("bought deal" vs "best efforts")
- **Source:** L19 (Investment Banks), Ch.1, about 00:05. "With a bought deal, the investment bank buys your shares."
  In a best-efforts deal the bank "doesn't promise anything". The same idea comes up in L15 (Forward and Futures),
  Ch.3, about 00:23: a forward sale is riskless "only if I own the underlying commodity".
- **Applied to Laura:** the 2031 range is a bought deal. Before saying the number, Laura already owns Treasuries for
  both its ends, so the range is a sale of something held, not a forecast. The kept shares are the best-efforts part:
  they're hers, and they're promised to nobody.
- **Where:** the IPS, as the logic of the 2031 paragraph, without naming Shiller (citations go in the Final Report).
  This is the strongest Shiller support for I86's "biggest strength" (the range owned at both ends before it's said).
- **Distinctive.** It's an investment-banking distinction that almost nobody applies to a personal pledge.
- **Critique:** the analogy is about who carries the risk, not about the instrument itself. Use it as reasoning, and
  don't invent a label for it (I83).

### 2. Riskless means the maturity matches the date you need the money
- **Source:** L4 (Portfolio Diversification), about 77% through: "the maturity matches my investment horizon. I know
  exactly what I'm going to get." L3, about 71%: "Most debts are done in nominal terms."
- **Applied to Laura:** there's no such thing as a riskless asset in general. There's only one riskless asset for
  each date. Laura has ten dates, so the ladder is ten riskless assets, one for each January. Her payments are
  nominal dollars, so nominal Treasuries match them exactly. This is the case's "high degree of certainty" stated
  as a definition rather than a probability.
- **Where:** the IPS, in the certainty definition. The Final Report can cite the source.
- **Generic idea, distinctive framing.** "Safe belongs to a date" is already in `voice/06_voice_guide.md` belief 2,
  and Shiller is the authority behind it.
- **Critique and the opponent's line:** the same lecture, about 61% through, says "never in a million years should you
  ever even think of being 100% bond investor". His example held 10-year bonds for a one-year horizon, a mismatch.
  The ladder is the matched case. That makes Shiller's own line the opponent the IPS can voice and answer.

### 3. Two budgets: operating money must already exist, capital can wait for growth
- **Source:** L22 (Public and Non-Profit Finance), Ch.5, about 00:37-00:40: "every state in the United States has a
  balanced budget rule". States must balance the operating budget but may borrow for capital projects. Shiller's
  sewer story about building ahead of demand: "What if they don't come? Your plan was wrong."
- **Applied to Laura:** the case itself splits her money into an operating commitment and a facility contribution.
  The ten operating payments are paid only from money already received and already invested in Treasuries. The
  facility is the capital side, the only place where growth is allowed.
- **Where:** the IPS, one clause where the 2028 deposit or the facility is introduced. It speaks the language of
  co-sponsors, grant-makers and governments.
- **Distinctive.** It's public-finance practice applied to a private donor, and it fits the case's own words.
- **Critique:** L22 also shows that rainy-day funds ran out exactly when they were needed. So the unpromised shares
  must never be described as a cushion for the operating payments. This supports shortlist idea 4 ("keep what she
  holds back unpromised").

### 4. People's 90% ranges are far too narrow: own the bottom instead of estimating it
- **Source:** L11 (Behavioral Finance), Ch.5 "Overconfidence", 00:40:40: "you are 90% sure that the true value lies
  in this range". Shiller's Yale class landed about 80% of answers inside their ranges for world population and
  about 10% for the mass of the Earth and the number of languages. He also names the opposite way to game it:
  "You could game me by just giving excessively wide confidence intervals".
- **Applied to Laura:** the case asks for exactly this kind of interval, a "credible range" said aloud in 2031.
  Intervals that come from judgement miss far more often than their stated confidence. The plan's answer is a
  bottom that comes from assets she already owns, not from a forecast, and a top she has also bought. That keeps the
  range from being too narrow (overconfidence) and from being so wide that it's useless.
- **Where:** the Final Report (the experiment, and the team could even run Shiller's three questions on
  themselves). The IPS needs only the reasoning: the range is set by holdings, not by forecasts.
- **Distinctive.** Calibration evidence applied to a fundraising pledge.
- **Critique:** the class data is anecdotal and from 2011. Back it with the formal overconfidence literature in the
  Final Report (READING_BEYOND may have it).

### 5. In a panic, only Treasuries diversify
- **Source:** L6 (guest David Swensen), about 67-68% through: "The only diversification that would matter in that
  instance is owning U.S. Treasuries." In 1987, 1998 and 2008, investors "sold everything that had risk associated
  with it to buy U.S. Treasuries". About 4% through, Barron's on endowments in 2008: "colleges cutting budgets,
  freezing hiring, scaling back building projects".
- **Applied to Laura:** this is the strongest authority behind putting the operating payments and the bottom of the
  range in Treasuries. The 2008 detail is the exact failure the plan avoids: building projects were the first thing
  cut when endowment shares fell.
- **Where:** the 2008 detail suits the IPS as one odd, true detail (voice guide section 9: "keeps one odd detail"),
  stated without citation. Swensen goes in the Final Report.
- **Distinctive** in its source (Yale's own endowment chief, in Shiller's course) and in the detail. The idea itself
  is well known.
- **Critique and opponent:** Swensen also says Treasuries carry "a huge opportunity cost". His answer is right for an
  endowment that lasts "in perpetuity" and can wait out a crash. Laura's payments end in 2042 and can't wait. Voicing
  Swensen and then answering him is a strong move.

### 6. "Portfolio insurance" wasn't insurance: change on dates, never on moves
- **Source:** L21 (Exchanges, Brokers, Dealers), Ch.6: "They called it portfolio insurance, but it wasn't really
  insurance." It was "an automatic sell strategy", and the Brady Commission blamed it for 1987. L17, Ch.8, about
  00:55, on implied volatility after 1987: "It came right back down." L12, Ch.7, about 00:55: a fake Emulex press
  release that "was picked up by all the news services" triggered selling.
- **Applied to Laura:** a plan that sells on market moves can be fired by a crash, a rumour or a fake headline. A plan
  that acts on dates can't be. The 2031 move of half the shares into Treasuries is a fixed-date transfer of owned
  shares, not a stop-loss.
- **Where:** the IPS already distinguishes the plan from portfolio insurance (V57). Shiller's line gives the Final
  Report its source. Ray found the V57 named-concept list "packaging" (I84), so in the IPS state the reason (dates
  can't be triggered by anyone else) rather than only the name.
- **Generic** as a fact about 1987. **Distinctive** as the reason for a date-only rule.
- **Critique:** a dated sale can still land just after a crash. Swensen says endowments took "six years" to rebuild
  their equity after 1987. The answer is that only half the shares move, and only the part about to be promised.

### 7. Prudence is judged by each pot's purpose, and it drifts unless written down
- **Source:** L20 (Professional Money Managers), Ch.3, about 00:19-00:25. ERISA's prudent-expert test, verbatim: "in
  the conduct of an enterprise of a like character and with like aims". How the standard loosened: "their mind
  allowed more and more leverage to be considered acceptable".
- **Applied to Laura:** whether a holding is prudent depends on its job. Shares are prudent for the facility's upside
  and imprudent for a January payment. Judgement-based prudence drifts with the market, so the plan writes its rules
  down in advance. The same lecture (Ch.7) has defined benefit, where the employer would "set aside and invest now
  enough money", against defined contribution, which "doesn't promise what you'll get". That's the operating
  payments against the facility.
- **Where:** the IPS reasoning for "one holding, one job". The Final Report cites ERISA and the pension pair.
- **Distinctive** (the legal standard, quoted).
- **Critique:** pension comparisons are already in V57 ("pension funds cover fixed payments ..."). Ray called that
  packaging, so use the reasoning, not the name.

### 8. Spend only the income: the spendthrift trust
- **Source:** L20, Ch.6: "the child cannot get at the assets, only the income".
- **Applied to Laura:** only about the interest that the 2028 Treasury fund will earn goes into shares. The deposit
  itself is never risked. This is a trust-law rule, centuries old, that protects the principal from the
  beneficiary's own future temptation, including a later Laura in a boom.
- **Where:** the IPS, as the reason behind the size of the share fund (an answer to the contrarian: "why so little in
  shares?"). The Final Report cites it.
- **Distinctive.** It gives the share sizing a pedigree that isn't "capital-protected note".
- **Critique:** the comparison is rough. A trust pays out income, while Laura reinvests the expected interest into
  shares at the start. Say "about the interest" honestly.

### 9. Hold to maturity, and the price risk is someone else's (Hicks's term premium)
- **Source:** L8 (Theory of Debt), Ch.4 "Forward Rates and the Term Structure", 00:47:50: "forward rates tend to be
  above the optimally forecasted future spot rates". Hicks's explanation is a premium paid to holders who might have
  to sell early. The same section, on the 1925 London Times: the whole term structure is printed in the morning paper.
- **Applied to Laura:** her dates are fixed, so she can earn the quoted yield without needing to sell early. The
  premium paid for that risk goes to holders like her, who never have to sell. Rolling cash instead bets on future
  rates (V58: "Cash only looks safer").
- **Where:** the Final Report (theory, with averages). The IPS could carry one clause on why cash isn't the safe
  choice.
- **Generic** among fixed-income people, **distinctive** for a high-school team.
- **Critique:** the term premium is an average claim, not a guarantee, and Shiller hedges it.

### 10. The strongest case against nominal Treasuries, and why it doesn't bite here
- **Source:** L23 (Finding Your Purpose), Ch.4, about 00:30-00:36. After the war the Zaibatsu were forced to "convert
  their holdings of industry in Japan into yen-denominated government bonds", which hyperinflation then wiped out.
  The same section supports the plan: new regimes "see the basic financial contracts and they preserve them".
- **Applied to Laura:** inflation can ruin a bondholder whose needs are real. Laura's operating payments are fixed in
  nominal dollars, so inflation can make $50,000 buy less, but it can't make $50,000 cost more (`voice/06` belief 3).
  The facility's cost, priced in Taiwan dollars and building materials, is real, so its upside belongs in shares.
  That splits the plan along the line Shiller's example draws.
- **Where:** the IPS, one sentence (nominal for the nominal payments, shares for the real costs). The Final Report
  tells the Zaibatsu story as the opponent's best case, answered.
- **Distinctive.** It's a rarely told example used as a steelman.
- **Critique:** the case doesn't ask for inflation analysis, so don't spend more than a clause on it in the IPS.

## Close runners-up (Final Report, or one clause each)
- **Tighten up in good times** (L13, Ch.7, about 00:53: "You have got to tighten up when times are good."). Basel's
  countercyclical buffer is the bank version of moving shares into Treasuries before 2031, whatever the market is
  doing.
- **No cover when the probabilities themselves change** (L5, about 96%: "we don't well-insure against changes in
  probabilities"). A range given as probabilities loses its meaning if the odds shift. A range given as holdings
  doesn't have that problem.
- **The amortising mortgage against the balloon** (L10, Ch.4, about 00:32: "an amortizing mortgage has no balloon
  payment at the end"). In the 1930s a balloon loan needed the bank's yes again on a fixed date ("The bank says no
  dice"). A ladder never needs anyone's yes again. Shiller's 1930s mortgage table is a model for a Final Report
  payout table (year, coupon, maturing principal, holdings left).
- **One farm mortgaged three times** (L10, Ch.1: a 1778 Hartford Courant advert). The German land register of 1872
  kept "a central book, who owns what, exactly". The Final Report could carry a one-page register of which dollars
  belong to which payment, which to the facility minimum, and which are free.
- **An unwritten guarantee becomes a written one** (L10, Ch.5: "you can say a million times that you're not going to
  guarantee something"). Fannie and Freddie were officially unguaranteed and were bailed out anyway. Whatever Laura
  says in 2031 will be heard as a promise, which supports owning it before she says it.
- **AIG was solvent and still ran out of cash** (L14, Greenberg, Ch.7, 00:32: "I don't care how big you are, you run
  out of cash"). A held-to-maturity ladder faces no margin call. Use the mechanism and leave Greenberg out of it: he
  is defending himself.
- **Speenhamland** (L22): a "pay the difference" promise got gamed. It's the reason the facility minimum is a fixed
  bought sum, not a top-up.
- **Darwin and Henslow** (L22): the patron paid for the voyage, and "Darwin would end up an atheist after his voyage".
  The funder buys the means, not the outcome. It's a wry Final Report aside for co-sponsors.
- **Limited liability** (L3, about 63%, Moss: "you have already put out all the money that you'll ever put out"). The
  share slice comes only from interest and is promised to no one, so the worst case is money already spent.

## Lecture 1: Introduction (mostly course admin; thin for the plan)

**1.1 Independence is the thing that can fail** (about 67%)
- Quote: "But in fact, risks are not as independent as you think"
- Context: Shiller previews lecture 2 and says the law of large numbers leaves "no risk left" only if risks are independent. A little earlier: "and there's no risk left. That's if they're independent."
- Bearing: This is the framing sentence for the whole strategy. The ladder is chosen so that none of the ten payments depends on independence of any kind.
- D/G: G on its own. It becomes useful when paired with 2.12 and 5.2.

**1.2 Tail risk accepted as the price of the system** (about 72%)
- Quote: "Airplanes crash from time to time. You must know that when you get on one."
- Bearing: AGAINST in spirit. Shiller says the crisis is "just a blip" and the system is worth having. Laura's promise is the reverse stance: for the ten payments she refuses to board a plane that sometimes crashes. Shiller's posture is the society-level view and hers is the single-household view.
- D/G: G.

**1.3 Finance is about managing risks in life** (about 2%)
- Quote: "managing risks, that anything that we do in life is uncertain"
- Context: "Anything big or important that we do is uncertain" is in the same passage.
- Bearing: Supports a framing for the artist residency. A decade-long cultural commitment is exactly the kind of "big or important" thing that needs risk management before it needs return.
- D/G: G.

**1.4 Haiti: pledges versus owned protection** (about 80%)
- Quote: "charity doesn't work on a big enough scale"
- Context: Shiller compares goodwill after the Haiti earthquake with insurance as a business model.
- Bearing: Laura's co-sponsors are in the same position. A promise backed by goodwill and book royalties is charity-like. A promise backed by owned Treasuries is insurance-like. Useful for explaining why the 2031 range must be owned before it is said.
- D/G: D as a framing for why "owned" matters. The Haiti example itself is generic.

**1.5 Nonprofit = structure with no shareholders** (about 98%)
- Quote: "a financial structure but no shareholders"
- Bearing: Marginal. If the Taiwan residency or facility ends up as a nonprofit, "nobody takes home the money" is a fact about who is the residual claimant.
- D/G: G.

Skippable: Carnegie "Gospel of Wealth", Forbes 400, TAs, the guest list. The Carnegie material is about giving wealth away while alive, which is thematically near Laura but contains nothing about the mechanics.

## Lecture 2: Risk and Financial Crises (the strongest early lecture)

**2.1 Ruin dominates averages (geometric mean)** (about 28%)
- Quotes: "Because you care a lot, if the guy wipes you out." and "Whatever else is done after that doesn't matter."
- Context: A manager reports +50%, +30% and one -100% year. Gross return is 0, so the geometric mean is 0. Shiller calls this "a good discipline".
- Bearing: This is the formal reason a 2033-2042 obligation cannot be run like an average-return problem. One failed payment year is a zero for that goal no matter what the other nine years did. Backs the idea that certainty is per payment, not on average.
- D/G: D if used as "the plan is built on the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean".

**2.2 Value at Risk is a statement the firm cannot own** (about 43%)
- Quote: "there's a 5% probability that we will lose $10 million in a year"
- Context: Shiller on post-1987 VaR statements. He says they were too optimistic because they quietly assumed independence.
- Bearing: The 2031 "credible range" must not be a VaR-style probability statement. That kind of sentence is the thing that failed. Laura's range should be a statement about what is owned (the floor is owned Treasuries, the top is owned Treasuries after the move) and not a forecast of what will happen.
- D/G: D. It gives a historical example of the exact failure mode the plan avoids.

**2.3 CoVaR: correlations jump in a crisis** (about 53%)
- Quote: "episodes when everything goes wrong at the same time"
- Bearing: Names the exact property a diversified stock-and-bond portfolio can lose in a crisis. Supports using a maturity-matched Treasury ladder for the promised part. Treasuries are the one asset Swensen says rises in a panic (see 6.1).
- D/G: G, but good to cite with 6.1.

**2.4 The normal distribution says it could not happen** (about 96%)
- Quote: "If you believe in normality, October 19, 1987 couldn't happen."
- Context: Shiller computes a probability of 10 to the minus 71 for the 20.47% one-day fall.
- Bearing: Any 2031 range built from a normal-distribution fan chart for the stock slice would be a repeat of this mistake. See also 6.8 (Swensen puts the same day at "a 25 standard deviation" event).
- D/G: G, but 2.4 plus 6.8 together are a neat pair of different numbers for the same event.

**2.5 Survivorship and the Yale class of 1954 gift** (about 64-69%)
- Quotes: "let's all, as an experiment, chip in some money", "give it to Yale on our 50th anniversary", "they presented Yale University with $90 million dollars", "there's 1,000 of them that got squashed", and "Maybe they're the smart ones. You just don't ever hear of them."
- Context: In 1979 the Yale class of 1954 gave $375,000 to a manager for a deliberately risky 25-year gift. It became $90 million by 2004. Shiller: "so it must be partly luck." Elsewhere in the lecture he says "for every one of them that you read about".
- Bearing: Two uses. (a) It is a 25-year fixed-date gift, which is a structural cousin of Laura's fixed-date facility contribution. The class chose to risk the whole amount because they did not promise anyone anything. The difference between that gift and Laura's plan is that Laura's ten payments are promises. (b) Survivorship: the winners are the ones who tell the story. The plan should not be pitched with an anecdote about what stocks "usually" do.
- D/G: D. A vivid, true, little-known detail and a ready contrast with the promised/unpromised split.

**2.6 Ten years of stock history: both extremes in 48 hours** (about 95%)
- Quote: "that was the biggest one-day increase ever"
- Context: Oct 28-29, 1929 saw about 24% in two days down, then +12.53% on Oct 30. Shiller: "Boy is that probability, independence doesn't seem right."
- Bearing: The largest daily gain and largest daily losses cluster together. That is an argument against the discretionary reaction ("sell after the crash"). The plan's rule (move half the stock on a fixed date) is date-based, so it does not depend on being on either side of a day like Oct 30. It also exposes the plan to the reverse risk (see 6.6).
- D/G: D as an odd detail.

**2.7 "Diversify through time or across stocks" is where independence fails** (about 99%)
- Quote: "if you diversify through time or you diversify across stocks, you're supposed to be safe"
- Context: Shiller's recap. The crisis showed that neither kind of diversification delivered, because independence failed.
- Bearing: This is the sharpest sentence for the plan. A ladder is not diversification through time in Shiller's sense. It does not depend on years being independent of each other, because each rung is a contractual promise of a dated payment. My inference: the ladder replaces statistical safety (needs independence) with contractual safety (needs only the Treasury paying).
- D/G: D (the contrast between "diversifying across time" and "matching in time").

**2.8 Forecast horizons end** (about 13%)
- Quote: "all hurricanes are a surprise two weeks before they happen"
- Context: Shiller's weather analogy for crisis forecasting.
- Bearing: Supports a plan that never forecasts and moves on dates. It is an analogy, so use it as support only, not as a concept.
- D/G: G.

**2.9 Idiosyncratic risk dominates single stocks** (about 80%)
- Quote: "But even so, the idiosyncratic risk just dominates."
- Context: Apple's beta of 1.45 but the Steve Jobs health news moved it more.
- Bearing: Supports putting the stock sleeve in one world ETF (VT) rather than a hand-picked stock. The Jobs story is a vivid single-name risk example.
- D/G: G (diversification is standard). The Jobs-health story is a usable odd detail.

**2.10 Big events are the accumulation of small ones** (about 6%)
- Quote: "The crisis we got into was the accumulation of a lot of little events."
- Bearing: Mild. Could support the "fixed dates" argument: you cannot spot the moment a crisis begins, so a rule that does not depend on spotting it is safer.
- D/G: G.

## Lecture 3: Technology and Invention in Finance

**3.1 Pooling works only when the houses do not burn at the same time** (about 25-29%)
- Quotes: "then we can pool them and they go away" and "it works only because they don't all burn down at the same time"
- Context: Shiller on the Old West barn-raising and informal risk pooling.
- Bearing: My inference: all ten payments come from one decision and one pot, so there is a single house and it either has the money or not. The ladder turns each year's payment into its own separately funded "house". Nothing is pooled across years, so nothing needs independence.
- D/G: D. The barn-raising is a better illustration than the usual insurance one.

**3.2 Limited liability: the stock sleeve cannot become a debt** (about 63% and 60%)
- Quotes: "you have already put out all the money that you'll ever put out" and (the Massachusetts counter-case) "We can take your house, your car."
- Context: David Moss's point that New York's 1811 law made share investing like a lottery ticket that cannot turn into a debt. Massachusetts kept shareholders liable and lost capital to New York.
- Bearing: Strongest concept in this lecture. My inference: the VT slice is funded only from interest and is not promised to anyone, so it is a limited-liability position inside Laura's finances. The worst case is the money she already put in, and no promise to the artists depends on it. If instead the facility contribution were promised out of stocks, her liability would be unlimited relative to the asset, which is the Massachusetts rule. Gives a plain-language, non-metaphor justification for where the line between promised and unpromised sits.
- D/G: D.

**3.3 The unit of the contract should match the unit of the need** (about 71%, 87%, 81%, 86%)
- Quotes: "Most debts are done in nominal terms.", "Something that cost $1 in 1913 will cost $22 today.", "we can create a unit of account that is stable in value", "What does it do to the value of the alimony?"
- Context: Chile's Unidad de Fomento (1967) indexes contracts to inflation. Shiller notes that alimony, a long fixed payment stream, is written in currency almost everywhere.
- Bearing: AGAINST and also support. AGAINST: ten fixed nominal $50k payments lose real value (about 3% a year over a long period gives a 22-fold CPI rise since 1913, Shiller says). Support: the liability is nominal by contract, so nominal Treasuries are the exact hedge and TIPS would over-insure. My inference: the real-value gap on the artists' side belongs to the unpromised remainder, and the 2031 range can be stated in nominal dollars with a stated 2027-dollar equivalent.
- D/G: D. The match-the-denomination argument is a clear, non-obvious defence of nominal ladders. The alimony parallel (a long fixed-currency payment stream) is an odd detail, but to stay out of metaphor territory treat it as an example, not a concept.

**3.4 Indexed bonds: invented 1780, forgotten, revived in 1997 by one person** (about 73-74%)
- Quotes: "the first indexed bond was issued in the state of Massachusetts in 1780" and "they forgot about them in the United States until 1997"
- Context: Shiller credits Larry Summers (Assistant Treasury Secretary) with the reintroduction. Indexed debt reached over 10% of US debt and "they're down to 6%".
- Bearing: Detail for the inflation-indexed Treasury discussion. It also reminds the team that sound inventions can be unused for 200 years, which is an argument for being unembarrassed about a plain ladder.
- D/G: D (the 1780 and 1997 dates are not commonly cited).

**3.5 Swaps/CDS: a new instrument that was "not understood well enough"** (about 99%)
- Quote: "They were new, and they weren't understood well enough."
- Context: Shiller on the credit default swap and AIG.

## Lecture 4: Portfolio Diversification (most directly supportive)

**4.1 A riskless asset is defined by horizon: matched maturity** (about 77%)
- Quote: "the maturity matches my investment horizon. I know exactly what I'm going to get."
- Context: Shiller says the one-year government bond is not risky because its maturity matches the investment horizon. Earlier (about 53%) he uses 10-year Treasuries with a one-year horizon, which are risky because the price moves.
- Bearing: The most important quote for the ladder. Riskless is a property of the match between maturity and horizon, not of the asset. Ten payments have ten horizons, so a ladder is ten riskless assets, each matched to its own date. It also answers 4.2 below, because the result that bonds are dominated assumed a mismatched horizon.
- D/G: D.

**4.2 AGAINST: "never 100% bonds"** (about 61%)
- Quote: "never in a million years should you ever even think of being 100% bond investor"
- Context: With 10-year Treasuries and a one-year horizon from 1983-2006, adding stocks raised expected return at no more risk.
- Bearing: This is the quote someone will throw at the Treasury ladder. The answer is 4.1: the dominance result is for a horizon that does not match the bond. For the promised payments, held-to-maturity bonds are riskless in Shiller's own definition. The stock sleeve is where the expected-return argument applies.
- D/G: D as an anticipated objection with a prepared answer.

**4.3 Two-fund theorem: riskless asset plus market portfolio** (about 87-89%)
- Quotes: "All you need is two assets in your portfolio.", "the market portfolio equals the Tangency Portfolio", "everyone wants to do the Tangency Portfolio"
- Context: Mutual Fund Theorem. Everyone holds a mixture of the riskless asset and one risky fund, and the mixture depends on taste.
- Bearing: Treasuries plus one world ETF (VT) is a literal implementation of the theorem. A refinement (my inference): the theory sets the split by taste. Laura's plan sets it by contract. The promised part is forced to be riskless, and taste applies only to the unpromised remainder.
- D/G: D if the "split set by contract, not taste" point is made.

**4.4 There is no best investment, only trade-offs** (about 49%, 38%)
- Quotes: "there is no best investment. There's only a trade-off between risk and return." and "There was no theory of risk until 1952."
- Bearing: Backs the stance that the team does not claim an optimal portfolio, only an explicit trade-off for each of Laura's goals. The 1952 date gives context but is not needed.
- D/G: G.

**4.5 The cost of certainty is about 4% a year** (about 25%)
- Quote: "So, the difference is 4% a year."
- Context: Siegel: US stocks returned 6.8% a year after inflation (1871-2006) versus 2.8% for short-term government bonds.
- Bearing: Gives the price of the ladder's certainty. It is the premium the promised part gives up. AGAINST: the team should state it openly and show why the facility contribution and the unpromised remainder are where the premium is collected. Caveat: it is US data with survivorship (see 6.9).
- D/G: G, but the explicit price tag is useful.

**4.6 Norway's concentration in oil: hedging was politically hard** (about 72%)
- Quote: "because if the market for oil collapses, Norway is in big trouble"
- Context: Shiller says Norway held about 70% of its portfolio in oil and resisted hedging because the oil was a national heritage.
- Bearing: My inference: Laura's biggest concentrated exposure is her own income, since the 2028 $150k and any later money come from advances, speaking and licensing, which depend on her career. The plan banks the 2028 deposit when it arrives (into the Treasury fund) rather than hoping to fund it later. The Norway story is a clean example of an asset you cannot diversify because of attachment.
- D/G: D (the human-capital reading).

**4.7 Risk is co-movement, not variance** (about 96%)
- Quote: "Risk is covariance. It's co-movements."
- Bearing: For the stock sleeve, the risk relevant to the promise is how it moves with the things Laura's promises rely on. A world ETF has beta near 1, which is what the plan needs: no extra bet.
- D/G: G.

**4.8 Sharpe ratio: ask for the leverage behind a headline return** (about 97%)
- Quote: "I want to know what your leverage was."
- Bearing: By analogy, a 2031 range should come with a statement of exactly what holds each end. Light support.
- D/G: G.

**4.9 Odd detail: Yale was once a bond-only investor** (about 61%)
- Quote: "Yale University was probably 100% bond investor, believe it or not."
- Bearing: Shiller says this about the Markowitz era. It sits oddly with Swensen's later statement (6.5) that endowments held about 40% in bonds and cash. Not for use unless checked.
- D/G: D as an odd detail, but low reliability.

## Lecture 5: Insurance (strong on pooling, independence, and design of promises)

**5.1 AIG: a failure of independence, and shareholders absorbed the loss** (about 36-37% and 44-48%)
- Quotes: "almost entirely due to a failure of the independence assumption", "Home prices fell everywhere, just exactly what they thought couldn't happen.", "The AIG shareholders lost almost everything", and "It didn't lose a penny"
- Context: The last quote is Goldman Sachs, AIG's counterparty. Shiller says AIG's modellers believed home prices "can never fall everywhere".
- Bearing: Two separate points. (a) Insurance and every diversified-risk story rest on independence, and the plan needs none of it. (b) My inference: a personal capital structure. The ladder is senior (never touched by the stock sleeve), and the stock sleeve and Laura's remainder are the equity layer that takes the loss first. In AIG the equity took the loss and the counterparties were paid, which is the structure Laura wants for the artists.
- D/G: D for (b). (a) is generic.

**5.2 Do not insure for more than the house is worth** (about 20%)
- Quote: "you don't insure the house for more than it's worth"
- Context: Moral hazard. If insurance does not cover the full value, "there's no incentive" to burn the house.
- Bearing: My inference: a mapping to promises. The 2031 range's top should not exceed what is owned, so there is no incentive to overpromise, which is the concern in the brief about credibility. It also states the moral hazard point for Laura herself: the fixed-date rule keeps her from changing the plan when a market move tempts her.
- D/G: D. A clean, non-metaphorical test: promised amount is at most owned amount.

**5.3 Insurers exclude correlated risks; the plan excludes them from the promise** (about 91%)
- Quote: "they have to exclude it, because those are correlated risks"
- Context: Terrorism and war are excluded from traditional policies, then TRIA added a government backstop.
- Bearing: The design rule is the same as the plan's. Remove the correlated (market) risk from the promised amount and leave it with the owner. The backstop the insurers wanted is, in the plan, the US Treasury.
- D/G: D.

**5.4 Define the covered loss before the storm** (about 90%)
- Quote: "So, there was wrangling over the definition."
- Context: After Katrina, insurers disputed wind damage versus flood damage because the two had different coverage.
- Bearing: The 2031 statement to co-sponsors should define exactly what the range covers (for example "the floor is the face value of the late-2032 Treasury fund; the top is the same plus the Treasuries bought in 2031"). Ambiguity leads to disputes in exactly the bad-outcome case.
- D/G: D as a practical drafting point.

**5.5 Ten in a row (Aristotle's dice)** (about 13%)
- Quote: "to repeat the same throw 10,000 times with the dice would be impossible"
- Bearing: My inference. Ten payments must all be made. If each were 99% reliable and independent, all ten would hold only about 90% of the time; at 95% each, about 60%. "High degree of certainty" is therefore a joint statement, and only a method with near-100% per payment (held-to-maturity Treasuries) can meet it. This is arithmetic, not Shiller's.
- D/G: D. A concrete number the team can compute and show.

**5.6 Insurance does not cover changes in the probabilities** (about 96%)
- Quote: "we don't well-insure against changes in probabilities"
- Context: Mold in the American South and hurricanes, where premiums rise and are not insured.
- Bearing: Strong for the 2031 range. A range based on stated probabilities has no cover against the probabilities shifting. A range based on assets owned has no probability in it.
- D/G: D.

**5.7 Insurers' own gap: not indexed to inflation** (about 98%)
- Quote: "insurance policies are not indexed to inflation"
- Bearing: Shiller lists life annuities as something that would benefit from indexing. A ladder of fixed nominal payments is an annuity in this sense. Support for the inflation caveat in 3.3.
- D/G: G.

**5.8 Counterparty limits: guarantee funds cap at $300k-$500k** (about 56%)
- Quote: "you have to look at the insurance company that you buy insurance from."
- Context: State guarantee funds cap at $500,000 in Connecticut and New York. Shiller contrasts this with FDIC splitting across banks.
- Bearing: At $450k total Laura is above the usual state caps, so she is relying on the issuer's own credit. In the plan the issuer is the US Treasury, with no chain of AIG-style counterparties between her and the payment. My inference, and the one concentrated counterparty is a reasonable thing to say openly.
- D/G: G.

**5.9 Cat bond: repayment only if an event does not occur** (about 94%)
- Quote: "need to be repaid only if Mexico does not have a major earthquake"
- Context: Shiller says Mexico issued "$160 billion" of cat bonds in 2006. That figure is probably a slip for $160 million. Check before quoting.
- Bearing: My inference, with a warning: a contingent contract term (not a derivative) in which the top of the facility range is payable only if a named, dated test is met, like a cat bond's trigger. That would let the upper end be stated without owning it. It conflicts with the plan's rule that both ends are owned before announcing, so treat it as an option the team can reject.
- D/G: D, but weak fit. Include only if the team wants a creative extension.

## Lecture 6: David Swensen (endowments, liquidity, timing, panics)

**6.1 In a panic only Treasuries diversify** (about 67-68%)
- Quotes: "in a panic, only two things matter: risk and safety", "The only diversification that would matter in that instance is owning U.S. Treasuries.", and "Investors sold everything that had risk associated with it to buy U.S. Treasuries."
- Context: Swensen answers Barron's. He cites 1987, 1998 and 2008.
- Bearing: The best single support for the plan. The one asset that works in the crisis is the one the ladder is made of, and moving half the stock into Treasuries before 2031 adds more of it exactly where the range must hold. From the person Shiller credits as the best endowment manager of his era.
- D/G: D (from Swensen, and the three dated crises).

**6.2 AGAINST: Swensen says 25-35% Treasuries has a "huge opportunity cost"** (about 68-69%)
- Quotes: "you're paying a huge opportunity cost" and "you can be happy for six or 12 or 18 months"
- Context: He argues a long-horizon investor should hold little in Treasuries, because the crisis only pays for six to eighteen months and then the cost continues.
- Bearing: This is the strongest opposing voice and the plan needs an answer. Suggested answer: Swensen's investor has an open-ended horizon ("in perpetuity") and no dated payments, so it can wait out a crisis. Laura has ten dated payments from January 2033, and a crisis can arrive in any one of them. The opportunity cost is the price of dated certainty, and it applies only to the promised part.
- D/G: D. Naming this objection and answering it is likely to impress judges.

**6.3 Finite horizon versus "in perpetuity"** (about 15%)
- Quote: "preserve the purchasing power of the portfolio in perpetuity"
- Context: Swensen on endowments. He also says endowments have "a longer time horizon than any investor that I know" and so should hold equities.
- Bearing: Laura's horizon ends in 2042. The "long run" that rewards equity risk is only 15 years away, and the first payment is due in 6 years. Her case is the opposite of an endowment's.
- D/G: D. The perpetual versus finite distinction is usable and accurate.

**6.4 Building projects are cut first** (about 4%)
- Quote: "colleges cutting budgets, freezing hiring, scaling back building projects"
- Context: Barron's, November 2008, describing the effect of the crash on endowments.
- Bearing: Odd and relevant. Facilities are what get deferred when a volatile asset falls. The plan funds the facility's minimum from Treasuries bought in 2028 so the floor cannot be scaled back by a market fall. It matches the "facility contribution in 2033" requirement closely.
- D/G: D.

**6.5 Precommitment: "I can't stand it"** (about 41-42%)
- Quotes: "when the dollars are turning into dimes" and "I can't stand it. I'm selling all my small stocks"
- Context: 1929-32, small stocks went to $0.10 per dollar. Swensen says even the longest-horizon investor sells.
- Bearing: Supports setting the rules on fixed dates in advance, because the investor who cannot stand it will sell at the bottom. The plan removes the decision.
- D/G: G, but vivid.

**6.6 Dollar-weighted returns trail time-weighted in every fund category** (about 48-50%)
- Quotes: "In every one of those categories, the dollar-weighted returns were less than the time-weighted returns." and "Investors invested $13.7 billion and lost $9.9 billion"
- Context: Morningstar 10-year study across 17 fund categories. Internet funds returned +1.5% a year (time-weighted), but investors lost 72% of the money they put in.
- Bearing: Evidence for rules-based timing. Laura's deposits arrive on set dates (2027, 2028), so they cannot chase performance, which protects against exactly this gap.
- D/G: D ($13.7bn/$9.9bn is a striking and little-quoted number).

**6.7 AGAINST: a planned sale is still "buying high, selling low" if the date is unlucky** (about 52-53%)
- Quotes: "Same thing, buying high, selling low." and "endowments took six years to get their post-crash equity allocations back up"
- Context: After the 1987 crash, institutions sold stocks and bought bonds.
- Bearing: The 2031 move of half the stock into Treasuries is a sale at whatever price the date brings. If 2031 follows a crash, the plan locks in a loss and then misses the recovery. The honest answer: the move is symmetric (it also locks in a gain), it is made to own both ends of the range, and the loss is bounded by the unpromised nature of the sleeve. This should be stated in the write-up and not hidden.
- D/G: D (a real weakness, and honest to acknowledge).

**6.8 1987 as "a 25 standard deviation" event** (about 52%)
- Quote: "a 25 standard deviation, which is essentially an impossibility"
- Bearing: Pair with 2.4. Shiller computes 10 to the minus 71, Swensen says 25 sigma, both for the same day. Supports a range that is not a sigma statement.
- D/G: D as a paired citation.

**6.9 Rebalance on a rule, and the valuation question is "incredibly tough"** (about 85-87%)
- Quotes: "If you set up your targets and you faithfully adhere to those targets" and "But the reality is that those questions are just incredibly tough to answer."
- Context: Swensen says that because the portfolio is diversified and rebalanced, whether stocks are expensive "doesn't really matter all that much".
- Bearing: Direct support for "plan changes on fixed dates, never on market moves". One distinction: Swensen's rebalancing is triggered by drift from targets, and the plan's changes are triggered by dates, so describe the plan's rule accurately.
- D/G: D with the drift versus date distinction.

**6.10 Keynes on wholesale shifts** (about 46%)
- Quote: "sell too late and buy too late and do both too often"
- Context: Swensen reads from Keynes's General Theory: "The idea of wholesale shifts is, for various reasons, impracticable and indeed undesirable."
- Bearing: A quotable, old and independent source for not timing. Use it as the epigraph for the fixed-date rule.
- D/G: D.

**6.11 Asset allocation explains more than 100% of returns** (about 30%)
- Quote: "asset allocation actually determined more than 100% of investor returns"
- Context: Timing and selection are negative-sum after costs, so allocation must account for more than all of it.
- Bearing: The plan is an allocation decision (what is promised and what is not) plus dates, with no selection or timing. Swensen's argument says that is the whole game.
- D/G: D.

**6.12 Completely passive or aggressively active; the middle loses** (about 82%)
- Quotes: "You're either completely passive or you're aggressively active." and "but in the middle you lose"
- Context: He says individuals lacking Yale's resources should use index funds.
- Bearing: Puts the plan at the passive extreme (maturity-dated Treasury funds plus one index fund), which is where Swensen says individuals should be.
- D/G: G.

**6.13 Risk is not the standard deviation of returns** (about 98%)
- Quotes: "aren't really captured by the standard deviation of the returns" and "There tends to be a huge stability bias in the appraisals."
- Context: His reply on why he does not use the Sharpe ratio, and the observation that appraised illiquid assets look less volatile than they are.
- Bearing: Mirror image for the ladder. A bond ETF's price moves daily, but a held-to-maturity ladder's risk is missing a dated payment, which does not move with the screen price. So Laura's risk measure should be "shortfall on a payment date", not volatility. Also, Shiller's "excess volatility" credit in the same passage ("stock prices are way more variable than they need to be") fits the stock sleeve being allowed to move.
- D/G: D.

**6.14 Survivorship and the 14% odds** (about 56%)
- Quote: "that 14% chance has to go to, essentially, zero"
- Context: Arnott: 14% chance of beating the market after fees and taxes. Swensen says survivorship bias takes it to about zero. In the CRSP database 11,232 of 30,361 funds had died.
- Bearing: Same caution applies to any historical claim in the write-up, for example "stocks beat bonds over every 30-year period" (Siegel, US only). Also supports not trying to beat the market with the stock sleeve.
- D/G: G.

**6.15 Japan: 20 years of -73% in a single country** (about 70%)
- Quote: "you would have lost 73%"
- Context: Nikkei about 38,000 at end-1989 and 10,500 at end-2009, offered as a reason to diversify globally.
- Bearing: AGAINST the idea that equities are safe over 15 years. A 20-year, one-country loss of 73% is why the facility contribution above the floor is not promised, and why the stock sleeve is the world fund and not a single market. Contrast with Siegel's "no 30-year period" (4.5).
- D/G: D (Japan versus Siegel's US is a good pairing).

**6.16 Taxes are a drag on individuals** (about 78%)
- Quote: "taxes are just a huge drag on investment returns"
- Context: Yale does not pay tax; individuals do.
- Bearing: A real gap in a pure pre-tax ladder. If Laura holds taxable Treasury funds, annual coupon tax reduces what reaches 2033-2042. Worth a sentence on whether the ladder is sized after tax. Not in the brief, so flag as an assumption.
- D/G: G, but a practical catch.

**6.17 Diversification is a free lunch** (about 13%)
- Quote: "it's a free lunch"
- Context: Swensen on Markowitz.
- Bearing: Support for the single world ETF as the stock sleeve. Contrast with Shiller's 4.3 (no best portfolio, only trade-offs).

## Lecture 7: Efficient Markets

**7.1 The "loose elastic" random walk: mean reversion exists but is too slow to use**
- Quote: "you have to wait 10, 20 years for them to get back to trend" (Ch.5, 00:47:04, final minutes)
- Bears on plan: Shiller says the real market may be an AR-1 with rho near 0.99, so cheap-looking stocks do bounce back, but on a horizon longer than Laura's 2031 / 2033 dates. That supports a plan that never tries to time the stock slice and treats it as unpromised.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (the elastic-on-the-drunk's-ankle idea, applied to a dated deadline). Also usable: "more like the drunk with the loose elastic" (same place).

**7.2 A forecast is a centre plus a cone that widens with the square root of time**
- Quote: "your best forecast is that he's right where he is now" (Ch.5, 00:47:04; the same passage says the standard deviation "goes up with √n steps")
- Bears on plan: the 2031 "credible range" for the stock part is naturally a cone: centre near today's value, width growing with horizon. It also shows why the Treasury end of the range needs no cone at all (its width is zero), so the stated range is honest at that end by construction.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE as a way to explain why a range, not a number, is the right object to give co-sponsors.

**7.3 His own simulation had no fat tails, and he says so**
- Quote: "I chose a normally distributed shock. No fat tails" (Ch.5, 00:47:04, discussing 1929)
- Bears on plan: a bell-curve simulation never produces the 1929 drop. A 2031 range for the stock slice built from a normal model would be too narrow on the downside, which is the damaging direction. Owning the low end in Treasuries removes dependence on any tail model.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a professor admitting the flaw in his own demo).

**7.4 Selling the tails to fake a high Sharpe ratio (Integral Investment Management, Art Institute of Chicago)**
- Quote: "if markets go down more than 30%, there would be a problem" (Ch.1, 00:00:00, fine-print defence)
- Second quote: "you have to get their attention and explain it to them" (Ch.1, the legal duty of active disclosure, not boilerplate)
- Bears on plan: a promise that looks safe because rare disasters were sold off is the exact opposite of the ladder, which gives up upside and keeps no hidden tail. In 2031 the message to co-sponsors must say actively which part is owned and which is not, not bury it in "past returns are not a guide to the future" language.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a real $43 million case and a legal standard for disclosure).

**7.5 Lehrer, "The Truth Wears Off": careful studies decay, and negative results get sent back**
- Quote: "that person would be told, look again. Maybe you've done something wrong." (Ch.3, 00:29:10)
- Second quote: "seem to generate results that are later discredited" (same chapter, the New Yorker piece)
- Bears on plan: Shiller applies the decline effect to Efficient Markets itself: early enthusiasm filtered out contrary findings, then later studies drifted. Any estimated return (stock premium, a published backtest) is the kind of number that wears off. The ladder rests on contract arithmetic, which cannot decay, and the stock slice is not promised. The team should also apply this to its own research notes.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a scientific-method argument for preferring contracts over estimates; this is one of the few items that cites Lehrer directly).

**7.6 Charts deceive; one 100-year history is one draw from many**
- Quote: "people get deceived when they look at stock price charts" (Ch.5, 00:47:04)
- Second quote: "These are all equally likely outcomes." (same chapter, repeated random-walk runs, one with no gain in 100 years)
- Bears on plan: supports "change the plan on dates, never on market moves": the team would narrate patterns that his pink lines prove can be pure noise. It also argues against presenting the past 100 years as the stock slice's future.
- Rating: GENERIC on its own; DISTINCTIVE if the team shows its own random-walk fan.

**7.7 Information that is 24 hours late; picking a game where efficiency does not hurt you**
- Quote: "You're now 24 hours late" (Ch.2, 00:16:06, the Wall Street Journal next morning)
- Second quote: "So, part of what makes Swensen a success is picking his game." (same chapter)
- Bears on plan: a high-school team cannot beat the market on news. Matching ten dated liabilities with Treasuries is a game where market efficiency is irrelevant, because the plan is not trying to beat anything. This can be framed as a choice of game, as Swensen picked private assets.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE as a reframe; the efficiency claim itself is GENERIC.

**7.8 The textbook that deleted its own certainty (Brealey and Myers, 1984 versus 2008)**
- Quote: "why asset prices sometimes get so out of line" (Ch.3, 00:29:10, 2008 edition)
- Bears on plan: the 1984 line, that prices reflect "true underlying value," was removed and replaced with an admission of mispricing. Treasuries held to maturity are the one asset whose value does not depend on that debate. Odd detail from the same lecture: the technical analyst McGee wanted "an interior office with no windows" (Ch.4, 00:40:49), a literal version of ignoring the real world.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (quotable evidence that the field changed its mind).

## Lecture 8: Theory of Debt, Interest, Term Structure, Forward Rates

**8.1 Forward rates: today's price list already contains every future year's rate**
- Quote: "the whole future is laid out here in this morning's paper" (Ch.4, 00:47:50)
- Second quote: "I want to lock in the interest rate now" (same chapter, Hicks coffee-hour trick: buy a two-year, short a one-year)
- Bears on plan: Laura's 2028 deposit buys the late-2032 fund at whatever rate holds in 2028. The 2027 curve already implies that rate (the forward). The 2028 slice is therefore a known bet on the forward: the team can state the break-even and say exactly how much the facility floor moves if the 2028 rate differs. Shorting is outside the allowed instruments, so the lock is only partial (buying longer maturities in 2027 would use up money meant to match the ten payments).
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (turns a textbook formula into a stated risk and a number for the one tranche that is not locked).

**8.2 Forward rates run above expected future rates because of risk, and a hold-to-maturity buyer need not pay that risk**
- Quote: "forward rates tend to be above the optimally forecasted future spot rates" (Ch.4, 00:47:50)
- Bears on plan: Hicks's risk premium compensates people who might have to sell before maturity. Laura's dates are fixed, so she holds to maturity and is the buyer who can collect the quoted yield without needing to bear the price risk that yield pays for. **8.5 A coupon bond is an annuity plus a discount bond**

**8.6 Interest is set by technology and impatience together, not by anyone's patience alone**

**9.5 A stock versus a flow: $160,000 is "one time only"**

## Lecture 10: Real Estate (the debt-cycle lecture in practice)

**10.1 The balloon mortgage: a promise that depends on a future yes**
- Quote: "The bank says no dice; you're not going to get renewed." (Ch.3, 00:28:12)
- Second quote: "home prices fell in many cases by more than half" (same chapter; unemployment also hit 25%)
- Bears on plan: the 1930s failure was that borrowers needed a new loan on a fixed date just when jobs and house values collapsed together. A ladder does not need anyone to say yes later. By contrast, a plan that rolls short-term Treasuries each year does depend on a future rate. Canada 1980 repeated this: "People couldn't afford to refinance their mortgages" (Ch.4).
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (turns the ladder-versus-roll choice into a documented historical failure).

**10.2 The ladder is the mirror image of the amortising mortgage, and the mortgage table is a template for the payout table**
- Quote: "an amortizing mortgage has no balloon payment at the end" (Ch.4, 00:32:19)
- Second quote: "but your total payment is fixed." (same chapter, interest and principal shifting inside a fixed payment)
- Bears on plan: Laura's ten payments are fixed and the holding runs to zero in 2042 with nothing left to refinance, as an amortising loan does. The 1930s mortgage book shows the format: payment, interest, principal, remaining balance each period (Shiller: "what do they still owe?"). A one-page table giving payment, coupon income, maturing principal and remaining holdings by year would show a judge the plan is a repayment schedule.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (specific, non-metaphorical, and the table is buildable).

**10.3 Certainty costs something, and buyers resist paying for it**
- Quote: "that guarantee costs something to them" (Ch.4, 00:32:19)
- Bears on plan: Shiller's explanation for why only two countries (the U.S. and Denmark) use fixed-rate mortgages is that the guarantee carries a higher rate and the public resists paying it. The ladder has the same price: lower expected return than stocks. The team should name that price openly instead of presenting the plan as free.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (explains why "high degree of certainty" is a choice with a stated cost).

**10.4 An unwritten guarantee turns into a written one**
- Quote: "you can say a million times that you're not going to guarantee something" (Ch.5, 00:48:02)
- Bears on plan: Fannie and Freddie were officially unguaranteed, appeared in a newspaper section headed "Government Securities," and the government ended up paying anyway. For Laura: anything said to co-sponsors in 2031 that sounds like a promise becomes one in their minds. Keep the unpromised stock separate in words, not only in accounts. Also: the plan uses only Treasuries because agency debt has this implicit-versus-explicit gap.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (strong, specific, and about reputation, which the brief cares about).

**10.5 Skin in the game: the 5% retention rule**
- Quote: "but you have to keep 5%" (Ch.6, 01:01:06; the European directive later copied into Dodd-Frank)
- Bears on plan: regulators required originators to keep some of what they sell so they cannot say "I'm out of here" ("I'll sell the mortgage to someone else, and I'm out of here"). The team's rule of owning both ends of the range before announcing it is the same principle with retention at 100% of the promised part.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a regulatory precedent for "owned before said").

**10.6 Padding the number to fit the loan (stated income)**
- Quote: "Everybody does this, you know." (Ch.6, 01:01:06; the broker coaching a couple to raise stated income from $40,000)
- Bears on plan: the opposite error to overpromising by omission is stretching the range upward until the desired contribution fits. A credible range is set from what is owned and then reported, not set from the target.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a specific fraud pattern turned into a check on the 2031 range).

**10.7 The double-pledged farm and the central ledger**
- Quote: "nobody had any systematic way of representing title" (Ch.1, 00:00:00; Hartford Courant 1778, the same farm mortgaged three times)
- Second quote: "a central book, who owns what, exactly" (same chapter, the 1872 German land register)
- Bears on plan: each dollar must have one job. A one-page register stating which dollars are pledged to which year's payment, which fund the facility floor, and which are free would prevent the dollar-used-twice error. This is a concrete deliverable, not a metaphor.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (the most buildable idea in the lecture).

**10.8 AAA ratings that failed**
- Quote: "rated AAA, ended up defaulting and losing money for their investors" (Ch.6, 01:01:06)
- Bears on plan: certainty in the plan rests on the issuer's obligation, not on a rating, and not on structure. Consistent with limiting the promise side to Treasuries.
- Rating: GENERIC.

Odd details: mortgages appear in Sogdian silk-trade contracts from the Tang dynasty (Ch.1); "Mortuus vadium" means death pledge (Ch.1). Low relevance, but the cross-border trade-finance link to Asia is a possible colour point for a Taiwan project.

## Lecture 11: Behavioural Finance

**11.1 A 90% range from intuition covers far less than 90%**
- Quote: "you are 90% sure that the true value lies in this range" (Ch.5, 00:40:40)
- Second quote: "So, we did 80 instead of 90." (same chapter; Yale class scored about 80% on world population and about 10% on both the Earth's mass and number of languages)
- Bears on plan: the 2031 "credible range" is exactly a confidence interval. The class hit 80% on the question it knew best and 10% on the ones it did not. Stock outcomes five years out are an unfamiliar domain. So the lower end should not be an estimate at all, but an owned Treasury. Shiller's explanation: "you tend to gravitate to the first one that comes to mind" (same chapter): build ranges from several scenarios, not the first story.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (an experiment the team can run on itself and Laura as a calibration drill).

**11.2 The opposite failure: a range so wide it says nothing**
- Quote: "You could game me by just giving excessively wide confidence intervals" (Ch.5, 00:40:40)
- Bears on plan: Shiller names both errors: too narrow (overconfidence) and too wide ("zero-to-infinity"). A credible range for co-sponsors must be both owned at the bottom and informative at the top.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (two-sided definition of "credible").

**11.3 The reference point is the promise, and the kink sits there**
- Quote: "The reference point is the zero, from which I measure things." (Ch.3, 00:20:14)
- Second quote: "Insurance should be concentrated on the really big things, like life insurance." (same chapter)
- Bears on plan: in prospect theory losses bite harder than gains around the reference point. For Laura and the co-sponsors, that point is the public promise of $50k a year, not today's wealth. The ladder covers the point where the kink is; risk (VT) is taken only above it. The ladder insures the big thing (the residency itself), not small things people watch.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (a descriptive-utility argument for floor plus upside; note prospect theory is descriptive, not a proof).

**11.4 People hear probabilities as can't happen / maybe / will happen**
- Quote: "Can't happen, may be, and will happen" (Ch.3, 00:20:14)
- Second quote: "We've rounded it to zero" (same chapter, airline crash)
- Bears on plan: co-sponsors will round whatever Laura says into one of three bins. State the 2031 message in those bins on purpose: owned (will happen), range (maybe), outside the range (not promised). Also, saying "high degree of certainty" rather than "certain" is itself honest, since Treasury default is rounded to zero in most heads.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (design rule for the 2031 conversation).

**11.5 Regret theory: a dated rule means you cannot regret not acting on a move**
- Quote: "trying to avoid doing anything that you might regret later" (Ch.4, 00:35:53)
- Second quote: "You may make bad decisions, because you're overly worried about regret." (same chapter)
- Bears on plan: changing the plan only on fixed dates, and moving half the stock to Treasuries before the range is named, are precommitments that remove the regret-driven selling after a fall or buying after a rise.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE as the behavioural reason for the fixed-date rule.

**11.6 Cognitive dissonance: "after you buy a car, you want to confirm your belief in it"**
- Quote: "So, after you buy a car, you want to confirm your belief in it." (Ch.6, 00:57:16)
- Second quote: "they found that these people didn't even know how badly the fund has done" (same chapter, Goetzmann study of mutual fund holders)
- Bears on plan: the team will read evidence that supports the ladder more readily than evidence against it, and fund holders in the study did not know how badly their fund had done. Counter-measure: write down in advance, before 2027, what outcomes would count against the plan, and review actual numbers on each fixed date.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (applies the bias to the team itself).

**11.7 The advisor as yes-man; the Ford employee with all his savings in Ford**
- Quote: "The 60% left them mostly in their own company stock." (Ch.6, 00:57:16, Mullainathan's actors)
- Second quote: "They treat me well; I believe in them, I'm not going to sell." (same chapter, Uncle Ralph)
- Bears on plan: only 40% of advisers told the actor to diversify away from the employer's stock. Laura's income (advances, speaking, licensing) and her project both depend on one brand, which is her own-company stock. The team's job is to be the one that tells her what she may not want to hear, including how much of her total exposure already sits in her own name.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (shifts the question from the portfolio to Laura's concentrated income, and tells the consultants to push back).

**11.8 Anchoring: the first number in the room**
- Quote: "you are swayed by the most silly and random things" (Ch.6, 00:57:16, Kahneman and Tversky wheel of fortune)
- Bears on plan: when the situation is ambiguous, the first number spoken drags later answers toward it. In 2031, state the owned floor first and the stock-dependent top second. Also, avoid leading with the $500k nominal total.
- Rating: DISTINCTIVE (an ordering rule for the conversation, with a named experiment).

Also noted: Adam Smith's shift "from a desire for praise to a desire for praise-worthiness" (Ch.1, 00:00:00). The test: would Laura still own both ends if no one could ever check? Credibility rests on being worth believing, not on sounding so. The lecture also says successful firms "consider their long-term advantage and the reputation they have" (Ch.1).

## LECTURE 21 - Exchanges, Brokers, Dealers, Clearinghouses

### 21.1 Setting the range is setting a bid-ask spread (strongest item from this lecture)
- Quote 1: "set your bid-ask spread wide enough, that you can be picked off"
- Quote 2 (the constraint on the other side): "you can't just set your bid-ask spread arbitrarily high" (ch.7, ~01:03-01:05; context: "because then you lose all the business").
- Bearing: A quoted range is a two-sided price that better-informed co-sponsors can "pick off" (they know facility costs better). Too narrow: Laura is ruined. Too wide: nobody uses it. The lecture gives a principled way to say why her range has the width it has: wide enough to survive being picked off, narrow enough to be a credible offer.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 21.2 Dealer owns inventory; broker only finds the other side
- Quote: "A dealer trades for himself or herself, acting as a principal, not an agent"
- Also: "a broker acts on behalf of others as an agent to earn a commission" (ch.2, ~00:05).

- Distinctive: MEDIUM-HIGH.

### 21.3 Dealer's ruin: repeated bets always end in ruin if the edge is thin
- Quote: "the probability of my being ruined eventually is 1" (when win probability is 1/2, ch.7, ~01:03-01:05). Also: "You don't sleep well at night" (ch.7).
- Bearing: The plan avoids repeated bets: a ladder held to maturity has one resolution per payment and no re-trading, so no dealer's-ruin dynamic. If the team ever trades the sleeve on market moves, it re-enters the ruin game. Good argument for hold-to-maturity.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 21.4 "Portfolio insurance... wasn't really insurance": 1987 vs a static hedge
- Quote: "They called it portfolio insurance, but it wasn't really insurance." (ch.6, ~00:45-00:47)
- Also: "It was an automatic sell strategy." and "It's like a stop loss order", and the stop-loss text: "I could put in a stop loss order at 80" (ch.4).
- Bearing: Portfolio insurance sold after falls; everyone doing it at once caused the crash. Laura's version (move half of stock into owned Treasuries on a fixed date) is a static, owned hedge, not a replicated one, and it triggers on the calendar, not on prices. Derivatives are banned anyway, and 1987 is the case for why that does not hurt. My inference, not Shiller's: on 6 May 2010 "you could buy a $30 stock for $0.30", so a stop order's promised 20% floor was not what a seller got.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 21.5 Circuit breakers: rules that pause, not trade
- Quote: "automatic market halts that stop the market, when prices are falling" (ch.6).
- Bearing: The one market-move rule that survived scrutiny halts action rather than forcing a sale. A "no action on market moves; wait for the fixed date" rule is the portfolio equivalent: the pause is the policy.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM-HIGH.

### 21.6 Limit orders: a promise needs a price limit
- Quote: "There's always some price that you'd be unhappy with." (ch.4, ~00:30; "A lot of advisors say, never place a market order.")
- Bearing: A promise without a stated limit is a market order. The minimum facility contribution is the limit price: "at least X". Also practical: buy the iBonds with limit orders, and expect wider spreads in thinner ETFs.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 21.7 Gift versus exchange: why the range changes the relationship
- Quote: "you would solidify a relationship by making a gift to someone else" (Polanyi, ch.1, ~00:03).
- Bearing (my reading): Before 2031 the co-sponsors are in a gift relationship with Laura; naming a range converts it to an arm's-length quote ("a price and a quantity") that can be held against her. That is why overpromising "damages credibility", and why the range should be stated once, owned, and not improvised.
- Distinctive: HIGH, but a stretch. Offer as optional.

## LECTURE 22 - Public and Non-Profit Finance (read most carefully)

### 22.1 Two budgets: only received money backs the promise
- Quote 1: "every state in the United States has a balanced budget rule" (ch.5, ~00:37).
- Quote 2: "build the whole system in advance" ("You've got to build the whole system in advance", sewage, ch.5, ~00:38-00:40; and on failure: "What if they don't come? Your plan was wrong.")
- Bearing: States run an operating budget that must balance and a capital budget that may borrow against growth. Laura's ten payments are the operating budget: balanced by money already received (2027 deposit), so nothing depends on "if they come". The facility is the capital budget: it depends on the 2028 deposit and VT growth, so it is a floor-plus-range. The sewer story is the case against promising ahead of money; the plan is the opposite of a city that borrows for 20,000 people when 30 live there. This is a rule, borrowed from real public finance, with a clear test.
- Distinctive: HIGH (strongest single item for the co-sponsor conversation).

### 22.2 Speenhamland: "pay the difference" promises are gamed
- Quote: "couldn't identify what a person's income really was, accurately" (ch.8, ~01:01).
- Context: the town "would have the difference paid", and recipients reported less than they earned.
- Bearing: A top-up or make-whole promise ("I'll cover whatever the facility is short") invites co-sponsors to understate their own contribution. A fixed floor contribution, not a gap-filler, avoids it. This is the best real "how a gap-filling promise fails" example and fits matching gifts, which these lectures otherwise lack.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 22.3 Nonprofit status changes the room; and the nonprofit is where weird missions get funded
- Quote 1: "it changes the whole atmosphere, because they know that I'm not profiting" (Tufano, ch.2, ~00:07).
- Quote 2: "it's often in those controversial things, that some of our biggest progress is made" (ch.4, ~00:33).
- Also: "But it's this kind of weird stuff that gets financed." (ch.4); Teach for America: Kopp raised $2.5 million at 21 because "you don't have to convince the government" (ch.2).
- Bearing: Sponsors respond to visible absence of private gain. Laura's personal upside (the unpromised stock) should be kept visibly apart from the promised, non-profit money so the "atmosphere" is not damaged. A Taiwan artist residency is exactly a weird, controversial mission with no government backstop, so its balance sheet should be the least adventurous part of it: bold mission, boring funding.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM-HIGH.

### 22.4 Darwin and Henslow: a patron funds the voyage, not the finding
- Quote: "Little did he know, Darwin would end up an atheist after his voyage." (ch.4, ~00:34)
- Also: "he got financing from private donors for the Beagle" (ch.1, ~00:03).
- Bearing: Henslow financed a voyage he thought would prove one thing and it proved another. Laura promises inputs (the dated $50k), never outputs. A sponsor-facing line: "we guarantee the funding, not the result". Strong odd detail for a 2031 pitch to co-sponsors.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 22.5 The team problem and what the plan leaves out
- Quote: "the problem with teams is that people have their own individual concerns and incentives" (ch.1, ~00:01); also "Finance is really about incentivizing people to do good work, and managing risks." (ch.1).
- Bearing: The strategy handles the risk half. The incentive half (why co-sponsors commit, why they stay) is not in it. Flag as a gap to answer at the 2031 conversation. Also an honest counter-argument, not a flaw in the maths.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 22.6 Rainy-day funds are exhausted exactly on the rainy day
- Quote: "saved for a rainy day, and this was a rainy day" (ch.5, ~00:45; "most of the rainy day funds have been exhausted").
- Bearing: Do not rely on the unpromised VT sleeve as a cushion for the promised payments. A correlated crisis drains buffers and widens spreads on the same day. The plan's cushion for the promise is the ladder itself, not the buffer.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 22.7 Seniority: what GM's bankruptcy shows about claim order
- Quote: "the shareholders were wiped out" (ch.3, ~00:26; bondholders and workers took the new equity).
- Bearing: Plainly states the order of claims in Laura's design: ten payments (senior, debt-like), facility floor (Treasury-backed), VT (residual equity that gets hit first). The TEPCO example in the same chapter ("$25 a share" falling to "$5 a share") also shows why the stock leg is the one allowed to be lost.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 22.8 Records for 15 years: Bismarck and the London Times
- Quote: "They've got to keep records for 40 years." (ch.8, ~01:03)
- Also: "Technology, particularly information technology, was advancing rapidly in the 19th century." Social security ran on forms, filing cabinets and post-office stamps.
- Bearing: The plan runs 2027-2042 and the team will have graduated. The plan is the form, not a forecast: dated, written, with no judgment required. Hand over a one-page calendar of fixed dates. The Times said it would fail; it worked because the record-keeping was standardized.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 22.9 Odd details
- 1.6 million US nonprofits in 2010, about 4% of GDP; UK about 120,000 (ch.1). "42% of nonprofits pay bonuses to their executives" (ch.4). Yale: $16 billion endowment, $2.5 billion debt (ch.6): net $13.5 billion, because tax-exempt borrowing is cheap. Doorways to Dreams: "why don't we create savings plans that pay out randomly, like winning the lottery" (ch.2). Insurance companies don't remind you that you have government life insurance, "because you might say, well, I got enough already" (ch.7).
- Hospital founded by physicians but affiliated to a religious order because it "gives a sense of moral mission and social purpose" (ch.2): donors respond to visible mission affiliation.

## LECTURE 23 - Finding Your Purpose in a World of Financial Capitalism

### 23.1 Contracts survive regimes; the Zaibatsu cut the other way (and nominal vs real)
- Quote 1: "they see the basic financial contracts and they preserve them" (Iran after the Shah, ch.4, ~00:31-00:32; also Germany after WWI, South Africa 1994).
- Quote 2: "financial arrangements are capable of enduring and surviving wars and catastrophes" (ch.4, ~00:30).
- Quote 3: "convert their holdings of industry in Japan into yen-denominated government bonds" (Zaibatsu, ch.4, ~00:36; the bonds then lost to "a huge hyperinflation").
- Also: nationalizers (Lenin, Cardenas, Mao, Mosaddegh, Nasser, Indira Gandhi) hit shareholders; pensions in Iran and South Africa were kept.
- Bearing: For: senior fixed claims outlast regimes better than equity, so the promise goes in contracts (Treasuries), the risk in the residual. Against: government bonds can be inflated away (Zaibatsu). Resolution that is more than a slogan: the ten payments are nominal, so nominal Treasuries match them exactly and inflation hurts the artist, not Laura's ability to pay. The facility's cost is real (construction and fit-out inflate), so its upside belongs in a real asset (VT). A nominal promise gets a nominal bond; a real need gets a real asset.
- Distinctive: HIGH (and it faces the strongest counter-argument).

### 23.2 Unger's $100: intention does not become payment unless made visible and pre-committed
- Quote 1: "I realized later, a month later, that I never had written out $100 check." (ch.2, ~00:07)
- Quote 2: "when they're not visible to us, we don't take action to make them visible" (ch.2).
- Also: "futility is a rationalization" (ch.3).
- Bearing: Donors mean to give and don't. A ladder buys each future year's funding today, each named by its maturity: the 2037 payment is a bond already owned. That is the cheque already written, and it makes a distant, invisible artist visible. Directly about donor behaviour and the co-sponsors' own follow-through.
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 23.3 Ownership, fences and endowments: the Nature Conservancy
- Quote 1: "the way to protect habitat is to buy land, and put up fences around that" (ch.3, ~00:26; "conservation by design"; $5.6 billion in assets, third-largest US charity).
- Quote 2: "Someone is managing an endowment for the gorillas" (ch.3: "This is creative finance.")
- Bearing: A 1/3 of 1% of the world's land is protected because the charity owns it, not because it pledges. The strategy follows the same logic: own the funding, ring-fence it. Not a slogan: "extinction is forever", and a skipped residency year cannot be made up.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM-HIGH.

### 23.4 Continuous workout mortgage: write the adjustment rule in advance
- Quote 1: "they should be continuous and automatic. And they don't require anyone to apply" (ch.7, ~00:58-01:02).
- Quote 2: "They say, you have got to be kidding." (a second workout, same chapter).
- Bearing: Shiller's fix for crisis renegotiation is to pre-write the adjustment so nobody has to ask. The 2031 range works the same way if disclosed up front: here is how the facility contribution moves with the stock leg, set by the calendar and the rules, so there is nothing to renegotiate. (His triggers are income and house price; Laura's is the calendar. Say so.)
- Distinctive: HIGH.

### 23.5 Insure the real risk, not the visible one; livelihoods
- Quote: "It's the loss of economic value of a home." (home equity insurance, ch.7, ~00:55-00:58).
- Also: "Most threats to livelihoods are not due to medical events."
- Bearing: The real risk for Laura is not a 20% fall in the portfolio but a missed payment in 2037, and, separately, her own income (books, speaking) falling. Hence the promise is sized from money already received and does not count on the 2028 $150k.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM.

### 23.6 Grameen: peer enforcement in a group of sponsors
- Quote: "they then interact with each other and enforce the good behavior of each other" (ch.8, ~01:06; group "jointly liable to the debt").
- Bearing: Co-sponsors pledging conditional on each other's pledges keep each other honest at no cost to Laura. But Laura must not accept the joint-liability role; the plan's fence between the promised money and everything else says why.
- Distinctive: MEDIUM-HIGH.

### 23.7 Cosmopolitans and locals
- Quote: "Inequality is substantially due to a failure to manage risks." (ch.7, ~00:50); also: "they know how to manage their risks, so they don't get into trouble" (cosmopolitans).
- Bearing: Laura, a travelling author, is a Merton cosmopolitan; the Taiwan co-sponsors are locals who value connections. A published ladder lets them share risk management they could not buy. Optional.
- Distinctive: LOW-MEDIUM.

### 23.8 Framing quotes
- "finance is like engineering, so you have to design it" (ch.1, ~00:03): permission to present the strategy as a design.
- "talk in vague terms, not, how are we going to make something happen" (ch.1): the plan answers the "how", and vagueness is the overpromising problem.
- "time and chance happeneth to them all" (Ecclesiastes, ch.8, ~01:10; Shiller had it inscribed in Latin; he drew 362 in the draft lottery): the honest case for giving a range at all.
- "maintain an orientation toward history in the making" (ch.8).
- Malthus: "increased in a geometrical ratio and subsistence for man in an arithmetical ratio" (ch.3). Weak analogy to flat nominal payments vs compounding costs; not recommended.
- Sumner: "wages of superintendents" (ch.2). Not useful.
