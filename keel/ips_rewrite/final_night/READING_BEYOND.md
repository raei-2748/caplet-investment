## The twelve strongest concepts, ranked for this case

### 1. She hibernates the payments on day one (pension endgame, done at the start) — DISTINCTIVE
- **Source:** S5 (Insight calls hibernation "a self-managed defeasance strategy"); S3 (Leibowitz: cash matching, the
  simplest dedication, lines coupons and maturities up with a liability schedule; a "least-risk" asset for nominal
  liabilities).
- **Applies to Laura:** A pension plan only "hibernates" at the end of its life, once it is finally funded enough to
  match every payment with bonds. Laura is funded for that on her first day, because the $300,000 is enough to buy the
  ten payments outright, so her plan starts where a pension plan's ends, six years before the first payment is due.
- **Where:** IPS (the idea, unnamed or named once as "what a pension does at the end"); Final Report (cite S3, S5; the
  funded-ratio arithmetic from FACTS.md).
- **Critique:** Hibernation is a sponsor's choice made when the surplus is already safe; Laura's ladder costs nearly the
  whole deposit (headroom thin and rate-dependent, FACTS.md), so the "fully funded on day one" claim holds only on the
  holdings basis at the October fills. The ladder is also only nearly a cash match: coupons arrive before their
  January and must wait (the IPS's own weakness paragraph), which is exactly where true dedication differs from
  hibernation-by-buyout.

### 2. Pricing promises at hoped-for returns pushes people into risk (the evidence under R4's opening) — DISTINCTIVE
- **Source:** S1 (US public funds whose liability discount rate is tied to expected returns took more risk and used
  higher discount rates; more so when underfunded or with more politicians on the board; the extra risk-taking is
  negatively related to performance; compared against Canadian, European and US private funds). S2 for the size of the
  valuation gap.
- **Applies to Laura:** Cost her ten payments at what shares might earn and they look cheaper, and the gap is an
  invitation to hold more shares to "afford" them. That is the mechanism S1 measures in real pension funds; Laura's
  rule removes it by costing every sum at what Treasuries charge.
- **Where:** IPS (the mechanism, uncited, as R4 already does); Final Report (cite S1 and S2).
- **Fact-check flag for R3:** R4 says US public pensions "have spent two decades taking on more risk". S1 supports the
  direction (incentive → more risk), but its sample years were not verified from the full text (paywalled). "Two
  decades" is UNVERIFIED as stated; safer is a claim about the mechanism, not a span of years.
- **Critique:** S1 is about agency (boards, politicians) as much as accounting; Laura has no board. The lesson carries
  (the cheaper-looking price tempts risk) but the evidence's strongest driver does not.

### 3. Rules beat discretion when someone is planning around your word — DISTINCTIVE
- **Source:** S6 (Kydland & Prescott: re-optimising at each new situation does not give the best outcome, because
  planning is a game against people who form expectations; hence binding rules, the basis of central-bank
  independence).
- **Applies to Laura:** The plan changes only on dated decisions (2027, 2028, 2031, 2033), never on a market move. That
  is a commitment device in Kydland–Prescott's exact sense: co-sponsors plan around what she says, so a plan she might
  re-optimise in a boom ("sell a rung, the building needs it") is worth less to them than one she can't. The plan's real
  temptation is a good year, not a bad one (`voice/06_voice_guide.md` belief 6).
- **Where:** IPS (the "dates, not market moves" principle, explained by its reason); Final Report (cite S6, the Nobel
  lineage, the boom temptation).
- **Critique:** K–P's agents are a whole economy reacting to a policymaker; here they are a few co-sponsors, and Laura
  can't make her rule legally binding. The rule is credible only because the money is already in Treasuries that mature
  on the dates (the commitment is physical, not a promise), which is a stronger form than K–P's.

### 4. Cash only looks safe: for a fixed future payment, the "risk-free" asset is the matching bond — DISTINCTIVE
- **Source:** S7 (Merton: a T-bill portfolio barely moves in value, yet is risky measured as the income it will buy;
  measure risk against the goal, and defease the goal with assets that pay it).
- **Applies to Laura:** Holding her $300,000 in cash until 2033 would look like the safest choice on any statement and
  would actually be a bet that rates don't fall before then. The Treasury maturing before each January is the asset that
  is safe *for that January*. ("Safe belongs to a date, not to an asset", voice guide belief 2.)
- **Where:** IPS (one sentence; V58 had it as "Cash only looks safer"); Final Report (cite S7 with the T-bill example).
- **Critique:** Merton's goal is inflation-proof income, so his safe asset is an indexed annuity or TIPS. Laura's payments
  are fixed nominal dollars, so nominal Treasuries are the exact match and TIPS would be the mismatch (and individual
  TIPS are banned anyway, WHARTON_WORD §5). Say "Merton's logic, our case's asset".

### 5. Her bought minimum is seed money for a capital campaign — DISTINCTIVE (adds to Gneezy)
- **Source:** S8 (field experiment, about 3,000 households, a university capital campaign: raising announced seed money
  from 10% to 67% of the goal raised contributions roughly sixfold; it raised both the share who gave and the average
  gift).
- **Applies to Laura:** A building is a "threshold" good: it gets built only if enough money arrives. The bottom of her
  2031 range is money she already owns in Treasuries, so when she names it, it works as announced seed money, which is
  the strongest lever in this literature. Gneezy 2014 (already in `ladder_opus.md`) covers the other half: her ten
  payments being bought means co-sponsors' money goes to the building, not running costs.
- **Where:** IPS (half a sentence, uncited, at most); Final Report (cite S8 + Gneezy; it is the co-sponsor
  communication's evidence).
- **Critique:** S8's donors are alumni giving small sums by mail; Laura's co-sponsors are organisations negotiating
  larger ones. The direction likely carries; the sixfold size does not. Never put the multiple in the IPS.

### 6. Time makes a shortfall rarer, not cheaper to insure (why the payments hold no shares) — GENERIC IDEA, SHARP USE
- **Source:** S9 (Bodie 1995: the price of a put insuring shares against trailing the risk-free return rises with the
  horizon; restated 2020; disputed in FAJ 1996 as a measure of risk, though the rebuttal accepts it as the market price
  of that insurance).
- **Maths (checked):** at-the-money-forward put / spot = 2N(σ√T/2) − 1 under Black–Scholes. σ = 16%: T = 1 → 6.4%;
  T = 6 → 15.5%; T = 10 → 20.0% of the money insured. So insuring a share fund to match Treasuries over the six years
  to 2033 costs about two and a half times the one-year price.
- **Applies to Laura:** "Shares are safe over ten years" is the opponent's line; the counter is that the cost of making
  them safe grows with time, and a fixed January can't wait for a recovery. R4's "A patient investor can wait out a bad
  decade. A January cannot." is this idea in plain words.
- **Where:** IPS (the idea, no numbers); Final Report (cite S9 and the rebuttal, show the three put prices).
- **Critique:** Assumes lognormal returns with no mean reversion; critics argue mean reversion cheapens long puts
  (S9 sources). Present Siegel's side in the Final Report.

### 7. Two portfolios, one per kind of goal (goal-hedging vs performance-seeking) — GENERIC LABEL, CRITIQUE IS THE VALUE
- **Source:** S10 (secure essential goals with the highest confidence, maximise the chance of aspirational ones; a
  goal-hedging portfolio plus a performance-seeking one); S11 (each mental account gets its own risk attitude, defined
  as the probability of missing that account's threshold; the total is still mean-variance efficient).
- **Applies to Laura:** The ten payments are the essential goal (hedged exactly); the facility contribution above its
  bought bottom is the aspirational one. S11's result is the useful bit: splitting money by goal costs almost nothing in
  efficiency, so the separate accounts are not a behavioural weakness.
- **Where:** Final Report (cite both; show how our split differs). IPS: no label.
- **Critique (the point to make):** EDHEC sizes the risky portfolio from the gap between wealth and a floor and resizes
  it as markets move (portfolio insurance, CPPI-like). Our plan does not: the share fund is fixed at roughly the
  2028 fund's interest and moves only on dated decisions. That is deliberate (concept 3). Never call our plan CPPI
  (BIBLE: a static set-aside plus a cushion is not CPPI).

### 8. Promise the low quantile: the newsvendor's critical fractile — DISTINCTIVE, already half-found
- **Source:** S12 (the formal inventory model; the optimal order is the demand distribution's critical fractile).
- **Maths (checked):** if over-promising costs k times what under-promising costs, promise the 1/(1+k) quantile. A
  range top reached about 3 times in 4 sits at the 25th percentile, which is k = 3: the plan treats over-promising as
  three times worse. That matches the line Ahaan praised in Book 9 and turns it into a derivation, not a judgement.
- **Applies to Laura:** The case says over-promising damages credibility; the newsvendor gives the exact trade-off a
  given top implies.
- **Where:** Final Report only (it is a model output; Book 11 bans models in the IPS). Commandment V: "we calculate",
  never "we judge".
- **Critique:** Credibility costs aren't linear or known; k is a choice dressed as a number. Say so.

### 9. A named number is a dividend: cutting it is punished — DISTINCTIVE PAIRING
- **Source:** S13 (managers avoid dividend changes they may have to reverse, because markets punish cuts; partial
  adjustment toward a target). Later survey (Brav, Graham, Harvey & Michaely, *JFE* 2005, UNVERIFIED citation details
  here) finds less reluctance to omit than in 1956.
- **Applies to Laura:** Once co-sponsors hear her range they treat it like a dividend: an increase is welcome, a cut is
  remembered. So she names only what she has already moved into Treasuries (R4's "both ends bought before anyone hears
  it"), the same instinct as a firm that raises its dividend only when it can keep it.
- **Where:** Final Report (cite S13); the IPS already carries the logic without the name.
- **Critique:** Dividend signalling works through share prices; co-sponsor trust has no market price. Analogy, not
  evidence.

### 10. Her career is already an equity holding (human capital) — USE WITH CARE
- **Source:** S14 (Bodie–Merton–Samuelson: the ability to change how much one works later lets a person take more
  investment risk now). Related, UNVERIFIED here: Campbell & Viceira (*Strategic Asset Allocation*, 2002) on labour
  income correlated with stocks reducing the optimal share weight.
- **Applies to Laura:** Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can fall in the
  same recessions as shares (the case's own list). Income that behaves like shares argues for fewer shares in the
  portfolio that depends on it. R4 makes this point.
- **Where:** IPS (as R4 has it, uncited); Final Report (cite, with the critique).
- **Critique (important, honest):** S14's headline result cuts the *other* way: flexibility to work more later
  justifies more risk now. Laura is an entrepreneur who can write another book. The Final Report should face that:
  her flexibility is real but it is hers, not the residency's, and the operating payments can't rely on her working
  harder in a bad year. Don't cite S14 as if it supported "fewer shares"; cite it as the opponent.

### 11. The rejected alternative: contingent immunisation — FINAL REPORT
- **Source:** S4 (active management inside a framework that guarantees a minimum return; switch to full immunisation
  only when the minimum is at risk); S3 Part II (immunisation needs rebalancing; horizon matching mixes cash matching
  near term with immunisation later).
- **Applies to Laura:** It is the sophisticated version of "take risk now, lock later", the plan a strong rival might
  run. We refuse it for the same reason as concept 3: its switch is triggered by a market move, and after a crash it
  locks in at the bottom. Our lock happens on dated decisions with the payments already bought.
- **Where:** Final Report ("what we considered and why we didn't").
- **Critique of ourselves:** contingent immunisation would likely earn more for the facility in most paths. That is the
  cost we accept in public (voice guide F3).

### 12. Safety-first and surplus risk: the old names for our definition of certainty — GENERIC, FINAL REPORT
- **Source:** S15 (Roy: choose the portfolio that minimises the chance of falling below a disaster level; regarded as
  co-founding portfolio theory with Markowitz, per secondary sources); S16 (Sharpe–Tint: optimise the surplus of
  assets over liabilities, not assets alone).
- **Applies to Laura:** Our "high degree of certainty" = no operating payment depends on a share price is Roy's
  criterion pushed to its limit for the payments: the shortfall probability is set to whatever a Treasury's default
  risk is. Sharpe–Tint is the frame for everything above the payments.
- **Where:** Final Report (lineage). IPS: no names.
- **Critique:** Both are single-period models; Laura's problem is a dated sequence. Lineage, not method.

## Checked and rejected
- **Endowment spending rules (Tobin 1974, S17; Swensen).** Tobin's intergenerational equity is about spending from a
  *perpetual* fund without starving later generations. Laura's commitment is finite (ten payments, then done), so the
  right tool is defeasance, not a spending rule. Use only as a contrast in the Final Report: "the residency is not an
  endowment, so we didn't run it like one".
- **CPPI / TIPP (Black–Jones, Perold; Estep–Kritzman).** Accurate as theory, wrong as a description of our plan, and
  the BIBLE forbids calling a static plan CPPI. Final Report, rejected-alternatives table only.
- **Redington immunisation.** Matches duration and convexity and needs rebalancing; our ladder is cash-flow matching
  (BIBLE: a cash-flow-matched ladder is not immunisation). Mention only as the method we did not use.
- **Barbell vs ladder, Nelson–Siegel, Brier scores, Annie Duke's "resulting".** Final Report process material at best.

## Two facts for the Writer and R3
1. **iBond ETFs "terminate", they are not "redeemed".** S18: the fund terminates on or about 15 December of its year
   and distributes substantially all net assets; as it nears termination it holds more money-market funds. The
   Treasuries *inside* are redeemed. "R for redemption, the bond market's word for the day a bond is repaid" is exact
   for the five T-bonds and true of the iBonds' contents; a sceptical practitioner could note the fund itself
   liquidates. No change needed if the sentence speaks of the bonds, not the funds.
2. **"Two decades" (R4 paragraph 1) is UNVERIFIED** as a span; the mechanism is verified (S1).
