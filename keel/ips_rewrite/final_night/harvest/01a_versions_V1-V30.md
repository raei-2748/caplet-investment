# Harvest 01a: what is worth keeping in V1-V30

Reading notes by Claude, Thu 5 Nov 2026 (Sydney), for tonight's writer. These notes are not IPS text and have no new
sentences in them. Every quote is copied exactly from the file listed in `keel/ips_rewrite/VERSIONS.md`, rows 1-30.

How to read this:
- The dollar figures in V1-V9 were priced on September and mid-October curves, so they are out of date. The case card
  (`drafts/FINAL/CASE_CARD.md`) says the IPS carries no modelled numbers. Treat any figure below as a pointer to an
  idea, not as a number to use.
- Ray's 4 Nov word rule asks for the case's own words ("operating payments", "facility contribution", "credible
  range") in place of "floor", "cheques", "promises" and "gift". Many quotes below use the old words. Take the idea
  and leave the label.
- "Overclaim" means a sentence claims more certainty than the plan delivers. Examples: "cannot miss" when the 2032
  fund's payout is not fixed, or "never" when a rare path breaks it.

## Part 1: version by version

### V1: Doc tab "V1", Root-and-Branch (by Wed 30 Sep; Caplet doc, marked exemplar only)
Best bits
- "This cash-flow matching, unlike immunization, needs no rebalancing." Why: one exact piece of finance vocabulary, used correctly, saying why the ladder can be left alone. A professional judge will recognise it.
- "We tested this on every Treasury curve since 2000." Why: plain and confident, and it says how certainty was tested. This historical test disappears after V8.
- "For the operating payments, the main risk is a shortfall, and her tolerance is below average because the residency's artists rely on that money." Why: sets risk tolerance by goal and ties it to the people who would bear the loss.
- "Her living costs are met elsewhere, so the portfolio needs no liquidity before 2033." Why: answers liquidity in one sentence taken from the case.

Worst bits
- "Her 2033 contribution will be the floor plus half the fund's value then, capped at the announced top, so it cannot fall outside the range." Why: overclaim. The 2032 fund's payout is not fixed.
- "This policy governs the portfolio Laura Gao will fund with $300,000 in January 2027 and $150,000 in January 2028 for the Taiwan creative residency she plans to open in 2033." Why: retells the case to judges who wrote it (the fault the students found in V57).
- "We call our liability-driven approach Root-and-Branch. The roots, meaning the payments and facility floor, are secured before anything is invested in the branch, the return-seeking portfolio." Why: the metaphor needs its own glossary, and "liability-hedging / return-seeking portfolio" reads like a textbook template.

Verdict: the plainest early statement of the whole plan, with the best historical evidence; textbook register and an overclaimed range.

### V2: Doc tab "V2" (by Wed 14 Oct; Caplet doc)
Best bits
- "The plan does not depend on today's unusually high yields: with both deposits, only 2020-level yields could leave part of one payment unfunded." Why: answers a sceptic's first question ("what if rates are lower?") directly.
- "We checked this by pricing the ten payments on every month-end Treasury curve since 2000." Why: says exactly how the team evaluated certainty, which the case asks for.
- "Inflation leaves the fixed payments unchanged but raises facility costs and erodes the floor's real value; the equity half Laura keeps is her first defence." Why: covers inflation in one clear sentence and gives the remedy.

Worst bits
- "Caplet’s strategy “Root-and-Branch” secures Laura’s promised ten $50,000 payments" Why: an unfinished sentence left in the pitch, so the pitch box holds two pitches.
- "From the remainder, we buy just enough zero-coupon Treasuries maturing in late 2032 to grow back to the whole remainder, which becomes Laura's facility floor." Why: zero-coupon Treasuries (STRIPS) are now banned for both deliverables.
- "By high certainty, we mean that each payment is matched by a dated Treasury ETF or bond maturing just before it, so once purchased, it depends only on the US government." Why: "only" overclaims, because coupon reinvestment and fund payouts also matter.

Verdict: V1 tightened with a clearer answer to the sceptic; it also has a banned instrument and a broken pitch.

### V3: Doc tab "V3" (by Wed 14 Oct; Caplet doc)
Best bits
- "We diversify where we take risk; the roots rely on one issuer deliberately, because no dollar asset is safer." Why: an honest one-line answer on diversification that turns an apparent weakness into a reason.
- "The 2027 deposit buys the payments latest first, locking the most rate-sensitive ones at today's yields." Why: gives the reason for the buying order in plain words.
- "A range certain to hold protects her credibility better than a narrow one she might miss." Why: reasoning straight from the case's credibility worry.
- "Inflation or a stronger Taiwan dollar leaves the payments unchanged but raises facility costs; the equity half Laura keeps is her defense." Why: the first version to name the Taiwan dollar, a risk specific to this client.

Worst bits
- "Co-sponsors hear a range from that floor upward, and her contribution cannot miss it." Why: overclaim. The locked IBTM trade reflection says the payout is not fixed.
- "Laura, a statistics graduate and entrepreneur, takes thoughtful risks, but her capacity for risk differs by goal." Why: mixes up capacity and willingness. Her capacity is high; her willingness is what differs by goal (V5 fixes this).
- "Until 2033 the portfolio is held, not traded, with income reinvested in the holding that paid it." Why: contradicts the 2028 purchase (V8 fixes this).

Verdict: of the early Caplet tabs, the one with the most reasons per sentence; still overclaims the range.

### V5: Doc tab "Tab 10", formal register (by Wed 14 Oct; Caplet doc, IPS only)
Best bits
- "As a statistics graduate and entrepreneur she accepts measured risk where the consequences are her own, but where they would fall on artists, as a missed payment would, her willingness is "low."" Why: the best risk-profile reasoning in V1-V30. Her willingness depends on who bears the loss.
- "Since other income covers her living costs and the Portfolio requires no withdrawals before 2033, Laura's ability to bear risk is "above average."" Why: keeps ability and willingness apart correctly, as an IPS should.
- "Each January we review it with Laura, and we act in only two cases." Why: a clear, short decision rule.

Worst bits
- "This policy (the "Policy") governs the portfolio (the "Portfolio")" Why: contract-style legal register that wastes words. Judges reward plain English.
- "Until 2033 the Portfolio shall be held without trading, with income reinvested in the holding that paid it." Why: "shall" is legalese, and the sentence contradicts the 2028 purchase.
- "In 2033 she contributes the floor plus half the fund's value, capped at the top of that range, so she cannot miss it." Why: overclaim.

Verdict: the best risk-profile paragraph of all thirty, buried in a legal register nobody should copy.

### V7: Doc tab "Version 5" (by Tue 13 Oct; Caplet doc; "best version that AI generated")
Best bits

- "In 2033 she contributes the floor plus half the fund's value, capped at that top, so she cannot miss the range." Why: overclaim.

Verdict: the team's own favourite through mid-October and the cleanest conventional IPS of the early drafts; it overclaims and uses terms before defining them.

### V9: AI exemplar v12 (Sat 17 Oct; a model to learn from)
Best bits
- "Laura has committed ten $50,000 payments to her Taiwan residency, due each January from 2033 to 2042, $500,000 against $450,000 deposited." Why: one fact shows the plan has to earn a return. It is a fresh way into the case.
- "The payments require a 5.1% annual return on the 2027 deposit, and Treasuries currently yield about 5.15%, so we buy on receipt rather than wait." Why: a real required-return calculation, the classic IPS move, and it justifies buying at once. No later version in V1-V30 has it. (The figures date from 16 Oct.)
- "The result is about two-thirds payment Treasuries, one quarter floor and under one tenth equities, concentrated in one issuer with minimal credit risk." Why: the only version that states the whole allocation in one line.
- "Bonds are held to maturity, so each payment's liquidity comes from a maturity, not a sale." Why: liquidity explained by how the plan works.

Worst bits
- "Certainty here means each payment is covered at purchase by Treasuries maturing shortly before it, with any shortfall met from her other holdings and the facility floor last." Why: "her other holdings" can be read as outside money, which the case forbids.
- "Our Root-and-Branch strategy, echoing her memoir Messy Roots, buys the roots first: Treasuries for every payment, then a Treasury floor for her facility contribution." Why: the memoir link is the kind of packaging the students now reject.
- "Because the floor is owned, a 40% fall in world stocks during 2032 would lower a typical contribution from about $169,000 to $162,000." Why: the idea is strong (a crash barely dents the contribution), but it is a modelled number the case card keeps out of the IPS.

Verdict: the most numerate and most IPS-shaped text in the set. Its numbers are dated, but no later version beats its required-return and allocation lines.

### V10: Keel IPS v1, "We buy Laura's promise first" (Fri 23 Oct; Keel)
Best bits
- "The ten $50,000 operating payments are a promise she must keep with a high degree of certainty. The facility contribution is a hope she wants to share credibly with co-sponsors." Why: the promise/hope split that later becomes the backbone (V26-V30), here in two plain sentences.
- "But the sail is kept to a minority of the fund, because in 2031 Laura will be asking strangers to trust her, and a crash should cost her upside, not her word." Why: genuinely human. It puts the stakes in a person's terms.
- "By construction it falls inside the range; the confidence we state is about where in the range it lands." Why: the clearest answer in the set to the case's "state how confident". It is honest about what the confidence figure measures.
- "The plan changes only on dates we have named in advance, never because markets moved." Why: answers Wharton's "planned changes" request in one sentence.

Worst bits
- "We define a high degree of certainty as: every payment is paid in full from bonds Laura already owns unless the U.S. government defaults. The one remaining risk is that coupons are reinvested at lower rates; the Final Report will measure it and show how the facility fund absorbs it." Why: the second sentence contradicts the first and defers to the Final Report.
- "Principle 2: the facility fund has a floor and a sail." Why: labelled headings plus a second metaphor (the sail) are packaging.
- "The January 2028 deposit buys a Treasury bond maturing just before 2033 (the floor) and a broad, low-cost world stock fund (the sail)." Why: WInS lists no individual 2032 Treasury, so the bottom became a 2032 fund.

Verdict: the most human early draft. Three of its ideas are worth keeping: promise vs hope, confidence about where in the range the contribution lands, and changes only on dated reviews.

### V19: Rewrite Draft 1 (Mon 26 Oct; from scratch, name Caplet XR)

### V25: Rewrite assessment draft (Tue 27 Oct)

- "But it imagines one ten-year bill, when Laura has ten one-year bills, each due whatever the market did last year, and while a withdrawal of a fixed percentage can ride out a crash, a withdrawal of fixed dollars is a forced sale at the bottom." Why: the best explanation in the set of why fixed payments need bonds rather than stocks (sequence risk).
- "They are $50,000 each, every January from 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars, and nobody else may help pay them – questions for a contract, not a forecast." Why: frames the payments as something to buy, not to predict.
- "That is our high degree of certainty: we can name exactly where it breaks – yields falling more than two percentage points before the purchase while her 2028 deposit more than halves." Why: the strongest idea for defining certainty, by its breaking point. The numbers were wrong for this plan and later versions corrected them.

Worst bits

- "So in January 2033 the reserve changes once, setting any such gap aside in Treasury bills from Laura's half; after that it changes itself." Why: the idea is good, but V27 dropped Treasury bills as a rule risk.

Verdict: the most thoughtful arguments of the rewrite (contract vs forecast, ten one-year bills, where certainty breaks), wrapped in slogans.

## Part 2: Top 25 ideas across V1-V30 a final IPS should consider

These describe ideas only, not wording.

1. **Buy the operating payments up front.** Each $50,000 is matched by a Treasury bond or fixed-maturity Treasury fund that matures before its January, and all of it is bought before any shares. Versions: V1-V30. Why a judge cares: it answers "high degree of certainty" with something owned, not forecast (Investment Strategy, Client Knowledge).
2. **Define certainty by what it rests on and where it breaks.** No payment depends on shares. It fails only if the US government defaults or, before purchase, yields fall far while the 2028 deposit also shrinks far. Versions: V1-V8 ("depends mainly on the US government paying its debts"), V13-V17, V25, and V10 in a weaker form. Why: the case asks teams to define certainty, say how they evaluated it, and name the assumptions.
3. **Historical stress test.** The ten payments were priced on every month-end Treasury curve since 2000, and only 2020-level yields would have left part of one unfunded after both deposits. Versions: V1-V6 (dropped from V7 on). Why: evidence a sceptic cannot wave away; it shows the plan does not rely on today's high yields. The claim would need re-checking before use.
4. **Fixed dollars, not fixed percent.** A fixed $50,000 withdrawal after a crash is a forced sale at the bottom. Laura faces ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill. Versions: V25-V30. Why: it explains why the claim that stocks win over a decade does not apply here, which shows real understanding of sequence risk.
5. **Her 2028 money moves with the economy.** The deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing, which can shrink in the same slump that cuts yields and makes bonds dearer. So the money she has buys the certainty and the money she expects carries the risk. Versions: V9 and V11-V18 (the income source); V19-V30 (the slump-and-yields link; V22-V24 put it as stock-market risk she already owns outside a brokerage account). Why: the most client-specific reason in thirty drafts, and judges score Client Knowledge.
6. **Buying order and who absorbs a rate fall.** The latest payments are bought first because their prices move most with rates. Any gap lands on near payments the 2028 deposit can still fill, so a rate fall before purchase lowers the bottom of the range, never a payment, and a smaller deposit shrinks the facility contribution long before it touches a payment. Versions: V1-V9 (latest first), V13-V17 (the bottom of the range, not a payment), V23-V24 (the smaller deposit). Why: shows rate sensitivity is understood and gives a clear order of who bears what.
7. **A home-made capital-protected note.** The 2032 Treasury fund is sized to grow back to the leftover, and stocks are bought with roughly that fund's expected interest. The design keeps the bond, holds shares instead of an option, and drops the bank's fee and the bank's own credit risk (Lehman, 2008). Versions: V19-V25 (stated outright), V15-V17 (as "the interest that ETF will earn"). Why: Creativity through an investing idea rather than packaging. Caution: do not word it so that a judge thinks options are traded.
8. **Risk only interest not yet earned.** Because the stock share equals the 2032 fund's expected interest (about one dollar in fifteen in V29), even a total stock loss leaves the bottom intact, and the stock share shrinks by itself when yields are low. Versions: V15-V30 (the sizing), V19-V22 (risking only interest not yet earned), V21 (the share resizes with yields), V29 (one dollar in fifteen). Why: an objective sizing rule for the case's "balance between pursuing growth and protecting the capital".
9. **No stocks under the bottom of the range, even over five years.** Time makes a stock loss less likely but not smaller, and a number said aloud cannot be half-kept. Moving money from the bottom into stocks would raise the expected contribution, and the team refuses on purpose. Versions: V1-V9 and V11-V24 (the refusal), V22-V24 (the time argument). Why: an explicit trade-off with its reason, which IPS judges look for.
10. **Concentrate on purpose where no risk is wanted, spread where it is taken.** Stocks are spread over thousands of companies worldwide, while the payments rest on one borrower because no dollar asset is safer. (Ten separate 99%-safe bets would miss a payment about one decade in ten.) Versions: V3-V9, V11-V19, V21, V26-V30. Why: answers the IPS's "diversification" honestly instead of pretending the ladder is diversified.
11. **The range's bottom is money already owned.** It is the 2032 fund's projected payout, rounded down because a fund's payout is not fixed, so co-sponsors hear a commitment rather than a forecast. Versions: V1-V30; V8 and V9 give the rounding reason; V19-V21 the "commitment, not a forecast" framing. Why: credibility with co-sponsors is the case's main worry.
12. **The top is the bottom plus half her shares.** The other half stays hers, which is the case's "financial flexibility". Versions: V1-V30. Why: answers the facility-contribution and flexibility questions together.
13. **Say what the confidence figure measures.** The rule keeps the contribution inside the range by design, so the confidence the team states is about where inside it lands (V9: top reached in about three of four paths). Never say "cannot miss". Versions: V9, V10, V25. Why: the case asks teams to "state how confident" they are; an honest answer beats an overclaim.
14. **Each end of the range is as sure as its miss is costly.** Missing the bottom breaks her word; missing the top only trims co-sponsors' plans. A range set too low has a cost too, because bigger lead gifts draw bigger gifts. Versions: V25 (as a two-cost choice), V26-V29 (lead gifts), V30 (each end as sure as its miss is costly). Why: reasoned judgement about an asymmetric range, using the case's own point about a meaningful contribution.
15. **The capped range's hidden flaw, and the 2031 lock.** Once named, a capped range lets the contribution keep every fall while Laura keeps every rise. So on the day she names it, the promised half of the shares moves into Treasuries. Versions: V26-V30 (V14-V17 describe the flaw without seeing it). Why: the team stress-tested its own rule, which reads as a real investment committee.
16. **Make each commitment safe no later than the day it becomes one.** The payments are safe from 2027, the bottom from 2028, and the top on the day it is named; until then a goal may carry risk. Versions: V26-V30 (V10 as promise vs hope). Why: one principle that explains every date and serves as the "decision-making framework" Wharton says the IPS establishes. Note that Ray's word rule avoids "promise".
17. **Coupon reinvestment is the one honest weak spot.** A lasting fall in yields hurts twice: the ladder costs more in January, and its coupons earn less afterwards. Versions: V10, V12-V18, V19-V30. Why: naming your own weakness is what a credible IPS does.
18. **Price the reinvestment gap and set it aside before the range is named.** The rule then has no exception, because the gap comes off the range before it is quoted, never after. Versions: V12-V18 and V24 (before the 2033 contribution), V29-V30 (in 2031, before the range is named). Why: shows that the range "must also protect" the payments, as the case requires.
19. **A stated order for who absorbs any shortfall.** Spare cash first, then Laura's half, then the part above the bottom, never a payment and never a number already quoted. Versions: V7, V8, V11, V12, V18, V19, V24, V29-V30. Why: a clear priority order reads as policy, not hope.
20. **Flexibility with a purpose.** Her half stays liquid for overruns, Taiwan inflation, a stronger Taiwan dollar or a change of plan as the project develops. Versions: V1-V30, with named purposes in V10, V11, V23 and V25-V30. Why: answers the case's line that committing everything "could limit her financial flexibility as the project develops".
21. **Inflation, said exactly.** Inflation cannot raise a fixed $50,000 payment, but it can raise costs in Taiwan and erode the real value of the bottom, and so can a stronger Taiwan dollar. Versions: V1-V30 (V3 adds the Taiwan dollar, V9 sizes the real loss, V25 is the clearest). Why: the case asks for the effect of inflation on projections and facility costs.
22. **Liquidity comes from maturities, not sales.** Every payment's cash comes from a holding that matures before it; the funds also trade daily; her living costs are met outside the portfolio. Versions: V1, V9, V11-V14, V18, V19-V24. Why: the IPS instructions list liquidity, and this answers it from the structure of the plan.
23. **In 2033 the reserve is renamed, not rebuilt.** Five fixed-maturity Treasury funds cover 2033-37 and five bonds cover 2038-42, one maturing each year; the reserve is topped up once and then only shrinks, with no rebalancing (cash-flow matching rather than immunization). Versions: V1, V12-V18, V25, V29, V30. Why: the case asks for the reserve's composition and how it changes "if at all".
24. **Changes happen only on dates fixed in advance.** There is a January review with Laura; a delisted holding is swapped for an earlier-maturing Treasury; nothing moves because markets moved. Versions: V3-V18, V30. Why: Wharton's IPS asks for "planned changes" as funding needs approach.
25. **Required return and allocation in plain sight.** The payments total $500,000 against $450,000 deposited; the return they need roughly equals what Treasuries pay, so buy on receipt; and the whole portfolio is about two-thirds payment Treasuries, a quarter the 2032 fund and under a tenth stocks. Versions: V9 only. Why: the classic IPS numbers (required return, target allocation) that every judge looks for, and no later version in V1-V30 has them. Re-price before use, and check against the no-modelled-numbers rule.

Two runners-up that nearly made the list:
- **The team's own history:** the first plan put three-quarters in stocks, and the model had it missing payments (V27-V30). It is a real event, allowed under Wharton's ethics rule, and it shows the strategy being tested.
- **Risk tolerance by goal:** willingness is low where the loss would fall on artists and above average where it falls on Laura (V1-V9, best in V5). It is the standard IPS risk-profile structure, which judges expect.

## Part 3: Top 15 recurring faults

1. **The opening retells the case.** Example: "This policy governs the portfolio Laura Gao funds with $300,000 in January 2027 and $150,000 in January 2028 for the Taiwan creative residency she opens in 2033." (V3, V4, V7, V8; V1-V2, V5-V6 and V11-V18 open the same way) and "Laura, a graphic novelist who studied statistics at Wharton, is planning a residency for artists, writers, designers, entrepreneurs and educators." (V19-V21). Judges wrote the case, so these are wasted words.
2. **Absolutes that overclaim.** "her contribution cannot miss it" (V3-V4 pitch), "so she cannot miss the range" (V3, V4, V7, V8), "Markets can shrink the facility contribution, but never below the floor." (V3-V5, V7), "A payment never does." (V23), "ten Januaries for certain" (V27), "Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque is worse off" (V29, false in some paths).
3. **Metaphor mistaken for creativity.** Roots and branch with a bracketed glossary (V1-V9, V11-V12, V18), "floor and a sail" (V10), the pharmacy "extended release" tablet (V22-V30), "Keep the seed; gamble the harvest." (V26-V27), "on its birthday" (V26-V27), "echoing her memoir Messy Roots" (V9).
4. **Kicker and one-line endings.** "We refuse." as its own paragraph (V20-V24), "Do not crush." (V23-V25), "Ten Januaries, ten cheques. Every one was bought before the first was written." (V20-V21), "Ten cheques, then – each bought before the first is written." (V24), "Laura owes ten Januaries. From 2028 she holds a Treasury for each." (V19).
5. **Over-compression: two ideas in one breath, joined by a semicolon or colon.** "So money she has buys certainty; money she expects insures it, then carries risk." (V30), "One yield sets both the ladder's January price and what its coupons earn later, so a lasting fall works twice." (V12, V18), "each end as sure as its miss is costly" (V30), "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one." (V26-V27; V28-V30 the same idea as "Ten independent bets, each 99% safe").
6. **Frameworks named but not explained.** "Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, options given away free, returns assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell." (V26-V27; V28 the same with "guarantees nobody priced") gives four abstract phrases with no example a reader can picture. Likewise "To a rates trader a caplet is an option; this one holds none" (V19; V20-V21 the same in two sentences).
7. **Staged voice devices.** "This is the obvious trade. But ..." (V13), "Fair enough. But it imagines ..." (V25), "And the part that sounds wrong:" (V26), "Then the part that sounds wrong:" (V27), "Now the odd part:" (V28), and fragment runs such as "No stock has to rise." (V13-V17). Readers hear the trick, not a person.
8. **Talking about rivals.** "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." (V25-V30), banned by Book 10.
9. **Judgements with no source.** "We judge the second about three times worse." (V25), "which we judge three times worse" (V26-V29), "we judge her tolerance above average" (V7-V8). Book 10 says every number says where it came from.
10. **Jargon and insider winks.** "This cash-flow matching, unlike immunization" (V1-V2) is fine for a judge but borderline for students to defend. Worse: "Sovereign concentration" (V6), "capital-protected note minus the derivative" in a 50-word pitch (V19), "(ask Lehman's customers why)" (V24), "nothing can expire worthless" (V19-V21; V22 the same), and the tickers "IBTM" and "VT" (V25; "VT" again in V26).
11. **Corporate or legal register.** "This policy (the "Policy") governs the portfolio (the "Portfolio")" and "shall be held" (V5), "Our analysis of month-end Treasury curves since 2000 indicates ..." and "This rule limits the contribution’s variability and supports a credible commitment to co-sponsors." (V6).
12. **Ambiguous rules and pronouns.** "quoted rounded down after any expected shortfall" (V7, two readings), "Stocks ending below their 2031 value move the gift toward the floor she already owns; anything above it stays with Laura." (V15-V17, what is "it"?), "floor and stocks are bought smaller together" (V29), "comes off the quoted bottom" (V29), and "the half Laura keeps" used before it is defined (V7).
13. **Contradictions inside one text.** "every payment is paid in full from bonds Laura already owns unless the U.S. government defaults" followed at once by "The one remaining risk is that coupons ..." (V10). "held, not traded" or "held without trading" beside the 2028 purchase (V3-V7). "and last from the floor itself" beside "a number Laura has quoted" (V17). "R for release" (V19-V21) and "Caplet XR releases Laura's money on schedule." (V22-V24 pitches) where the team's rule is R = redemption.
14. **Instruments that invite a rules query.** "zero-coupon Treasuries" (V2; STRIPS now banned), "Treasury bills" (V25-V26; dropped in V27 as a rule risk), option and derivative vocabulary in the strategy's own name paragraph (V19-V21), and "a Treasury bond maturing just before 2033" (V10; WInS lists none).
15. **Stale or modelled numbers, drifting terms and copy errors.** Dated figures such as "$292,000", "0.3 percentage points" (V2-V8) and "$136,000", "$169,000 to $162,000" (V9). One idea under five names: floor / minimum / bottom / gift / facility contribution (V19-V30). Copy errors: "At late-September 2026 yields cost about $292,000" (V4), "The rest form the branch" (V4, V18), "Ten independent" capitalised after a colon (V29-V30), and numerals "9" and "10 Januaries" (V20-V21).
