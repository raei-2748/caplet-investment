## Version by version

### V31 · Fri 30 Oct · `drafts/D11/` · pitch 50, IPS 497

**Best bits**
1. “The cheques add up to $500,000 but cost about $300,000; the government's interest pays the rest, provided nobody sells early.” Explains in one breath why the ladder is affordable and why nothing may be sold. Dropped after V31 and never came back; the V34 cold reader listed it as a lost idea.
2. “An endowment spending a fixed percentage can sit through a crash, but anyone owing a fixed amount must sell at the bottom.” The cleanest statement of why shares fail this client: fixed dollars, not fixed percentages.
3. “But a capped range hides a flaw: once it's named, any fall in stocks comes off the gift, and any rise past the top goes to Laura.” The one-sided bet behind locking the top before it is said. Real investing reasoning.
4. “Every number she says aloud is already owned.” The plan's biggest strength (the one Ray asked for on 5 Nov) already stated here, in six words.

**Worst bits**
1. “Pharmacists call a pill that releases the same dose every hour, however much is left, extended release – XR.” The medicine frame, second paragraph in, under a heading. It is what made Laura sound like a patient.
2. Four headings: “One dose, every January”, “A hope, with a floor”, “Say it, then lock it”, “Do not crush”. Panel 1's teacher: "a little too neat, with headings and refrains".
3. “It is a bank's capital-protected note with the bank, and its fee, taken out. Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque loses a cent. The price is a smaller boom.” Three kickers in a row, and the middle claim was false in some model paths (V32 dropped it).

**What readers stumbled on:** no cold read. Panel 1 (`judges/panel_V31/`): the actuary said it "asserts the safety rather than testing it" and could not tell whether the coupon cushion protects the payments or the gift; the programme manager found it "the easiest to follow" but said it "leaves the certainty claim undefined".

**Verdict:** a plain, readable skeleton with three ideas no later draft beat, dressed in pill headings and kickers.

### V32 · Fri 30 Oct · `drafts/D12/` · pitch 50, IPS 500

**Best bits**
1. “Stocks beat bonds over most decades, but Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and anyone owing a fixed amount must sell at the bottom of a crash.” Concedes the sceptic's best point, then turns it. Judges retold "ten one-year bills" for days.
2. “Laura's "thoughtful risks" are already taken: the residency is the boldest bet of her life, and her income from advances, speaking and licensing shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear.” Willingness against capacity, using the case's own phrase. Every panel 2 judge praised it.
3. “That is our definition of a high degree of certainty: a cheque can fail only if the US government defaults, or if yields fall two points before we buy and her 2028 deposit more than halves.” Turns the case's adjective into a test. The idea won the panel (the number was later corrected and "only if" proved false).

**Worst bits**
1. “(Our first plan, three-quarters stocks, missed cheques in our model.)” The team's story squeezed into brackets; two judges marked Articulation down for it.
2. “The price is a smaller boom: in our model a balanced portfolio usually leaves her richer, but breaks a promise one time in four.” Wrong number (one in three on the 7.5% basis) and "promise" is ambiguous: payment or range?
3. “Nothing is sold before it pays (do not crush); each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down.” A bracketed riddle.

**What readers stumbled on:** panel 2: why the top is reached only 97 times in 100 if the promised half is locked (three judges); which promise a balanced portfolio breaks (two); how "two points" and "more than halves" combine (one).

**Verdict:** the best-received draft of 30 Oct because it defined certainty and priced the cost of safety, but its numbers and its two meanings of "promise" tripped readers.

### V33 · Fri 30 Oct · `drafts/D13/` · pitch 50, IPS 500

**Best bits**
1. “Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, but she has already taken the big one.” The best single client line of the run; a concession with a real turn.
2. “A capped range has a catch. Once it's named, a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past the top goes to Laura. So on the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries.” The cold reader: "clear and the cleverest paragraph".
3. “It's a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee.” The home-made structured product in eleven words; panel 3's professor called it one of the sharpest ideas in the field.
4. “We started where most investors would: three-quarters in stocks. Then we modelled it, and our plan missed a cheque in about one future in thirty.” An honest decision path (judges rewarded it; Book 11 now bans team history and model numbers, so only the reasoning survives).

**Worst bits**
1. “Laura has made her residency one promise and has one hope.” Garbled on first read: it sounds as if the residency made a promise.
2. “A cheque can fail only if the US government defaults, or if yields drop two points before we buy and her 2028 deposit comes in under about $85,000.” The paragraph the cold reader "could least follow": why yields matter, before buying when, $85,000 against what.
3. “In our model a balanced 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer, but breaks a promise – a cheque, or the gift it named – about one time in four.” Read as a contradiction of "one in thirty" for a plan with more shares.

**What readers stumbled on** (`D13/COLD_READ.md`): the garbled first sentence; "iBond Treasury funds" never explained; "So we call it" names the plan before describing it; "never carries a cheque" against "plugs any gap" (the deposit seems needed and not needed); "sized to grow back to the whole remainder" (of what?); "a fifteenth of everything she owns" (of what?); "dear" for expensive.

**Verdict:** panels 3 and 4 both put it first and concluded "V33's set of ideas is the one judges trust", but at least three of its sentences fail on one read.

### V34 · Fri 30 Oct · `drafts/D14/` · pitch 48, IPS 500

**Best bits**
1. “Every cheque rests on one borrower, the US government, by design: ten safe-looking borrowers would miss a January more often.” Answers Wharton's diversification question head-on by refusing to diversify the payments. Few teams will say this.
2. “She gives up that half's upside – the price of saying a number out loud.” The lock's cost, stated honestly. Never seen again after V34.
3. “Our rules fire on dates, never on market moves.” A real principle (Wharton: the IPS should not be rewritten simply because markets move). The voice editor heard it as a slogan close; the idea returns in V56i-V58.
4. “the other half stays hers, as flexibility for a building priced in Taiwan dollars.” Flexibility named with the case's currency fact.

**Worst bits**
1. “In our model, a cheque fails only if the US government defaults, or if yields fall two points before we buy, making the ladder dearer, and her $150,000 deposit in 2028 shrinks below about $85,000.” The voice editor: "the hardest sentence in the piece to read aloud".
2. “Most of it buys a floor: Treasuries bought at a discount, worth everything the deposit had left by late 2032.” "buys ... bought", and the second half has to be decoded.
3. “Her human capital – advances, speaking, licensing – shrinks in the very recessions that make bonds dear. So her 2028 deposit plugs any gap in the ladder, then builds her gift.” The cold reader: "the 'so' runs the wrong way": the risky money is made the backstop.

**What readers stumbled on** (`D14/COLD_READ.md`, `VOICE_EDIT.md`): paragraphs 5, 6 and 7 could not be followed; seven apparent contradictions; ten terms used before or without explanation (cheque, redemption, coupons, 60/40, capital-protected note, funds that "mature", operating reserve); "each sellable any day" beside "nothing is sold before it pays". Voice edit: 7 of 33 sentences off-voice.

**Verdict:** the audit's every fix packed in at once; it proved that adding ideas costs more clarity than it buys.

### V35 · Fri 30 Oct · `drafts/D15/` · pitch 48, IPS 499

21. **Two ways to meet fixed payments: match the bonds' rate-sensitivity and keep rebalancing, or buy one bond per payment and hold it; the plan does the second, which never needs rebalancing.** V56i-V57. Shows the professional framework behind the ladder (keep only if explained, not just named).
22. **One rule for every commitment: price it at what Treasuries charge to meet it, never at what shares might earn, and fund it the day it is made (payments 2027, minimum 2028, range 2031).** V58. A single connected argument, which Ray asked for on 5 Nov.
23. **Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates it bets that rates won't fall.** V58. Counter-intuitive, true, and tied to the reinvestment weakness.
24. **The bought payments are her strongest fact for co-sponsors: their money can go to the building, which funders prefer to running costs.** V58. Turns the dullest choice into a fundraising argument (Client Knowledge).
25. **State the trade-off honestly: putting the 2028 deposit in shares would usually give a higher top, but the bottom would be whatever a crash left; the lock gives up the promised half's upside.** V34 (the lock's cost), V37b (the counterfactual), V41-V49 (with model numbers, now Final Report). Wharton's "reasoning, assumptions, and tradeoffs"; panel 2's growth hawk asked for the upside given away to be stated.

Also present once and worth a look: inflation can make $50,000 buy less but cannot make it cost more, so Taiwan costs threaten the building, not the payments (V31 only; the case asks about inflation and no later draft answered it). The operating reserve from 2033 is the ladder plus the set-aside, paying one holding per payment (V54-V58; the case asks for the reserve's composition).

## 2. Top 15 recurring faults

1. **The medicine motif.** “Pharmacists call a pill that releases the same dose every hour, however much is left, extended release – XR.” (V31); “On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule.” (V33, V36-V41); “Crush it and the schedule is gone.” (V52-V54); “We call the plan Caplet XR, after extended-release tablets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled "do not crush".” (V55-V57). 2. **Kicker endings, two-word kickers and "That is X." runs.** “Do not crush.” (V43-V54, one line on its own); “Shares don't.” (V53); “That is her floor.” and “That is under a tenth of her money.” (V52-V54); “Sounds good, on average.” (V48-V49); “Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.” (V55-V57). ## 4. Sentences readers could not follow

Each: the sentence (version), who lost it, and what it was trying to say in plain words.

**The reinvestment gap (the most-rewritten idea of the run)**
1. V33-V38 (V35 cut it): “The ladder has one leak – coupons reinvested at lower yields – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031, before she names anything.” Three cold readers in a row (V32-V34), panel 5's skimmer, the V38 cold reader. *Meant:* the bonds pay interest before they mature; that interest is reinvested, and if rates fall it earns less, so the ladder could end up slightly short. In 2031, before Laura announces her range, we work out how short it could be and put that money aside.
2. V39-V41: “early cash earns less if yields fall”; V47: “One leak remains: cash paid early earns less if yields fall.” The V40 cold reader ("a guess on one read"), the V48 voice reader ("cryptic"). *Meant:* money that arrives before a payment is due (interest, and two bonds that mature months early) sits waiting and earns less if rates are low.
3. V43: “The ladder's one leak – coupons that arrive early, reinvested at lower rates if yields fall – we price and set aside in 2031.” Four panel V43 judges. *Meant:* as item 1, plus "and the money comes from Laura's own spare shares".
4. V51: “Before she names the range, that gap is set aside from the stocks Laura keeps; whatever they can't cover comes off the bottom.” LOST (V51 cold read): "that gap" had no antecedent. *Meant:* before announcing the range, enough of Laura's own spare shares are sold into Treasuries to cover the possible shortfall; if her spare shares are not enough, she announces a lower range.
5. V54: “we price that gap at that day's yields, plus a cushion, and move that much from the stocks Laura keeps into Treasuries, so her flexibility shrinks before her gift does.” LOST for the final cold reader: "five steps in one sentence". *Meant:* the same, said as one action at a time.
6. V56-V57 (pre-fix): “as if nothing more were reinvested” LOST for the V57 cold reader, who read it as "stop reinvesting", a change of plan. *Meant:* we size the set-aside by assuming the worst, that every dollar waiting for a payment earns nothing from then on, so the amount set aside is more than enough.
7. V56: “the whole range comes down” Paused (two checkers). *Meant:* the range she announces is lower; nothing already announced is cut. (V57: “she names a lower range”.)

**The bank's protected note**
8. V51: “A bank would call this a capital-protected note, except a bank would spend that interest on an option, which is worth nothing if the market just stands still.” V52 cold read 3 LOST ("the 'except' does not set up a contrast I can finish"); V53 (V52 ends "Shares don't expire."): “Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend that interest on an option, which expires worthless if the market stands still. Shares don't.” LOST three cold reads running; V57: “they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless” LOST ("option" never explained). *Meant:* banks sell a product with the same shape: a bond that returns your money, plus a bet on the market bought with the bond's interest. If the market does not rise, the bet pays nothing and the interest is gone. This plan uses the same interest to buy actual shares, which keep their value even if the market goes nowhere.
9. V44: “Ours swaps the bank's option for shares and leaves out the bank (and its fee).” and V45: “Ours swaps the bank's option for shares, which can't expire worthless.” The skimmer and the programme manager on panels V44-V45. *Meant:* as item 8.

**Certainty and the break point**
10. V33 (V36 adds "instead of $150,000"): “A cheque can fail only if the US government defaults, or if yields drop two points before we buy and her 2028 deposit comes in under about $85,000.” The V33 cold reader ("the paragraph I could least follow"); the V34 voice editor said of V34's version: "the hardest sentence in the piece to read aloud". *Meant:* a payment can be missed only if the US defaults, or if interest rates fall sharply before the bonds are bought in January 2027 (falling rates make bonds dearer, so $300,000 buys less) and the 2028 deposit is too small to buy the rest.
11. V39: “after yields fall two points, it needs about $125,000 of its $150,000.” Panel 6's programme manager. V40: “Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does.” The V40 cold reader (two reads; could be the size of the gap). *Meant:* even after a large fall in rates, a 2028 deposit of only about $125,000, not the full $150,000, would still be enough to finish buying the payments.
12. V43: “A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective.” The V43 checker: "a list of what?" *Meant:* "high certainty" should be shown as the short list of ways a payment could be missed, not asserted.

**The 2028 deposit and the minimum**

16. V42 pitch: “Her 2028 deposit finishes it, then a floor that grows back into itself.” The V42 checker. *Meant:* the 2028 deposit finishes buying the payments, then buys bonds that will be worth the deposit again by 2032.

**The range and its lock**
