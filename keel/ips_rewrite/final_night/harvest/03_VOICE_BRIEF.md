## The short version

The students are right about the cause. V57 and V58 are not hard to read because the ideas are hard. They are hard
because most sentences carry two or three ideas with the joining words taken out, and because terms ("credible
range", "set-aside", "kept shares", "redemption") turn up before anyone has said what they mean. Every human writer
in the log does the opposite. They explain one step per sentence, keep the small words ("of", "that", "which is",
"so"), repeat a noun rather than risk an unclear "it", and spend their wit on one image or one aside in the middle
of a paragraph, never on its last line.

The voice the team asked for ("cerebral yet witty, passionate yet thoughtful, insightful yet confident") lives in
three places: the page takes a position someone could disagree with, it gives that someone a fair hearing, and it
explains the working so plainly that a judge feels clever for following it. None of the three is a sentence trick.

## (a) Moves from the human writers (27)

Each entry gives the writer, the log number, an exact quote from the log, and how it applies to a 500-word IPS.

### Taking a position

1. **Open on your own claim, said plainly.** Page and Brin, #066: "Google is not a conventional company." Debelle,
   #630, opens on a number: "The inflation rate is currently just over 2 per cent (Graph 1)."
   *IPS:* both V57 and V58 open by restating the case, which the judges wrote. The first sentence should carry
   Caplet's reading of the case. It should be a plain claim, not an aphorism (Ray banned those).

2. **Grant the objection, then answer it in one turn.** Terry Smith, #426: "People sometimes ask us whether it is
   dangerous to own consumer stocks in an economic downturn." "To which we reply yes, but it is not as dangerous as
   not being close to the consumer in those circumstances."
   *IPS:* the obvious objection is "shares are safe over ten years" or "why not just hold cash?". Let the objection
   ask its question in a full, fair sentence. Say "yes" to the part that is true, then give the one reason it
   doesn't hold for fixed payments on fixed dates.

3. **Name the doubt the reader already has about you.** Deresiewicz, #649: "My title must seem like a
   contradiction."
   *IPS:* a plan that is mostly Treasuries, for a client with a building to fund, can look timid. Saying so early
   and plainly turns the rest of the page into the answer.

4. **Say what you will not do, and what that costs.** The log's note on Page and Brin, #066: "Name what you will
   *not* do, and the cost of that refusal to the reader."
   *IPS:* the plan refuses to let shares pay any of the ten payments. The cost is a smaller expected facility
   contribution in good years. Put both in the same paragraph.

5. **Say what you are not claiming.** John Locke essay, #083: "The implication is not that anyone in the public
   spotlight should be deprived of any interest in their reputation."
   *IPS:* the page doesn't claim shares are bad, only that they are the wrong tool for a dated, fixed bill. One
   clause does it, and it heads off the critic who thinks you are anti-equity.

6. **Doubt the forecast once, then commit to the policy.** Marks, #458: "I know my views on the market's direction
   aren't worth betting on." and "I'd rather bet on risk control than risk bearing as the route to superior
   results." Hunter, #447: "If we can be confident about anything, it's that our forecasts will be wrong at least in
   some way."
   *IPS:* one sentence admitting nobody knows where rates or markets go, followed by the plan that doesn't need to
   know. Doubt goes on the future, never on the plan, and appears only once.

7. **Give one alternative a full hearing, then answer it from history.** Marks, #038 (the log's note): "Lay out the
   other side's case in full, then answer each claim with history." His line: "But I recoil any time I hear a
   prediction that trees will grow to the sky, or that centuries of history are irrelevant."
   *IPS:* at most one rejected alternative gets this treatment, with a real reason. A row of rejected ideas at one
   clause each, as V57 has, is the opposite.

### Explaining the mechanism

8. **Announce a simple model, then walk through it.** Levine, #147: "A simple model of US Treasury bonds could go
   something like:"
   *IPS:* the 2028 deposit is the hardest part to follow. Walk it in order: what Laura buys first, what that pays
   and when, and what is left over and where it goes. Give each step its reason.

9. **Write the causal chain out link by link.** Lowe, #868: "Low growth in wages means low inflation, which means
   low interest rates, which means high asset valuations."
   *IPS:* two ideas need a chain: why Laura's income and share prices can fall together, and why reinvested
   interest can fall short. Name every link and never jump from cause to conclusion. One chain per paragraph.

10. **State the rule like a kitchen rule.** Lowe, #869: "Inflation is too low, so you do what you can to get
    inflation up."
    *IPS:* the core logic of the plan has this shape: a plain fact, "so", a plain action. A reason and an action
    joined by "so" beats a principle stated in the abstract.

11. **Define a term in ordinary words the first time it appears.** Bullock, #616: "So financial stability is really
    when the financial system is doing its job." Buffett, #332, defines risk "using dictionary terms": "the
    possibility of loss or injury".
    *IPS:* "high degree of certainty", "credible range" and "operating reserve" each get one plain meaning when they
    first appear. Keep the case's words, and say what Caplet takes them to mean.

12. **Translate yourself.** Haldane, #459: "Put in rather plainer English, I am gloomier." Damodaran, #097:
    "marginal incremental returns (an academic code word for really small)".
    *IPS:* if a technical word has to stay (ladder, matures, coupon), follow it once with what physically happens.

13. **Make the hidden thing visible.** Ellis, #545: "The price they actually pay is the same. They're just getting
    more for it."
    *IPS:* reinvestment risk is invisible to a lay reader. Say what happens to an interest payment that arrives
    years before the payment it is meant for, then say what goes wrong if rates fall.

14. **Explain something by its job.** Damodaran, #186: holding bitcoin as corporate cash is "analogous to replacing
    your shock absorbers with pogo sticks."
    *IPS:* money for a dated payment has one job, to be there on the day. If the page uses one image, it should be
    like this one: it carries the reasoning, and nobody has to explain it afterwards.

15. **The test is whether you can hold, not whether you intend to.** Haldane, #233: "the relevant test is the ability
    to hold, not just the intention".
    *IPS:* "held to maturity" is credible only if nothing forces a sale. Say why nothing will.

16. **Treat not acting as a decision.** Marks, #229: "deciding not to act isn't the opposite of acting; it's an act in
    itself."
    *IPS:* trading only on fixed dates is a choice with a reason. Present it as a decision, not as a "never".

17. **Show how you found the idea.** Zweig, #154: "I try to tell people not only what I know and what I have learned,
    but I try to tell them how I have learned it."
    *IPS:* one clause on how the team got there (for example, pricing the payments before anything else) can buy
    trust, but only if it is a real team event on the record.

### Numbers

18. **Few numbers, each said flat and set against something familiar.** Stripe, #176: "$1 trillion on Stripe,
    equivalent to 1% of global GDP and growing."
    *IPS:* each number says who produced it ("we modelled", "the case says") and is set beside something the
    reader can feel. Cut any number that gets no reaction.

19. **Talk about the spread, not the average.** Bullock, #199: "It is important to look at the distribution of this
    debt". Kent, #909: "What matters when it comes to financial stability is not what the average borrowers are
    doing".
    *IPS:* the credible range is the place to say what happens in a bad case, not just in the middle.

20. **Be roughly right.** Buffett, #332: "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."
    *IPS:* use round figures with "about". Exact figures only where exactness is the point, such as the $50,000
    payments.

### Admitting costs and weak points

21. **Admit a past mistake plainly, in the past tense.** Lowe, #595: "With the benefit of hindsight, my view is that
    we did do too much." Ellis, #865: "The facts had changed, and we had changed our minds."
    *IPS:* if the page mentions what changed in the team's thinking (the record shows the team's first, mostly
    shares plan missed payments in the model), say it in this flat tense, with no drama. Real events only.

22. **Put the bad news in the middle, with the fix right after it.** The log's note on Lincoln, #166, says the
    hardest news "sits in the *middle*". Watsa, #503, names the mistake, its owner and its price in one line: "We
    have written off our investment in Carbovan (cost $5 million) – I was too optimistic last year".
    *IPS:* the team names the reinvestment weakness itself, before a judge finds it, follows it at once with what
    the plan does about it, and keeps it out of the final paragraph.

23. **Name the price of safety.** Lowe, #912: "Stronger buffers give us more options." "You pay a premium for
    protection against future uncertainties and to provide resilience."
    *IPS:* say once, plainly, what Laura gives up (less expected money for the building in good years) and what she
    gets (payments that don't depend on markets).

### Register and wit

24. **Write as you would speak.** Graham, #209: "just don't let a sentence through unless it's the way you'd say it
    to a friend." Hazlitt, #680: "It is not to take the first word that offers, but the best word in common use;"
    *IPS:* every sentence should survive being read aloud to a parent at dinner without you stopping to rephrase it.

25. **One dry, self-aware aside, from your own kitchen.** Damodaran, #131: "As someone who stinks at market timing, I
    made no attempt to buy and sell the market through the month". Debelle, #623: "There are so many black swans
    identified each day that we may as well all be living in Perth." Montaigne, #875, prefers "the paltry pieces of
    my own kitchen door" to grand terms.
    *IPS:* for a plan built on not forecasting, a modest aside about forecasting is the safest wit there is. Use at
    most one, in the middle of a paragraph and never as its last line.

26. **Understate.** Hunter, #488: "We think the labour market is currently a bit tight". Lowe, #613: "by global
    standards we have done pretty well."
    *IPS:* calm and exact beats "robust", "powerful" and "ironclad" stacked together. A strong word belongs only on
    something that is true without qualification.

27. **Bold, true and narrow.** Graham, #208: "Useful writing is bold, but true." Graham, #211: "Better a narrow
    description than a vague one." Tufts, #276: "students who take their work very seriously, but don't take
    themselves too seriously."
    *IPS:* make specific claims you can defend ("no payment depends on shares"), not broad ones ("maximum safety").
    The last quote is the tone target for the whole page.

## (b) The AI fingerprints in V57 and V58, and the human fix

1. **Spelling out the name.** "X for ten, R for redemption" (V57); "X for its ten Treasury holdings, R for their
   redemption" (V58).
   *Fix:* the students said explaining the name kills it, and the name is meant to stay subtle. Name the plan once
   at most, and let the description itself show that each holding is redeemed before a payment. Never go letter
   by letter.

2. **A metaphor explained, then called back.** V57's tablet paragraph and "Like the tablet it is named after, the
   plan works as long as nobody crushes it."
   *Fix:* Ray took the tablet out. Bring back no metaphor that needs its own sentence of explanation.

3. **"X, not Y" and "never" used for rhythm.** "time makes a loss rarer, not smaller"; "never at what shares might
   earn"; "changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move"; "is never promised to the building".
   *Fix:* keep a contrast only when a real person holds the "Y". Otherwise state the positive claim alone. One
   contrast per page is plenty.

4. **A slogan where a reason should be.** "her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice"; "We would rather she owned
   the shares."; "Cash only looks safer"; "because that counts a return nobody has earned yet".
   *Fix:* write the plain causal sentence the slogan stands for. Once the reason is on the page, the slogan adds
   nothing.

5. **A catalogue of rejected ideas, one clause each.** V57 runs through pension funds' two methods, the bank's
   capital-protected note, portfolio insurance and the 1987 crash.
   *Fix:* cut whole ideas. At most one alternative gets a fair hearing (move 7). The rest belong in the Final
   Report.

6. **Fact-sheet runs.** "The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury
   fund maturing in late 2032, which sets her minimum facility contribution. What remains, roughly the interest that
   fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies." Nearly every blind reader in the
   voice-first round flagged this sentence and the operating-reserve sentence as "form language".
   *Fix:* tell it as what Laura does and why, in order, with a person as the subject and a reason for each step.

7. **Reflex tags and inventory lists.** "holding thousands of companies"; "book advances, speaking fees and
   licensing"; "co-sponsors, grants, program fees and her business". One reader called lists like these "inventory,
   not voice".
   *Fix:* name the thing once in general terms and spend the words on why it matters.

8. **An opponent with no face.** "Shares are called safe over ten years".
   *Fix:* say who says it, or put it as the question a sensible reader would ask, in its strongest form (move 2).

9. **Stilted asides and colon reveals.** "So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares." "Our plan
   rests on one rule:"
   *Fix:* say it straight. Ahaan's submitted reflection already has the human shape: "we take Laura's requested
   'high degree of certainty' to mean a near-ironclad guarantee, and we provide exactly that."

10. **Announcing.** "The ladder's main weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest" (V57); "The
    ladder's main weakness is its reliance on reinvested interest" (V58).
    *Fix:* Ray's list allows one announcement if normal sentences explain it straight away. The explanation has to
    say what physically happens to the interest (move 13).

11. **Restating the case as the opening.** Both drafts open with "From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each
    January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty."
    *Fix:* if the brief is restated, it should carry Caplet's reading of it, not only the case's words (move 1).

12. **Mirror pairs.** "Once Laura owes a sum, she owns the Treasuries that pay it"; "so her contribution helps most as
    the part that doesn't".
    *Fix:* break the symmetry and write it as cause and effect. The voice study found symmetry is "the tell that
    survives every word list".

13. **Claims about the world with no source.** "which funders much prefer to running costs"; "money a project knows
    it can call on tends to get spent".
    *Fix:* say who found it, or cut it. Every claim and every number says where it came from.

14. **Synonym cycling.** Shares, world index fund, kept shares. Facility, building. Minimum facility contribution,
    bottom, payout, that fund's payout. Ladder, ten Treasury holdings.
    *Fix:* one word for each thing, the case's word where it has one ("operating payments", "facility
    contribution", "credible range"), kept all the way through.

15. **An ending that winds up.** V57 closes on the tablet; V58 counts down: "until the tenth pays the last in 2042."
    *Fix:* stop on the last real point. A human paragraph explains and then stops.

16. **A pitch that is a principle.** V58's pitch: "We price each commitment Laura makes at what Treasuries charge,
    not what shares might earn, and fund it the day she makes it." That is an abstract rule, a "not Y" and a mirrored
    "makes ... makes" in one sentence. The D38 README notes the pitch still says "each commitment" after the 2028
    minimum stopped being called one.
    *Fix:* the pitch says what each deposit buys, in calendar order, in the case's words.

## (c) Why Keel's sentences are hard to understand

| Pattern | Examples from V57 and V58 | The rule that prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| **Pronouns with no clear noun** | "She buys its bottom in 2028." (its = the credible range, which hasn't been explained yet); "because that counts a return nobody has earned yet"; "the part that doesn't" (doesn't what?); "if they can't cover it" (they = kept shares, it = the gap); "We do the second"; "Crushing one"; "before she names it"; "if it can't cover the gap" | Every it, this, that, they and one points back to exactly one noun in the same or the previous sentence. When in doubt, repeat the noun. Humans repeat nouns. |
| **Terms used before they are explained, or never explained** | redemption and redeemed; credible range (V58, second paragraph); minimum facility contribution; operating reserve; kept shares; set-aside; "fixed-date Treasury funds" and "funds with fixed end dates"; reinvested interest; "as if every waiting dollar earned nothing"; capital-protected note; "an option that can expire worthless"; portfolio insurance; "matching their bonds' average timing to the payments'" | A term appears only after, or in the same sentence as, a plain description of what it is. The case's own terms get Caplet's meaning once, the first time. |
| **Compressed causal chains** | "Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it bets that rates won't fall."; "time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for a recovery"; "so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice"; "so her contribution helps most as the part that doesn't"; "because a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year"; "since money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent" | One "because" or "so" per sentence, with every link said out loud (move 9). A semicolon means two sentences. A "but ... even if ... since" chain is three sentences or none. |

## (d) Ahaan's tone, in 12 bullets

Tone only. Do not borrow his structure, his subheadings or his lines.

1. **He concedes first, then claims, so the claim is believed.** From his submitted reflection: "We know Treasury
   bonds ... won't create wealth, but they will remove uncertainty". The essays do the same at full length: the
   critics are "not without ammunition", and the answer turns on "this proves less than it appears to".
2. **He commits without hedging.** "we take Laura's requested 'high degree of certainty' to mean a near-ironclad
   guarantee, and we provide exactly that." The confidence comes from a clear definition, not from adjectives.
3. **He thinks about time like a person.** "Every year the bonds sit untouched is a good year – one less year of
   Laura's money being exposed to anything the restless markets might decide to do." Warm, a little dry, and
   "restless markets" is the only flourish in it.
4. **He spends words on the joins.** In the reflections: "In other words", "Notably", "Together", "The catch is",
   "but in return", "so". In the essays: "it bears emphasising", "to put it gently". This is why his long sentences
   are easy to read. In the voice study's count, about 18% of his sentences run past 30 words, against 3-4% of
   Keel's (`keel/voice/03_why_plastic.md`).
5. **He names the cost and what it buys in one breath.** "The catch is, their terminal payout is a net asset value,
   as opposed to a promise of par – but in return we gain a level of liquidity which no odd-lot bond offers."
6. **He admits constraints flatly.** "Some years, WInS lists no Treasuries maturing – so we purchased iShares
   iBonds ... instead." There is no apology and no spin, just the fact and what the team did.
7. **His brackets carry content.** "(like IBTM for 2032)", "(not merely a forecast)"; in the essays, "(though
   small)". Each one adds a fact or a shade of meaning, never decoration. One per paragraph at most.
8. **He defines a term in the flow.** In the essays, almost every technical term is explained between dashes the
   moment it appears, so the reader never stalls. This is the habit Keel's drafts most lack.
9. **His wit lives in homely images that carry the argument:** "revenge with a wig on", bailing out a ship with "a
   teaspoon", "furniture arrangements on a very small raft", boats "chained to the bottom". Each image is the
   argument in miniature. For the IPS that means at most one image, placed mid-paragraph, never a closing line.
10. **He wears his learning lightly.** He names an idea and its owner as a tool, in passing (Pigou on
    externalities), and states structural claims as observations: "this is not a polemical claim but a mathematical
    observation". That is cerebral without showing off. In 500 words, one named idea at most, and only if it is used.
11. **He doubts once, precisely, and then carries on.** In the essays he concedes the evidence doesn't settle
    everything, says in the next breath that it is enough for his point, and moves on. There is no spread of
    "may", "could" and "might".
12. **He solves problems rather than preaching.** "We do not beat a broken car for crashing. We ask what caused the
    failure, and we fix it." This practical, fix-it register suits an IPS: the client has a problem, and here is how
    it gets solved.

**What not to take from him, and why.** The essays have pun subheadings, one-word paragraphs ("Miserably.", "We
do."), a closing run of repeated openings, and a callback ending. Those work in a 2,000-word essay. The students
have rejected all of them for the IPS: Ray found the pun headings corny, and kickers and one-line endings are
banned. The IPS is also a 500-word form field. What carries over is the tone: confident, fair to the other side,
generous with joining words, and funny only in passing. His British spelling and en dashes are fine to keep.

## (e) The read-aloud test: 12 questions for every paragraph

Read the paragraph aloud, slowly, to someone who hasn't seen the plan.

1. **Friend test.** Could you say every sentence to a parent at dinner without stopping to rephrase it?
2. **Breath.** Did you need a breath in the middle of a sentence? The full stop goes where you breathed.
3. **Pointing words.** Does every it, this, that, they, one, "the gap" and "the money" point back to exactly one
   noun the listener has just heard?
4. **Terms.** Is every word that isn't everyday English either the case's own word or explained in plain words
   before, or as, it appears?
5. **The point.** Can you say in one sentence what this paragraph claims (a claim, not a fact)?
6. **One step per sentence.** Does any sentence have more than one "because", "so", "which" or "since"?
7. **The other side.** Who would disagree here, and did they get a full, fair sentence before the "but"?
8. **Order.** Are the years in calendar order, with each action told before its result?
9. **Actors.** Is a person or a thing doing every verb, rather than a noun like "reliance" or "set-aside"?
10. **Numbers.** Is there at most one number that matters, said plainly, with who says so?
11. **The ending.** Does the paragraph stop on its last real point, with no flourish, no contrast and no callback?
12. **Fingerprints.** Is there any "X, not Y" rhythm, mirror pair, list of three or explained name? Cover the team's
    name: could any team have written this paragraph? If so, what in it is Caplet's?

## (f) The ten most important "never do" items, ranked

1. **Never squeeze a sentence to save words.** Cut a whole idea instead. This is the students' main complaint
   ("I literally can't understand half of the sentences").
2. **Never use a pronoun, "the X" or a term before the reader has met its noun or its meaning.** This is the
   commonest reason V57 and V58 fail on a first read.
3. **Never end on a punchline.** No slogan flips, no two-word kickers, no "That is X." runs, no one-line endings and
   no callbacks, whether at the end of a paragraph or of the IPS.
4. **Never explain the plan's name or a metaphor.** No tablet, and no letter-by-letter XR. If the name has to be
   explained, leave it out.
5. **Never put more than one "because", "so", "which" or "since" in a sentence, or skip a link in a causal chain.**
6. **Never list rejected ideas at one clause each.** Hear one alternative fairly, or none.
7. **Never use "X, not Y", "never" or mirrored pairs for rhythm.** Keep a contrast only when someone actually holds
   the other view.
8. **Never write fact-sheet sentences (no person, no reason) or inventory lists.** Tell what Laura does and why, in
   order.
9. **Never give a number or a claim about the world without who says so.** Never pile numbers up, and never hedge
   more than once.
10. **Never invent an event, borrow a famous line, drop a name that does no work, or write about what "every team"
    does.** Use only real team events, the team's own words, and Ahaan's moves, never his lines.
