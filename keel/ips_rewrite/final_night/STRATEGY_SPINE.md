## 1. The strategy in three sentences a judge could retell

1. Each time Laura makes a commitment, she buys the Treasuries that will pay it that same day: the ten operating
   payments in 2027, the smallest facility contribution in 2028, and both ends of the credible range in 2031.
2. Shares get only the money nobody has been promised yet, which in practice is about the interest her 2028 deposit
   would have earned, so a market fall can shrink the range she names next but can never break one already named.
3. Nothing is sold early and nothing changes on a market move. The portfolio changes only on those three dates, and
   after 2033 the reserve just gets shorter as one holding is redeemed before each January.

Short form (the chain a listener could repeat): *promise → buy it in Treasuries → only unpromised money takes risk →
so markets change how big her next promise is, never whether an old one is kept.*

## 2. The single biggest strength, and the chain that proves it

**Biggest strength in one line: no market outcome can make Laura break a number she has already said.** Market risk is
turned from *default risk* (missing a January, or giving co-sponsors less than she named) into *ambition risk* (naming
a smaller range next time). The case's worst outcome is the first kind. Our plan only ever has the second.

The chain, taken to its end (Book 9's "logic"):
1. The case names two things that must not fail. The ten payments must be met "with a high degree of certainty" and
   with no outside money. The range must be "credible", because "If Laura promises more than she can ultimately
   contribute, she could damage her credibility." (case, quoted in `WHARTON_WORD.md` s6). Both are *promises with a
   date on them*.
2. A promise with a fixed dollar amount and a fixed date has one market price: what Treasuries maturing before that
   date cost today. Pricing it at what shares are expected to earn makes it look cheaper, but the saving is a return
   nobody has earned yet. (US public pensions are allowed to discount at expected returns; research finds that funds
   with higher assumed returns took more risk to keep them up: Novy-Marx and Rauh on the true size of the liabilities;
   Andonov, Bauer and Cremers, *Review of Financial Studies* 2017. Final Report citations, not IPS.)
3. So the moment a promise is made, it is bought. That is the only rule. It explains every allocation change in the plan
   and needs no forecast: the 2027 ladder (October notes: "each of Laura's payments rests on a dedicated instrument
   (not merely a forecast)"), the 2028 late-2032 Treasury fund (IBTM note: "a floor for her facility contribution,
   giving co-sponsors a minimum figure in 2031"; say "minimum figure", never "floor", I83), and the 2031 move of half the
   shares into Treasuries before she names the top.
4. Then every dollar that is still in shares is, by construction, a dollar no one has been promised. That is October's
   own reflection 3: "VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong – because it's the only investment not standing between
   Laura and a payment deadline." (Archive C1: grows from the locked record.)
5. Logical conclusion: a crash before 2031 lowers the range she names; a crash after 2031 lowers only what she keeps.
   Neither touches a payment or a named number. **Her credibility is not a forecast about markets; it is a list of
   things she owns.** Her confidence level for co-sponsors is therefore not "80% of our model paths" but "both ends are
   already bought".
6. The two honest exceptions, stated once, flat: (a) the iBond funds pay their net asset value, not a promised par
   (reflection 2: "as opposed to a promise of par"), and (b) the five T-bonds rely on reinvesting coupons. Both are
   covered by the same rule: before she names the range, the gap is priced as if every waiting dollar earned nothing
   and that much is moved from her kept shares into Treasuries; if her kept shares cannot cover it, she names a lower
   range. Even the exceptions land on ambition, not on a promise. (L-17: 100% of payments met in the base case and in a
   lasting 2-point rate fall, 98.71% in the worst tested world; MODEL, 22 Oct curve. So "near-ironclad", never
   "guaranteed".)

Why this is "very, very strong" for a judge: it answers four of Wharton's five IPS questions with one mechanism (see
s6), it agrees with every locked October note (C1, C3, C6, C7, C9 all grow from it), and it is the only kind of plan in
which "credible range" means something checkable rather than a probability.

## 3. Why it beats the three strongest alternatives (each argued at its best first)

### A. All Treasuries (no shares at all)
**At its best.** Simplest possible plan, most certain number. With the 2028 deposit all in a Treasury maturing in late
2032, she owns about $190,000 for the facility (MODEL, September curve, `ladder_sonnet.md` W2), against our owned bottom
of roughly $145,000 plus shares. Every dollar is bought; the range is a point. The equity premium over 5%-ish Treasuries
is thin: over five years shares beat a Treasury held to maturity only about two times in three (pasted list's estimate;
check before use). A sharp practitioner judge may prefer it.
**Why ours is better for this case.** The case asks for "an appropriate balance between pursuing growth and protecting
the capital required for her goals", says Laura "has been willing to take thoughtful risks", and Wharton scores
"appropriate diversification". An all-Treasury plan fails the first two on its face. Ours keeps all-Treasury
certainty for every promise and adds a growth stake sized so that, if shares went to nothing, her 2028 deposit would
still come back roughly as its own face value (only "the interest" is risked). **The honest cost, which the IPS should
state rather than hide (F3, accept a cost in public):** she gives up about $40,000 of certain money for the chance of
more. That trade is the plan's one deliberate risk, and it is taken with money nobody has been promised. If the Writer
can say this trade in words without a number, it is the single most persuasive sentence available against the
"too cautious" and the "why not all Treasuries?" readers at once.

### B. Ladder for the payments, plus a stock/bond mix that glides to safety by 2033 (the field's default)
**At its best.** Higher expected facility contribution; a familiar, defensible shape (target-date logic); shares get
years to compound; de-risking on a schedule controls the 2033 risk.
**Why ours is better.** A glide path keys risk to *time*, but Laura's risk is keyed to *promises*. In 2031 she must say a
number while her money is still, say, 40-60% in shares, so the range has to be a forecast, and the case says a
forecast she misses costs credibility. The leverage point from the pasted list is the cleanest proof (maths checked):
in 2033 the reserve is roughly $400,000 against roughly $200,000 for the facility, so the money she cares about sits on
about 2 dollars of fixed commitment per dollar of surplus. A "balanced" 60/40 on about $600,000 holds about $360,000 of
shares, which is about 1.8 times the surplus: the plan that looks moderate is a geared bet on exactly the money she
will name to co-sponsors (Modigliani-Miller / Hamada logic, Damodaran's capital-structure lectures; Final Report).
Ours holds shares worth well under a third of the surplus, all of it unpromised. It also needs no "sell on schedule"
step that can be skipped in a boom (`ladder_opus.md` s6: the default when a step is missed must be the safe outcome;
ours has no step to miss after 2031).

### C. A range read off model percentiles (e.g. 10th-90th, "80% confidence")
**At its best.** Uses all the information; honest about uncertainty; a higher top; it is what Wharton's own wording
("a credible range ... with a stated confidence") seems to invite.
**Why ours is better.** A percentile range is built to be wrong one time in five, and the case tells us a miss is
costly. Ours turns "confidence" into ownership: the bottom is what the late-2032 Treasury fund pays (rounded down),
and the top is bought by moving half her shares into Treasuries before she names it. Newsvendor logic says that if
over-promising costs k times more than under-promising, promise the 1/(1+k) quantile; buying both ends is the limit
where a missed named number is treated as unacceptable, which is the case's own stance. **Cost:** the top is lower
than a p90 forecast would be, and what she keeps above the top is "hers, liquid and promised to no one" (that is the
financial flexibility the case asks for, not a reserve for the building: `ladder_opus.md` s2, soft budgets).

## 4. The most creative idea, tested

**Candidate: the portfolio follows a calendar of promises, not a glide path.** Risk leaves the portfolio on the three
dates Laura gives her word (2027, 2028, 2031), in exactly the amount she gives it, and at no other time. The 2031 range
is bought, at both ends, before anyone hears it.

**Would a judge who has read 400 IPSs have met it?**
- A Treasury ladder for the payments: yes, in most strong entries (Commandment I's "field default").
- Stocks for the facility, de-risked by 2033: yes, commonly, on a time schedule.
- A 2031 range from Monte Carlo percentiles at 80-95%: yes, the expected answer (`ladder_sonnet.md`: "Typical strong
  team ... reads the range off a Monte Carlo").
- **Buying the range before saying it, so its confidence is ownership rather than probability: rare.** We found no
  room file or reading that saw it in a typical entry; it follows directly from the one pricing rule, and it is
  checkable. It also grows from October's locked words (VT note: "a rise before 2031 lifts the top of the range
  co-sponsors hear"; IBTM note: the minimum figure). This is the answer to "what is most creative?"
- **Supporting, also rare:** pricing each commitment at Treasury cost and saying why (the pension story) is common in
  finance research but unusual in a student IPS. Use it as the reason for the rule, in one or two sentences, not as a
  topic.

What is *not* creative and should not be sold as such (I84): the name Caplet XR, the tablet, "do not crush", the
capital-protected-note and portfolio-insurance comparisons (Ray called the V57 named concepts "packaging"). The
redemption meaning of R can stay as a name with a reason, once, but carries no argument.

## 5. The opponent's best case against us, and the honest answer

**(a) "Too timid for a client willing to take thoughtful risks: under a tenth in shares."** (WInS book 8.6%; Laura's
plan about 6.6%, `FACTS.md` N9.) Strongest form: she has seven years and an entrepreneur's temperament; at 5-10%
equities the upside is a rounding error, and the all-Treasury plan beats us on certainty anyway, so the shares are
neither growth nor safety.
*Answer.* "Thoughtful" risk is risk placed where it cannot hurt a promise, sized to what she can lose without breaking
one. Three reasons it stays small, all real: (1) every dollar above the minimum facility contribution that sat in shares
would be a dollar co-sponsors might be told about, which is the credibility risk the case names; (2) at today's
Treasury yields the expected reward for shares over a Treasury held five years is thin, so each extra share buys little
expected money and a lot of spread (the pasted list's "master equation" table makes the same point: going from no
shares to half in shares adds about $19k to the median and cuts the bad-case outcome sharply; Final Report, recompute
first); (3) her own career already carries economic risk (her 2028 deposit "can" fall in the same recessions that hit
shares; case wording only, Archive C5; Bodie-Merton-Samuelson on human capital, Final Report). The share stake is not
the plan's ambition; the ambition is a facility contribution she can actually stand behind. Say the trade-off plainly
once (s3A) and the "timid" charge becomes "they chose it and know its price".

**(b) "Your bond rungs are not cash-flow matched: the 2038-2042 T-bonds pay coupons early and mature months before
January, so you carry reinvestment risk, and your $300,000 no longer buys the ladder."** (Facts: the Feb-2037 bond ends
10.5 months early; on the 4 Nov close the ladder costs about $300,608, so the 2028 deposit tops it up, 65% chance on the
model, `FACTS.md` N1-N3; Nov-2040 note: "A lower-coupon bond would reduce reliance on reinvested coupons, but WInS lists
none".)
*Answer.* True, and October said so in its own notes, so the IPS should own it as the ladder's one weakness, not
dodge it. Two rules cover it: (i) if rates leave the ladder short, the 2028 deposit finishes it first and the facility
gets less (IBTR note; Archive C9); (ii) before naming the range, the gap is priced as if every waiting dollar earned
nothing and covered from her kept shares. The risk is real but lands on the facility, never on a January. Do not write
"her first $300,000 buys every payment" flat (false on the 4 Nov close).

**(c) "The funds pay NAV, not par; you have no guarantee."** Answer: correct; near-ironclad, held to their December
close (Book 5); the zero-reinvestment pricing in (b) also absorbs a small NAV shortfall.

**(d) "Bought both ends? Then the range is just two numbers you chose, with no confidence level."** Answer: the
confidence is stated in the strongest form available: the bottom and top are owned in Treasuries maturing before 2033.
The *probability* language belongs to what she keeps above the top, and to the Final Report.

## 6. How Wharton's five IPS questions map onto the spine (`WHARTON_WORD.md` s2)

| Wharton's question | Where the spine answers it |
|---|---|
| Central idea | One rule: a commitment is bought in Treasuries the day it is made; only unpromised money takes risk |
| Principles guiding decisions | (1) price a fixed promise at what Treasuries charge; (2) the safe asset is the one that matches the debt, so cash and inflation-linked bonds are risks for fixed dollars on fixed dates (Archive C3, reflection 1); (3) change only on promise dates, never on a market move; (4) a shortfall always lands on the facility, never on a payment |
| Balance of growth, risk, liquidity, reliability, flexibility | Growth = shares with unpromised money; reliability = holdings to maturity; liquidity = funds sellable any day at the cost of a fixed payout (Archive C6, reflection 2); flexibility = what she keeps above the top, promised to no one; risk = only ambition risk |
| How the allocation changes as needs approach | 2027 ladder; 2028 minimum bought, interest to shares; 2031 half the shares into Treasuries before naming, gap priced at zero reinvestment; 2033 reserve formed, one holding redeemed before each January to 2042 |
| Protect the operating commitment while allowing a responsible facility contribution | The payments are bought first and topped up first; the facility is what remains, and its named part is bought before it is named |

Diversification (criterion 1): one world index fund of about 10,000 companies for the only risky money; optionally one
clause that her contribution is the part of the residency's money that does not shrink in a recession when co-sponsors,
grants and fees might (shortlist idea 3). The clause is optional; if it costs a whole idea elsewhere, cut it.
