## 2. Best lines across all versions: the 25 strongest sentences, verbatim

Test for each: plain words, concrete, human, and it carries a strategic claim. Tags give the first version it appears in.
Remember Ray's I83 rule when reusing the idea: "cheque", "floor" and "ladder" are old labels.

1. "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." (V25; kept to V30)
   Why: the sentence a judge remembers; says what rivals do and what this plan does differently, in two lines of ordinary English.
2. "Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom." (V32; also V36-V40)
   Why: the whole case against stocks for the payments in one picture; a student can repeat it.
3. "A patient investor can wait out a bad decade. A January cannot." (V42)
   Why: human, short, true; it is the strategy (fixed dollars on fixed dates) without a technical word.
4. "A fixed-percentage withdrawal can ride out a crash; a fixed-dollar withdrawal is a forced sale at the bottom." (V26)
   Why: names the difference between an endowment and Laura's obligation, which is the sceptic's best point answered.
5. "Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more." (V25)
   Why: answers the case's inflation question in plain words; a fixed dollar debt is hedged by a dollar bond.
6. "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one." (V27)
   Why: a counter-intuitive diversification point (do not diversify the payments) said in numbers a Year 11 can check.
7. "Because Laura already owns plenty of stock-market risk – it just isn't in a brokerage account." (V22)
   Why: human capital in 17 words, with a dry joke; the plain-English version of Merton and Bodie.
8. "So money she has buys certainty; money she expects carries risk." (V29)
   Why: states who pays for what and why, in a balanced line that is true rather than clever.
9. "A lasting fall in yields hurts twice: the payment Treasuries cost more in January, and their coupons earn less afterwards." (V15)
   Why: the real mechanism of the plan's one weak point, in one sentence, with no jargon.
10. "We swap the IOU for a Treasury (ask Lehman's customers why), the option for the stocks themselves, and skip the fee." (V24)
    Why: shows knowledge (issuer credit risk of bank notes) with wit and in one breath; the only mention of counterparty risk in any version.
11. "Shares are called safe over ten years, but time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for a recovery." (V57; first as V56i)
    Why: answers the obvious objection with a real idea (time diversification) and links it to the fixed payment.
12. "Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same downturns that hit share prices, so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice." (V57)
    Why: ties the strategy to the client's actual income; uses the case's own words.
13. "Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it bets that rates won't fall." (V58)
    Why: answers the obvious "why not cash?" with the reinvestment point; short and original (but see section 5 on how it is argued).
14. "Pension funds cover fixed payments either by matching their bonds' average timing to the payments' and rebalancing as rates move, or by buying one bond per payment and holding it." (V57)
    Why: shows the choice that was made among real methods, which is what the judges asked for ("central idea behind your approach").
15. "Nor do we copy portfolio insurance, which sells shares as they fall and helped deepen the 1987 crash." (V57)
    Why: rejects a well-known rival strategy with a fact; a reader learns something.
16. "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless." (V56i/V57)
    Why: explains what the plan copies and what it improves, in nine plain words after the comma.
17. "Had her 2028 deposit gone into stocks, the top would usually be higher, and the bottom whatever a 2030 crash left." (V37b)
    Why: the best answer in any draft to "why not put the second deposit in stocks?"; concrete and honest about the trade-off.
18. "What a panicked manager sells in 2032, Laura sells calmly on naming day, moving the promised half into Treasuries." (V48)
    Why: explains the lock on the range with a human contrast; the idea stays in V57 as "a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year".
19. "a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn." (V52: "But a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn.")
    Why: the credibility argument for the range at its plainest.
20. "The ladder costs less than the payments because its bonds pay interest along the way, which we reinvest." (V55)
    Why: the only sentence anywhere that says why a held-to-maturity ladder reinvests anything; readers in V57's tests could not find it in V57.
21. "If that deposit arrives smaller, the gift shrinks. A cheque funded by a Treasury already bought does not." (V52-V54)
    Why: the order of loss in two plain sentences: what shrinks, what cannot.
22. "We'd pay the $15,000." (V46-V49)
    Why: a rival's edge conceded in four words, and the price named; human and confident. (The numbers behind it are model figures; the move is the idea.)
23. "Laura takes thoughtful risks; the strongest alternative takes one for her" (V48/V49: "Laura takes thoughtful risks; the strongest alternative takes one for her – keeping our ladder")
    Why: uses the case's phrase and turns it on the rival.
24. "Laura's career already behaves like a stock." (V53)
    Why: five words that carry the human-capital argument (use the case's word for her income, not the label).
25. "Each is answered by a Treasury bond, or an iBond Treasury fund that turns itself into cash, maturing before its January." (V25; adapt "iBond" out)
    Why: states exactly what is bought, in one line, for both bonds and funds.

## 3. Recurring failures

### 3.1 The opening paragraph retells the case
Every version from V19 on opens by restating the $50,000, ten Januaries and the Taiwan residency, which the judge has just read in the case.
- V19: "From 2033 Laura owes her residency $50,000 every January for ten years, and only her portfolio may pay it."
- V22/V23: "In January 2033 Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten $50,000 cheques to her Taiwan residency, and nobody else may help."
- V30/V31: "In January 2033, Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten cheques – $50,000 each, one every January, to fund her Taiwan residency. Nobody else can pay them."
- V57: "From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty."
- V58: the same sentence, unchanged. The one opening that does strategy first is V25-V29's "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." The panel (V27) ranked that draft first with all three judges.

- "cheque" for the case's "operating payments"; "building gift", "the hope", "the promise".
- "Root-and-Branch", "the roots", "the branch" (V1-V18): "secures the roots, the payments and a facility floor, before anything is invested in the branch".
- "sail" (V10): "a bond floor plus a measured stock sail".
- "ladder" and "rung": "a ladder of Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one rung falling due before each January" (V43).

- "set-aside", "naming day", "soft spot", "the leak": "Our one soft spot is coupons reinvested as rates fall" (V30); "The ladder has one leak" (V39).

## 7. For the Writer: steal and avoid (one screen)

**Steal:** V58's one rule (price every commitment at what Treasuries charge, buy it the day it is made) as the spine, said slowly in plain sentences; Ahaan's "VT is where we allow ourselves to be wrong" logic for the risk budget and his concede-then-"but" rhythm; the nominal-payments point (no TIPS needed, because inflation can't make $50,000 cost more); the 2031 range bought at both ends before it is said; human capital ("her income already rides on the economy"); residency-level diversification; flexibility as money nobody else can count on; the close on the tenth redemption in 2042 (full circle without medicine).
