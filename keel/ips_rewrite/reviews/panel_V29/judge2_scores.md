## 2. Per-entry lines

**Entry B**
- Line I remember: "Wipe out every share she owns and no cheque is worse off – only the gift."
- Biggest strength: it closes its own known weakness (coupon reinvestment) in 2031, before the range is quoted, and makes the bottom of the range absorb anything left uncovered. That keeps its one rule intact: once a number is named, it is already funded.
- Biggest weakness: the growth sleeve is never sized in plain words ("roughly the floor's expected interest buys a world index fund"), so a reader cannot see how much growth Laura is actually buying. The 2033 "tops up once" also goes unexplained when the gap was supposedly settled in 2031.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? **yes**

**Entry A**
- Line I remember: "Keep the seed; risk only the harvest."
- Biggest strength: the same clear liability-first thesis. Treasuries mature before each January, the 2028 deposit is treated as risky because it is correlated with her career, and the 2031 range is tied to assets she already owns.
- Biggest weakness: it breaks its own rule. A promise is "safe no later than the day it becomes one", yet the gift is named in 2031 and the reinvestment shortfall is fixed only in January 2033, "the one thing that can still trim the gift". A named gift can therefore still shrink.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? **maybe** (borderline yes; the internal contradiction is what a careful judge catches)

## 3. Sameness

The two entries are near-identical: the same pitch, the same "buy, don't promise" opening, the same XR/pharmacist device, the same 2027/2028/2031 sequence and the same closing line. They differ in only three places: (1) what the floor does before 2031, (2) where the promised half goes and (3) when the reinvestment gap is settled (2031 in B, 2033 in A). Both say: fixed-dollar cheques are bought outright with dated Treasuries, stocks are only a small world fund grown from expected interest, and the 2031 range is the owned floor plus half the stocks, locked into Treasuries when named.

## 4. Top 3

With only two entries, the ranking is B, then A.
1. B: its timing is consistent. The reinvestment gap is priced and set aside before the range is spoken, and any shortfall lowers the quoted bottom rather than breaking a promise. "Wipe out every share..." is a stress test a judge can verify in one read.
2. A: the same thesis and voice, but its 2033 fix leaves the named gift exposed, contradicting its own headline rule. Its "four ways fixed promises have failed" paragraph is a strong idea but is asserted without support.

## 5. What a winning IPS would have to do that neither does

It would keep this thesis and voice but state the shape of the portfolio in plain proportions at each stage (2027, 2028, 2031, 2033), so a judge sees the growth-versus-certainty trade-off instead of inferring it from "roughly the floor's expected interest". It would also say in one sentence what Laura gives up: the expected upside of a stock-heavy plan, traded for a cheque schedule that cannot fail short of a US default. In both entries the cost of certainty goes unstated, and a portfolio manager wants to see that cost owned.

## (a) Which entry I would advance

I would advance Entry B. The two share the same strong idea: buy the fixed-dollar cheques with dated Treasuries, treat the career-linked 2028 deposit as the risky money, and quote the 2031 range only from assets already owned. Only B carries that rule through to the end. It settles the coupon-reinvestment gap in 2031, before the range is spoken, and lets any shortfall lower the quoted bottom. Entry A admits its gift "can still trim" after it is named, which contradicts its own headline.

## (b) Sentences that are false, unclear or read machine-written

**Entry A**
- "Caplet XR in one rule: each promise is made safe no later than the day it becomes one. Until then it is a hope." The paragraph on the fourth failure mode ("the one thing that can still trim the gift" … "in January 2033 the reserve changes once") contradicts this. The gift is named in 2031 but not fully safe until 2033. This is an internal inconsistency, not a factual error, but it is the entry's central claim.
- "That deposit fills any gap in the ladder, then buys the floor – more of the first cheque's fund – sized to regrow the remainder by 2033." This is unclear: what is "the remainder"? The reader has to guess that it means the part of the deposit not used for the floor.
- "Roughly its expected interest buys a world index fund." "Its" is ambiguous (the deposit or the floor?), and the stock sleeve's size is never given in words a client could repeat.
- "Too high, and she breaks her word, which we judge three times worse." The 3x weight is unsupported and reads as a model parameter dropped into prose.
- "Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, guarantees nobody priced, returns assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell." This is a sweeping historical claim with no example; it sounds authoritative but is not shown.
- "To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush." This is clever, but the polished aside reads as crafted by a writer (or a machine) rather than by students. It costs about 40 words and adds no investment content.
- "Now the odd part:" and "Keep the seed; risk only the harvest." The aphoristic cadence repeats often enough to start reading machine-written.

**Entry B**
- "We spread the stocks across the world, but not the cheques: Ten independent bets..." This has a capital letter after the colon (a typo).
- "Roughly the floor's expected interest buys a world index fund." This is clearer than A but still opaque. The interest over what period? How big is that relative to $450,000?
- "...the gap today's yields imply, plus a cushion, moves from spare cash and her half into the first cheque's fund..." "Today's" is ambiguous: 2031 yields or the yields at writing?
- "In 2033 the reserve tops up once, then never changes." This is unclear. If the gap was settled in 2031, what is topped up in 2033, and from where?
- "If needs change, cheques first; floor and stocks are bought smaller together." The tense is awkward and the meaning uncertain. It seems to describe only the 2028 purchase, not later changes in need.
- "Too high, and she breaks her word, which we judge three times worse." The 3x weight is unsupported, as in A.
- Pharmacist paragraph: same comment as for A.
- No sentence is factually false. The 99%-over-ten arithmetic (about 9.6%), "a slump that makes bonds dearer", and "inflation cannot make $50,000 cost more" (nominal) are all correct.

## (c) The single change that would most improve the winner (Entry B)

Replace "Roughly the floor's expected interest buys a world index fund" with one plain sentence giving the portfolio's shape in proportions, using words taken from the pharmacist gloss. For example: after 2028 roughly nine dollars in ten sit in dated Treasuries and about one in ten in a world index fund. B's whole case (certainty bought, growth kept small and isolated) is right, but a judge currently has to infer how small the growth sleeve is. Stating it, and owning the upside Laura gives up for it, turns an elegant idea into a visible, defensible trade-off.
