## 2. Entry notes

**01 (CPPI)**
- Remember: "stock exposure = 3 × cushion".
- Strength: a real, correctly described dynamic strategy (floor = PV of the liabilities, multiplier, quarterly reset, gap risk named), done without derivatives.
- Weakness/error: in January 2027 the floor (~$300k by its own figure) equals the portfolio, so the cushion is about zero and the rule holds almost no stock; the worked "$50,000 cushion" example does not exist in year one and this is never addressed. "The floor ... rises toward $500,000 as the payments approach" is wrong: the PV peaks at roughly $400k in January 2033 at today's yields, then falls as payments are made. The pitch's "the floor never crossed" overclaims what the IPS itself calls a 99% simulated outcome (gap risk), so the payments rest on a model, not on owned cash flows.
- Advance: no.

**02 (Safety Bucket / Growth Bucket)**
- Remember: nothing.
- Strength: correct and complete: a ladder held to maturity, the 2028 deposit filling the gap first, a floor locked in 2031, the right rule list (TIPS/STRIPS banned), willingness vs capacity named.
- Weakness: entirely generic; it is the template every competent team writes, with no number, no tested trade-off and no voice.
- Advance: no.

**03 (Max-Sharpe Monte Carlo)**
- Remember: "the smallest amount that funds all ten payments in at least 95% of the 10,000 simulated paths ... about $445,000".
- Strength: the inputs are stated and internally consistent (the 5.9% expected return checks), and model risk is admitted.
- Weakness/error: asset-only mean-variance is the wrong framework for a dated nominal liability. The fatal error: a reserve costing ~$445k for 95% certainty is more expensive than a fully matched Treasury ladder (about $400k at 2033 yields), which gives near-100%. They pay more for less certainty and call it "upside". The 2028 deposit is never mentioned; "15% a year" for two years gives 30% equity, not "about 25%"; a 10th-90th percentile range misses one time in five, which is exactly the overpromise the case warns against.
- Advance: no.

**04 (LDI / immunisation)**
- Remember: "a method that measures what she owes before deciding what she can give."
- Strength: correct textbook ALM: PV and duration of the liability, funding ratio, duration-matched hedge, de-risking the next two payments into cash-like Treasuries.
- Weakness/error: it immunises with rolling ETFs when individual Treasuries held to maturity (allowed) would dedicate the cash flows outright, which leaves needless rebalancing, convexity and curve-twist risk. A 105% funding-ratio floor is impossible in 2027 ($300k against a ~$300k PV), and this is not addressed. Two sections are numbered 8. There is almost no client in it.
- Advance: no.

**05 (Pencil and ink)**
- Remember: "We never sell ink to buy pencil."
- Strength: the metaphor carries real decision rules (what gets locked and when), with a gradual 2031-33 lock of the gift and a clear flexibility margin, and it ties to the client's craft.
- Weakness: "inks as many panels as it can while leaving room to grow" implies leaving rungs unbought in 2027, which is unhedged rate risk on the promise, and the IPS doesn't see this. There are no numbers or tested trade-offs, and "only ... a U.S. government default" ignores coupon-reinvestment risk.
- Advance: maybe.

**06 (Buy the promise, one growth engine)**
- Remember: "She takes risks with ideas, not with promises to other people."
- Strength: disciplined and specific: an iBond/Treasury ladder, the 2028 deposit split into a dated Treasury minimum for 2031 plus one world fund, honest return and drawdown assumptions, inflation risk put where it belongs.
- Weakness: "The only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution" overclaims: coupon reinvestment on the long rungs is unmentioned, and an iBond fund's payout at liquidation is near-known, not exact. The flexibility policy is one vague line.
- Advance: maybe.

**07 (Ladder + floor-plus-participation gift)**
- Remember: "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." And: "Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more."
- Strength: the only entry that applies investing knowledge rather than reciting it. It covers sequence risk ("a withdrawal of fixed dollars is a forced sale at the bottom"). It names the correlation between her human capital and rates (the 2028 deposit shrinks in the same slump that raises ladder costs). It states the breaking point in numbers, which I checked: a ~2pt fall raises the ladder to roughly $365k, a gap only a halved deposit could not fill. The gift is built as a capital-protected note (dated Treasury floor plus equity sized to the floor's interest). The range comes from an asymmetric loss (3:1), and the top is set at roughly 3-in-4, which is the correct quantile for that loss. It names its own soft spot (coupon reinvestment) and a fix. Treasuries described as "debts owed to her" against her debts: that is ALM in one phrase.
- Weakness: the motif (a product name, "XR", the pharmacist, "Do not crush") spends scarce words and will strike some judges as a gimmick. The prose is dense enough that a non-specialist may miss the note structure. The growth sleeve is small (about the floor's interest) and Laura's flexibility is only half of it, so the growth and flexibility side of W3 is thin. Naming a specific ETF goes against the "strategy, not individual investments" guidance. "About three times in four" is slightly generous: my estimate of equities being flat or up over ~2 years is ~70%.
- Advance: yes.

**08 (Promise First, Then Grow)**
- Remember: nothing distinctive ("Gambling money already spoken for would be risk without reward" comes closest).
- Strength: the most complete of the standard designs: coupons parked in short Treasuries, a 5-point drift band, explicit assumptions, a gift cap at the announced top, half kept as flexibility.
- Weakness: interchangeable with 02/05/12. It is competent and correct, but nothing would make a judge reread it.
- Advance: no.

**09 (Startup stage gates)**
- Remember: "Each gate is a real option: Laura has the right, not the obligation, to invest more."
- Strength: an original framing of the gift as staged, conditional tranches, a genuinely useful idea for co-sponsors.
- Weakness/error: it puts 25% in QQQ "because Laura's own career sits in technology and creative industries". That adds to the risk she already carries rather than diversifying it, which is the opposite of what human-capital reasoning prescribes, and it is performance-chasing. Tranches paid in 2034-35 go beyond the case's 2033 contribution and are left unreconciled.
- Advance: no.

**10 (Lead gift + matching pledge)**
- Remember: "never let Laura announce a number her portfolio cannot keep."
- Strength: the best co-sponsor thinking in the field. Splitting the range into a committed lead gift and a conditional match turns uncertainty into a fundraising tool.
- Weakness/error: the reserve is hedged with IEF (duration ~7) against a ~10-year liability until 2031. A 2pt rally would open a gap larger than the 5% margin, and that margin cannot be funded from $300k in 2027 anyway. Certainty is "97% in our simulations" when owned cash flows were available. The SCHD dividend tilt is decoration.
- Advance: no.

**11 (Graphic novel chapters)**
- Remember: "the gutter is the white space between panels".
- Strength: the range bottom is defined sensibly (locked value plus the remaining stocks after a severe fall), and the plan includes a TWD-linked slice for a facility priced in Taiwan.
- Weakness: the chapter device is longer than the substance it carries. The 15% EWT slice is a concentrated single-country, semiconductor-heavy bet presented as client fit. "Treasuries earn their purchase yield" ignores reinvestment.
- Advance: no.

**12 (Foundation and garden)**
- Remember: "We chose a smaller garden over a shaky foundation."
- Strength: honest and clear. It is one of the few entries to name liquidity and coupon reinvestment and to state the trade-off plainly; three usable rules.
- Weakness: generic beneath the metaphor. "The harvested half is the bottom of her range and the stock half is the top" is loosely worded (the top is both halves). It has no quantified view.
- Advance: no.

**13 (All-Treasury dedication)**
- Remember: "A fixed nominal payment on a known date is, financially, a bond Laura has already sold."
- Strength: the most rigorous fixed-income reasoning after 07: dedication not forecasting, buying the longest rungs first because they carry the most duration, and the 2022 gilt crisis correctly attributed to leverage. The S&P's negative 2000-09 return is used accurately.
- Weakness: it refuses the "balance growth" part of the mandate outright. A client with stated risk appetite and a facility exposed to inflation gets zero equity, and "coupon reinvestment ... remote" understates a 15-year exposure. It is admirably honest about the cost, but it answers one of the case's two questions.
- Advance: maybe.

## 3. Sameness
02, 05, 08, 11 and 12, with 06 close behind, are interchangeable: a Treasury ladder held to maturity, "the only risk is a U.S. government default", the 2028 deposit fills any gap first, a diversified equity sleeve rebalanced yearly, some of it moved to Treasuries in 2031 to form the bottom of the range, stocks set the top, and "keep part for flexibility". 05 and 11 even share the same graphic-novel ink/pencil metaphor. Seven entries repeat the "only a U.S. default" line, and most ignore coupon reinvestment while doing so. 01, 03 and 04 form a second cluster ("we use a named quant technique"), but in each the technique is recited more than applied to this liability.

## 4. Top 3
1. **07.** It was the only entry I stopped and reread. Every claim is a piece of applied finance that I could check, and it checked: sequence risk on fixed-dollar withdrawals, the correlation between human capital and rates, a numerical breaking point, the gift built as a capital-protected note, and a range quantile derived from an asymmetric loss. It also names its own weak spot and the fix. It is the only entry that sounds like a person arguing, not a template filled in.
2. **06.** A clean, specific and disciplined design: dated instruments for each rung, a dated Treasury minimum for 2031 funded from the 2028 deposit, one low-cost world fund left alone, honest drawdown assumptions and a sharp client line. It loses to 07 on depth and on its overclaim of "only default and execution".
3. **05.** The metaphor actually generates rules ("never sell ink to buy pencil", lock the gift gradually from 2031, leave white space), and it is client-rooted and readable. It edges out 13 and 08 on clarity and voice, but it lacks numbers and has the unhedged "leave panels unbought" slip.

## 5. What the winning IPS would have to do
It would have to make the price of certainty explicit and let that price drive every decision. That means stating in plain words how many facility dollars the ladder costs in expectation versus a riskier plan, and why Laura should pay it. It would then govern both halves with the same discipline, cash-flow dedication for the payments and a quantified, loss-asymmetric rule for the range, while giving growth and flexibility a real, sized role rather than a closing sentence. It would also give contingent decision rules for the cases that actually threaten this client: rates falling before the purchases, a reduced or late 2028 deposit, a crash in 2031-32, and facility costs rising in Taiwan dollars. Finally, it would be honest about every residual risk (reinvestment, execution, FX) without hedging the conclusion, and do all of it in plain prose with no device the reader has to decode. Entry 07 comes closest but is short on growth, flexibility and plainness. No entry does all of it.
