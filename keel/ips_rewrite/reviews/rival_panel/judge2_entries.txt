INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT:
What we are trying to do
Laura has an obligation and an ambition. The obligation is ten fixed $50,000 payments, 2033 to 2042, funded with high certainty and no outside help. The ambition is a facility contribution big enough to bring co-sponsors in. We secure the obligation first, at today's prices, and only then pursue growth.

Principle 1: Match each payment to a maturity
In January 2027 Laura's $300,000 buys a ladder of ten rungs. Payments due 2033-2036 are matched with iShares iBonds Treasury ETFs maturing the December before; they hold only Treasuries and pay out at maturity. Payments due 2037-2042 are matched with individual Treasury bonds maturing a few months earlier. With 2027 Treasury yields around 4-5%, $500,000 of future payments costs a little under $300,000 today. If the first deposit falls short, the gap is the first claim on the 2028 deposit.

Our definition of high certainty
A payment is "highly certain" when it does not depend on any forecast. Held to maturity, every rung pays its known value whatever happens to stocks, interest rates or inflation. The only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution, which we control by checking every maturity date. We are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question.

Principle 2: One growth engine, left alone
After any gap is filled, the 2028 deposit is split in two. One part buys a Treasury maturing in late 2032; this sets a minimum facility contribution Laura can promise in 2031 with near-certainty. The rest goes into Vanguard Total World Stock (VT): thousands of companies worldwide for under 0.1% a year. We hold it untouched until 2033: over six years, cost and discipline beat prediction. We assume roughly 6-7% a year with years of -30% or worse possible.

How the portfolio changes as 2033 approaches
In 2033 the ladder becomes the operating reserve: ten rungs for ten payments, one maturing each year, with any cash awaiting a payment held in short-term Treasuries. The reserve shortens by itself, as a shrinking obligation should, and nothing is ever sold to meet a payment.

The 2031 range and the facility
Laura's range runs from the minimum already locked in Treasuries to that minimum plus a share of VT's value in 2031. The bottom is close to guaranteed; the top depends on markets, and we will state its likelihood in the Final Report. Good markets raise the contribution; bad markets can only shrink the upside, never the payments or the minimum.

Flexibility
Part of the VT holding stays with her after 2033, invested, as a buffer for overruns and new ideas.

Inflation
The payments are fixed in dollars, so a nominal ladder matches them exactly. Facility costs will rise with inflation; that risk sits with the growth fund, the asset best placed to outpace it.

Why this fits Laura
She takes risks with ideas, not with promises to other people. This strategy lets her do both.

Purpose

Central idea: match, don't forecast

Laura is willing to take thoughtful risk, but nothing in her goals requires it. Over seven to sixteen years, stocks are not reliably safe: the S&P 500 returned less than nothing over 2000-2009. Objective
Laura must pay $50,000 a year from 2033 to 2042 with high certainty, and wants to contribute as much as responsibly possible to her residency's facility. Most strategies choose a fixed stock-bond mix and hope it works. We use a rule that adjusts risk automatically to how much safety margin Laura actually has.

The floor
The floor is the present value of the ten payments: what it would cost, at current Treasury yields, to buy bonds that pay every instalment. At 2027 yields this is close to $300,000, and it rises toward $500,000 as the payments approach. The floor is recalculated every quarter from the Treasury yield curve.

The cushion and the multiplier
The cushion is the portfolio's value minus the floor. Our rule: stock exposure = 3 × cushion; everything else is held in Treasuries. With a cushion of $50,000, Laura holds $150,000 in stocks. If stocks fall 20%, the cushion shrinks and the rule sells stocks, buying Treasuries. If stocks rise, it buys more. We chose a multiplier of 3 because historical data show US stocks have rarely fallen more than a third in a single quarter, so a quarterly-rebalanced portfolio should not breach the floor even in a crash like 2008. The $150,000 contribution in 2028 enlarges the cushion and is invested by the same rule.

Assets
Risky asset: VT, for global diversification at very low cost. Safe asset: a blend of Treasury ETFs (SHY, IEF and TLT) weighted to move with the floor when interest rates change, plus individual Treasury bonds as payments approach. No derivatives, leverage or options are used; "insurance" comes from rebalancing, not from buying puts.

High degree of certainty
We define it as a 99% probability that the floor is never breached, tested by 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations with historical stock volatility and interest-rate shocks, including sudden 30% crashes between rebalancing dates.

As 2033 approaches
CPPI de-risks naturally: as the floor grows, stocks fall as a share of the portfolio unless the cushion grows faster. On 1 January 2033 the floor becomes the operating reserve, and we lock it into individual Treasuries maturing before each payment. The reserve then only shortens over time.

Facility contribution and flexibility
The cushion in 2033 is what Laura can give. We recommend contributing about 70% and keeping 30% invested under the same rules as her flexibility fund.

2031 range
Because CPPI gives a known floor, the downside of the cushion is limited. In 2031 we will simulate the cushion's possible 2033 values from that day's portfolio and quote a range Laura reaches with about 80% confidence.

Why this suits Laura
She is an entrepreneur: willing to take risk, but never with money she has promised. CPPI lets her risk only what she can afford to lose, and every quarter it measures that amount precisely.

=== ENTRY 12 ===
ELEVATOR PITCH:
We manage Laura's portfolio like a pension fund: first the liabilities, then the surplus. Treasury ETFs are combined so their value and interest-rate sensitivity match the ten $50,000 payments, keeping them funded whatever rates do. Only the surplus is invested in global equities for her residency's facility.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT:
1. Objective
Laura has a defined liability: ten nominal payments of $50,000 at the start of each year from 2033 to 2042. Pension funds solve this with liability-driven investing. It answers the case's central question precisely: how much is needed for the payments, and how much is free to grow.

2. Measuring the liability
The liability is measured by its present value (PV), discounted with the current US Treasury yield curve, and by its duration, the sensitivity of that PV to interest rates. At early-2027 yields of about 4.5-5%, the PV is close to $300,000 and the duration is roughly 10 years. Both are recalculated every January.

3. The hedging portfolio
We hold a Treasury portfolio whose PV equals the liability's PV and whose duration equals the liability's duration. We use three ETFs: SHY (short), IEF (intermediate) and TLT (long), weighted so the combined duration equals the liability's. If rates fall, the liability's value rises, but so does the hedge, by almost the same amount; if rates rise, both fall. The funding ratio, hedge value divided by liability PV, therefore stays stable. This is called immunisation.

4. High degree of certainty
We define it as maintaining a funding ratio of at least 105% at every annual review. The extra 5% absorbs small hedging errors from rebalancing only once a year and from the fact that ETF and liability durations never match perfectly. We stress-test it against ±2% rate moves and curve twists. If the funding ratio falls below 105%, surplus assets are transferred into the hedge before anything else happens.

5. The surplus portfolio
Assets above the hedge form the surplus: 80% VT (global equities) and 20% IEF for stability and rebalancing. The 2028 contribution first restores the funding ratio if needed; the rest joins the surplus.

6. Evolution over time
Each year the payments come one year closer, so the liability's duration shortens. At each annual rebalance we shift the hedge from TLT toward IEF and SHY to keep durations aligned. In January 2033 the hedge becomes the operating reserve. From then on we keep immunising the remaining payments, moving the reserve fully into SHY and cash-like Treasuries for the next two payments, so that payments due within two years never depend on bond prices.

7. Facility contribution and 2031 range
The facility contribution comes only from the surplus. The 2031 range will be derived from the distribution of possible surplus values in 2033, with the lower end set at a level reached in at least 90% of scenarios and the funding ratio still at 105%. Laura should keep a part of the surplus as her own flexibility reserve rather than commit it all.

8. Governance
Annual review every January: recompute liability PV and duration, rebalance the hedge, check the funding ratio, rebalance the surplus. No discretionary changes between reviews.

8. Why this suits Laura
Laura studied statistics at Wharton. She will recognise a method that measures what she owes before deciding what she can give.

=== ENTRY 13 ===
ELEVATOR PITCH:
Caplet XR, extended release: Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are debts, so we pay them with debts owed to her – Treasuries maturing before each January, bought before any shares. Her facility gift is a hope, so world stocks decide how far it rises above a floor she already owns.

INVESTMENT POLICY STATEMENT:
Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them.

They are $50,000 each, every January from 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars, and nobody else may help pay them – questions for a contract, not a forecast. Each is answered by a Treasury bond, or an iBond Treasury fund that turns itself into cash, maturing before its January. We call the plan Caplet XR. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush. To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten holdings, each redeemed in time for one January.

The sceptic says stocks beat bonds over most decades, Laura has a decade, so why pay for certainty? Fair enough. But it imagines one ten-year bill, when Laura has ten one-year bills, each due whatever the market did last year, and while a withdrawal of a fixed percentage can ride out a crash, a withdrawal of fixed dollars is a forced sale at the bottom.

A quieter reason to buy the cheques first: Laura already owns plenty of stock-market risk, because her 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – income that shrinks in the same slump that makes bonds dearer. So her first $300,000 buys the ladder in January 2027, and the second deposit fills any gap before it buys anything else. That is our high degree of certainty: we can name exactly where it breaks – yields falling more than two percentage points before the purchase while her 2028 deposit more than halves.

Whatever that deposit leaves becomes the facility gift, built like a bank's capital-protected note with the bank taken out. Most of it buys more IBTM, the fund that pays the first cheque, sized to grow back to the whole remainder by December 2032: the floor. The rest – roughly the interest the floor will earn – buys VT, a world index fund.

In 2031 Laura names a range, and a range can embarrass its owner in two ways: too low and co-sponsors shrug, too high and she breaks her word. We judge the second about three times worse. So the bottom is the floor she owns, rounded down, and the top adds half the stocks, which it reaches whenever stocks are no lower in 2033 than in 2031 – about three times in four.

The one soft spot: five long bonds reinvest their coupons, so lower yields leave a small gap. So in January 2033 the reserve changes once, setting any such gap aside in Treasury bills from Laura's half; after that it changes itself.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more. It can hurt the building, though, as can the Taiwan dollar – which is why half the stocks stay Laura's, sellable any day, the project's second cheque. If anything else changes: cheques first, then the floor and the stocks shrink together.

Do not crush.
