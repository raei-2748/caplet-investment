## 2. Entry by entry

**Entry 01**
- Remember: "We are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question." Also: "She takes risks with ideas, not with promises to other people."
- Strength: the cleanest complete plan in the pile: a matched ladder (iBond funds for the near rungs, bonds for the far ones), a 2032 Treasury floor for the gift, one world fund left alone, and an inflation paragraph that knows which risk belongs to which pot.
- Weakness: it is the archetype the other promise-first entries copy. Correct and calm, but the voice is a well-run template, and "a share of VT's value" leaves the top of the range undefined.
- Advance: maybe (high maybe)

**Entry 02**
- Remember: nothing (the "Safety Bucket / Growth Bucket" label is the most common one there is).
- Strength: it separates willingness from capacity and defines risk as "missing a payment or overpromising", which is the right way to define it.
- Weakness: a textbook two-bucket IPS with bold headings, a constraints list that repeats the rulebook and "rebalance annually back to target weights" with no targets. Nothing in it is wrong, and nothing is theirs.
- Advance: no

**Entry 03**
- Remember: "a crash in the gutter changes the facility, never the commitment" (and the "bleed" as the overrun margin).
- Strength: the comic-book metaphor is tied to the client and does real work: the gutter as 2028-31, pencils as the 2031 range, the bleed as the flexibility margin. A 2031 range bottom of "locked value plus the rest after a severe fall" is sensible.
- Weakness: a 15% single-country slice (EWT) is a big concentration for a "small" slice, and its hedge logic (Taiwan facility costs) is asserted, not argued. The chapter headings eat words that analysis needed. It is almost the same as 07.
- Advance: no

**Entry 04**
- Remember: "Gambling money already spoken for would be risk without reward."
- Strength: technically the tidiest of the generic set. It has a clear payout rule (floor plus at most half the growth portfolio, capped at the announced top), a 5-point rebalancing band, and states its assumptions.
- Weakness: "Promise First, Then Grow" is the field's default slogan. It ticks all five Wharton points but never says anything only this team would say.
- Advance: no

**Entry 05**
- Remember: "A fixed nominal payment on a known date is, financially, a bond Laura has already sold." "No stocks, no forecasts, no hope required."
- Strength: the most courageous call in the field. It goes all Treasuries, defends that with real knowledge (LDI, the 2022 gilt crisis, the S&P 500's lost decade 2000-09), and owns the cost plainly: "Good markets will not raise it, and we say so plainly."
- Weakness: it sacrifices growth for a client who is willing to take thoughtful risk and whose facility costs will rise with inflation. It leaves that risk "visible" instead of managing it, so W3 (balance) and flexibility are thin. On the word count, 502 by a crude count.
- Advance: maybe

**Entry 06**
- Remember: "a committed lead gift ... a matching pledge", which turns market uncertainty "into a reason to give rather than a reason to hesitate."
- Strength: it is the only entry that thinks about the co-sponsors as people. Using the seed-money and matching research to shape the 2031 range is genuinely original and client-centred.
- Weakness: the money engine does not match the idea. Until 2031 the reserve sits in IEF, whose duration is shorter than the liability's, so a large fall in rates opens a gap, contrary to the claim of "even in ... a large fall in interest rates". High certainty is also only "97% in our simulations". And the match is conditional on co-sponsors, not on markets, so letting it "shrink" in a bad market is still a broken promise. The SCHD tilt is decoration.
- Advance: maybe

**Entry 07**
- Remember: "We never sell ink to buy pencil."
- Strength: a gradual one-third-a-year lock-in from 2031 gives an honest, rule-based route from range to commitment.
- Weakness: "The 2027 deposit inks as many panels as it can while leaving room to grow" contradicts its own promise-first thesis, because it leaves rungs unbought, and exposed to falling yields, so it can buy stocks a year early. It is a near-twin of 03.
- Advance: no

**Entry 08**
- Remember: "a bad market can shrink the garden, never the house."
- Strength: it is the most honest about the trade-off ("if stocks fall hard just before 2033 ... co-sponsors may need to cover more"). It also names liquidity, which most entries skip, and notes coupon reinvestment risk.
- Weakness: a house-and-garden metaphor any adviser would use; the strategy itself is the standard one.
- Advance: no

**Entry 09**
- Remember: "Data, not guesses", for the wrong reason.
- Strength: it states its capital-market assumptions explicitly and admits that lognormal models understate crashes.
- Weakness: several errors. (a) A reserve sized to fund all payments in "95% of simulated futures" is not a high degree of certainty when a matched Treasury ladder gives near-100%. (b) It says the risky reserve "requires about $445,000", which is more than a fully matched Treasury ladder would cost in 2033, so the equity slice makes the reserve dearer and riskier at once. (c) "No other mix in our model offers more return per unit of risk" is false under a 60% equity cap; a capped portfolio is not the tangency portfolio. (d) Nothing is locked against rates until 2031. Laura's statistics degree is used as decoration. The jargon in the pitch is a red flag for a template.
- Advance: no

**Entry 10**
- Remember: "Investors never fund a startup's full plan on day one; they fund milestones."
- Strength: staged tranches with gates are a real-options view of the facility gift. It fits an entrepreneur and gives a decision framework (W4) beyond 2033.
- Weakness: 25% QQQ is justified by the fact that "Laura's own career sits in technology and creative industries", which is exactly why it should not be held, because it doubles her human-capital risk. "Innovation-driven companies have led" is performance chasing. Gate 3 (2034-35) may sit awkwardly with a 2033 contribution that co-sponsors are counting on.
- Advance: no (a maybe if the QQQ logic were fixed)

**Entry 11**
- Remember: "stock exposure = 3 × cushion".
- Strength: CPPI is a real, rule-based framework, and the entry explains it without derivatives.
- Weakness: at 2027 yields it says the floor is "close to $300,000", against a $300,000 portfolio, so the cushion is about zero and the rule holds almost no stocks until 2028. The entry never notices this. It also ignores CPPI's cash-lock problem, and the floor is tracked with constant-duration ETFs rather than locked with maturities until 2033. A quarterly rebalance gap is defended by a loose historical claim. In short, it chases sophistication at the expense of certainty.
- Advance: no

**Entry 12**
- Remember: nothing beyond "funding ratio of at least 105%".
- Strength: it understands LDI properly: PV, duration matching, a funding-ratio trigger and an annual governance cycle.
- Weakness: immunisation with ETFs that never mature is approximate (curve twists, a once-a-year rebalance). A cash-flow-matched ladder is simpler and surer, and the entry never says why it chose the harder tool. It reads like a CFA Level III answer with Laura's name added. Section "8." appears twice, which looks sloppy.
- Advance: no

**Entry 13**
- Remember: "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." Also: "Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more."
- Strength: depth of thinking that no other entry has. (1) It meets the sceptic head-on: "ten one-year bills", where a fixed-dollar withdrawal becomes a forced sale at the bottom (sequence risk). (2) It sees that her 2028 deposit is correlated with markets (income that shrinks in the same slump). (3) It builds the gift as a capital-protected note "with the bank taken out", with an asymmetric-loss rule for the range ("three times worse") and a stated hit rate. (4) It names exactly where certainty breaks and its one soft spot (coupon reinvestment). This is a voice I would back.
- Weakness: it is dense, and in places cryptic. "X is ten and R is redemption" plus "Do not crush" spend words on branding. The break-point sentence ("yields falling ... before the purchase") does not say which purchase. It names tickers (IBTM, VT) where Wharton asks for strategy over individual investments. Diversification in the growth piece is a single world fund, and the stock slice (about the floor's interest) is small, so the upside is modest; that is defended, but only implicitly.
- Advance: yes

## 3. Sameness

Entries 01, 02, 04, 07 and 08 (and to a lesser degree 03) are interchangeable. They all say the same thing: "two jobs; payments
are a promise, the facility is a hope; buy Treasuries maturing just before each payment and hold them to maturity;
high certainty = only a US default can break it; the 2028 deposit fills any gap first; everything else goes into
diversified global ETFs rebalanced annually; in 2031 lock part into short Treasuries as the floor of the range, with the
rest as the top; keep some back for flexibility; review each January." They differ only in the metaphor laid over it
(buckets, ink/pencil twice, a house/garden, a promise/vision). Two of them (03 and 07) even chose the same graphic-novel metaphor. A second, smaller cluster
(09, 11, 12) swaps the metaphor for a named quant technique (Monte Carlo, CPPI, immunisation) and claims a
statistics degree as the reason; each makes certainty weaker than the plain ladder it competes with.

## 4. Top 3

1. **Entry 13**: it is the only entry that argues with an opponent, and it wins. It sees two risks nobody else saw
   (sequence risk on fixed-dollar withdrawals; the 2028 deposit falling in the same slump that makes bonds dearer), it
   prices the 2031 range by the asymmetry of embarrassment, and it says where it breaks. An hour later I still
   remember "we would rather buy them" and the inflation line. Sharp people, not a template.
2. **Entry 01**: the ladder plan done best. Each piece has a reason, the definition of certainty ("removing the market
   from the question") is crisp, and it lays out the 2033 path without a wasted word. It loses to 13 only on originality
   and voice.
3. **Entry 05**: the conviction of going all Treasuries, defended with real market history and an honest statement
   of what it gives up. It is the one entry that took a position most teams would be afraid to take. It ranks below 01
   because it under-serves growth and inflation in the facility costs.

(Close fourth: 06, for the lead-gift/matching-pledge insight, which is the most original client idea in the field;
it is held back by a reserve that is not actually matched until 2031.)

## 5. What the winning IPS would have to do

The winner would combine 13's thinking with 01's clarity and 06's understanding of the co-sponsors. It would make one
idea the whole document, and make it plain enough to repeat after one read. It would prove that idea against the
strongest objection, and still read as if six sharp teenagers wrote it, not a pension consultant or a copywriter. None of these
entries gives a clear, plain-English rule for how the 2031 range is set, one that a co-sponsor could follow and that
ties the confidence to the money already owned. None says what Laura should do if the facility costs more than planned
or the 2028 deposit arrives short, as a decision rule rather than a sentiment. And none shows, even in one sentence,
how the team reached its call (what it tested, what it threw out), which is what the Articulation criterion is looking
for. Entry 13 comes closest; the winner would keep its depth and drop its puzzles.
