## 2. Entry by entry

**Entry A**
- Line I remember: "We are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question."
- Biggest strength: it is the clearest liability-matching plan in the field. Each payment has a rung, the ladder becomes
  the reserve on its own, and the range runs from a locked minimum to that minimum plus a share of VT. Every one of
  Wharton's points gets a plain answer.
- Biggest weakness or error: "The only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution" is false.
  Coupon bonds held to 2037-2042 carry reinvestment risk on their coupons, and an iBond ETF does not pay a "known
  value" at liquidation. Client fit is one closing line, and the entry shows no decision process.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? Maybe.

**Entry B**
- Line I remember: "it imagines one ten-year bill, when Laura has ten one-year bills, each due whatever the market did
  last year."
- Biggest strength: real asset-liability reasoning. It covers sequence risk for fixed-dollar withdrawals, the link
  between her 2028 income and the market, a named breaking point ("yields falling more than two percentage points ...
  while her 2028 deposit more than halves"), and the facility gift built as a capital-protected note. It also states
  its own soft spot, coupon reinvestment, and gives the fix. This is the only entry besides C that knows where its
  ladder leaks.
- Biggest weakness or error: too many metaphors (pharmacist XR, cheques as debts, a bank note "with the bank taken
  out", "Do not crush"), plus an unexplained ticker (IBTM). A lay judge would lose the thread. It never says how it
  reached its view, and how the reserve is managed after 2033 gets only one sentence.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? Yes.

**Entry C**
- Line I remember: "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or
  fall as one."
- Biggest strength: one rule ("each promise is made safe by the day it becomes one") runs the whole plan. It funds the
  ladder in 2027, sets the floor in 2028 and locks the named gift in 2031. The rule also finds a flaw I would have
  marked down in B: a capped range keeps every fall and gives every rise to Laura, in effect a short option. It
  answers the diversification criterion directly instead of avoiding it, and it shows a team changing its mind ("Our
  first plan put three-quarters in stocks").
- Biggest weakness or error: dense and full of aphorisms. "Fixed promises have failed in four ways" is pension and
  insurance history that only a specialist will decode. "On its birthday" and the "do not crush" ending, which C never
  set up, are unclear. "Every coupon buys more of the bond that paid it" is a policy, not a cure for reinvestment risk.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? Yes.

**Entry D**
- Line I remember: "Does it protect the promise before it pursues the upside?"
- Biggest strength: complete and well organised. It covers liquidity, inflation, the reserve after 2033, flexibility,
  a scheduled glide path and assumptions, each under its own heading.
- Biggest weakness or error: "matching them with fixed Treasury cash flows removes market, interest-rate and
  reinvestment risk, leaving only U.S. government default risk" is false. Coupon bonds do not remove reinvestment risk,
  and without STRIPS that risk cannot be fully removed. The "Three Acts" framing is decoration, and the client
  reasoning is generic ("entrepreneurial income and willingness to take thoughtful risks").
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? No.

**Entry E**
- Line I remember: "Bonds pay the promise. Stocks drive growth, and they never stand behind a payment."
- Biggest strength: the facility rule is cautious and testable. The low end is the surplus after a 30% fall that does
  not recover, half the surplus is locked in 2031, and the ladder is checked every year with any gap closed before the
  facility gets money.
- Biggest weakness or error: "never commits the last $100,000 of her surplus" is a number with no reasoning behind it,
  and on a surplus of about $150k it could leave the facility almost nothing. Inflation, the Taiwan dollar and
  reinvestment risk are missing, "a promise that markets cannot break" overclaims, and the entry shows no thinking
  process.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? No.

## 3. Sameness

A, D and E could be swapped for one another, and B and C share their skeleton. All five say the same thing: buy a
Treasury ladder that matures just before each of the ten January payments, fill any gap with the 2028 deposit, put the
surplus in a broad stock fund, and build the 2031 range from a locked floor plus part of the equity upside, so that
"the contribution shrinks, the payments never do". Each of A, D and E then adds a label ("Three Acts", "Ten
Lanterns") or a heading list. B and C differ in two ways: they explain why the ladder comes first (sequence risk, the
link between her income and the market) and they name where it can fail. B and C are close drafts of each other: same
opening line, same XR passage, same sentences on inflation and the "second cheque".

## 4. Top 3

1. **C.** It is the only entry whose principle produces new decisions rather than restating the ladder. Applied in
   2031, "safe by the day it becomes a promise" exposes the hidden short option in a capped range and fixes it. It also
   handles diversification by argument (correlated sovereign debt against independent 99% bets) and shows a team
   learning from a failed first model. That is the asset-liability insight I would want from a graduate student.
2. **B.** Same core design, with the sharpest single argument in the field: ten one-year bills, not one ten-year bill.
   Its breaking point is stated precisely enough to check. It ranks below C because it stops at the capped range
   without seeing the range's flaw, and because the metaphors and jargon weigh more on the reasoning.
3. **A.** Plain, correct in structure and easy to follow. It defines "high certainty" in operational terms and keeps
   the range honest. It ranks above D and E because its design is coherent and its range is tied to assets already
   owned. It ranks below B and C because it claims the ladder has no risk except default.

## 5. What the winning IPS would have to do

The winning IPS would keep C's depth and say it in A's plain language. It would show one quantified trade-off, not
just assert it. For example: what Laura gives up in expected facility money by buying the ladder before buying stocks,
and why that price is worth paying for this client. It would also turn its rules into a short decision map that a
co-sponsor could follow (if yields fall X, do Y; if the 2028 deposit is late or small, do Z). None of these entries
prices its own caution. Each one says what it protects, and none says what that protection costs Laura.

## 6. Problem sentences in the top two entries

**Entry C (rank 1)**
- "Caplet XR protects each promise by the day it is made." Unclear. "By the day" could mean "on" or "no later than",
  and a reader cannot tell yet what "made" refers to.
- "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten" is true only if the bets are independent (1 - 0.99^10
  = 9.6%), and the sentence does not say so. The 99% figure has no source.
- "Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, options given away free, returns assumed
  instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell." Unclear to a general judge because no example is named.
  The rhythm of four parallel phrases also reads as machine-written.
- "Ours shuts the first three." This overclaims. The 2031 gift range still gives away an option until C's own fix in
  the later paragraph.
- "So every coupon buys more of the bond that paid it". A rule, not a fix: those coupons are still reinvested at
  whatever yields hold by then, so reinvestment risk stays, as the next clause admits.
- "Keep the seed; gamble the harvest." "Gamble" is the wrong word to put in front of a client and co-sponsors. It reads
  as a slogan writer's line.
- "So on its birthday the promised half moves into Treasuries maturing before 2033." Unclear: "its birthday" means the
  day the range is named in 2031, but the reader has to work that out.
- "No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush." Unclear. C never says what "do not crush" refers to, because it
  dropped the "labelled do not crush" setup that B has.
- "The residency gets ten Januaries for certain". This overclaims: elsewhere the entry says coupon reinvestment is a
  soft spot, so the payments are not certain.
- Machine-written feel across the entry: en-dash asides, short sentences that turn on a contrast ("Until then it is a
  hope, and may carry risk."; "Inflation can make $50,000 buy less; it cannot make $50,000 cost more."), and an
  aphorism in almost every paragraph.

**Entry B (rank 2)**
- "Caplet XR, extended release: ... debts owed to her". The pitch spends its first words on a name and a drug
  metaphor, not on the client. "Extended release" is unclear to anyone outside pharmacy until the IPS explains it.
- "– questions for a contract, not a forecast." A fragment, and "questions" is unclear: it means the payments are
  questions that a contract answers.
- "But it imagines one ten-year bill, when Laura has ten one-year bills, ... and while a withdrawal of a fixed
  percentage can ride out a crash, a withdrawal of fixed dollars is a forced sale at the bottom." Too long: two
  arguments joined by "and while". The second half also applies only to a portfolio that holds stocks, which the
  sentence does not say.
- "income that shrinks in the same slump that makes bonds dearer." True only on average (bonds usually rally in
  recessions). It is stated as a certainty.
- "we can name exactly where it breaks – yields falling more than two percentage points before the purchase while her
  2028 deposit more than halves." Unclear: "before the purchase" sounds like before January 2027, but the risk is the
  price at the purchase date. "Exactly" overclaims for a modelled threshold.
- "Most of it buys more IBTM, the fund that pays the first cheque, sized to grow back to the whole remainder by
  December 2032". This uses an unexplained ticker, and "grow back to" is not exact: an iBond ETF's payout at
  liquidation depends on its holdings and on the yields at which it reinvests, so it is not a fixed amount.
- "the top adds half the stocks, which it reaches whenever stocks are no lower in 2033 than in 2031 – about three times
  in four." The probability has no stated basis. It also misses that, once the range is named, the capped gift absorbs
  every fall (the flaw C fixes).
- "Do not crush." Reads as a gimmick and leaves the judge without a final statement of what the plan does.
- Machine-written feel: the opening "Every team will promise ... We would rather buy them." and the stacked metaphors
  read as built for effect. "Fair enough." is a common machine move: concede, then turn the argument.
