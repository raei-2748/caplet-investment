## 2. Entry by entry

**Entry A**
- What I remember: "Bonds pay the promise. Stocks drive growth, and they never stand behind a payment."
- Biggest strength: a clean, correct separation of the payments from market risk. The ladder is funded before any stock is bought, and it is checked every year.
- Biggest weakness: the range logic contradicts itself. Section 5 defines a high end and then says "We never promise above the low end", so the high end has no job. The 30% stress rule is also unrelated to the 2031 half-surplus switch in section 4. The fixed "$100,000" flexibility reserve is arbitrary and never justified. Client fit in section 6 is a paraphrase of the case.
- Advance to Top 50 of 6,000? **No.**

**Entry B**
- What I remember: "Inflation can make $50,000 buy less. It cannot make $50,000 cost more."
- Biggest strength: real financial reasoning, applied to the client. It argues that fixed-dollar withdrawals turn a crash into a forced sale. It shows that Laura's 2028 income is correlated with markets. It frames the gift as a capital-protected note minus the bank. It names the exact break point and weighs the cost of an overpromise against an underpromise.
- Biggest weakness: density and private shorthand get in the way of clarity. A judge has to decode "IBTM", "five long bonds reinvest their coupons", "after that it changes itself", "the project's second cheque" and "Do not crush." It also names a ticker, although the instructions ask for strategy over individual investments. The claim that an overpromise is "about three times worse" is asserted with no basis.
- Advance to Top 50 of 6,000? **Maybe** (leaning yes).

**Entry C**
- What I remember: "She takes risks with ideas, not with promises to other people."
- Biggest strength: the clearest and most credible entry. It defines "high certainty" operationally ("does not depend on any forecast"), builds a sensible floor-plus-upside range, and is honest that the likelihood of the top comes later.
- Biggest weakness: it overclaims. "The only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution" is not true. The coupons on individual bonds have to be reinvested or held, and an iBond ETF's payout at maturity is not a fixed amount. "Every rung pays its known value whatever happens to... interest rates" has the same problem. The flexibility section ("Part of the VT holding") is vague. Nothing protects the named range between 2031 and 2033.
- Advance to Top 50 of 6,000? **Maybe.**

**Entry D**
- What I remember: "So the money she has buys the certainty, and the money she expects carries the risk." (Runner-up: "Keep the seed; gamble the harvest.")
- Biggest strength: one governing rule ("each promise is made safe by the day it becomes one") that decides every stage. That covers the cheques in 2027, the floor in 2028 and the named gift in 2031. It also gives the sharpest insight in the field: a capped range gives every fall to the gift and every rise to Laura, so it is locked once it is named. It shows how the team's thinking changed ("Our first plan put three-quarters in stocks..."), and it reasons about correlation ("ten debts of one government stand or fall as one").
- Biggest weakness: too many metaphors (pharmacist, seed and harvest, birthday, "do not crush") and some undefined shorthand ("Laura's half", "options given away free", "on its birthday"). There is also a logic gap. If the promised top is locked on the day it is named, the entry does not say why a range, not a single figure, is still announced.
- Advance to Top 50 of 6,000? **Yes.**

**Entry E**
- What I remember: nothing beyond the "Three Acts" label.
- Biggest strength: it covers every Wharton point in order: certainty, liquidity, inflation, glide path and flexibility.
- Biggest weakness: a false claim. Matching with coupon Treasuries "removes market, interest-rate and reinvestment risk". It does not remove reinvestment risk, because coupons arrive years before the payments. The acts also overlap in time (Act Two 2028-2031, Act Three 2029-2033). The writing is template-like ("non-negotiable", "Every decision faces one question").
- Advance to Top 50 of 6,000? **No.**

## 3. Sameness

- A, C and E are interchangeable in substance. Each says: buy a Treasury ladder that matures before each payment, put the surplus in a broad stock fund, define certainty as "held to maturity, not forecast", and treat the facility as surplus only. A, C and E then each add one branding device (Ten Lanterns, plain headings, Three Acts). Remove the label and A and E read as the same document.
- B and D are near-duplicates of each other. They share the opener ("Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them."), the XR pharmacist metaphor, the 2028-income correlation argument, the "three times worse" weighting, the inflation line, "the project's second cheque" and "do not crush". A judge reading both would suspect the same author. D is the better developed version.
- All five say the payments come first and that the facility gets only what is left over.

## 4. Top 3

1. **D (81).** It is the only entry with a single principle that drives every decision across the horizons. It finds and fixes a real flaw in its own design: a named, capped range carries all the downside and none of the upside, so it is locked once named. It also shows a decision-making process (a rejected 75%-stock first plan, and the four ways fixed promises have historically failed). Those are the two things the rubric rewards most and the field mostly lacks.
2. **B (75).** It uses the most sophisticated tools: sequence risk on fixed-dollar withdrawals, Laura's income correlated with markets, the gift built like a capital-protected note, an exact break point, and a stated probability for the top of the range. It ranks below D because it is harder to read and has no articulation of process.
3. **C (69).** The most credible plain-English plan, with the best client line in the field. It ranks below B and D because its risk analysis overclaims (default and execution as the "only" risks), and it neither protects the range after 2031 nor sizes the flexibility.

## 5. What the winning IPS would have to do

The winner would need D's governing principle and B's analytical depth, written with C's clarity. That means one idea a judge can repeat after a single reading, and no private shorthand or ticker decoding. It must state its residual risks honestly and precisely. In particular, it must say what coupon reinvestment and fund payouts leave uncertain, not claim that only default remains. It must explain why a range is still the right thing to announce once part of it is locked, and give the flexibility reserve a reason, not just a number. Above all, none of these entries uses Laura herself as the source of the strategy: her career, her books, what the residency is for. A winning IPS would make the reader feel that this plan could only have been written for her.

## 6. Top two entries: sentences that are false, unclear or read as machine-written

### Entry D (rank 1)
- "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one." The arithmetic is right (1 - 0.99^10 is about 9.6%), but the sentence is unclear. "99% bets" is never defined, so a reader cannot tell whether it is a criticism of diversifying or a defence of not diversifying until the second clause. The phrase "we do not diversify the cheques" also invites a judge to mark the entry down on the "diversification" criterion unless it is explained.
- "Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, options given away free, returns assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell." The list is unclear. "Options given away free" is jargon a non-specialist judge cannot decode, and no example is given. The neat four-item rhetorical list reads as machine-written.
- "The fourth is our soft spot, so every coupon buys more of the bond that paid it." This is misleading as a remedy. Reinvesting coupons into the same bond at lower yields is exactly the risk just named, so it does not close the gap. Only the 2033 cushion does.
- "...from leftover cash and Laura's half first." Unclear. "Laura's half" is used before it is defined (it is only explained later as the half of the stocks kept outside the gift).
- "So on its birthday the promised half moves into Treasuries maturing before 2033." Unclear. "Its birthday" means the day the range is named in 2031, but a reader has to work that out. The entry also leaves open why the top of the range is still uncertain once it is locked.
- "Keep the seed; gamble the harvest." Memorable, but "gamble" is a poor word for a client's charitable money in front of a judge looking for responsibility.
- "Then the part that sounds wrong:" / "A quieter reason to go first:" These set-up phrases read as machine-written.
- "No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush." Unclear. The "do not crush" callback only works if the reader kept the pharmacist image from four paragraphs earlier. Taken alone, it is opaque.

### Entry B (rank 2)
- "Each is answered by a Treasury bond, or an iBond Treasury fund that turns itself into cash, maturing before its January." Unclear for a lay judge. "iBond" can be confused with US Series I savings bonds (inflation-linked), which are a different product. The term needs one clarifying word.
- "...we can name exactly where it breaks – yields falling more than two percentage points before the purchase while her 2028 deposit more than halves." Unclear. "Before the purchase" is ambiguous (the 2027 purchase? the 2028 top-up?). "Exactly" overstates a model result.
- "Most of it buys more IBTM, the fund that pays the first cheque..." This names a ticker with no explanation of what it is. The instructions ask teams to focus on strategy, not individual investments.
- "We judge the second about three times worse." Asserted, never supported. It reads as a precise number with no source.
- "...which it reaches whenever stocks are no lower in 2033 than in 2031 – about three times in four." The probability is plausible for a two-year equity horizon, but it is a model output stated as a fact, with no assumption given.
- "The one soft spot: five long bonds reinvest their coupons, so lower yields leave a small gap." Unclear. "Five long bonds" appears for the first time here, and the size of the "small gap" is not given.
- "...after that it changes itself." Unclear. It means the ladder shortens as bonds mature, but it reads as if the portfolio rebalances itself.
- "...which is why half the stocks stay Laura's, sellable any day, the project's second cheque." Unclear metaphor. The project receives no "first cheque" from Laura's ten payments; those go to the residency's operations.
- "Do not crush." As a closing line it is opaque, and it reads as a stylistic flourish rather than content.
- "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." A strong line, but the same opener appears in entry D, which suggests template or machine origin.
