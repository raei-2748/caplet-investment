## 2. Entry by entry

**Entry A**
- Line I remember: "We are not quoting a model's probability; we are removing the market from the question."
- Biggest strength: the cleanest and most correct build in the file. The ladder, the 2032 Treasury floor, VT and a
  reserve that "shortens by itself" fit together with nothing wasted, and a fiduciary could run this tomorrow.
- Biggest weakness: there is no edge. Any strong team could have written it. The client section is two lines, and
  "only remaining risks are a US government default and our own execution" ignores the reinvestment risk on the
  coupons of the 2037-2042 bonds. "With 2027 Treasury yields around 4-5%" states a future rate as if it were known.
- Advance to the Top 50 of 6,000? **Maybe** (yes if the Final Report is strong).

**Entry B**
- Line I remember: "Stocks drive growth, and they never stand behind a payment."
- Biggest strength: the facility rule is honest. The low end of the range is what survives a 30% fall that never
  recovers, and the team never promises above it.
- Biggest weakness: the numbers are arbitrary and the parts pull against each other. A fixed "last $100,000" reserve
  could take most of a surplus that may itself be only about $150k. The 2031 half-lock and the "never promise above the
  low end" rule overlap without a reason. The "Ten Lanterns" name decorates the plan and does no work in it.
- Advance? **No.**

**Entry C**
- Line I remember: "Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them." A close second is "a
  bank's capital-protected note with the bank taken out".
- Biggest strength: real investment ideas, applied to her case. Sequence risk on fixed-dollar withdrawals, the 2028
  deposit as human capital that moves with the market, the facility built as a principal-protected note (floor plus the
  floor's expected interest in stocks), and a stated breaking point. This is the entry I would back.
- Biggest weakness: the strategy leaves gaps. Liquidity and diversification are barely addressed, the gift between
  2031 and 2033 stays exposed to stocks once the range is named, and the pharmacy device ("labelled do not crush" /
  "Do not crush.") takes words away from the strategy. "IBTM" is named with no explanation.
- Advance? **Yes.**

**Entry E**
- Line I remember: "Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or
  fall as one."
- Biggest strength: one governing rule ("each promise is made safe by the day it becomes one") drives every date:
  2027, 2028, 2031 and 2033. It finds a real flaw in its own capped range and fixes it ("the gift keeps every fall and
  hands every rise to Laura"). It also shows a decision process ("Our first plan put three-quarters in stocks, and the
  model had it missing cheques").
- Biggest weakness: too many aphorisms per inch ("Keep the seed; gamble the harvest", "on its birthday", "do not
  crush"). Some lines are too compressed to follow on one read, above all "options given away free". Locking the
  promised half in 2031 also makes the top near-certain, which weakens the point of naming a range at all.
- Advance? **Yes.**

**Entry D**
- Line I remember: nothing. The "Three Acts" frame is a label, not an idea.
- Biggest strength: it covers everything Wharton asks for, with tidy headings, a liquidity line and an
  inflation line.
- Biggest weakness: one claim is false and the rest is generic. It says bonds "whose maturities and coupons match the
  ten payments" and that the ladder "removes market, interest-rate and reinvestment risk". Coupons paid before 2033
  must be reinvested at future rates, so reinvestment risk remains. Phrases such as "Like a good story" and "never in
  reaction to headlines" could have come from any of the 6,000 teams.
- Advance? **No.**

## 3. Sameness

A, B and D are interchangeable. All three say: (1) buy a Treasury ladder maturing before each payment and hold it to
maturity; (2) define certainty as "bought, not forecast"; (3) put the surplus in one or more broad equity funds;
(4) the ladder becomes the operating reserve in 2033 and shortens naturally; (5) bad markets shrink the facility, never
the payments; (6) inflation risk sits with the stocks. They differ only in their labels (no name, "Ten Lanterns",
"Three Acts"). C and E share a skeleton, a voice and even an opening line ("Every team will promise Laura her ten
cheques. We would rather buy them."), so they read as two drafts from the same team. Across the whole field, every
entry funds the payments with Treasuries held to maturity, which is the correct answer and no longer a differentiator.

## 4. Top 3

1. **E (85).** It is the only entry with one rule that decides every future date, and the only one that spots the
   asymmetry of a capped range (once named, the gift keeps every loss and gives every gain to Laura) and then
   designs around it. It shows how the team learned (the 75%-stock first plan failed in the model). It also turns the
   rubric's "diversification" into an argued position: diversifying ten debts of one government buys nothing.
2. **C (79).** It has the strongest pure investment thinking: the fixed-dollar versus fixed-percentage withdrawal
   argument, the correlation of her 2028 income with the market, the capital-protected-note framing of the gift, and a
   named breaking point. It falls behind E because it never closes the 2031-2033 exposure and covers W3 and W4 more
   thinly.
3. **A (70).** It is correct, calm and complete, and it scores above B and D because it has no false claims and its
   2031 floor is real (a Treasury maturing in late 2032). What it lacks is anything a judge would stop and reread.

## 5. What the winning IPS would have to do

The winner would take E's architecture and spend its words on judgement instead of epigrams. Its range would make an
honest trade-off visible: the confidence attached to each end, and why locking in 2031 is still worth keeping a top
that can be missed. It would quantify its one soft spot (the coupon-reinvestment gap) in plain words, as C does with
its breaking point. It would give liquidity and flexibility a clear rule (how much stays Laura's, sellable, and for
what) instead of one clause. And it would read as if six teenagers had argued it out and a client could repeat it
back: one idea, one rule, one memorable image, and every other sentence doing work. None of these five combines
E's foresight, C's investing depth and A's plainness.

## 6. Top two entries: sentences that are false, unclear or read as machine-written

### Entry E (rank 1)
- "Caplet XR protects each promise by the day it is made." (pitch). **Unclear.** "By the day it is made" can be
  read as a deadline or as "day by day". The IPS's own wording ("made safe by the day it becomes one") is clearer.
- "To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule." **Reads as machine-written.** It is a
  decorative analogy that spends 15+ words of a 500-word limit, and the X/R decoding that follows is wordplay, not
  strategy.
- "Then the part that sounds wrong: we do not diversify the cheques." **Reads as machine-written** (the "the part
  that sounds wrong:" setup is a stock rhetorical device). It is also **risky with a Wharton rubric** that lists
  diversification. The argument is right, but it needs one plain clause saying the growth sleeve is diversified.
- "Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, options given away free, returns
  assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell." **Unclear.** "Options given away free" means
  nothing to a reader without a finance background or context, and the list format reads as generated.
  "Ours shuts the first three" is asserted, not shown.
- "every coupon buys more of the bond that paid it". **Partly misleading.** Reinvesting coupons into the same bond
  still reinvests at future yields, so this practice does not remove the soft spot it sits beside. It also does not
  apply to the iBond fund rungs, which distribute income.
- "Keep the seed; gamble the harvest." **Machine-written aphorism, and the wrong word.** A fiduciary should not call
  any part of a client's plan a "gamble".
- "But a capped range hides a flaw: once named, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. So on its
  birthday the promised half moves into Treasuries maturing before 2033." **"On its birthday" is unclear** (it means
  the day the range is named). The logic is also **incomplete**: if the top is locked that day, the range collapses
  to roughly one number, and the entry never says what confidence the range then carries or why a range is still
  needed.
- "No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush." **Reads as machine-written**: it is a callback flourish to
  close the piece.
- "a building built on money Laura already owns". **Slightly false**: the floor and the stocks are bought with the
  2028 deposit, which in 2027 she does not yet own. The entry itself calls it "the money she expects".

### Entry C (rank 2)
- "Caplet XR, extended release: Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are debts, so we pay them with debts owed to her".
  The second half is strong. "extended release" in the opening clause **is unclear** before the analogy has been
  explained.
- "questions for a contract, not a forecast". **A fragment that reads as machine-written**, and its subject is unclear.
- "To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush." **Decorative and
  machine-written**: it uses words on the device, not the strategy. "Do not crush." as the final line repeats it
  without content.
- "The sceptic says stocks beat bonds over most decades ... Fair enough." **"Fair enough" reads as a generated
  concession tic.** The argument after it is excellent.
- "we can name exactly where it breaks – yields falling more than two percentage points before the purchase while
  her 2028 deposit more than halves." **Unclear.** "Before the purchase" leaves open which purchase (January 2027 or
  2028). The "exactly" is not supported in the text: the IPS gives no reasoning for these thresholds.
- "Whatever that deposit leaves becomes the facility gift". **Inconsistent** with the later "half the stocks stay
  Laura's". Not everything left over becomes the gift.
- "Most of it buys more IBTM, the fund that pays the first cheque". **Unclear to a lay judge**: a ticker with no
  explanation, and Wharton asks for strategy rather than individual investments.
- "which it reaches whenever stocks are no lower in 2033 than in 2031 – about three times in four". This holds only
  if the top is defined at 2031 prices and the gift is marked at 2033 prices. "About three times in four" is a model
  figure stated without its basis, and it is fine only if the Final Report supports it.
- "five long bonds reinvest their coupons, so lower yields leave a small gap". **Unclear**: "small" is not sized,
  and readers will not know which five bonds are meant.
