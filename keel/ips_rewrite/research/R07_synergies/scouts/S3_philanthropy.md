## 1. The gift range chart and the quiet phase: the pyramid is sized from the top

**Verified.**
- Funraise, gift range chart calculator, https://www.funraise.org/tools/gift-range-chart-calculator: "The lead gift is usually
  10% to 20% of the total goal"; "Because the gift size halves as you go down, the number of gifts you need doubles";
  modern campaigns are more top-heavy than the classic "rule of thirds", "the top 10 to 15 gifts often delivering well over
  half the total".
- Maine State Library campaign FAQ, https://www.maine.gov/msl/libs/admin/funding/campaigns/capfaqs.doc: "The lead gift in a
  typical campaign could be 10-20% of the total campaign goal"; "so the top donors win your campaign in the quiet phase before
  you go into the public phase"; "leadership gifts make up 60% or more of the campaign goal."
- Nonprofit Quarterly, https://nonprofitquarterly.org/the-public-phase-of-your-capital-campaign-part-iv/: "We have created
  a gift range chart and found five to ten donors to give the first 40-50% of the goal."
- Soapbox Engage, https://www.soapboxengage.com/blog/2454-capital-campaigns-101-the-quiet-phase: the quiet phase aims to
  "secure the bulk of your campaign's total fundraising goal (at least 75%)" and "Enables you to change the goal (up or down)
  based on the success of the top solicitations."
- Caution on sources: these are practitioner and vendor pages, not research. They disagree on how much is raised before
  launch (40-50% versus at least 75%). Use the shape (top-heavy, private first, public after) and not any one percentage.

**What it is.** Professional fundraisers do not pick a goal and hope. They draw a table of the gifts needed (one at the top,
then tiers that halve in size and double in number), raise the top tiers privately in a "quiet phase", and only then go
public. The lead gift is roughly a tenth to a fifth of the goal, and every tier below it is sized as a fraction of it.

**Critique.**
- The chart is built from donor *capacity ratings* (how much a prospect could give), not from a donor's own balance sheet or
  risk. It says nothing about how a lead donor funds the number.
- The percentages are rules of thumb from consultants, not from data; they differ by source and by campaign size.
- A real chart is revised in the quiet phase ("change the goal up or down"). Laura's 2031 range cannot be revised after the
  announcement, so the chart's forgiving feature is exactly the one she does not have.
- A fundraiser would also say a *range* is weak: charts use a point number per tier, because a range invites co-sponsors to
  plan on the bottom.

**Fit to Laura: partly fits, and the part that fits is the interesting part.** The case says that in 2031 she gives co-sponsors
a dollar range with a confidence level, and it must protect the payments. Nothing in the case says she is *the* lead gift, so
"if she is the lead gift" is an assumption to flag. If she is, the chart's arithmetic turns the over-promise penalty from a
matter of taste into a multiplier. Illustration (Jun would need to model it): at a lead gift of 10-20% of the goal, a $25k
shortfall against a $150k lead number is a hole of $125k-$250k in the goal if the chart is rescaled (25 / 0.20 to 25 / 0.10),
about 5 to 10 times the miss. And the range she can announce ($145k-$170k) supports a campaign goal of roughly $0.7m-$1.7m
(145 / 0.20 up to 170 / 0.10). That is a useful sanity check for the Final Report's co-sponsor text: it says what size of
project her range can credibly lead.

**Fusion hook.** With the Courtroom's "lock what you announce" (Jan 2031) and the newsvendor 3x asymmetry (R05 13): the quiet
phase is the 2028-2030 stretch in which nothing is announced and the cushion grows; the lock in January 2031 is the end of
the quiet phase; she goes public only when the bottom is owned and the top is locked. Neither idea alone gives a *reason*
for the 3x: the pyramid does, because everyone beneath the lead gift is priced from it.

**Logic device.** A causal chain followed to its end: a smaller lead gift means a smaller second tier, which means a smaller
third tier, which means a campaign that misses its goal by a multiple of Laura's miss. Over-promising costs about 3x not
because donors are cross but because the table is proportional.

**Lands in:** Final Report (co-sponsor range text and the reason for the asymmetry). IPS: at most one clause of idea, "the
bottom of the range is what we own, because everything built on it is sized from it". Not more; the IPS must not carry the
campaign arithmetic.

## 2. Rosenwald: the seed-match-sunset formula in one man

**Verified.**
- Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosenwald_Fund: the fund was "designed to expend all of its funds for
  philanthropic purposes before a predetermined 'sunset date.'"; it had to end within 25 years of Rosenwald's death in 1932;
  for schools, local black communities had to contribute by "rais[ing] funds or donat[ing] property and labor to construct the
  schools"; "more than 5,000 one-room schools" (an exhibit title elsewhere on the page says 4,978); "completely depleted in
  1948".
- Philanthropy Roundtable, https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/magazine/debates-on-perpetuity/: Rosenwald said "I am
  opposed to the permanent or what might be styled the never-ending endowment" and "Real endowments are not money, but
  ideas." Created 1917, endowment $20 million in 1927; the fund finished spending in 1948, about a decade early. A fund with
  a 35-year limit (Falk Foundation, 1929) closed in December 1965.
- Philanthropy Roundtable, https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/resource/julius-rosenwald-s-timeless-legacy-of-donor-intent/:
  he "required his donations to be matched by local residents".

**What it is.** A famous donor who refused to endow perpetually, gave in a fixed window, and made every gift conditional on a
local match. The schools were built by the match plus the seed; the fund then spent itself out.

**Critique.**
- It is one Wikipedia-level case plus an advocacy magazine's account (Philanthropy Roundtable is a donor-intent advocacy
  group, so it reads Rosenwald as a hero of donor intent). Read the primary history before leaning on any claim of impact.
- The match carried a cost: communities with less capacity to raise money got fewer schools. A match favours those who can
  already match. I did not open a source on this, so it is a critique to check, not a finding.
- "Spend it all" works for a fund whose beneficiary can be handed on (schools passed to school boards). A residency needs
  running money after the gift; Rosenwald's example does not say who pays then.

**Fit to Laura: fits, as a template and as a story.** Her contribution is a dated, capped gift (not a perpetual endowment), and
the "rest stays hers" is the opposite of binding the future. The co-sponsors are the match. The facility's own running costs
are the part the case leaves out of scope ("Taiwan legal and facility cost estimates"), so the honest line is that her gift is
the seed and the match, not an operating endowment.

**Fusion hook.** With section 1: seed (lead gift sized so the others can match) plus sunset (dated, capped, ends) is a
complete gift design that is *Laura's own*, because the cap already does the sunsetting. Fused with Tobin (section 3):
Rosenwald is the dissent that makes the Tobin case worth stating.

**Logic device.** A case study a judge remembers: one donor, a rule, 4,978-plus buildings, and a fund that finished a decade
before its own deadline.

**Lands in:** Final Report (case study in the facility section). IPS: bin; it is a story, and the IPS is a framework.

## 3. Tobin's rule versus the spend-down: which kind of promise is Laura making?

**Verified.**
- Tobin quote, as cited in Yale's *Principles of Investment Management* (https://zoo.cs.yale.edu/classes/cs458/lectures/old2016/PPMrevision_Chap3.pdf),
  footnote: James Tobin, "What is Permanent Endowment Income?", *American Economic Review* 64, no. 2 (1974): 427-432:
  "The trustees of an endowed institution are the guardians of the future against the claims of the present." and "Their
  task is to preserve equity among generations." (I read the quote in the Yale document, not in Tobin's paper itself.)
- Atlantic Philanthropies, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Philanthropies: "following plans for a spend-down put
  forward in 2002"; spend-down "commenced ... in 2012"; Feeney "signed the documents to officially close the nonprofit" on
  14 September 2020; "donated over $8 billion from 1982 to 2020".

**What it is.** Two ways to run a pot of charitable money. Perpetual: spend only the sustainable amount so the pot keeps its
real value and no generation is favoured (Tobin). Spend-down: give it all within a stated window (Rosenwald, Feeney).

**Critique.**
- Tobin's rule assumes an endless life and a *real* value target. It is a rule for trustees of institutions, not for a donor
  funding one project, and it protects the future at a known cost to the present.
- Spend-down donors (Feeney's "Giving While Living") trade away the compounding that perpetuity buys; they argue needs are
  urgent now. Neither side has a clean empirical test.
- The Wikipedia page does not give Feeney's stated reason for spending down; do not attribute one without a primary source.

**Fit to Laura: seems relevant, mostly isn't, with one real exception.** Her ten payments and the facility gift are a
spend-down by construction, so Tobin's rule does not apply to the portfolio. The exception: Tobin's rule is stated in *real*
terms, and Laura's payments are fixed *nominal* $50k. At the 2.5% inflation in the JPM LTCMA that Keel already uses, the 2042
payment buys less than the 2033 one (Jun would need to model how much). That is the case's design, so it is not a flaw in the
plan, but it is a fair thing to name: the plan keeps *nominal* promises with certainty and puts any real-value growth in the
cushion. That sentence is Tobin's rule applied honestly, not borrowed.

**Fusion hook.** With the iBonds floor: the owned part is nominal, the cushion carries the real-value hope. It is a clean
way to say what "certainty" does and does not cover without hedging.

**Logic device.** An analogy that holds: Tobin's trustee guards the future against the present; Laura's plan guards the
*present promise* (the payments) against the *future uncertainty* (the stocks). Same shape, reversed direction.

**Lands in:** Final Report (a quote and the real-versus-nominal sentence in Portfolio Analysis). IPS: bin.

## 4. The waqf, and what its failures say about fixed promises

**Verified.**
- Kuran, "The Provision of Public Goods under Islamic Law: Origins, Impact, and Limitations of the Waqf System", *Law & Society
  Review* 35 (2001), https://sites.duke.edu/timurkuran/files/2016/10/waqf-2001-1.original.pdf (excerpt read): "In principle,
  the manager of a waqf had to obey the stipulations of its founder to the letter." (p. 841); "In practice, the founder's
  directives were often circumvented." (p. 841); "the system lacked the flexibility to keep up with rapidly changing economic
  conditions." (p. 843).
- Ottoman cash waqfs in Bursa, 1555-1823, https://muslimheritage.com/ottoman-cash-waqfs-revisited-bursa/ (a heritage-site
  summary of academic work; I did not open the original paper): capital was lent to borrowers who returned "the principal,
  plus a certain 'extra' amount"; "produced returns of between 9 and 12 per cent"; recorded rates ran from about 10.8% in
  1555 to 13% in 1823; "the market rate of interest prevailing in that city was substantially higher"; "The rigidity of this
  rate was caused by conditions stipulated by the founders"; trustees "must have earned substantial amounts by exploiting the
  difference between the two rates"; "about 20% of the Bursa cash endowments had survived for more than a century";
  "the enhancement of the initial capital appears to have been an important factor"; "State policy, more than anything else,
  was responsible for the final demise of the cash waqfs".

**What it is.** The waqf is a perpetual endowment in Islamic law: the founder's deed fixes the purpose and the rules. The
Ottoman *cash* waqf is the investing version: money lent out, the return spent on a mosque, school or soup kitchen.

**Critique.**
- Kuran's rigidity thesis is contested; the same article records that directives were "often circumvented". The Bursa page
  does not tell us how much of the failure was rigidity versus trustee self-dealing versus the state. Treat "rigidity killed the
  waqf" as one scholar's strong claim, not settled fact.
- The waqf is Islamic law and Ottoman history; nothing about it binds a Taiwan residency, and the interest-based cash waqf was
  itself controversial in its own time (the Cambridge article "Usurious Piety" is in the search results; I did not open it).
- I could not source capital erosion, inflation or bad debts in the cash waqf; the Bursa page does not cover them.

**Fit to Laura: seems relevant (a perpetual facility), isn't (hers is not perpetual), but its failure mode is the right
warning.** The mechanism that failed is a *fixed number promised for a long time in a changing world*: a founder's rate frozen
at about 10-13% while the market rate drifted away. Laura's ten payments are also fixed numbers promised for a long time. The
difference the plan relies on is that Laura's payments are *owned* (matched with Treasuries) and the *flexible* part (the
amount for the facility) was left flexible with a cap. The lesson is where to put the rigidity: rigid where certainty is the
whole point (ten dated payments), flexible everywhere else (the facility sum, the cushion). A waqf that fixed the wrong thing
died; a plan that fixes the right thing is the opposite.

**Fusion hook.** With Courtroom "lock what you announce": a self-announced range is a founder's stipulation. The cap (the rest
stays hers) is the escape hatch Kuran says the waqf lacked.

**Logic device.** A case across 268 years: the founders' rate moved from 10.8% to 13% while the market moved much more; the
~20% that survived over a century were the ones that grew their capital. Fixed promises survive on growth in the part that is
allowed to grow.

**Lands in:** Final Report (one paragraph, framed as "where we put the rigidity"). IPS: bin; the audience will not carry
the Ottoman detail and the claim is scholarly-contested.

## 5. The Taiwanese hui (biaohui, 合會, 標會): who guarantees the pot?

**Verified.**
- Besley, Coate and Loury (1993), "The Economics of Rotating Savings and Credit Associations", *American Economic Review*;
  abstract summarised at https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/1613 (I have the summary, not a quote): they compare random
  allocation of the pot with bidding, find random preferred when members are identical, and also ask whether a ROSCA is
  sustainable when members may default.
- Taiwan's Civil Code rules on the 合會, as quoted by https://www.legis-pedia.com/article/other/902 (a secondary legal
  commentary; the Ministry of Justice site refused automated access): Art. 709-5, "首期合會金不經投標，由會首取得" (the first
  pot goes to the organiser without bidding); Art. 709-6, "以出標金額最高者為得標" (the highest bid wins); Art. 709-7, "逾期未收
  取之會款，會首應代為給付" (an uncollected contribution must be paid by the organiser); Art. 709-9, "會首就已得標會員依前項規
  定應給付之各期會款，負連帶責任" (the organiser is jointly liable for the winners' payments) and "其遲付之數額已達兩期之總額
  時，該未得標會員得請求其給付全部會款" (once missed payments reach two periods, the members who have not yet won may demand
  everything at once). The same page says the 內標制 (winning bid deducted from the pot) is the usual practice.
- Journal abstract at https://lawdata.com.tw/tw/detail.aspx?no=403804 (月旦法學雜誌 No. 243, 2015): the custom was codified in
  2000 (民國八十九年), and the author argues the Code gives the 會首 less protection than active members when a winner stops paying.
- I did not retrieve Art. 709-1 (the definition) itself. Wikipedia's hui page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hui_(informal_loan_club),
  says "Typically the person who is ready to pay the highest interest will receive the pot" and lists "defaults, where a member
  fails to contribute after receiving the pot" as a common issue.

**What it is.** A circle of people each pay a fixed sum every period; each period one member takes the whole pot. Those who
win early are borrowers, those who win last are savers. In Taiwan's bidding version, members bid a discount for an early
pot. The organiser (會首) takes the first pot unbid and, by law, stands behind the circle.

**Critique.**
- A hui is a credit club for people without a bank. It is not an investment, and the implied interest in the bids is not a
  Treasury rate; I have no source for typical Taiwan bid rates, so do not quote any.
- Each member's contribution is a *loan to the other members*: the last winners carry the default risk, with nothing but the
  organiser's name behind it. 倒會 (collapse) is the name for what happens when it fails. The Wikipedia article's collapse examples
  are Vietnamese, not Taiwanese; I have no Taiwanese loss statistics.
- A judge at Wharton may never have met one. The value is for the Taiwanese reader (co-sponsors), not the IPS reader.

**Fit to Laura: seems relevant (fixed contributions, a fixed order of payment), isn't as a mechanism, but fits as an
argument about trust.** In a hui the payments rotate between people; Laura's ten payments all go one way, so the mechanics do
not map. What maps is the guarantee. Taiwanese law makes the organiser personally and jointly liable (709-7 and 709-9), so a
Taiwanese co-sponsor already knows the right question to ask of any pooled promise: "who stands behind the last payment?"
Laura's answer is that the portfolio is the organiser and its backing is Treasuries, and, for the 2031 range, that the bottom of
the range is owned. Laura herself is the last seat in the circle (paid in 2042 out of money she put in earlier), and she picked
the one organiser who cannot flee.

**Fusion hook.** With section 1: in a hui the organiser takes the first pot and guarantees the rest; in a campaign the lead
donor gives first and sets the chart. Fused: the lead gift is also the guarantee, which is why the bottom of the range has to
be owned. The two ideas, from two cultures, say the same thing.

**Logic device.** An analogy that holds, drawn in one line: "a hui where the organiser is the US Treasury". Followed to its end:
no 倒會 risk on the owned part, and the cushion (stocks) is the part where the organiser *could* come up short, which is why it
is never promised.

**Lands in:** Final Report (co-sponsor text; a line for a Taiwanese reader). IPS: bin; the IPS is judged by people who need it
in their own terms, and Art. 709 is a footnote.

## 6. Cy-pres and a gift's purpose: how broad should the stated purpose be? (PARTLY VERIFIED)

**Verified so far.** The search results confirm that cy-pres is codified in the US Uniform Trust Code, section 413
(for example https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/documents/076000030K413.htm, which I could not open before the fetch budget
ran out), that New South Wales has a Charitable Trusts Act 1993 section on it, and that the Girard College case reached the
US Supreme Court (353 U.S. 230, https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/353/353.US.230.769.html; not opened).
**I have no quoted text for any of these.** Everything in this section below the line is reasoning, not source.

**What it is (from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED in this run).** When a charitable purpose becomes impossible or impractical,
a court redirects the money to the nearest purpose, if the donor showed a general charitable intent. It is a rescue after a
failure.

**Critique.** It applies to charitable trusts, not to every gift. Laura's gift to a Taiwan facility is probably not a US
trust, and Taiwan has its own trust law (I searched for Taiwan's Trust Act provisions on changed circumstances for public
trusts and did not get a text; do not claim one exists). Courts are slow and cy-pres cannot be relied on in a plan.

**Fit to Laura: seems relevant, isn't.** The case asks for *flexibility as the project develops*, which means building
flexibility in before the fact, not asking a court for it afterwards. The useful content is a drafting idea, not a doctrine:
state the gift's purpose as the mission (an artists' and writers' residency in Taiwan) and not as a particular building or
site, and say in the co-sponsor text what happens to the money if the project changes (it stays Laura's, because the cap
leaves the surplus with her). That idea needs no court and no statute.

**Fusion hook.** With the Rosenwald cap and the waqf lesson: purpose broad, amount capped, remainder hers.

**Logic device.** None worth keeping; the doctrine is a safety net, and the plan has a floor under it already.

**Lands in:** bin as a doctrine. One idea survives for the Final Report's co-sponsor text (state the purpose as the mission).

## Best synergy from my domain
The strongest fusion is sections 1 and 5 joined to the Courtroom's "lock what you announce": fundraisers size every tier of a
campaign from the lead gift, and Taiwan's hui law makes the organiser personally stand behind the pot. Both say that the
first number given must be the one that is owned. That turns Keel's 3x over-promising penalty from a taste into a
*mechanism* (a smaller lead gift shrinks every tier below it, and a Taiwanese co-sponsor will ask who stands behind the last
payment), and it names the reason the bottom of the range is the owned floor. Rosenwald (seed, match, sunset) gives the whole
gift design one case a judge will remember, and the waqf gives the warning about where not to put rigidity: fix the ten
payments, and leave the facility sum capped and flexible.
