# Blind voice A/B comparison

Two IPS entries read against the essays' author's voice and the voice guide (06_voice_guide.md).
Entries are labelled A and B. The reader does not know which version is which.

## Winner

**Entry B**, narrowly. It sounds more like the essays' author wrote it. Entry A is the better-structured IPS
argument, but the question is voice, and Entry B has more of the author's mouth in it.

## Why Entry B sounds more like the essays' author

The essays have a distinctive pattern: long clause-chain sentences that carry the reader through a mechanism,
then a short sentence that lands it. The author uses pun headings ("Where there's no will, there's no way",
"Buy one cage, get one free"), rhetorical questions answered immediately, wry deflations ("revenge with a
wig on"), and "But" as a structural pivot. The register is formal-academic with sudden casual punches.

Entry B has more of these surface markers:
- **Pun headings**: "How to bounce a cheque" and "Know thy rival" echo the author's "A modest proposal: try
  fixing things instead" and "Laissez-faire, or laissez-unfair?"
- **Rhetorical question, answered**: The opening ("If you had to pay a residency $50,000 every January...
  how would you invest? Laura Gao has to.") mirrors the author's habit of posing a question and answering it
  in the next breath.
- **Wry deflation**: "Sounds good, on average." and "The cheques are a shopping problem, not an investing
  one" have the author's habit of cutting a big claim down to a blunt, slightly funny sentence.
- **Clause-chain builds**: "Asked in our model to name our typical bottom, $145,000, it leaves Laura about
  $15,000 more on average by 2042, gift and leftovers counted" is a long participial build that stacks
  information the way the author stacks clauses in his determinism essay.
- **"But" pivots that concede for real**: "But when a recession arrives, her advances, speaking and licensing
  shrink too" and "But in our model's worst twentieth for stocks in 2028-30" are genuine concessions followed
  by turns, the author's signature move.

Entry A is more controlled and even-toned. It reads like a confident adult wrote it. The author's essays read
like a sharp seventeen-year-old who has read too much and can't help showing you the mechanism.

## Why Entry A is the better IPS (even if it's less the author's voice)

Entry A follows the voice guide's IPS register ("Spine and hands, Ahaan's mouth at about a third") more
faithfully. It has:
- **One image, argued through**: Caplet XR appears in paragraph 2 and returns in the final line. Every
  paragraph between argues from it. In Entry B, XR appears only at the end, competing with the "shopping
  problem" and "bounce a cheque" framings.
- **No subheadings**: The IPS is a 500-word form, not an essay. Entry A flows as continuous argument. Entry B's
  headings ("How to bounce a cheque", "Know thy rival") break it into sections, which reads more like an essay
  than a form submission.
- **Fewer numbers**: Entry A avoids model outputs entirely. Entry B uses $145,000, $15,000, $90,000, $140,000,
  and "97 times in 100" -- at least five model-derived figures. The guide says "Most numbers belong in the
  Final Report" and the IPS allows at most six numbers total.
- **The confession is plainer**: Entry A's "The weak point is reinvestment" is one flat sentence, owned. Entry
  B's "our first plan was three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it" is good but belongs to the rival
  section, which dilutes it.

## Sentences most like the essays' author

**Entry B, sentence 1**: "The cheques are a shopping problem, not an investing one."
Why: It reframes a complex idea into a homely metaphor in one sentence, the way the author turns retribution
into "revenge with a wig on". The deflation is earned -- the build before it explains Laura's obligation -- and
the phrasing is something a seventeen-year-old debater would actually say across a table.

**Entry B, rival paragraph**: "Sounds good, on average. But in our model's worst twentieth for stocks in
2028-30, it names about $90,000, against our $140,000 after the 2031 set-aside. We'd pay the $15,000."
Why: The three-sentence sequence -- concede, then the devastating "But", then the blunt landing -- is the
author's essay rhythm exactly. "We'd pay the $15,000" has the same weight as "We do not beat a broken car for
crashing. We ask what caused the failure, and we fix it." Short, plain, final.

**Entry A, paragraph 6**: "But time makes a stock-market loss less likely without making it smaller, and a
number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn. The building deserves a floor that
was bought, not hoped for."
Why: The long sentence builds a mechanism, and the short one lands it. The "But" pivot is real. "Bought, not
hoped for" is the kind of mirrored pair the guide allows when it carries a genuine turn of thought. This is
Entry A's strongest moment of the author's voice.

## Sentences least like the essays' author

**Entry A, paragraph 5**: "Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing in late 2032, sized to grow back
to the deposit by maturity. That is her floor: money she will own outright even if every share in the world
fell to zero."
Why: Both sentences are mid-length, mid-energy, and explanatory. The author's essays never run two sentences at
the same register and the same length back to back. This is competent prose, but it could be any careful writer.
It lacks the long carry that builds to a short landing. The voice guide warns: "Prose where every sentence is
the same middling length reads like a form."

**Entry A, paragraph 3**: "In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds that
mature like bonds, one rung falling due before each cheque. We do not invest toward these payments. We buy
them."
Why: "We do not invest toward these payments. We buy them." is a strong pair, but it arrives too early and
without a build. In the author's essays, a punchy landing is always earned by a long carry before it. Here the
carry is one sentence of mechanism. It reads more like ad copy than the author's debater rhythm.

**Entry B, paragraph 4 (rival section)**: "Asked in our model to name our typical bottom, $145,000, it leaves
Laura about $15,000 more on average by 2042, gift and leftovers counted."
Why: Despite the good participial build, this sentence is so number-dense that it reads like a research memo.
The author's long sentences stack clauses of reasoning, not figures. His essays use numerals for impact ("46%
of individuals are unemployed", "180 million years") but never five numbers in one sentence.

## Long-build count

Sentences >30 words with dash-hinges, colon-hinges, or participial clause chains:

**Entry A**: 3
1. "Her 2028 deposit depends on publishing advances, speaking fees and licensing – income that dries up in
   exactly the kind of downturn that makes bonds more expensive." (dash-hinge, ~28 words -- borderline)
2. "But time makes a stock-market loss less likely without making it smaller, and a number said aloud to
   co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn." (two-clause build, ~28 words -- borderline)
3. "The alternative – exposing the floor to the share market – would swap a small, known weakness for a large,
   unknowable one." (dash-hinge parenthetical, ~21 words -- short but structurally a build)

Entry A's sentences tend toward the mid-range. Few truly long carries.

**Entry B**: 4
1. "But when a recession arrives, her advances, speaking and licensing shrink too, and so does the 2028 deposit
   topping up the ladder." (participial build, ~22 words)
2. "So in 2031 we price the shortfall and take it from what she keeps, before she names a gift, not after."
   (clause chain with landing, ~23 words)
3. "Asked in our model to name our typical bottom, $145,000, it leaves Laura about $15,000 more on average by
   2042, gift and leftovers counted." (participial opening, ~24 words)
4. "But in our model's worst twentieth for stocks in 2028-30, it names about $90,000, against our $140,000
   after the 2031 set-aside." ("But" pivot with contrast build, ~22 words)

Neither entry has truly long carries (40+ words) the way the author's essays do ("To be truly, ultimately
responsible for an action, you would need to be responsible for the mental state that produced it: your beliefs,
desires and character in that moment" = 33 words in one clause chain). Both are compressed by the 500-word
constraint, which limits how long any sentence can run.

## Kicker count

Quotable paragraph endings (short, punchy, could be repeated over dinner):

**Entry A**: 4-5 (over the guide's limit of 3)
1. "But the cheques come first." (paragraph 1)
2. "Break it open and you ruin how it works." (paragraph 2)
3. "That is what 'a high degree of certainty' means to us – not a probability, but a purchase." (paragraph 3)
4. "The building deserves a floor that was bought, not hoped for." (paragraph 6)
5. "Do not crush." (final line)

Entry A spends kickers generously. The guide says "More than 3 in 500 words is a stand-up set." Paragraphs 1,
2, 3, 6, and 8 all end on quotable lines. This is where Entry A drifts from the author's voice: the author
earns one or two punchy endings per section; the rest end on numbers, decisions, or the alternative refused.

**Entry B**: 3 (within the guide's limit)
1. "The cheques are a shopping problem, not an investing one." (paragraph 1)
2. "We'd pay the $15,000." (rival paragraph)
3. "Do not crush." (final line)

Entry B's other paragraph endings land on mechanisms, numbers, or decisions ("...one falling due before each
January", "...to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer", "Our model then puts the
gift at its top about 97 times in 100"). This is closer to the author's pattern and to the guide's instruction.

## Overall verdict

| Criterion | Entry A | Entry B |
|---|---|---|
| Sounds like the essays' author | Occasionally | More often |
| Follows IPS register (spine + hands, mouth at 1/3) | Yes | Partially (too much mouth, too many numbers) |
| One image, argued | Yes (XR throughout) | No (XR only at end; competing frames) |
| Opponent voiced | Implicitly (paragraph 6) | Explicitly ("Know thy rival" section) |
| One confession, plain | Yes ("The weak point is reinvestment") | Yes ("our first plan was three-quarters stocks") |
| Kickers within budget (max 3) | No (4-5) | Yes (3) |
| Numbers within budget (max 6) | Yes (~3-4 felt numbers) | No (5+ model-derived numbers) |
| Long-build / short-land rhythm | Present but mild | Stronger, closer to essays |
| "But" pivots | 2, both structural | 3, all structural |
| Wry deflation | 0 | 2 ("shopping problem"; "Sounds good, on average") |
| Pun headings | 0 (no headings) | 2 |

**Voice winner: Entry B.** It has more of the author's mouth -- the wry deflations, the pun headings, the
participial builds, the earned kickers. It sounds like a sharp seventeen-year-old who has done the work.

**IPS-craft winner: Entry A.** It follows the guide's IPS register more faithfully -- one image argued through,
fewer numbers, no subheadings, the motif paid off in the final line. It reads like a polished form submission.

**The gap is narrow.** Entry A's kicker overspend is its biggest voice problem; trim two kickers and it would
close the gap. Entry B's number density is its biggest IPS problem; move three model figures to the Final Report
and it would close its gap. Neither entry has the truly long clause chains (30-40 words of stacking mechanism)
that make the author's essays unmistakable, but the 500-word limit may make that impossible.

If forced to submit one today, the choice depends on the priority: voice (Entry B) or structure (Entry A). For
a competition where judges read hundreds of IPS submissions and remember the ones with personality, Entry B's
voice advantage may matter more than Entry A's structural purity -- but Entry B needs its numbers cut first.
