# Cold read: V50

Read blind, no prior versions seen. I have the voice guide and WHARTON_WORD for reference.

## First impression

This is clear, confident and human-sounding. The opening drops the reader straight into what's at stake — ten cheques, nobody else can help — and the rest follows as one continuous argument. The Caplet XR motif lands in the second paragraph and comes back at the close, which gives the piece an arc that most strategy documents don't have. I didn't need to re-read any paragraph to understand the strategy. The closing two lines are genuinely good.

## What I understood (retelling the strategy)

Laura must pay ten $50,000 cheques from 2033 to 2042 and hopes to contribute to a building in Taiwan. In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasuries, each maturing before a cheque date — buying the payments outright rather than investing toward them. Her 2028 deposit mostly goes into Treasuries maturing in late 2032 (her "floor"), with the leftover interest-equivalent buying a world stock index fund — a home-made capital-protected note. In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a range: floor at the bottom, plus half her stocks at the top, keeping the other half liquid. From 2033 the ladder unwinds as the operating reserve. The acknowledged weakness is reinvestment risk if yields fall.

## What I had to read twice

1. **"funds that mature like bonds"** — I inferred these are bond ETFs with target maturity dates, but a judge unfamiliar with iBond-style products might briefly wonder what "funds that mature like bonds" means. It's clear enough in context, but it's the one phrase that relies on the reader filling in a step.

2. **"sized to grow back to the deposit by maturity"** — This means the Treasuries are bought at a discount and grow to par, so par equals the deposit amount. Clear on reflection, but "grow back to the deposit" could momentarily read as "grow back to the deposit's location" rather than "grow back to equal the deposit in value." Minor.

3. **"roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn"** — This is the sizing logic for the stock purchase: the floor Treasuries will earn interest, and that interest amount is used to buy stocks. The word "roughly" does useful work here (it's an approximation, not exact). Clear.

Nothing required a third read.

## Voice

This reads like one person: a sharp student who has done serious work and wants to explain it plainly. The register is formal-casual — contractions where they're natural ("doesn't" isn't used but "can't" appears once as "cannot"), en dashes, British spelling ("realise", "cheques"). The sentences vary in length: some carry the reader through a mechanism (the 2028 deposit paragraph builds through dashes and consequences), others land hard ("We buy them." / "Do not crush.").

**Does it sound like AI?** Mostly not. The piece avoids the classic AI tells: no triads, no abstract noun stacks, no hedging on every claim, no "importantly" or "notably" or "it's worth noting", no mirrored "X, not Y" on every line. The sentences feel like someone talking through their thinking. A few places that nudge toward AI register:

- "a commitment rather than a forecast" — a balanced pair. It works because it's doing real argumentative work (distinguishing the floor from a projection), but it's the kind of construction AI reaches for. One instance is fine.
- "a small, known weakness for a large, unknowable one" — another balanced pair. Again, it works because the asymmetry IS the argument. But two such pairs in quick succession (both in the reinvestment paragraph) is the highest AI-density moment in the piece.

Overall: this sounds like a person who has thought carefully and is explaining their thinking. Not like a template.

## Three retellable ideas

1. **"We do not invest toward these payments. We buy them."** — This reframes the entire strategy in two sentences. It's the central idea, stated with conviction. A judge would remember it because it's counterintuitive (most teams will "invest toward" their goals) and it's immediately backed by the mechanism.

2. **The home-made capital-protected note.** "A bank would call this a capital-protected note; we prefer shares, which cannot expire worthless." — The team has reverse-engineered a structured product and improved on it. This is the most impressive investing idea in the piece. A judge who knows structured products would notice immediately.

3. **"The building deserves a floor that was bought, not hoped for."** — This is the conviction moment. The team states what they refused to do and why. It's the closest thing to "We'd pay the $15,000" in earlier versions — a sentence that costs the team something (they're giving up a potentially larger gift) and says so plainly.

## What's missing or unclear

1. **The 2028 deposit amount is never stated.** We know it exists and what it does, but not its size ($150,000). A judge might wonder how much money goes into the floor-plus-stocks split. Whether this matters depends on whether Wharton expects that number — their guidelines say "Not expected in the IPS: detailed portfolio projections" so leaving it unstated may be the right call, since the number is from the case and the judge has the case.

2. **What "funds that mature like bonds" are.** The piece never says "iBond ETFs" or names the specific products. This is probably deliberate (Wharton says focus on strategy, not individual investments), but a judge who knows the WInS platform might briefly wonder.

3. **Post-2042.** The ladder runs out after the tenth cheque. The text doesn't say what happens to whatever Laura keeps. Minor — probably belongs in the Final Report.

4. **No return assumption stated.** The stock fund's expected growth is never quantified. Again, possibly deliberate per Wharton's "not expected in the IPS" guidance.

5. **"Half her stocks" — why half?** The split (half to the range's top, half kept) is stated but not explained. A judge might ask: why not a third, or two-thirds? The piece implies it's about preserving flexibility, but doesn't justify the specific fraction. This is the one strategic decision that could use one more clause of reasoning.

## The "all kickers" test

**Long building sentences (over 20 words, carrying a mechanism):**
1. "Her 2028 deposit depends on publishing advances, speaking fees and licensing – income that dries up in exactly the kind of downturn that makes bonds more expensive." (27 words) — Builds through a dash-hinge to show the correlation between Laura's income risk and bond prices.
2. "But time makes a stock-market loss less likely without making it smaller, and a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn." (27 words) — Two-clause build, landing on a concrete consequence.
3. "The alternative – exposing the floor to the share market – would swap a small, known weakness for a large, unknowable one." (21 words) — A parenthetical build landing on an asymmetry.
4. "In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds that mature like bonds, one rung falling due before each cheque." (25 words) — Carries the reader through the ladder's structure.

**Short punchy kickers (under 10 words):**
1. "We buy them." (3 words)
2. "But the cheques come first." (5 words)
3. "Do not crush." (3 words)

**Kicker count: 3** (or 4 if "But the cheques come first" counts). Within the voice guide's limit of 3 per 500 words. The fourth is borderline — it's more of a priority statement than a rhetorical landing.

**Verdict:** Healthy mix. The piece alternates between long building sentences and short ones. It's not "all kickers" — the long sentences do genuine carrying work (especially the income-risk sentence and the "time makes a loss less likely" sentence). The rhythm feels natural.
