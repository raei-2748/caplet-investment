# The look-alike card

AI DRAFT (Claude, 4 Nov 2026). Keel is a simulated team, AI-written. Checked against the final IPS as it stands tonight
(495 words on the strict count, pitch 48).

**Note (5 Nov, after Ray's 4 Nov word rule):** this card was written before the IPS swapped its own labels for the case's
words. Where it says "cheque", the IPS now says "payment"; where it says "the floor", the IPS means the Treasury fund
maturing in late 2032 that "sets her minimum facility contribution". The facts on the card are unchanged. Don't bring
"floor" or "cheque" back into the IPS.

**How to use it.** Reword the IPS however you like; your own voice is the point. Each row gives a fact in our words (you
needn't keep them), a false look-alike and why it's false. Capitals show the difference: in the look-alike when a word
is swapped, in the fact when one is left out. The case card (`CASE_CARD.md`) is its partner: it checks that each thing
the case asks still has an answer; this card checks that each answer is still true.

**This card isn't everything.** If a changed sentence has no row, check each word you swapped, added or cut against its
IPS line. In our tests, most breaks hid in added finance words and cut small words.

**A. Before each January.** Each holding is redeemed BEFORE a January's cheque, one per cheque.
- False: "one redeemed each January" / "one maturing IN January"
- Why: each ends earlier (the funds in December, the bonds in February, May or November), so the cash is there first.

**B. Matching the payments.** One bond or fund per payment, held to the end, rather than matching the bonds' AVERAGE
timing to the payments' and rebalancing.
- False: "our ladder is DURATION-MATCHED" / "the other way is DURATION MATCHING"
- Why: our money comes back sooner than the cheques, on average, so the first is false; the second clashes with our
  submitted notes' "through duration-matching".

**C. Bonds.** Held to the end, a Treasury BOND pays exactly what it promised; a fund pays its value.
- False: "a Treasury held to maturity pays exactly" / "the funds pay EXACTLY what they PROMISED"
- Why: our submitted notes say the funds pay their value, "as opposed to a promise of par".

**D. What the cheques rest on.** No cheque depends on shares, but they can still need the second deposit: it completes
the ladder first, if needed, and what it buys covers any later gap.
- False: "every cheque is GUARANTEED" / "the second deposit NEVER touches the cheques"
- Why: the IPS says the ladder can fall short if rates fall and stay low, and our trade notes say the second deposit
  "would top up the ladder". Our submitted notes only say "near-ironclad".

**E. Her income.** It funds the second deposit and CAN dry up in the same downturns that hit shares.
- False: "which dry up in the same downturns" / "her income RIDES ON shares"
- Why: the case doesn't say her income falls in slumps, so we can only claim it might.

**F. Shares twice.** The ladder for the cheques holds NO shares, because her income can already fall with them. The one
world fund is bought with roughly the floor's interest.
- False: "she shouldn't own shares" / "her portfolio holds NO shares" / "the shares NEVER TOUCH the cheques"
- Why: we do own one world fund (a first-time reader took "bet on shares twice" as a ban on it), and before she names
  the range her kept shares pay for any gap (row J).

**G. The floor.** It's a Treasury fund, and the bottom of the range is the floor ROUNDED DOWN.
- False: "the floor is a Treasury BOND" / "the bottom is her floor"
- Why: the floor's payout isn't fixed (our submitted note says so), so the bottom must sit under it.

**H. The bank's note.** A capital-protected note is a bond plus an option bought with the interest. Say what the bank
sells: OUR MIX, the floor fund plus shares bought with its interest.
- False: "they spend the interest to pay for the PROTECTION" / "Banks sell THIS" with nothing just before it
- Why: the bond is what protects the money; the option is only a chance of a gain. Two of three first-time readers
  couldn't say what "this" was.

**I. The weakness, and its price.** The ladder's MAIN weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest; we
price the gap as if every dollar waiting for a cheque earned nothing.
- False: "the ladder's weakness is ..." / "as if no more INTEREST were reinvested"
- Why: our submitted notes name another catch (the funds pay their value), and the funds also hold cash and matured bond
  money, so pricing only interest can leave a cheque short.

**J. The gap money.** Before the range is named, it moves from her kept shares INTO TREASURIES.
- False: "sell shares to cover the gap WHEN IT APPEARS" / "set it aside from her shares"
- Why: our submitted notes keep every cheque off shares; money with no home is still shares.

**K. Shrinks first, then a lower range.** Her spare money (her kept shares) shrinks first; if that's not enough, she
NAMES a LOWER range: lower at both ends, the BOTTOM TOO.
- False: "EXCESS CASH absorbs the gap first" / "the range she has GIVEN is cut" / "the bottom NEVER moves"
- Why: the IPS names no spare cash; everything happens before she names the range, never after; and when her spare
  money can't cover the gap, the floor pays the rest, so the bottom falls with the top. Two of three first-time
  readers thought it never could.
