From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years to run her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty. Anything left can help build the facility.

We call the plan Caplet XR, after extended-release tablets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled "do not crush". Ours is ten Treasury holdings, one redeemed for each January: X for ten, R for redemption. Crushing one means selling it before it matures, even for the building.

In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, each maturing before one of the ten Januaries. A Treasury held to maturity pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do. That is what we mean by a high degree of certainty: no payment depends on shares.

Her income is a second reason to keep shares away from the payments. Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which tend to dry up in the same downturns that hit share prices. If her portfolio leaned on shares too, one bad year could hurt her twice. Under our plan, a smaller deposit shrinks the building gift, but every payment is already paid for.

The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032: the floor of her building gift. What remains, roughly the interest that floor will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies, less than a tenth of her $450,000. Banks sell this idea as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless. We would rather she owned the shares. We assume about 7.5% a year from them, but no payment relies on it.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range for her contribution. The bottom is the floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said to co-sponsors can't quietly be halved after a bad year. The other half stays hers, in shares she can sell any day, as a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.

The plan's one real weakness is reinvestment. The ladder costs less than the payments because its bonds pay interest along the way, which we reinvest. If rates fall and stay low, that interest earns less and the ladder can fall short. So before Laura names her range, we price the shortfall as if nothing more were reinvested, and move that much from the shares she keeps into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first, and if it can't cover the shortfall, the whole range comes down.

From 2033, the ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve, paying one cheque each January until 2042. Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.
