Laura's promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone. Her hope is a gift for the building. They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart.

We started with three-quarters in stocks, and our model sank it: about one future in thirty missed a cheque. $50,000 falls due every January, good year or dreadful, and shares sold in a dreadful year never come back.

So we call the plan Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule, however much is left. Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption.

Her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques in January 2027: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each before its January, each sellable any day. Every cheque rests on one borrower, the US government, by design: ten safe-looking borrowers would miss a January more often.

So what is a high degree of certainty? In our model, a cheque fails only if the US government defaults, or if yields fall two points before we buy, making the ladder dearer, and her $150,000 deposit in 2028 shrinks below about $85,000. The ladder has one leak – coupons reinvested at lower yields – and we price it and set the money aside in 2031, before she names anything.

Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, but she has already taken the big one: the residency. Her human capital – advances, speaking, licensing – shrinks in the very recessions that make bonds dear. So her 2028 deposit plugs any gap in the ladder, then builds her gift.

Most of it buys a floor: Treasuries bought at a discount, worth everything the deposit had left by late 2032. The difference – about a fifteenth of her money, by our sums – goes into a world index fund. It's a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee. In our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer, but one time in four it breaks a promise, mostly the gift.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range: low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones. The bottom is the floor she owns. The top adds half her stocks; the other half stays hers, as flexibility for a building priced in Taiwan dollars.

A capped range has a catch. Once it's named, a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past the top goes to Laura. On the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries. She gives up that half's upside – the price of saying a number out loud.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Nothing is sold before it pays – you don't crush an XR pill – and each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down. Our rules fire on dates, never on market moves.
