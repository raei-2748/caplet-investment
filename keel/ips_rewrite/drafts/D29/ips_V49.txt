If you had to pay a residency $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042 out of one portfolio, how would you invest? Laura Gao has to. She also hopes to give towards the building in Taiwan. The cheques are a shopping problem, not an investing one.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one falling due before each January – all ten, or nearly.

How to bounce a cheque

"A high degree of certainty" earns its keep only beside a list of ways it might fail. The US government defaults. A recession arrives before January 2027 and pushes yields down – making the ladder cost more at precisely the moment that Laura's advances, speaking and licensing shrink, and with them the 2028 deposit that was meant to top it up. Or yields fall so gradually that nobody notices, and cash from bonds that mature early earns less while it waits. In 2031 we price the shortfall and take it from what she keeps, before she names a gift, not after.

Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, enough to grow back into all of it: her floor. The rest, the interest they should earn, buys one world index fund. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note and spend the interest on an option; ours buys shares, which cannot expire worthless.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is her floor, owned since 2028. The top adds half her stocks. The other half stays hers – liquid any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer.

Every fall in stocks before naming day comes off the gift; every rise past the top goes to Laura. But what a panicked manager sells in 2032, Laura sells calmly on naming day, moving the promised half into Treasuries. Our model puts the gift at its top about 97 times in 100.

Know thy rival

We know this rival because we built it first: three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it. Laura takes thoughtful risks; the strongest alternative takes one for her – keeping our ladder, putting the 2028 deposit mostly in stocks, buying its bottom in January 2031 with whatever they fetch that morning. Asked in our model to name our typical bottom, $145,000, it leaves Laura about $15,000 more on average by 2042, gift and leftovers counted. Sounds good, on average. But in our model's worst twentieth for stocks over 2028–30, it names about $90,000, against our $140,000 after the set-aside. We'd pay the $15,000.

From 2033 the ladder is the operating reserve, running itself down as each Treasury turns to cash before its cheque. Whatever breaks, the order will not change: the cheques, then the floor, then the hope. We call it Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole.

Do not crush.
