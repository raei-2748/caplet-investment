## Job 1: one read, as a judge, before opening anything else
(Written before any evidence file was opened. A scratch copy of these notes was saved first.)

**Pitch.** Laura owes ten $50,000 Januarys, and the plan buys them first with Treasuries that mature in time. The
second deposit backs the gift with bonds, and only the interest goes into world stocks. *Followed on one read.*
"Buys all ten cheques first" is the claim the IPS then qualifies (see contradiction 1). There are no years, which is
fine in 50 words.

**P1 (promise and hope).** One fixed promise ($50k a January, 2033-2042, from this portfolio alone) and one hope (a
gift), kept apart because they need different money. *Clear.* It is the best paragraph on the page.

**P2 (our first plan).** The team started at 75% stocks, its model missed a cheque about 1 time in 30, and the reason is
that ten yearly bills mean a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom. *Clear.* A finance judge may hear
"one-year bills" as Treasury bills for a moment, but the next clause rescues it.

**P3 (the name).** The plan is called Caplet XR: extended release on a pill (the same dose on schedule), plus X for ten
and R for redemption. *Clear now.* "So we built" follows from P2. "Redemption" is left to the reader (a bond redeems at
maturity), which is acceptable.

**P4 (the ladder).** In January 2027 the first $300,000 buys Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds,
one before each January. *Clear*, but it says "buys **all ten**", flat, and the next paragraph has a "gap".

**P5 (certainty).** Only two things break a cheque: a US default, or the 2028 deposit failing to fill "the gap". Even
after a two-point fall, about $125,000 is enough. There is one leak, "early cash earns less if yields fall", which is
priced and set aside in 2031. *Partly lost.*
- "**The gap**" arrives with a definite article, as if introduced earlier. It wasn't: P4 said all ten were bought.
  On one read I didn't know what gap.
- "**Even if yields fall two points first, about $125,000 still does.**" I had to read it twice. "Still does" what?
  Fill the gap, I worked out, so the deposit could come in $25,000 light. But "$125,000" could just as easily be
  read as the size of the gap. And "first" means before what? (The January 2027 purchase, it turns out, but the
  sentence doesn't say.)
- "**Early cash**": what cash, and why is it early? A non-finance judge won't know that coupons, and two bonds that
  mature months before their January, sit waiting. This is better than "coupons reinvested", but it is still a
  guess on one read.
- "Only **two** things break a cheque" is followed at once by "**one leak**". On one read that is a third thing.
  Nothing says the leak is paid from the 2028 deposit, which is what makes it part of "the gap".

**P6 (willingness and capacity).** Laura already took her big risk (the residency), and her income falls in the
recessions that make bonds expensive, so the cheques use money she already has; the 2028 deposit plugs any gap, then
builds the gift. *Clear, and the sharpest client insight.* "Dear" is a British idiom. Then a tension: "the cheques use
money she already has", yet the deposit she *doesn't* yet have is what plugs the gap. The paragraph has just said that
money is weakest in exactly the years the gap is biggest. A sharp judge sees the backstop is the risky money.

**P7 (floor, interest, 60/40).** Most of the deposit buys Treasuries that grow back to its full amount by late 2032.
Their expected interest (about 1/15 of "everything she owns") goes into a world index fund, a capital-protected note
without the bank. A 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer, but one time in three misses a cheque or lands below its
own range. *Mostly followed.*
- "Most of it": the referent ("her 2028 deposit") is in the previous paragraph. "All of it": the whole deposit, or
  what is left after the gap? Probably the latter.
- "**Everything she owns**" is literally her whole wealth. The case says she has money outside the portfolio, so I
  stopped to wonder whether the base was $450k or more.
- "**60/40**" arrives from nowhere: whose 60/40, and compared to what? "Its own range": a 60/40 portfolio has a range?
  I took it to mean the range it would let her name.
- **The 1-in-30 against 1-in-3 clash** (contradiction 3 below) is milder than in V38 because "that portfolio" now names
  the subject, but it is still there.

**P8 (the 2031 range).** In 2031 she names a range: low enough to keep her word, high enough to draw bigger gifts. The
bottom is the floor; the top adds half her stocks; the other half stays hers, sellable any day, for a dearer building.
*Clear.* The word "flexibility" is never used, though this is the flexibility answer.

**P9 (the cap's catch).** Once a range is named, falls come off the gift and rises past the top go to Laura, so the
promised half moves into Treasuries that day. The top is reached about 97 times in 100, and the rare miss is about
$1,000. *The mechanism is clear. The number is not.* If the promised half is locked in Treasuries, why does the top
ever miss? Nothing on the page says (it is the cheques' later claim; P5's set-aside makes it rare).

**P10 (from 2033).** The ten holdings become the operating reserve, nothing is sold early, each turns to cash before
its cheque, and the reserve runs itself down; if anything changes, cheques first, then the floor, then the hope.
*Clear.* The close is a triad.

**Statements that look contradictory on one read**
1. P4 "buys all ten cheques" (and the pitch's "buys all ten cheques first") against P5 "can't fill **the gap**" and P6
   "plugs any gap in the ladder". Does it buy all ten or not?
2. P5 "only two things break a cheque" against "the ladder has one leak" in the next sentence.
3. P2: 75% stocks missed a cheque 1 time in 30. P7: a 60/40, with *less* stock, fails 1 time in 3. The "or lands below
   its own range" is what resolves it, and on one read it is easy to miss.
4. P6 "the cheques use money she already has" against "her 2028 deposit plugs any gap".
5. P9: the promised half is in Treasuries, yet the top misses 3 times in 100.

**Terms used before they are explained (or never):** "the gap" (P5, never introduced); "early cash" (P5); "her range"
(P5, before the range is introduced in P8; tolerable, since "range" explains itself); "dear/dearer" (P6, P8); "60/40"
(P7); "its own range" (P7). "Ladder" (P4) and "floor" (P7) are each explained where they first appear.
