# Pitch Framework Research — AI DRAFT (Claude, 4 Nov 2026)

Research into creative pitch frameworks from outside the financial industry, for Team Caplet's
50-word IPS elevator pitch. Frameworks are ranked by fit to our constraints: 50 words, one
read, a sceptical finance judge, and a strategy that is genuinely unusual (a bond ladder
funding fixed payments is textbook; the correlated-income insight and the
capital-protected-note-without-the-option are not).

---

## The twelve frameworks, grouped by origin

### A. SCREENWRITING & FILM

#### 1. The Cold Open
**Origin:** TV drama convention. The teaser before the opening credits drops the audience into
a crisis mid-action. *Breaking Bad* opens with a man in underwear driving an RV through the
desert with a body in the back. The audience can't look away.

**Mechanism:** Bypasses the "skim and sort" reflex. A judge reading hundreds of pitches is
skimming; a crisis in the first sentence triggers the stress-testing instinct instead. If
the claim holds, they must understand the plan; if it doesn't, they've found a team to
eliminate. Either way, they read every word.

**Source:** No Film School: a good cold open establishes the central conflict and drops the
audience into the action without over-explaining. Celtx: give just enough to make the
audience curious. Industrial Scripts: a drama can start mid-action, leaving viewers
wondering how the story reached that point.

**Fit for Laura:** Strong. "Shares crash forty percent the year before Laura's first cheque.
Every payment still arrives." The claim is true (the payments are in Treasuries). The judge
must read on to understand why.

**Risk:** The plan's real weakness is falling interest rates, not share crashes. A sharp
judge may ask why the pitch picked shares as the threat.

---

#### 2. The Pixar Story Spine (adapted)
**Origin:** Improv teacher Kenn Adams, late 1980s. Popularised by Pixar storyboard artist
Emma Coats in 2011 as rule #4 of "Pixar's 22 Rules for Storytelling." The template:

> Once upon a time… / Every day… / Until one day… / Because of that… / Because of that… /
> Until finally… / And ever since then…

**Mechanism:** The "every day / until one day" break forces a turning point. The audience
feels the before-and-after, not just the plan.

**Fit for Laura:** Moderate. The turning point is the insight that she already has enough,
but the 50-word limit forces heavy compression. Could work as a skeleton:

> Every year from 2033, Laura owes $50,000. / Until we noticed: at today's rates, her first
> deposit already covers all ten. / Because of that, her $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one
> per cheque. / Her 2028 deposit sets a floor for the building gift; only its interest buys
> shares. / And ever since then, a crash can shrink the gift but break nothing she was
> promised.

That's 65 words — too long. The spine's power is in the emotional arc, which needs more
room than 50 words gives.

**Risk:** Sounds like storytelling, not strategy. A finance judge may dismiss the form.

---

#### 3. The Heist Structure (Ocean's Eleven)
**Origin:** Heist films explain the plan by assigning each person one job. The audience
remembers the plan because each part is one sentence and each part is complete on its own.

**Mechanism:** Breaks complexity into independent modules. Each module is falsifiable on its
own. If any one fails, the whole plan fails — but the pitch shows that none can fail.

**Fit for Laura:** Strong. The plan has three natural parts: cheques (Treasuries), building
gift floor (Treasuries), and upside (shares). "Laura's money has three jobs and each dollar
does exactly one."

**Risk:** The most conventional shape of the strong options. Clear but not surprising.

---

### B. LAW & RHETORIC

#### 4. The Closing Argument (verdict first)
**Origin:** Trial lawyers open the closing with the verdict, not the evidence. The jury hears
the conclusion when their attention is highest. Everything after is proof.

**Mechanism:** Front-loads the punchline. In a stack of pitches, only the first sentence is
guaranteed to be read carefully. "Laura already has enough" is a verdict that demands proof.

**Source:** Cochran's "if it doesn't fit, you must acquit" worked because it was a single
test the jury could apply to every piece of evidence. Our version: "No share needs to rise;
no market needs to recover" — a single test the judge can apply to every part of the plan.

**Fit for Laura:** Strong. The reframe ("she already has enough") is true and surprising.

**Risk:** "Already has enough" might sound overconfident. The $300k covers the payments at
today's rates, but rates could fall before January 2027. The IPS body handles this.

---

#### 5. The Cross-Examination
**Origin:** Irving Younger's "Ten Commandments of Cross-Examination" (1975): only ask
questions you know the answer to. Build to the verdict one step at a time. Each question is
so obvious the jury nods. Then the conclusion feels inevitable.

**Mechanism:** Forces the reader to agree three times before they realise they've accepted
the strategy. Each question has one obvious answer, and each answer proves one part of the
plan.

**Fit for Laura:** "Can the US Treasury pay its debts? Then Laura's cheques arrive." The
questions are genuine (each maps to a real risk the plan does or doesn't take), not
rhetorical.

**Risk:** Most stylistically unusual. A judge who finds questions gimmicky in a written pitch
may penalise it. Questions work in speech because the speaker controls the pause; on paper
the reader controls the pace.

---

#### 6. Cochran's Refrain ("if it doesn't fit")
**Origin:** Johnnie Cochran's closing in *People v. Simpson* (1995). Co-defence lawyer Gerald
Uelmen reportedly suggested the line. Cochran used it as a recurring test applied to each
piece of evidence, turning a complex case into a single repeated question.

**Mechanism:** A refrain gives the audience a rule they can apply themselves. Once they have
the rule, every new fact is tested against it without the speaker saying so.

**Fit for Laura:** The refrain could be: "If it needs a share to rise, it doesn't belong in
the cheques." Or simply: "The crash shrinks the gift, not the cheques." Repeated once at the
end, it becomes the single idea the judge carries away.

**Risk:** A refrain needs at least two appearances to land, which costs words. In 50 words,
you get the refrain once, maybe twice.

---

### C. CONSULTING & BUSINESS STRATEGY

#### 7. SCQA (Minto Pyramid Principle)
**Origin:** Barbara Minto, developed at McKinsey. Published in *The Pyramid Principle* (1985,
revised 1996). SCQA = Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. It serves as the
introduction; the pyramid organises the supporting arguments underneath.

**Mechanism:** The Complication is the engine. It names the change that creates the problem.
Without it, the pitch is just a description of the plan.

**Source:** "SCQA earns attention and delivers the governing thought; the pyramid organises
every supporting argument underneath" (SlideModel, Management Consulted).

**Fit for Laura:**
- **S:** Laura needs ten $50,000 payments from 2033, with high certainty.
- **C:** Her 2028 income depends on book advances and speaking fees, which can fall in the
  same downturns that hit shares.
- **Q:** How do you fund the payments without betting on shares?
- **A:** Her first $300,000 buys ten Treasuries, one per payment.

The SCQA forces you to state the complication, which is where your original insight lives
(the correlated-income observation). That's the part most teams will skip.

**Risk:** SCQA is a consulting framework. Wharton judges may recognise it, which cuts both
ways: it signals structured thinking, but it also signals "I read *The Pyramid Principle*."

---

#### 8. The Challenger Reframe
**Origin:** *The Challenger Sale* (Dixon & Adamson, 2011). The framework says the most
effective salespeople teach the customer something that changes how they see their own
problem, before mentioning the product.

**Mechanism:** The "commercial insight" reframes the buyer's situation. A good reframe
provokes rethinking, not agreement. If the customer nods immediately, the insight isn't
strong enough.

**Source:** "The reframe connects a prospect's challenges to a bigger trend that changes how
they think about their situation" (IDC). "The solution comes only after the reframe"
(Weflow). "Enthusiastic agreement actually indicates failure" (Antoine Buteau summary).

**Fit for Laura:** The reframe: "Laura's payments are not a growth problem. She already has
the money. They are a preservation problem." If a judge nods immediately, they already know
the answer and won't be surprised. If they pause — "wait, $300k covers $500k in payments?"
— the reframe worked.

**Risk:** The Challenger Sale is designed for a dialogue where the seller watches the
buyer's reaction. In a written pitch, you can't calibrate. The reframe must be strong enough
to land in one read.

---

#### 9. Andy Raskin's Strategic Narrative
**Origin:** Andy Raskin's Medium post "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (analysing
Zuora's pitch). Nearly 3 million views (claimed, unverified).

**Structure:**
1. Name a big, undeniable shift in the world
2. Show there are winners and losers
3. Tease the "promised land"
4. Introduce "magic gifts" (features as superpowers)
5. Show evidence that you can deliver

**Fit for Laura:** The "shift" could be: "Rates are high enough, for the first time in
years, that $300,000 can buy ten $50,000 payments outright." But this is a market fact, not
a strategic narrative about a changing world. Raskin's framework fits products better than
investment plans.

**Risk:** The framework is built for B2B SaaS, and it shows. Forcing Laura's plan into it
would feel unnatural.

---

### D. SCIENCE COMMUNICATION

#### 10. ABT (And, But, Therefore)
**Origin:** Randy Olson, a marine biologist turned Hollywood filmmaker. Published in
*Houston, We Have a Narrative* (2015). Drawn from Hollywood editing rules and adapted for
science communication. Olson calls ABT "the DNA of story."

**Structure:** "[Context], AND [more context], BUT [problem], THEREFORE [resolution]."

**Mechanism:** The "but" is the engine. Without it, you have a list of facts
(And…And…And…, which Olson calls the "AAA" pattern). The "but" creates a tension the
"therefore" resolves.

**Fit for Laura:**
> Laura needs ten $50,000 payments from 2033 AND her 2028 deposit depends on income that
> can shrink in downturns, BUT at today's rates her first $300,000 already covers all ten
> cheques, THEREFORE only the interest on her building-gift floor goes into shares.

That's one sentence, ~45 words. The ABT forces the insight (the "but") into the centre of
the pitch, which is exactly where it should be.

**Risk:** The ABT is a one-sentence framework. 50 words in one sentence is a long sentence.
It may read as breathless. You could break it into three sentences (context / but / therefore)
and keep the ABT structure without the single-sentence constraint.

---

#### 11. The Pre-Mortem (Gary Klein)
**Origin:** Psychologist Gary Klein, published in *The Power of Intuition* (2007) and a
Harvard Business Review article, "Performing a project premortem" (2007). Based on
"prospective hindsight": imagine the project has already failed, then generate plausible
reasons. Research by Russo and Pennington found prospective hindsight increases the ability
to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30% (secondary citation, unverified against the
original study).

**Note:** The NASA connection I used earlier is unverified. NASA's FMEA (Failure Mode and
Effects Analysis) is real engineering practice, but I found no authoritative source linking
it to Klein's method. Corrected.

**Mechanism:** Opening with the failure mode signals the team stress-tested the plan, not
just designed it. It answers the judge's first question ("what could go wrong?") before
they ask it.

**Fit for Laura:** "We asked what would have to go wrong for Laura to miss a cheque.
Answer: the US Treasury defaults." The failure mode is real and extreme. It proves the
payments are as certain as anything in finance.

**Risk:** "The US Treasury defaults" might sound flippant. But it is the literal truth for
a Treasury held to maturity, and stating it plainly is more honest than claiming "high
certainty" without saying what certainty means.

---

### E. COMMUNICATION STYLE

#### 12. The Buffett Letter
**Origin:** Warren Buffett's annual letters to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders. The SEC's
1998 *A Plain English Handbook* formalised the style; Buffett wrote its preface, advising
writers to "picture a specific person" (he pictures his sisters Doris and Bertie).

**Principles:**
- Write to one specific person (not "the market" or "investors")
- Use analogies to explain complex ideas
- Lead with bad news and own it
- No jargon; every sentence must pass the "would my sister understand this?" test
- Drafting forces clear thinking

**Fit for Laura:** This is less a pitch structure and more a voice guide. The IPS already
follows some of these principles ("like the tablet it is named after"). It reinforces the
rule that the pitch should be plain English, not finance-speak.

**Risk:** It's a writing style, not a structure. It tells you *how* to write the pitch, not
*what* to put in it. Best used as a filter on top of another framework.

---

## How to combine them

The strongest pitch will probably use **two layers**: a **structure** (how the 50 words are
organised) and a **device** (the single move that makes the judge stop skimming).

| Structure options | Device options |
|-------------------|----------------|
| Cold Open (crisis → proof) | The reframe ("she already has enough") |
| SCQA (situation → complication → question → answer) | The pre-mortem ("what would have to go wrong?") |
| ABT (context, but problem, therefore solution) | The refrain ("the crash shrinks the gift, not the cheques") |
| Heist (three jobs) | The unfalsifiable claim ("if shares halved…") |
| Verdict first (conclusion → evidence) | The correlated-income insight |

**Recommended combination:** Cold Open structure + reframe device.

Open on the crisis (shares crash), show the payments survive (the reframe: she already had
enough), then explain the plan in two sentences (the ladder + the building gift). Close with
the refrain that separates what's protected from what isn't.
