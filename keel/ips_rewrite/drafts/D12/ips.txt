Laura has made her residency one promise and has one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone; the hope is a gift for the building, big enough to win co-sponsors over. They need different kinds of money, so Caplet XR keeps them apart.

Pharmacists call a pill that releases the same dose every hour, however much is left, extended release: XR. Her cheques are like that: $50,000 falls due, good year or dreadful. Stocks beat bonds over most decades, but Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and anyone owing a fixed amount must sell at the bottom of a crash. (Our first plan, three-quarters stocks, missed cheques in our model.)

So her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques in January 2027: a ladder of Treasury bonds and iBond Treasury funds, each maturing before its January. That's our XR, too: X for ten, R for redemption. The cheques add up to $500,000 but cost about $300,000, because interest pays the rest.

That is our definition of a high degree of certainty: a cheque can fail only if the US government defaults, or if yields fall two points before we buy and her 2028 deposit more than halves. The ladder's one leak, coupons reinvested at lower yields, is priced and set aside in 2031, before she names anything.

Laura's "thoughtful risks" are already taken: the residency is the boldest bet of her life, and her income from advances, speaking and licensing shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear. So her 2028 deposit never carries a cheque: it plugs any gap in the ladder, then builds her gift.

Most of it buys her floor: Treasuries maturing late in 2032, sized to grow back to the whole remainder. The interest they should earn, about a fifteenth of all she owns, goes into a world index fund. It's a bank's capital-protected note, minus the bank and its fee. The price is a smaller boom: in our model a balanced portfolio usually leaves her richer, but breaks a promise one time in four.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range: low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones. The bottom is the floor she owns; the top adds half her stocks, and the other half stays hers, for a dearer building.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once it's named, a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past the top goes to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries. In our model the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Nothing is sold before it pays (do not crush); each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope.
