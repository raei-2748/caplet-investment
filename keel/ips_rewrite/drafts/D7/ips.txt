Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them.

Caplet XR in one rule: each promise is made safe by the day it becomes one. Until then it is a hope, and may carry risk.

The cheques are promises from page one: $50,000 every January, 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars. So her first $300,000 buys them in January 2027: Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that turn to cash before each January. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule. To us, X is ten and R is redemption: ten holdings, each redeemed in time for one cheque.

Our first plan put three-quarters in stocks, and the model had it missing cheques. A fixed-percentage withdrawal can ride out a crash; a fixed-dollar withdrawal is a forced sale at the bottom. Then the part that sounds wrong: we do not diversify the cheques. Ten separate 99% bets would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one.

A quieter reason to go first: Laura's 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – income that can shrink in the same slump that makes bonds dearer. So the money she has buys the certainty, and the money she expects carries the risk.

Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, options given away free, returns assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell. Ours shuts the first three. The fourth is our soft spot, so every coupon buys more of the bond that paid it, and in January 2033 the reserve changes once: any projected shortfall, plus a cushion, moves into the shortest Treasuries, from leftover cash and Laura's half first.

The 2028 deposit fills any gap in the ladder, then buys the floor: more of the fund paying the first cheque, sized to regrow the whole remainder by 2033. Roughly its expected interest buys a world index fund. Keep the seed; gamble the harvest.

In 2031 Laura names a range, which can embarrass her twice over. Too low, and co-sponsors give less – bigger lead gifts draw bigger gifts. Too high, and she breaks her word, which we judge three times worse. So the bottom is the floor she owns, rounded down; the top adds half the stocks.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once named, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. So on its birthday the promised half moves into Treasuries maturing before 2033.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less; it cannot make $50,000 cost more. It and the Taiwan dollar can hurt the building – so Laura's half stays in stocks, sellable any day: the project's second cheque. If needs change, cheques come first; floor and stocks shrink together.

No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush. The residency gets ten Januaries for certain, and a building built on money Laura already owns.
