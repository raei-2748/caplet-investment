## How to read this log

**What was borrowed.** From each source sentence we took its shape: its syntax, its rhythm, its turn (the place it
pivots) and, where it has one, its move (a question answered in four words, a concession opened with "Admittedly",
a numbered prescription). We filled each shape with Caplet's own meaning. No source sentence is copied, and no
distinctive phrase is paraphrased closely: the IPS can't carry citations, and a judge who spotted a famous writer's
wording would have an academic-integrity problem with a Wharton entry. Where a few words do overlap, they are common
English (an idiom or a stock phrase), and the "Overlap" column names them so Caplet can see each one and decide.
Caplet rewrites in its own words before anything is submitted; these sources can go in the Final Report's Works Cited.

**How the originals were read.** Most sites block this environment's direct downloads, so every page went through a
web reader that returns short quotes (125 characters at most). The quotes below are as that reader returned them from
the page, on 1 Nov 2026. One context (Didion's question) was checked with a second, targeted read. Before any of these
quotes is printed in the Final Report, check it against the printed source.

**The writers (15, plus a pharmacy label).** Chosen for the best-made sentences we could reach, and for range: three
great essayists (Orwell, Didion, E. B. White), a great physicist writing plainly about risk (Feynman), the finance
writers with the most personality (Buffett, Marks, Munger, Galbraith, Schwed, Housel, Levine, Bogle), Paul Graham, Sun
Tzu for one heading, and one Australian, RBA Governor Glenn Stevens. Ahaan's three essays are the tuning fork, not a source: no sentence is built on them, but
every borrowed shape had to sound like them once filled (pun headings, a long build and a blunt landing, a concession
and then "But", homely images in formal sentences, en dashes, British spelling).

## Read but not used, and why

Kept for the Final Report, where the room is bigger: more of Orwell's "Shooting an Elephant" ("He wears a mask, and his face
grows to fit it."; "I often wondered whether any of the others grasped that I had done it solely to avoid looking a
fool."), for the reflection on our first plan; Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" ("The great enemy of clear
language is insincerity."), for the voice section; Feynman's last line ("For a successful technology, reality must
take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."), for co-sponsor credibility; Galbraith ("There
can be few fields of human endeavor in which history counts for so little as in the world of finance."), for the
capital-protected note's history; Twain's October calendar line, for the rival's dated sale; Taleb's river "four feet
deep on average" (*The Black Swan*, p. 161), for the rival's average; Schwed on losing money ("Like all of life's rich
emotional experiences, the full flavor of losing important money cannot be conveyed by literature."); Housel on
"scared enough to save for the short run and brave enough to invest for the long run", for the floor-and-stocks split;
the Royal Warrant's "By Appointment to ...", tried as the rival's heading ("Sell low, by appointment") and dropped
when a blind reader saw that Laura's own lock also sells on a set day, so the joke could land on our plan.
Left out of the IPS because each is recognisable enough that a paraphrase would read as borrowed.
