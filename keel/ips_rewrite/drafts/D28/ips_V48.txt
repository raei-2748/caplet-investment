If you had to pay a residency $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042 from one portfolio, how would you invest? Laura Gao has to. She also hopes to give towards the residency's building in Taiwan. The cheques are a shopping problem, not an investing one.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten – or nearly. They form a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one falling due before each January.

How to bounce a cheque

Without a list of ways to fail, "a high degree of certainty" is just an adjective. First, the US government defaults. Second, a recession arrives before January 2027; yields fall and the ladder costs more. But when a recession arrives, her advances, speaking and licensing shrink too, and so does the 2028 deposit topping up the ladder. The third is too slow to notice: cash that arrives before it's needed earns less if yields fall. So in 2031 we price the shortfall and take it from what she keeps, before she names a gift, not after.

Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, enough to grow back into all of it: her floor. The rest, the interest they should earn, buys one world index fund. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note and spend the interest on an option; ours buys shares, which can't expire worthless.

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is her floor, owned since 2028. The top adds half her stocks. The other half stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer.

A named range needs a warning label: every fall in stocks comes off the gift, every rise past the top goes to Laura. What a panicked manager sells in 2032, Laura sells calmly on naming day, moving the promised half into Treasuries. Our model then puts the gift at its top about 97 times in 100.

Know thy rival

We know it well: our first plan was three-quarters stocks, until our own model sank it. Laura takes thoughtful risks; the strongest rival takes one for her. It keeps our ladder, puts the 2028 deposit mostly in stocks and buys its bottom in January 2031 with whatever they fetch that morning. Asked in our model to name our typical bottom, $145,000, it leaves Laura about $15,000 more on average by 2042, gift and leftovers counted. Sounds good, on average. But in our model's worst twentieth for stocks in 2028-30, it names about $90,000, against our $140,000 after the 2031 set-aside. We'd pay the $15,000.

From 2033 the ladder is the operating reserve, running itself down as each Treasury turns to cash before its cheque. Whatever breaks, the order won't change: the cheques, then the floor, then the hope. We call it Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule: ten redemptions, one a year, and it only works whole.

Do not crush.
