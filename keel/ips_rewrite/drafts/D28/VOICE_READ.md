**Ranking: 1st Entry 2, 2nd Entry 1.** A clear margin, not a wide one.

## 1. Which sounds like the essays' author

Entry 2 sounds more like the essays' author. It sets the reader a problem before arguing ("how would you invest? Laura Gao has to"), uses punning subheads, explains before it jokes, and deflates its own good news ("Sounds good, on average."). Its pharmacy thread pays off in "Do not crush" the way the opening image of his space essay returns at the end. Entry 1 is the steadier voice and has the two best lines in either entry, but its middle is packed: chained clauses, colon after colon, shorthand like "cash paid early". It sounds like a disciplined analyst. Entry 2's fault runs the other way: too many metaphors and register changes for 500 words.

## 2. Most and least like the author

**Entry 1, most like him**
- "So we don't invest the promise. We buy it." A one-line turn, like the two-word last line of his space essay.
- "A high degree of certainty is a list of ways to fail, not an adjective." He likes turning a stock phrase inside out.
- "There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first" His steelman entrance (the way his first essay grants its critics their weapons).

**Entry 1, least like him**
- "Or a recession arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, so the ladder costs more, and it shrinks the advances..." Two blows in one breath; he'd give the second its own sentence.
- "Matched in our model to name our $145,000 median bottom, it usually wins: counting the gift..." Report syntax nobody says aloud.
- "One leak remains: cash paid early earns less if yields fall." Cryptic; the metaphor is gone before we know what leaks.

**Entry 2, most like him**
- "If you had to pay ... how would you invest? Laura Gao has to." Puts the reader in the dock, like the second-person lines that open his first essay.
- "Sounds good, on average." His deflating aside (the two-word "very well" and the pub-joke aside in his second essay).
- "Sell low, by appointment" A pun on a known phrase, like his shop-slogan heading in the first essay.

**Entry 2, least like him**
- "She also hopes to give its building in Taiwan a gift." Flat and clumsy right after the best opening.
- "The proper fix is to price the shortfall in 2031..." A consultant announcing; he argues his way there.
- "Admittedly, our first plan was three-quarters stocks" A stiff hinge; his concessions get a proper entrance.

## 3. Collage?

Entry 1: no. One terse voice. Small seams: "leak" appears once and vanishes; "Banks sell this as a capital-protected note" lurches into insider talk.

Entry 2: mildly, yes. One author changing costume: chatty question, flat case summary, pharmacy pun, textbook "proper fix", Marks-style aphorism ("What a panicked manager sells in 2032..."), essay "Admittedly", chat "Sounds good". Loudest seam: "shopping problem" runs straight into the heading "Prescriptions for a bounced cheque", two metaphor families side by side, the second borrowing a capsule the reader hasn't met. Second seam: "Sell low, by appointment" sits under a paragraph where Laura herself sells on a set day, so the joke can land on the plan, not the rival. His essays mix registers too, but with room to set the jokes up.

## 4. The judge

**Enjoy more: Entry 2.** It talks to the reader and earns its last line.

**Understand more easily on one read: Entry 2, narrowly.** It numbers the risks, names the slow one plainly, says what the bank spends the interest on, and explains why the promised half moves into Treasuries. Entry 1's frame is clearer ("one promise and one hope"), but its catch and rival paragraphs need a second pass.

## 5. Passages to rewrite for voice

**Entry 1**
1. The risks paragraph: split the recession's double blow into two sentences; say what "cash paid early" is before calling it a leak.
2. "On the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries...": no "so" links fix to catch; give the one-clause reason.
3. "Matched in our model to name our $145,000 median bottom...": one idea per sentence: what the rival promises, that it usually wins, by how much.

**Entry 2**
1. "Prescriptions for a bounced cheque" arrives before the capsule: pun on something the reader already has.
2. "The proper fix is to price the shortfall...": make it the team acting ("In 2031 we price..."), not a consultant prescribing.
3. "The order won't change: the cheques, then the floor, then the hope.": give it a trigger ("if anything goes wrong") so it reads as a promise, not a decree.

Both pitches: in "Treasuries that repay it", "it" has slid from the promise to the deposit.
