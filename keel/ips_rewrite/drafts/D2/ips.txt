In January 2033 Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten $50,000 cheques to her Taiwan residency, and nobody else may help. So we start at the end and work backwards.

We call it Caplet XR. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a dose let out on schedule, never all at once. To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten Treasuries, each redeemed in time for one January.

We take "a high degree of certainty" to mean by January 2028 every payment sits in its own Treasury bond or defined-maturity Treasury fund, maturing before its date. Held to their December close, the funds are near-ironclad too; their bonds have matured into cash.

Why every payment before a single share? Because Laura already owns plenty of stock-market risk – it just isn't in a brokerage account. Her 2028 deposit rests on book advances and speaking fees, and the slump that shrinks that income would likely cut yields too, making bonds dearer just as the cheque shrinks. So the $300,000 she already has buys the payments, latest-dated first; any gap is the second deposit's first job.

For the facility, a bank would sell Laura a capital-protected note: a bond that returns her money, an option on the market, and a fee. We keep the bond and drop the rest. The rest of the 2028 deposit buys a Treasury fund maturing in December 2032, sized to grow back to about that sum – her floor, intact even if every share went to zero. Its expected interest buys one world index fund of 10,000 companies. Laura risks only money she hasn't earned yet. The price is a smaller boom; the reward, nothing that can expire worthless.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range: from the floor's projected payout, rounded down – money she already holds, a commitment rather than a forecast – to that plus half her stocks' value on the day. In 2033 the same rule sets the gift, capped at that top; the other half stays with Laura, for flexibility.

Moving floor money into stocks would raise the expected gift. But time shrinks the chance of a stock loss, not its size, and a number said aloud cannot be half-kept.

We refuse.

The soft spot is reinvestment: if yields fall for good, coupons earn less, and any gap comes from Laura's half first, the floor last. We assume stocks beat Treasuries over decades, not every two years. Inflation cannot touch fixed payments; rising Taiwan costs are the index fund's job.

Until 2033 nothing is sold early – do not crush. Coupons and early maturities wait in a short-term Treasury fund. For liquidity, something matures before every January, and the funds trade daily. In 2033 the payment holdings become the operating reserve, one fewer each January; we review the plan with Laura yearly. Our WInS portfolio is this plan in miniature.

Ten cheques, ten Januaries. Every one bought before the first is written.
