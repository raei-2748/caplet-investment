## Part 1. Paraphrases (written before the re-read)

**Pitch.** Laura's ten $50,000 payments are a promise, so the team buys them outright rather than investing and hoping. Her first $300,000 buys Treasuries that each come due before a January. Her 2028 deposit buys a safe Treasury "floor" for the building gift, and only the interest that floor should earn goes into world stocks.

**P1.** From January 2033 Laura pays $50,000 every January for ten years to fund her Taiwan residency. She would also like to put money toward the building, but the ten payments take priority.

**P2.** The strategy is named Caplet XR, borrowing the pharmacy term "extended release": a capsule that pays out on a schedule and must not be crushed. X stands for ten and R for redemption: ten holdings, one cashed in each January. (The point seems to be that the ladder only works if left to run its schedule.)

**P3.** In January 2027 the first $300,000 goes into a ladder of Treasuries and bond-like funds, with one rung maturing before each payment. The team buys the payments rather than investing toward them, because a Treasury held to maturity pays what it says whatever markets do, and that is their meaning of "a high degree of certainty".

**P4.** Laura's income (advances, speaking, licensing) is risky in the same way stocks are, and it would fall in a recession, which is when bonds rise. So if her 2028 deposit is smaller, the building gift shrinks, but a payment backed by a Treasury already bought is unaffected.

**P5.** The 2028 deposit first buys any rungs the first $300,000 could not afford. Most of what is left buys Treasuries maturing late 2032, which is the "floor" under the building gift. What remains after that, about the interest those Treasuries should earn and roughly a fifteenth of all her money, goes into a world index fund. This is like a bank's capital-protected note, except the bank would spend the interest on an option and the team buys shares instead. They assume shares return about 7.5% a year, and if they return less, only the gift gets smaller.

**P6.** In 2031 Laura tells co-sponsors a range for her building contribution. The bottom is the floor rounded down (money she already owns); the top is the floor plus half of her stocks, valued that day. Before announcing it, the half of the stocks in the top end is switched into Treasuries so everything promised is in bonds. The other half stays with her, available at any time, as protection against cost overruns in Taiwan dollars or as general flexibility.

**P7.** The team could have put the floor into stocks and aimed for a bigger gift, but a figure announced to co-sponsors cannot quietly be cut in half if markets fall, so they did not.

**P8.** The risk is that if interest rates fall and stay low, the coupons paid by the bonds get reinvested at lower rates and the ladder may end up short of the ten payments. Before announcing the range in 2031, the team sets aside an amount for that shortfall out of the stocks Laura keeps; if the stocks cannot cover it, the whole range is lowered. (Weak paraphrase: I could not tell how a ladder that was "bought" can depend on reinvestment, nor how a shortfall that plays out to 2042 can be sized in 2031. I filled the gap by assuming the ladder was costed assuming reinvested coupons.)

**P9.** From 2033 the ladder is the operating reserve and is paid out bond by bond as each payment falls due. The priority never changes: payments first, then the floor, then the extra gift.

**P10.** "Do not crush": the slogan from the capsule label, i.e. do not break the ladder before its schedule is done.

## Part 3. Sentences whose logic does not hold, or that contradict each other

1. **P3 versus P8.** P3: "A Treasury held to maturity pays exactly what it promises, whatever markets do ... We buy them." P8: "the ladder could fall short" because of reinvestment. Both cannot be left unexplained. If the ladder is bought as ten $50,000 maturities, there is nothing to reinvest. If it relies on reinvested coupons, then P3's "exactly what it promises" is overstated and the "we buy them" claim is only partly true. The IPS needs one sentence in P3 or P8 saying which it is.
2. **P5 versus P8.** P5: "no cheque and no floor depends on it [the index fund]". P8: the ladder's shortfall is set aside "from the stocks Laura keeps", and what the stocks "can't cover lowers the whole range". So in the low-yield case a cheque's margin does depend on the stocks, and the floor (bottom of the range) is cut if they fall short. Literally the two sentences can both be true, but a judge will read them as inconsistent.
3. **P4 versus P5.** P4: "A cheque funded by a Treasury already bought does not [depend on the 2028 deposit]." P5: "Her 2028 deposit first finishes any rung the first $300,000 couldn't buy." So for any rung the $300,000 could not buy, the cheque does depend on the deposit. The size of that gap is never given (a reader who does the arithmetic will find it small, but the IPS does not say).
4. **P4's argument does not reach its conclusion.** Bonds rising in downturns is a reason to hold bonds as a hedge or to rebalance, not a reason to prefer held-to-maturity Treasuries. The real argument (do not fund the promise from uncertain income) is sound but is not what the sentence says.
5. **P6 versus P8.** P6 sells the stocks Laura keeps as protection for Taiwan-dollar cost overruns and flexibility. P8 then spends part of them on the reinvestment shortfall. Not a contradiction, but the flexibility promised in P6 is smaller than it reads, and P6 does not say so.
6. **P5 "roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn"**: for a bond held to maturity the interest is known, not expected, so "expected" is wrong or means something else (e.g. rates when bought).
7. **Phrase "ten holdings" (P2) versus P5.** After the 2028 deposit she holds the ladder, a floor and an index fund; "ten holdings, one redeemed for each January" describes only the ladder.
8. **"Gift" is never defined.** It appears in P4 and later paragraphs. P1 says "contribute to the building", the pitch says "building gift", P6 says "facility contribution". A judge reads three words for one thing.

## Part 5. Sentences that read as if a machine, not a student, wrote them

- "We don't invest toward these payments. We buy them." (P3; the pitch says nearly the same thing.) Tidy "not X, but Y" slogan. A student would more likely explain why, in a clause.
- "Whatever else changes, the order will not: the cheques, then the floor, then the gift." (P9) A rhythmic list of three and a vague "whatever else changes" with nothing to refer to. It sounds like a motto written to end a section.
- "Do not crush." (P10) A tagline closer. The pharmacy conceit and a one-line sign-off is more copywriter than teenager.
- "a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn." (P7) A polished aphorism, too finished for the plain point it makes (we won't promise what we can't deliver).
- "Laura's career already behaves a lot like a stock." (P4) A neat sound bite that stands in for evidence.
- "To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten holdings, one redeemed for each January." (P2) A forced backronym that reads engineered; the tie to the real portfolio is loose (see logic item 7).
- "A bank would call this a capital-protected note, except a bank would spend that interest on an option, which is worth nothing if the market stands still." (P5) Textbook voice; the sentence works hard to show the author knows structured products.
- Repeated spaced dashes ( – ) in P1, P2 and P4, and the repeated tight pairs ("small on purpose: no cheque and no floor depends on it") give a uniform, over-balanced rhythm throughout.
