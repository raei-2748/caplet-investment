## Step 3: sentences that read as machine-written, and why

1. "We don't invest toward these payments. We buy them." and "not a probability, but a purchase" (P3). A matched pair of punchy lines plus a "not X, but Y" tag with alliteration is the standard AI aphorism shape. A student could write it; two in one paragraph reads as polish.
2. "But a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn." (P7). A quotable closing line for a paragraph that otherwise gives no reasoning. It sounds like a line written to be quoted.
3. "Whatever else changes, the order will not: the cheques, then the floor, then the gift." (P9). Slogan and neat three-part list, used as a closer.
4. "Do not crush." (P10). A tagline sign-off. It is the one sentence a reader is most likely to take as a marketing device rather than a student's own idea, because the metaphor is never connected to the portfolio.
5. "To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten holdings, one redeemed for each January." (P2). A tidy backronym; the pharmacist-to-portfolio leap is too smooth and too clever for the surrounding plain writing.
6. "money she will own outright even if every share in the world fell to zero" (P5). The hyperbole feels engineered for effect, like a stress-test line from a template.
7. "A bank would call this a capital-protected note, except a bank would spend that interest on an option" (P5). Accurate, but a very compact piece of structured-products knowledge handed over without explanation; it reads as an expert summary, not a student working it out.
8. Whole-text rhythm: almost every paragraph is one or two short declaratives followed by a long sentence, and four of them end on a clipped line. That uniform cadence is the clearest overall machine signal.

## Step 4: sentences where the logic does not hold or is incomplete

1. **P3 against P5 and P4.** P3 says the first $300,000 "buys" the ladder and P4 says the cheques do not depend on the deposit. P5 then says the 2028 deposit "first finishes any rung the first $300,000 couldn't buy". If the $300k may not be enough, the cheques do depend on the 2028 deposit, which is what P4 said they did not. The paper does not say whether $300k is enough.
2. **P3, "Treasury bonds and funds that mature like bonds ... A Treasury held to maturity pays exactly what it promises".** The guarantee is true for Treasuries, not for funds. A fund that "matures like a bond" has no promised payout. The proof of certainty therefore covers only part of the ladder, and the paper never says how much.
3. **P3 against P8.** "Not a probability, but a purchase" is flatly stated, then P8 says the ladder "could fall short". The paper reconciles this only by moving the shortfall onto the floor and the kept stocks, which makes the "purchase" claim less absolute than the first claim sounded.
4. **P4, "income that dries up in exactly the kind of downturn that pushes bond prices up".** The bond price clause does not support "don't rely on the deposit"; it argues for holding bonds as a hedge, which is a separate point. Also the claim that her income falls in downturns is an assertion, not from the case.
5. **P5, "Most of what is left buys Treasuries ... The remainder, roughly the interest those Treasuries are expected to earn, goes into one world index fund".** No figure for either piece. The reader cannot tell the size of the stock sleeve, so cannot tell whether "half her stocks" in P6 is a small or a large top-up. Also "capital-protected" suggests the floor equals the whole deposit, but P5 buys the Treasuries with only "most of what is left".
6. **P5, "about 7.5% a year from those stocks".** No source. It is the one return assumption in the paper and the reader cannot tell where it comes from.
7. **P6, missing range and confidence.** The case says teams "must recommend the dollar range" and "state how confident they are". The IPS gives the structure (floor to floor plus half the stocks) but no dollar figures and no confidence level. A judge scoring against the case cannot find either.
8. **P6, "half".** The share kept and the share promised are split 50/50 with no reason given.
9. **P6 and P8, one pool of stocks doing two jobs.** The stocks Laura keeps are described as "liquid any day, whether the building costs more ... or she needs flexibility" (P6) and are also where the reinvestment shortfall is set aside (P8). The flexible money is therefore partly committed to the ladder, and the paper does not say how much flexibility remains. Also stocks are not liquid at a good price on "any day" after a fall.
10. **P8, "cash paid early earns less while it waits".** Unclear what "paid early" is (see LOST above). It also does not say how the shortfall is measured or how big it could be.
11. **P8, "whatever they can't cover comes off the bottom".** This makes the floor, described in P5 and P6 as "money she already owns", not fully certain. The text does not say how large that cut could be.
12. **P4/P9, "the gift" is used for the facility contribution before it is defined**, and P9's order "floor, then gift" treats the floor as separate from the gift when P6 says the floor is the bottom of the gift range.
13. **P2 against the closing line.** The metaphor says breaking the capsule ruins it; the portfolio's version of "breaking it" (selling early, or touching the ladder) is never stated, so the closing rule has no stated content.

## Overall

The pitch and P1, P3, P7 and P9 carry the idea well: payments are bought, the building gift sits on a Treasury floor, and only the interest goes into shares. The weak spots are P8 (the one real risk is explained in a way I misread), P5 (too much in one paragraph, floor never sized), P6 (no dollar range or confidence level, unexplained "half"), and the contradiction over whether the first $300,000 is enough.
