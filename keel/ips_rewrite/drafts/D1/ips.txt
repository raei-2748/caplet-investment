From 2033 Laura owes her residency $50,000 every January for ten years, and only her portfolio may pay it. Caplet XR buys those ten Januaries first, then builds her facility gift like a bank's capital-protected note, minus the option. X for ten, R for release: one Treasury holding timed to each January. To a rates trader a caplet is an option; this one holds none: the floor is a Treasury fund, the cap a rule.

Laura, a graphic novelist who studied statistics at Wharton, is planning a residency for artists, writers, designers, entrepreneurs and educators. With her living costs met elsewhere, she can afford "thoughtful risks". The payments cannot. We take "high degree of certainty" to mean every payment has its own Treasury bond or fund, bought by January 2028, maturing before it, interest reinvested.

Why first? Her 2028 deposit comes from publishing, speaking and licensing, and the slump that squeezes that income could also cut yields, making bonds dearer. So the $300,000 she already has buys them; any January gap is the 2028 deposit's first job.

From the rest of that deposit, a Treasury fund ending in 2032 is sized to grow back to about what remains: her floor, intact even if stocks go to zero. A sum equal to its expected interest buys one world index fund of about 10,000 companies, diversifying a plan whose payments rest on one issuer. The stock share resizes with yields, and Laura risks only interest her floor has not yet earned. The catch is less upside in a boom; in return, nothing can expire worthless. We assume stocks beat Treasuries over decades, not every two years.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a window. The bottom is the floor's projected payout, rounded down with a margin: money she already holds, so they hear a commitment, not a forecast. The top adds half the stocks' value that day; the other half stays hers, for flexibility as the project develops. In 2033 the same rule sets the gift, capped at that top, so mainly a two-year stock fall, or a payment needing help first, can pull it lower. Inflation cannot raise the fixed payments; rising facility costs are the index fund's job.

Until 2033 each holding is kept whole to its date; we trade only to invest new money, parked in a short-term Treasury fund. Reinvestment is the soft spot: if yields fall for good, coupons earn less; any gap comes from Laura's half first, the floor last. For liquidity, the funds sell any day and something matures before every payment. In 2033 the payment holdings become the operating reserve, one fewer each January. Each January we review the plan with Laura.

Moving floor money into stocks would raise the expected gift and could break a number Laura has said aloud. We refuse.

Laura owes ten Januaries. From 2028 she holds a Treasury for each.
