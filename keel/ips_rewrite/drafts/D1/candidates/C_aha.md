## PITCH

Caplet XR: a capital-protected note, no derivative inside. Laura's $300,000 goes first to ten $50,000 payments, in Treasuries held to maturity. From 2028, a Treasury fund grows back to about what remains: her gift's floor. Its expected interest buys stocks. They lift the gift, never sink it.

## IPS

A bank would sell Laura's facility gift as a capital-protected note: a bond that returns her money, plus an option on stocks. Wharton bans the option. Caplet XR keeps the bond and swaps the option for the stocks themselves, bought with nothing but the interest the bond is expected to earn. If stocks went to zero, Laura would still get about her money back. To a trader a caplet is an option; this one holds none. The floor is a Treasury fund, the cap a promise. XR: ten releases, one holding timed to each January from 2033.

The order follows. The ten $50,000 payments behind her residency for artists, writers, designers, entrepreneurs and educators come first, and nobody else may pay a cent. A slump that squeezed Laura's publishing, speaking and licensing income could also make payment bonds dearer, so the $300,000 she already has buys them before a single share. Any January gap is the first job of the $150,000 she has yet to earn.

We take "high degree of certainty" to mean each payment is owned in a Treasury holding that ends before it falls due, held whole to its date, interest reinvested. In 2033 they become the operating reserve, which shrinks by one holding each January; its ETFs give liquidity no small bond lot offers.

From the 2028 remainder, just enough buys a Treasury fund ending in 2032, sized to grow back to about that remainder: Laura's floor. The rest buys one world index fund, diversified across about 10,000 companies. The stock share is thus a rule, not a guess, resizing with yields. Laura has been willing to take "thoughtful risks"; here she risks only interest her floor has not yet earned. The catch, against a bank's note, is less upside in a boom. But nothing can expire worthless and no bank can fail her.

In 2031 Laura offers co-sponsors a window, not a number. The bottom is the floor's projected payout, rounded down with a margin: money she already holds, so they hear a commitment, not a forecast. The top adds half the stock fund's value that day. In 2033 the same rule sets her contribution, capped at that top, so only a two-year fall in stocks, or a payment needing help, can pull it lower. A statistics graduate seeking co-sponsors needs a number she never has to take back.

The other half of the stocks stays hers, for flexibility as the project develops and for Taiwan costs, where inflation bites; the payments are fixed in dollars, like Treasuries. A coupon gap from falling yields is filled from Laura's half, then the gift above the floor, the floor last.

Moving floor money into stocks would raise the expected gift and could break a number Laura has said aloud. We refuse. Stocks decide how generous Laura can be, never whether she pays.
