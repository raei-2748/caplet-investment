From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty. Part of what's left can help build the facility.

Our plan rests on one rule: a fixed payment is priced at what Treasuries charge to meet it, never at what shares might earn, because that counts a return nobody has earned yet. So once Laura owes a sum, she owns the Treasuries that pay it: the ten operating payments from 2027, and the credible range once she names it in 2031. She buys its bottom in 2028. The plan changes on those dates, never on a market move. We call it Caplet XR: X for its ten Treasury holdings, R for their redemption.

In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and fixed-date Treasury funds, each maturing before one January's payment. A Treasury bond held to maturity pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do, so we define high certainty as no payment depending on shares. Cash only looks safer: for fixed dollars on fixed dates, it bets that rates won't fall. No holding is sold early, even for the building.

The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, which sets her minimum facility contribution. What remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies.

Shares get only that because the deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in a recession that hits shares, so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice. The building's other money, from co-sponsors, grants, program fees and her business, can shrink in that same recession, so her contribution helps most as the part that doesn't.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a credible range. Her ten operating payments are already bought, so their money can go to the building, which funders much prefer to running costs. The bottom is what that late-2032 fund will pay, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, moved into Treasuries before she names it, because co-sponsors count on a number once she says it. The other half stays hers and liquid but is never promised to the building, even if costs rise in Taiwan dollars, since money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent.

The ladder's main weakness is its reliance on reinvested interest, which falls short if rates fall and stay low. Before Laura names her range, we price that gap as if every waiting dollar earned nothing and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries; if they can't cover it, she names a lower range.

From 2033, the ladder and that set-aside become her operating reserve, and each January's $50,000 rests on the holding redeemed before it, until the tenth pays the last in 2042.
