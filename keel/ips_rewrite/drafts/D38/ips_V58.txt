From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty. Part of what's left can help build the facility.

We call our plan Datum, the surveyors' word for the fixed point heights are measured from. Her portfolio isn't the residency's only money: co-sponsors, grants, program fees and her business income may also help, and each can shrink when share prices fall. Her portfolio is the one source whose amount can be settled before anyone is asked and then held there. So for every dollar, we ask whether the residency would miss it in a recession. It would miss the operating payments and her minimum facility contribution, so each goes into Treasuries as soon as its deposit arrives; shares hold only money nobody is counting on.

In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds with fixed end dates, each maturing before a payment and held until it does, so the ladder never needs rebalancing. A Treasury bond held to maturity pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do. So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares.

Shares are called safe over ten years, but time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed payment can't wait for a recovery. Laura's living costs are paid from outside the portfolio, so she could afford more risk; the residency couldn't, since a bad year would also cut its other money.

The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032, which sets her minimum facility contribution. What remains, roughly the interest that fund will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless. We would rather she owned the shares. Our plan changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a credible range. The bottom is that fund's payout, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year. The other half stays hers and liquid, as a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.

The ladder's main weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest; if rates fall and stay low, it can fall short. Before Laura names her range, we price that shortfall as if every dollar waiting for a payment earned nothing, and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first, and if it can't cover the gap, she names a lower range.

From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside become her operating reserve, paying each January's $50,000 until 2042, whatever happens to the residency's other money.
