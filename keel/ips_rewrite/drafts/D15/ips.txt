Laura has one promise and one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone. The hope is a gift for the building. They need different kinds of money, so we keep them apart.

We started with three-quarters in stocks, and our model sank it: about one future in thirty missed a cheque. $50,000 falls due every January, good year or dreadful, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom.

So we call it Caplet XR. On a pill, XR means extended release: the same dose on schedule, however much is left. Ours also means X for ten, R for redemption.

Her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques in January 2027: a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, each before its January but sellable any day. Every cheque rests on one borrower, the US government, deliberately: spread across ten safe-looking borrowers, a January would be missed more often.

What, then, is a high degree of certainty? Only two things break a cheque in our model. The US government defaults. Or bond yields fall two points before we buy, so the ladder costs more than $300,000, and her 2028 deposit comes in under about $85,000 instead of $150,000.

Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, but she has already taken the big one: the residency. And her income from advances, speaking and licensing shrinks in exactly the recessions that make bonds dear. So the cheques are bought with money she already has. Her 2028 deposit plugs any gap in the ladder, then builds her gift.

Most of it buys a floor: Treasuries that repay what's left of the deposit in late 2032. Bought in 2028, they cost less, and the difference – about a fifteenth of her money, by our sums – goes into a world index fund. In our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a balanced 60/40 portfolio usually leaves her richer. But one time in four it breaks a promise, mostly the gift.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a range: low enough to keep her word, high enough that her lead gift draws bigger ones. Anything the cheques might still need comes off first. The bottom is the floor she owns; the top adds half her stocks, and the other half stays hers as flexibility for a building priced in Taiwan dollars.

A capped range has a catch. Once it's named, a fall in stocks comes off the gift while a rise past the top goes to Laura. On the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries. In our model the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100, and the rare miss is about $1,000.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve. Nothing is sold before it pays – you don't crush an XR pill – and each turns to cash before its cheque, so the reserve runs itself down.
