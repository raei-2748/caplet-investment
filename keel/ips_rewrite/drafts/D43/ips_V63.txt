We call our plan Caplet XR. It’s named after extended-release caplets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled ‘do not crush’. Our ‘dose’ is Treasuries – Laura will, under our strategy, own the exact T-Bonds, T-Notes and Term Treasury ETFs she’ll need to fund her operating commitment. We chose Treasuries because her $50,000 payments are fixed and must be met with a high degree of certainty, and Treasuries pay fixed amounts on fixed dates, backed by the US government. At current Treasury prices her 10 operating payments would cost about $300,000 – which would generate roughly $200,000 in interest, therefore covering the $500,000 required in total – and whilst yields could of course look different by January 2027, we use these numbers as a guideline (and our plan is not dependent on them).

In January 2027 we will use her $300,000 deposit to buy a ladder of 10 Treasury holdings (one maturing before each January from 2033 to 2042), and the 2028 deposit will fund any which the 2027 one couldn’t, if necessary. Each holding is bought so that what it repays, with its interest, meets that year’s $50,000. X is the Roman numeral for 10, and our XR (10 Rungs) will not be crushed: we won’t sell them before they mature. Bond prices will rise and fall before then, but a holding kept to maturity still pays what it was bought to pay, so those swings don’t affect the payments.

The ladder is also her operating reserve. It is complete by January 2028 at the latest, five years before the first payment, so in 2033 there is nothing left to set aside or rearrange. From then on it releases its dose on schedule, like the caplet: one rung matures just before each January and pays that year’s $50,000, until the tenth pays the last one in 2042.

Any funds from the 2027 and/or 2028 deposits not needed by the ladder will go into a world index fund (VT, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF). This is the money for her facility contribution in 2033 and for the flexibility she wants as the project develops. Because no operating payment depends on it, this money can take the risk of shares, which over long periods have grown faster than bonds. That suits Laura, who has taken thoughtful risks throughout her career. One fund holding thousands of companies across the world spreads that risk widely, costs little, and can be sold on any trading day when the money is needed. Shares can fall, and the fund could be worth less in 2033 than it cost, but a fall would reduce only what she can contribute.

Our plan’s biggest strength, and our interpretation of ‘high degree of certainty’, is that – barring a US default – no operating payment is ever at risk, and they certainly never depend on a share price. Like the caplet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.
