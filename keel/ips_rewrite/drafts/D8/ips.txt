Every team will promise Laura her ten cheques. We would rather buy them.

Caplet XR in one rule: each promise is made safe no later than the day it becomes one. Until then it is a hope.

The cheques are promises from page one: $50,000 every January, 2033 to 2042, fixed in dollars. So her first $300,000 buys them in January 2027: Treasury bonds and funds that turn to cash before each January. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release – a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush. To us, X is ten and R is redemption: ten holdings, each redeemed in time for one cheque.

Our first plan put three-quarters in stocks, and the model had it missing cheques. A fixed-percentage withdrawal can ride out a crash; a fixed-dollar withdrawal is a forced sale at the bottom. Now the odd part: we spread the stocks across the world, but not the cheques. Ten independent bets, each 99% safe, would miss a cheque one decade in ten; ten debts of one government stand or fall as one.

And one reason only Laura has: her 2028 deposit comes from advances, speaking and licensing – income that can shrink in the same slump that makes bonds dearer. So money she has buys certainty; money she expects carries risk.

That deposit fills any gap in the ladder, then buys the floor – more of the first cheque's fund – sized to regrow the remainder by 2033. Roughly its expected interest buys a world index fund. Keep the seed; risk only the harvest.

Her 2031 range can embarrass her twice over. Too low, and co-sponsors give less – bigger lead gifts draw bigger gifts. Too high, and she breaks her word, which we judge three times worse. So the bottom is the floor she owns, rounded down; the top adds half the stocks, and the other half stays hers.

But a capped range hides a flaw: once named, the gift keeps every fall and hands every rise to Laura. So the day she names it, the promised half moves into Treasuries maturing before 2033.

Fixed promises have failed in four ways: debts that outgrew their assets, guarantees nobody priced, returns assumed instead of bought, and coupons reinvested as rates fell. Ours shuts the first three. The fourth is our soft spot, the one thing that can still trim the gift. So in January 2033 the reserve changes once: any projected shortfall, plus a cushion, moves into the shortest Treasuries, from leftover cash and Laura's half first.

Inflation can make $50,000 buy less; it cannot make $50,000 cost more. It and the Taiwan dollar can hurt the building – that is what Laura's half, sellable any day, is for. If needs change, cheques come first; floor and stocks shrink together.

No Treasury is sold before it pays – do not crush. The residency gets ten Januaries it can count on, and a building promised only from money already in Treasuries.
