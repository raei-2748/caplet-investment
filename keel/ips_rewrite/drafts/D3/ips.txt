In January 2033 Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten $50,000 cheques to her Taiwan residency, and nobody else may help.

We call it Caplet XR. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a dose let out on schedule, labelled do not crush. To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten Treasuries, each redeemed in time for one January.

We take "a high degree of certainty" to mean by January 2028 every payment sits in its own Treasury bond or defined-maturity Treasury fund, maturing before its date. The funds are near-ironclad too. By their December close, they hold nothing but cash.

Why every payment before a single share? Because Laura already owns plenty of stock-market risk – it just isn't in a brokerage account. Her 2028 deposit rests on book advances and speaking fees, and a slump that shrinks those would likely cut yields too, making bonds dearer as the deposit dwindles. So the $300,000 she already has buys the payments, latest-dated first; any gap is the 2028 deposit's first job. If that deposit comes in smaller, the facility gift shrinks. A payment never does.

A bank would sell Laura a capital-protected note: a bond that returns her money, an option on the market, and a fee. We keep the bond, swap the option for the stocks themselves and skip the fee. Most of the 2028 remainder buys a Treasury fund maturing in December 2032, sized to grow back to the whole – her floor, intact even if every share went to zero. The rest, its expected interest, buys one world index fund of 10,000 companies (picking winners is not on the menu). Laura risks only money she hasn't earned yet. The catch: a boom pays her less than an option would.

In 2031 Laura names a range. The bottom is the floor's projected payout, rounded down: money she already holds. The top adds half her stocks, valued that day. In 2033 the same rule sets the gift, capped at that top, so after 2031 only a two-year stock fall or a payment in need can pull it lower. The other half stays with Laura for overruns, inflation in Taiwan or a change of plan.

Moving floor money into stocks would raise the expected gift. But time shrinks the chance of a stock loss, not its size, and a number said aloud cannot be half-kept.

We refuse.

The soft spot is reinvestment. If yields fall for good, coupons earn less; any gap comes from Laura's half first and the floor last. We assume stocks beat Treasuries over decades, and inflation cannot touch fixed payments.

Until 2033 nothing is sold early. Do not crush. For liquidity, something matures before every January. In 2033 the payment holdings become the operating reserve, one fewer each January; we review the plan with Laura yearly. Our WInS portfolio is this plan in miniature.

Ten cheques, ten Januaries. Every one bought before the first is written.
