In January 2033 a $50,000 cheque leaves Laura's portfolio for her residency. Then 9 more, and only her portfolio may pay them. Caplet XR (X for ten, R for release) buys those 10 Januaries before a single share: one Treasury holding timed to each. To a rates trader, a caplet is an option. Ours holds none.

Laura, a graphic novelist who studied statistics at Wharton, is planning a residency for artists, writers, designers, entrepreneurs and educators. Her living costs are met elsewhere and nothing leaves before 2033, so she can afford "thoughtful risks". Her payments cannot. "High degree of certainty" means that by January 2028 every payment has its own Treasury bond or fund, maturing before it is due, interest reinvested.

Why put $300,000 into bonds before the 2028 deposit arrives? Because that deposit comes from publishing, speaking and licensing, and the slump that squeezes that income could also cut yields, making the bonds dearer. So the money she already has buys the payments, latest first; any January gap is the 2028 deposit's first job.

A bank would sell Laura a capital-protected note: a bond that returns her money, an option on the market, and the bank's fees. We keep the bond and drop the rest. From what remains of the 2028 deposit, a Treasury fund ending in 2032 is sized to grow back to about that sum: her floor, intact if stocks go to zero. The rest, equal to the interest that fund is expected to earn, buys one world index fund of about 10,000 companies. Laura risks only interest she has not yet earned. The price is less upside in a boom; in return, nothing can expire worthless.

In 2031 Laura gives co-sponsors a window. The bottom is the floor's projected payout, rounded down with a margin: money she already holds, so they hear a commitment, not a forecast. The top adds half the stocks' value that day; the other half stays hers, for flexibility as the project develops. In 2033 the same rule sets the gift, capped at that top. We assume stocks beat Treasuries over decades; over two years, anything goes. Inflation cannot touch fixed payments. Rising facility costs are the index fund's problem.

Until 2033 every holding is kept whole to its date. We trade only to invest new money; early maturities wait in a short-term Treasury fund. For liquidity, the funds sell any day and something matures before every payment. The soft spot is reinvestment. If yields fall and stay down, coupons earn less, and any gap comes from Laura's half first, the floor last. In 2033 the payment holdings become the operating reserve, 1 fewer each January. Each January we review the plan with Laura.

Moving floor money into stocks would raise the expected gift. It could also break a number Laura has said aloud.

We refuse.

Ten Januaries, ten cheques. Every one was bought before the first was written.
