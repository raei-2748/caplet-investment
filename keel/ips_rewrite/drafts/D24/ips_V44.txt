Laura Gao has one promise and one hope. The promise is $50,000 every January from 2033 to 2042, paid from this portfolio alone with a high degree of certainty. The hope is a gift towards the residency's building in Taiwan.

So we don't invest the promise. We buy it.

In January 2027 her first $300,000 buys all ten cheques – or nearly – as a ladder of Treasury bonds, and Treasury funds that mature like bonds, one rung falling due before each January.

A high degree of certainty is a list, not an adjective. The US government defaults. Or a recession arrives before we buy: it pushes yields down, so the ladder costs more, and it shrinks the advances, speaking and licensing behind the 2028 deposit that must cover the gap. One leak remains: coupons paid early earn less if yields fall. In 2031 we price that shortfall and set it aside from the hope, before any range is named.

Sell low, by appointment

There is a serious case against all this, and we made it first. Laura is willing to take thoughtful risks, and in our model, with world stocks at 7.5% a year, a 60/40 portfolio usually ends 2042 almost $200,000 ahead of ours.

But "usually" is the whole trick: about one time in three the 60/40 misses a cheque or breaks its word to co-sponsors; ours, under one in sixty. Laura doesn't owe one ten-year bill. She owes ten one-year bills, and a crash in the wrong year forces a sale at the bottom.

Have your cake and invest the icing

Most of the 2028 deposit buys Treasuries maturing late in 2032, just enough to grow back into all of it: her floor. What's left – the interest those bonds should earn – buys one world index fund. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note. Ours swaps the bank's option for shares and leaves out the bank (and its fee).

In 2031 she gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor she has owned since 2028. The top is the floor plus half her stocks. The other half stays hers, to sell any day if a building priced in Taiwan dollars comes in dearer.

A capped range has a catch: once it is named, every fall in stocks comes off the gift while every rise past the top goes to Laura. So on the day she names it, the half she promised moves into Treasuries, and in our model the gift reaches its top about 97 times in 100.

From 2033 the ten holdings are the operating reserve, and it runs itself down: each turns to cash before its cheque, with nothing sold early and nothing to rebalance. If anything changes, the cheques come first, then the floor, then the hope. We call the plan Caplet XR, after the extended-release capsule (X for ten, R for redemption): one cheque a year, and it only works whole.

Do not crush.
