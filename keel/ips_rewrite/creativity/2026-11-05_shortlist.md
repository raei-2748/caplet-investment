# Four ideas that are thinking, not packaging

AI DRAFT (Claude, Thu 5 Nov 2026). Keel is a simulated team, AI-written. For Ray and Ahaan to choose from before
anything is rewritten. Asked for by Ray, 5 Nov: "We want intellectual creativity, yours just packaging."

**The honest read.** A Treasury ladder plus a world share fund is what many strong teams will submit. Our guess at
their version: bonds for the payments, 10-25% in shares, "95-99% of model paths pay every payment", a glide path, and a
2031 range read off model percentiles at 80-90% confidence. What can set us apart is four ideas. Two are already in our
own work (one is Ray's October reflection) but the final draft hides them behind the tablet; two are new tonight. All
four agree with the submitted trading notes and TNA, need no trade, and need no numbers in the IPS.

## 1. Price every commitment at what Treasuries charge, and buy it the day it's made
A fixed payment has one honest price: what Treasuries charge to deliver it. Price it at what shares might earn and it
looks cheaper, but the difference is a return nobody has earned yet, and every extra share makes it look cheaper
still. US public pensions price their promises that way because their rules allow it, and research finds it pushed
them into more risk. Laura makes three commitments on three dates: the ten operating payments (2027), a minimum facility
contribution (2028) and a credible range (2031). Each one is matched with Treasuries the day she makes it. Naming a
range counts: once she says it aloud, co-sponsors hold her to it whatever markets do, so she owns it before she says it.
- **Why it's clever:** it is the central idea of liability investing, and one rule explains every move the plan makes.
  It grows from Ray's own reflection 1 ("a nominal Treasury matches the liability perfectly"). A typical strong team
  prices certainty at share-like returns and quotes a range it doesn't own.
- **In the IPS:** becomes the central idea, in the pitch and where the tablet paragraph sits now.

## 2. Her ten bought payments are her strongest fact for co-sponsors (new)
Funders avoid paying running costs. In a field test of 40,000 mailed appeals, telling donors that a lead donor had
already paid the overhead raised total giving 75% more than presenting the same gift as seed money (Gneezy, Keenan and
Gneezy, *Science*, 2014). When Laura meets co-sponsors in 2031, her ten operating payments are already bought, so their
money can go to the building, where funders like it to go.
- **Why it's clever:** the plan's dullest choice, buying the payments first, is also its best fundraising move, and it
  is what the case means by a contribution that shows the residency is "financially viable".
- **In the IPS:** one sentence in the 2031 paragraph. It covers her $50,000 a year, not all the running costs.

## 3. Diversify the residency, not just her account (new framing)
The case lists where the rest of the building's money comes from: co-sponsors, grants, collaborators, program fees and
business income. All of them can shrink in the same recession. US giving fell in 2008 and again in 2009, and after past
recessions took three to five years to recover (Giving USA 2010), and foundations must pay out a share of their own investments each year, so their grants
follow the market. The most useful thing Laura's contribution can be is the part of the residency's money that doesn't
shrink, which is why it is bought in Treasuries early.
- **Why it's clever:** it answers Wharton's "appropriate diversification" for the whole residency. The final draft
  answers it only with "thousands of companies".
- **In the IPS:** one sentence beside the 2028 deposit.

## 4. Keep what she holds back unpromised (new)
Money a project knows it can call on tends to get spent: economists call it a soft budget (Kornai, Maskin and Roland,
2003). In a study of over 8,000 charities, a government grant cut private giving by about 75 cents per dollar, mostly
because the charity raised less itself (Andreoni and Payne, 2011). And more than 80% of the US arts buildings in one
University of Chicago study ran over budget. So what Laura keeps stays hers and is never promised to the building. The
final draft says the opposite: it calls her kept half "a cushion if the building costs more".
- **Why it's clever:** it turns flexibility, our weakest answer, into a reason. Flexibility is money nobody else can
  count on, not just money kept back.
- **In the IPS:** replaces the cushion clause.

## The hardest question a judge could ask
"Treasuries alone would leave her about $190,000 for the building by 2033 (we modelled, September curve). Why is your
bottom lower?" Our answer: she risks only the interest, never the deposit, because at today's yields shares are
expected to add only a little over five years, and the case asks for some growth. A higher bottom would need a smaller
share fund and a WInS trade before Sat 7 Nov 09:00 Sydney. We don't recommend changing the trades this late.
