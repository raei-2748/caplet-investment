## Rewrite A

From 2033, Laura's portfolio has to pay $50,000 every January for ten years so she can run her Taiwan residency, with high certainty. Any surplus can help pay for the facility.

We call the plan Caplet XR, after extended-release tablets, which come with a label saying don't crush them. Our plan is ten Treasury holdings, one repaid each January. X is for ten, R is for redemption. If you crush one, you sell it before it matures, even to pay for the building.

Pension funds cover fixed payments in one of two ways: they match the timing of their bonds to the payments and keep adjusting, or they buy one bond for each payment and hold it. We do the second, which never needs adjusting. In January 2027, Laura's first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, each one ending before a January payment. A Treasury bond held to the end pays what it promised. So for us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares.

Shares are called safe over ten years, but over time a loss becomes rarer, not smaller, and a fixed cheque can't wait for a recovery. Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same downturns that hit share prices. So her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice.

The 2028 deposit first finishes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund that matures in late 2032: the floor of her gift. What's left, about the interest the floor will earn, goes into one world index fund. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless. We'd rather she owned the shares. We also don't copy portfolio insurance, which sells shares as they fall and helped make the 1987 crash worse. Our plan only changes on fixed dates.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said out loud can't be quietly cut in half after a bad year. The other half stays hers and easy to reach, a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.

The ladder's main weakness is that it relies on reinvesting the interest its bonds pay. If rates fall and stay low, it can fall short. So before Laura names her range, we work out that shortfall as if every dollar waiting for a cheque earned nothing, and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first. If that can't cover the gap, the whole range comes down.

From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside become her operating reserve, paying each January's cheque until 2042. Like the tablet it's named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.
