A student should open the page before anyone cites it.
- How I understand it (my words, plus Mum's stockroom): two names are easy to confuse, so you capitalise only the letters where they split (hydrOXYzine / hydrALAZINE style). It works because the eye is pulled to the part that matters, at the moment of picking the box, and because it is used sparingly on pairs that really have been mixed up. It fails when (a) too many names are marked, so nothing stands out, (b) the capitals sit on the wrong letters, (c) people treat "unmarked" as "safe", (d) it is somewhere you only look once, not where your hand is. That list is from the shelf, not from the PDF; the PDF says only the first part.

## The tests (each applied in about a minute; one fail = send back)

1. **Voice test.** Read the card top to bottom and ask: does it hand anyone a sentence to copy? PASS: no row gives a "say it like this" line longer than a short phrase, no "words to keep" list, and the card says in a line that they should reword freely. FAIL: a row reads like a replacement sentence, or six students would end up with the same phrase. (Wharton wants an authentic team voice; the card fences facts, it does not write the IPS.)

2. **Real-fact test.** For each row, find the true fact in the final IPS in under 30 seconds. PASS: the true side matches a sentence or clause actually in the final IPS (or the submitted trading notes), nothing more, nothing rounder. FAIL: any row states something the IPS does not say, or a truer-sounding version the IPS never claims.

3. **Real-twin test.** Would a Year 11 student, rewriting quickly, really write the false version? PASS: each false line is something I could imagine in a rewrite, or one that already stood in a draft. FAIL: any twin that is silly, or that only an expert could make, or that is the true line with a random word changed.

4. **Capitals test (the actual tall-man part).** PASS: the capitals fall only on the word or two where false and true differ, and the rest of the two lines is identical. FAIL: capitals on a whole phrase, on shared words, or on the true line only. If I cannot see the difference in two seconds, it is not working.

5. **Could-a-row-mislead test.** Read each false twin cold, then each "why it's false" line. PASS: the reason is correct on its own and does not itself overclaim (no new "always", "exactly", "guaranteed" that the IPS avoids); and no twin is partly true in some other reading. FAIL: a row that teaches a student a wrong rule by being too blunt, e.g. "funds never pay out" when the point is that they pay their value that day, not a promised par amount.

6. **One-page, plain-English test.** PASS: fits one printed page at normal size, and a Year 11 gets each row first time without a glossary; no room names, no F- or version labels, no file names, no numbers, no codes (Ray, I80). FAIL: spills onto a second page, uses a label only Keel knows, or needs a footnote.

7. **Fence-not-script test (cap on rows).** PASS: every row is a place where a break has actually happened or is clearly likely, and the card is short enough that all rows get read. FAIL: padded with "just in case" rows; in the stockroom, too many marked pairs and you stop seeing them.

8. **My own test: the false-comfort test.** A pharmacy's danger with tall man lettering is that a name with no capitals feels safe. PASS: the card says plainly, once, that it is not a list of everything that can go wrong, and tells the student what to do when their sentence matches no row (check the changed word against the IPS line it came from). FAIL: the card reads as complete, so a student whose break is not on it thinks they are clear.

## Pass mark
All eight or send back. Tests 1, 2 and 5 are the ones I will not bend on: a card that hands out wording, invents a fact, or teaches a wrong reason does more harm than no card.
