You are a 67-year-old retired carpenter in Hobart who keeps his savings in a bank term deposit and has never bought a share or a bond. Someone hands you a short investment plan written by a team of high-school students for a client called Laura Gao, and asks what you understood. Read as that person would, honestly. If a word means nothing to you, say so; don't be cleverer than the person you are playing.

Rules: answer only from the texts; don't use finance knowledge the person you play wouldn't have; never look anything up or open any file; keep each answer short; number your answers 1-14.

THE PITCH (sits above the plan):
Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are a promise, so we don't invest for them. We buy them. Her first $300,000 buys Treasuries that each mature before a January. Her 2028 deposit buys a Treasury floor for her building gift, and only the interest that floor should earn goes into world stocks.

FIRST TEXT:
In January 2033, Laura's portfolio writes the first of ten cheques: $50,000 each, one every January, toward running her Taiwan residency. She also hopes to contribute to its building.

We call our plan Caplet XR. To a pharmacist, XR means extended release: a caplet that delivers its dose on schedule, labelled do not crush. Crush it and the schedule is gone. To us, X is ten and R is redemption – ten holdings, one redeemed for each January. Crushing one means selling it before it matures, even for the building.

In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and funds with an end date, one rung falling due before each cheque. A Treasury held to maturity pays exactly what it promises, whatever markets do. That, to us, is what "a high degree of certainty" means.

Laura's career already behaves like a stock. Her 2028 deposit depends on publishing advances, speaking fees and licensing – income that can dry up in a downturn, when interest rates usually fall, making bonds cost more. If that deposit arrives smaller, the gift shrinks. A cheque funded by a Treasury already bought does not.

Her 2028 deposit finishes any rung the first $300,000 couldn't buy. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032. That is her floor. The remainder, roughly the interest the floor should earn by then, goes into one world index fund, spread across thousands of companies. That is under a tenth of both her deposits. A bank's capital-protected note also starts with a bond, but spends that interest on an option, a bet that expires worthless unless the market rises. Stocks don't expire. We assume world stocks earn about 7.5% a year, but the cheques never depend on it.

In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range for her gift to the building. The bottom is her floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her stocks. Before she names it, that half moves into Treasuries, so every dollar she promises is already in bonds. The other half stays with Laura, liquid any day, as her flexibility, in case the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.

We could put the floor into stocks and chase a bigger gift. But a number said aloud to co-sponsors cannot be quietly halved if markets turn.

The weak point is reinvestment. The ladder costs less than the cheques because its bonds pay interest along the way, which we reinvest. If yields fall and stay low, that interest earns less, and the ladder falls short. Before she names the range, we price that gap as if nothing more is reinvested, and move that much from the stocks Laura keeps into Treasuries, so her flexibility shrinks before her gift does. Whatever the stocks can't cover lowers the whole range.

From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside for its gap become the operating reserve, paying out one rung per cheque.

Do not crush.
