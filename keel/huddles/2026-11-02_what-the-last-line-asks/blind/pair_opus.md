**Better overall:** UCPK

**Reasons:**

UCPK: Stress-tested against the case's cash flows, the plan holds. The ten payments are bought, not forecast; a short 2027 ladder is finished from the 2028 deposit; and the one real hole, reinvesting coupons before 2033, is named, priced in 2031, and paid for from Laura's own stocks before the floor is touched. The floor pays only if the stocks can't, so the cheques come first even in the bad case where yields fall and shares fall together. This version closes that loop: the gap money joins the operating reserve in 2033, so the reserve's size is the ladder plus what its known shortfall needs. It also turns the metaphor into a rule: no rung is sold before its January, "even for the building". Weaknesses shared with DYEY: diversification is thin by design (Treasuries plus one world fund); target-maturity funds are said to pay "exactly what it promises", which only a single Treasury does; Laura's taste for thoughtful risk gets under a tenth of her money; and there is almost no teamwork or learning.

DYEY: The strategy and analysis are the same, and so are the scores. It ends with a clear waterfall ("the cheques, then the floor, then everything above it"), a good statement of principle, and it states the central idea inside the IPS itself. Read with the pitch, though, that idea is an almost word-for-word repeat. More important to an actuary, its reserve is "the ladder" alone. The reinvestment cushion set aside in 2031 is never placed in the 2033 reserve, so the reserve as defined is the one the text has just said may fall short. "Do not crush" also never gets the concrete no-early-sale rule behind it. I checked that no single line made the call. UCPK's two differences, the reserve closure and the sell rule, both strengthen its matching discipline. DYEY's two are a pitch echo and a summary of an order the text already shows. Taken as a whole, UCPK is the tighter policy.
