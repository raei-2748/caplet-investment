# Judge fable: rankings

Judge's note before the table. The three texts are the same plan in the same words; they differ in four sentences (the end of the Caplet XR paragraph, a "we buy them" line in the ladder paragraph, the last line of the reinvestment paragraph, and the definition of the operating reserve). I caught myself about to move a criterion score on one of those lines, so I re-read each text as a whole: the whole is identical in thesis, evidence, voice and gaps, and the criterion scores are therefore identical. The order below is a tie-break on which whole holds together best, and I say so.

What I learned that I did not know (the test I set every entry, since a bond ladder is table stakes): three things, present in all three texts. First, the growth sleeve is sized to the floor Treasury's expected interest, so the structure is a capital-protected note with shares in place of the option, and the team says why shares beat the option (they do not expire worthless if markets stand still). Second, Laura's 2028 deposit is correlated with the cost of the ladder: her income falls in the downturns that make Treasuries dearer, so the cheques are bought with the deposit already in hand. Third, the promised half of the stocks is moved into Treasuries before the range is spoken, so the promise is a holding, not a forecast, and the reinvestment gap is priced and set aside before the range is named. Those lift the strategy from a 3 to a 4; a 5 would need the diversification and inflation questions to be met rather than argued around (Treasuries and one world fund only; a fixed nominal liability against a Taiwan-dollar facility cost, met with one clause on flexibility).

Scores common to all three. Investment Strategy 4: a clear thesis (buy the ten cheques, keep the floor, risk only the interest), disciplined across 2027, 2028, 2031 and 2033-2042, with the rejected alternative named; diversification is thin and defended rather than built. Client Knowledge 4: her income sources, the credibility of a number said aloud to co-sponsors and the Taiwan-dollar exposure come straight from the case; her stated appetite for thoughtful risk is answered only by "her career already behaves like a stock", which is a good answer but the only one. Portfolio Analysis 4: hold-to-maturity certainty, reinvestment risk, the note-minus-option decomposition and a stated 7.5% assumption; no probabilities or modelled outcomes, and "how it changes as payments approach" is answered with one clause. Articulation 2: the decision framework and a weak point are stated, but nothing about teamwork, research process or learning. Creativity and Presentation 4: an authentic voice, a chronological narrative, a metaphor that does work, and a range mechanism a co-sponsor could follow; data use is light.

**Overall order:** UHPT > KMGK > DYEY

**Reasons:**

UHPT. The most coherent whole. Its reinvestment paragraph ends by subordinating the promise to the cheques ("whatever the stocks can't cover lowers the whole range"), and its reserve definition then includes "everything set aside for it", so the cushion priced in 2031 lands where the text said it would. Pairwise it beats KMGK because the unfavourable case is closed explicitly rather than left to the priority order, and beats DYEY because every paragraph agrees with the next. Its cost is plainness: "none sold" is the tersest statement of the rule.

KMGK. The best statement of the rule: "Crushing one means selling it before its January, even for the building" names the actual temptation and turns the label into a commitment device, which as a decision scientist I rate highly. Its reserve definition is also complete. It ranks second because the reinvestment paragraph stops at "her flexibility shrinks before her promise does" and never says what happens if the stocks she keeps cannot cover the gap; the closing priority order patches that, but the reader has to do the patching.

DYEY. The sharpest thesis sentence ("We don't invest toward these payments. We buy them.") and the complete treatment of the shortfall, but the last paragraph does not agree with the one before it: a cushion is set aside to cover the ladder's gap, and then the operating reserve is "the ladder" alone, "one bond per cheque", after the ladder was defined as bonds and funds that mature like bonds. The ideas are identical to the other two; the whole is the least consistent of the three, which is why it is third on a tie-break and not on the scores.
