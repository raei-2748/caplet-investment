## The pass-mark maths (exact binomial; re-run by hand: for n of n passing, the bound is alpha^(1/n))

Assume each reader passes a question independently with the same true rate p. The exact (Clopper-Pearson) one-sided 95%
lower bound on p, for x of n passing:

| Readers passing | Lower bound on the true pass rate (one-sided 95%) | Two-sided 95% |
|---|---|---|
| 3 of 3 | 0.05^(1/3) = **0.368** | 0.025^(1/3) = 0.292 |
| 2 of 2 (today's two strangers) | 0.05^(1/2) = 0.224 | 0.158 |
| 2 of 3 | 0.135 | 0.094 |
| 16 of 20 (the regulator's mark) | 0.599 | 0.563 |
| 20 of 20 | 0.861 | 0.832 |

- **Certify?** No. Three of three is consistent with a true pass rate as low as 37%, as opposed to the regulator's 81%.
  To show 81% at 95% with no misses takes n with 0.81^n <= 0.05, so n >= ln 0.05 / ln 0.81 = 14.2: **fifteen readers, all
  passing**. For 90% it is 29.
- **Falsify?** Only in the logical sense. A question sitting exactly at 81% gets all three right 0.81^3 = 53.1% of the
  time, so it trips at least one reader 46.9% of the time; one miss does not prove the true rate is below 81%. Against 81%,
  1 of 3 passing has p = 0.095 and only 0 of 3 (p = 0.19^3 = 0.0069) rejects it at 5%. What one miss does prove is that
  the sentence **can** be misread by a reader of that kind: an existence proof, enough to send a line back to the Writing
  Room, never a rate.
- **Even the regulator** doesn't certify at 95%: 16 of 20 has a lower bound of 0.599, and a question truly at 81% meets
  16 of 20 only 67.3% of the time. Its mark is a convention held up by method.
- **Our bound flatters us:** our readers are two model families playing readers ("Simulated people are tests, never
  quotes"), not independent draws from real people.
- **One honest sentence for Nora's judge sheet:** "Three readers are the regulator's pilot, not its test: a question any
  one of them gets wrong goes back to the Writing Room, and a question all three get right is marked 'not failed', never
  'passed', because three out of three fits a true pass rate as low as 37%, as opposed to the 81% a regulator asks of
  every question."
