## The text (the final, V57 after today's fixes; pitch 48, IPS 495 strict; AI DRAFT, GPTZero not yet checked)
Pitch: "Laura's ten $50,000 cheques are promises, so we pay for them up front: her first $300,000 buys Treasuries that
mature one before each January from 2033. Her 2028 deposit buys Treasuries that set a minimum building gift, and only
the interest those would earn goes into world shares."

- **P1** From 2033, Laura's portfolio must pay $50,000 each January for ten years towards running her Taiwan residency, with a high degree of certainty. Part of what's left can help build the facility.
- **P2** We call the plan Caplet XR, after extended-release tablets, which release their dose on schedule and come labelled "do not crush". Ours is ten Treasury holdings, one redeemed before each January: X for ten, R for redemption. Crushing one means selling it before it matures, even for the building.
- **P3** Pension funds cover fixed payments either by matching their bonds' average timing to the payments' and rebalancing as rates move, or by buying one bond per payment and holding it. We do the second, which never needs rebalancing. In January 2027, her first $300,000 buys a ladder of Treasury bonds and Treasury funds with fixed end dates, each maturing before a January payment. A Treasury bond held to maturity pays exactly what it promised, whatever markets do. So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares.
- **P4** Shares are called safe over ten years, but time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed cheque can't wait for a recovery. Her 2028 deposit comes from book advances, speaking fees and licensing, which can dry up in the same downturns that hit share prices, so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice.
- **P5** The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late 2032: the floor of her gift. What remains, roughly the interest that floor will earn, goes into one world index fund holding thousands of companies. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option that can expire worthless. We would rather she owned the shares. Nor do we copy portfolio insurance, which sells shares as they fall and helped deepen the 1987 crash. Our plan changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move.
- **P6** In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud can't quietly be halved after a bad year. The other half stays hers and liquid, as a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan dollars.
- **P7** The ladder's main weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest; if rates fall and stay low, it can fall short. Before Laura names her range, we price that shortfall as if every dollar waiting for a cheque earned nothing, and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first, and if it can't cover the gap, she names a lower range.
- **P8** From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside become her operating reserve, paying each January's cheque until 2042. Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it.

## Facts you may lean on (checked today at their pages; sources in `../2026-11-04_checked-only-by-its-writers/`)
- **The two methods.** CFA Institute refresher (2026): "immunization is achieved by matching the Macaulay duration" of
  assets and liabilities, and "the portfolio needs to be rebalanced". Western Asset (2023): "Cash Flow Matching
  Eliminates Rebalancing". Kocherlakota, Rosenbloom and Shiu, *Transactions of the Society of Actuaries* (1988):
  "cash carry-forward (at low or zero interest)" is "a major variation" of cash-flow matching. In general, an
  immunised book pays each liability partly by **selling** bonds at that day's price; a matched book pays it from a
  bond that **matures** into it.
- **Our ladder isn't duration-matched, and can't be.** Rung by rung the ladder's duration is about 7.6 years against
  10.0 for the cheques (MODEL, 23 Oct prices; idea bank #167), because coupon bonds pay interest years before their
  cheque. That early interest is exactly what P7 must reinvest. STRIPS (zero-coupon Treasuries, which would remove the
  gap) are banned by Wharton this year.
- **Capital-protected notes.** SEC, *Structured Notes with Principal Protection* (2 Jun 2011): "typically reflect the
  combination of a zero-coupon bond" with a derivative; protection "will depend on the creditworthiness of the
  issuer". FINRA (12 Apr 2023): the holder "could earn no return for the entire term of the note, even if you hold the
  note to maturity". Caps on the upside and the bank's fees are standard; Lehman's protected notes (2008) are the
  famous credit failure (Final Report material, not re-read today).
- **Portfolio insurance, 1987.** Brady report (1988), p. 41: formulas "dictated the sale of $20 to $30 billion of
  equities"; Shiller (1988) dissents: "the simple story does not settle the issue". The final says "helped deepen".
- **Time diversification.** Bodie, FAJ 1995: the cost of insuring against underperforming bonds rises with the horizon.
- **Human capital.** Ibbotson et al., *Lifetime Financial Advice* (CFA Institute RF, 2007): earning power "can be
  viewed as a 'stock' if both the correlation ... and the volatility of labor income are high".
- **The case.** "a high degree of certainty" is the case's phrase for the payments; she "will contribute an
  additional $150,000 at the beginning of 2028, using earnings from publishing advances, speaking engagements,
  licensing, and other entrepreneurial ventures" (`../../../competition/official/2026_27/Laura_Gao_2026_Client_Profile.txt` :43-45).
- **A chair's note while preparing (not the question):** P3's last sentence, "So, to us, high certainty means ...", was
  added after the cold check ran, so the I80 list was never applied to it; V55 and V56 said "That is what we mean by a
