Note: the five versions are the same word for word except the third paragraph (the "Pension funds..." paragraph). Lengths run from 490 to 494 words. Problems that appear in every version are listed once, under V.

Version V
Q1. Each cheque is paid for in advance. A Treasury bond that matures just before that January is bought and held to the end, so the payment never depends on what a bond sells for on the day, or on shares. This suits Laura because her own income (advances, speaking, licensing) dries up in the same downturns that sink shares. The cheques shouldn't depend on shares as well. Growth comes only from the interest the gift floor would have earned.
Q2. The closing line, which is in all five: "Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it." "X for ten, R for redemption" is cute too. In V only, "High certainty means no payment depends on shares." stands on its own without "So", so it reads as a slogan rather than a conclusion.
Q3. "In 2027" is vaguer than "January 2027", and that matters for a ladder built around January dates. Problems in every version:
- "Treasury funds with fixed end dates" is unexplained jargon, and a fund's final payout is not fixed the way a bond's is.
- The reinvestment "weakness" is unclear. The text never says the bond interest arrives years before the cheques start, so a reader wonders why one-bond-per-payment needs any reinvesting.
- "the money set aside" in the last paragraph is unclear: which money?

Version N
Q1. The same plan. The paragraph ends "So high certainty means...", which presents the definition as following from how a bond behaves.
Q2. Only the shared closing line.
Q3. Unclear: "matching their bonds' average timing to the payments' and rebalancing". Rebalancing for what reason? Without "as rates move", a reader can't see why that method needs constant upkeep. That upkeep is the whole reason to prefer one bond per payment.

Version G
Q1. The same plan, but the reason given for one bond per payment is the team's convenience ("never needs rebalancing"), not the benefit to Laura.
Q2. "We do the second, which never needs rebalancing." It reads as a mic-drop.
Q3. Untrue: "never needs rebalancing". The plan itself reinvests bond interest and, in 2031, moves shares into Treasuries to cover the reinvestment gap. That is rebalancing. Also an overclaim: "pays exactly what it promised" is true of a single bond, but the ladder also holds funds whose end value is not promised.

Q2. "So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares." The "to us" is throat-clearing before the line lands.
Q3. The same two problems as G: "never needs rebalancing" is untrue, and "exactly" overclaims for the funds.

Version B
Q1. The same as N, but it keeps the reason the other method needs rebalancing, and it states the benefit in Laura's terms: no cheque depends on a sale price that January.
Q2. Only the shared closing line.
Q3. Nothing beyond the problems shared by all five. Dropping "whatever markets do" costs nothing, because the sentence before it already makes that point, and the shorter claim is harder to attack.

Across all five
Q4. B. It keeps the full contrast between the two methods pension funds use: matching the bonds' average timing ("and rebalancing as rates move") versus buying one bond per payment. It then gives the reason in terms of the client: "We do the second, so no cheque depends on what a bond sells for that January." That sentence names the exact risk being removed, which is having to sell at whatever price that January offers. It also promises nothing that the reinvestment paragraph later takes back. V is a close second and N third, held back by its unexplained "rebalancing". G and P come last, because their version of that sentence is the one that is untrue. The best investing sentence in the whole piece is shared by all five: "time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed cheque can't wait for a recovery." It answers the "stocks are safe over ten years" belief in a way that fits fixed cheques.

Q5. V, with N close behind. In a bad year Laura wants a statement about her cheques, not about method. V gives her two in a row: "A Treasury bond held to maturity pays what it promised, whatever markets do. High certainty means no payment depends on shares." Without the "So", the second sentence reads as a flat promise. Add the shared "Our plan changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move." and she knows nobody will panic-sell for her. G and P sound even calmer ("exactly", "never needs rebalancing"), but they would cost her trust in 2031 when shares are moved to cover the interest shortfall. B is the most accurate, but it drops "whatever markets do", which is the phrase that would actually reassure a client watching a downturn.
