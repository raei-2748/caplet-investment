**Jun (round 3):** Amara, I checked my own line first. It was half wrong.

Diehl and Stroebe did count good ideas, once, in their first experiment: real groups 3.00, alone-then-pooled 13.33. Good ideas tracked total ideas (r = .82), so they stopped rating. Nobody there measured the best idea.

Girotra, Terwiesch and Ulrich did, at Wharton of all places. Ten minutes alone, then twenty together, beat thirty together: 28.45 ideas to 11.82, and a better top five. You win the order.

Two things you won't like. Building on each other's ideas made them worse, not better. And nobody can pick: the makers' rankings barely matched outside judges (about 0.16, teams worse). Rietzschel found the chosen ideas no better than chance.

Numbers are their own fight. Lorenz: show people each other's guesses and they bunch up and feel surer, no more accurate. Navajas: guess alone, argue in fives, guess again, and four groups beat a crowd of thousands. Graefe and Armstrong, groups our size: open meetings came last, if not significantly. A plain average of sealed guesses got most of the gain.

Rule I'd propose:
- Ideas: ten minutes alone, posted, then together to compare and cut. Someone outside the pile does the pick.
- Computed numbers: nobody votes. Re-run it.
- Judgement numbers (the shortfall odds, the range's confidence): sealed guess with one line of why, then reasons, not numbers, then guess again alone. Median, both rounds on the sheet.

Never open a call with "so what do we reckon?"
