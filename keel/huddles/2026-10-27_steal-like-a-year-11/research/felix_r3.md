## 1. The curse of knowledge: why we can't cold-read our own IPS

**Newton's tappers (Stanford, 1990), via Chip and Dan Heath, "The Curse of Knowledge", Harvard Business Review,
December 2006** (source 1; a copy of the HBR article hosted on a school's site, not HBR's own page). I did not open
Newton's dissertation itself: everything below is the Heaths' account of it.
- "Over the course of Newton's experiment, 120 songs were tapped out."
- "Listeners guessed only three of the songs correctly: a success ratio of 2.5%."
- The tappers had guessed in advance how often they'd be understood: "They predicted 50%."
- Why: "When a tapper taps, it is impossible for her to avoid hearing the tune playing along to her taps."
- "The problem is that once we know something—say, the melody of a song—we find it hard to imagine not knowing it."
- Their cure: "Leaders can thwart the curse of knowledge by 'translating' their strategies into concrete language."

**We take that to mean:** asking anyone in Keel or Caplet "is this clear?" is asking a tapper. All twelve of us hear
the tune when we read "ladder", "headroom", "iBond" or "floor". The ratio is the warning, not the exact number: they
expected to be understood twenty times more often than they were. A cold read is the only test of clarity that
isn't the author grading himself.

## 2. Murder boards: hostile questions from people who know the subject

**Wikipedia, "Murder board"** (source 2; tertiary, a starting point only, not a source to cite):
- A murder board "is a committee of questioners set up to critically review a proposal" "and/or help someone prepare
  for a difficult oral examination."
- "The term originated in the U.S. military, specifically from the Pentagon." The page also reports a NASA claim that
  it "was created by Hans Mark, Director of Ames Research Center from 1969 to 1977."
- "murder boards are used in preparing candidates for debates and presidential appointees for Senate confirmation
  hearings."

**We take that to mean:** a murder board is insiders being hostile on purpose. It rehearses the hard question before
the real audience asks it. That is Kai in the Courtroom, not Jun's sister. It is a different job from a cold read,
and mixing them up is how you end up asking a non-finance reader whether the duration match is right.

## 3. Red teams: the UK Ministry of Defence handbook says mix experts and non-experts

**Red Teaming Handbook, UK Ministry of Defence, Development, Concepts and Doctrine Centre, 25 Jun 2021** (source 3;
primary, read through the page reader; quotes split by the reader into short pieces).
- Definition (para 1.9): red teaming is "the independent application of a range of structured, creative and critical
  thinking techniques" "to assist the end user make a better-informed decision".
- It gives "an external viewpoint separate to that of 'home team' decision-makers" (para 1.3).
- Why outsiders (para 7.1): "not invested in the plan and agnostic about its success or failure"; "not involved in the
  planning and decision-making and so can bring fresh perspectives to established problems".
- The costs, in the handbook's own words (para 7.2): outsiders can be "resented and mistrusted by the original
  planning team"; "insensitive if not managed well"; "used selectively to do 'hatchet jobs' by an uninformed or
  unscrupulous end user"; "seen as not sufficiently expert or well placed to comment if members are not selected
  carefully"; "time-consuming to establish"; and "resource hungry, particularly in overstretched organisations."
- Who sits on it (para 7.5): two kinds. "Subject matter experts on the topic being examined who are highly experienced
  and technically proficient." And "Non-subject matter expert individuals who are intelligent and good analytical
  thinkers but who do not know the topic area", who "bring fresh and novel perspectives to the analysis."
- Size (para 7.6): the "optimum number is between four and eight – six is ideal." A team with "fewer than four members
  will begin to lack diversity and creativity in thought."
- The person who asked for it (para 7.4) must "accept and act on the red team's findings" and "not just pay lip
  service to it as an academic exercise."
- No pre-mortem section, by that name. The nearest (paras 4.10-4.12, paraphrased by the reader): "Traditional what if
  analysis involves imagining that a plan has failed and working backwards."

**We take that to mean:** the handbook already settled half of Fight C. Outsiders are not one kind of person; they are
two jobs. The expert is there for what is wrong. The non-expert is there for what is invisible to everyone who built
it. And the handbook names the two failure modes Kai's dissent hunt has to avoid: the outsider brushed off as "not
expert enough" by the people who built the plan, and the outsider used as a hatchet. (Yes, "six is ideal". Kai will
enjoy that. We are six people, but we are all home team, so it doesn't count.)

## 4. What a reader who knows no finance reliably catches: can they find it and say it back?

**Package-leaflet user testing, Norwegian Medicines Agency (DMP), "Readability testing and bridging"** (source 4;
a national regulator's page on the EU rule; paraphrased from the page reader).
- The aim, quoted: "The aim of the user test is to ensure that the package leaflet is legible and easy to use."
- How: ordinary people, not doctors, are given the leaflet and asked set questions. "A total of 10 persons in the first
  round is usual", and a second round checks the first.
- The pass mark: "90% of test participants can find the information", and of those who find it, 90% must show they
  understood it; the page's example is "16 out of 20 participants". (The reader noted the page's own numbers don't
  quite agree: it also says "each and every question meets criterion of 81 % correct answers", and 16 of 20 is 80%.)

**We take that to mean:** Europe trusts non-experts to test a medicine leaflet, but only on two things: can you find
it, and can you say it back. Nobody asks the participant whether the dose is right. That is the shape of a cold read
that works: fixed questions with right answers, not "what did you think?"

## 5. What they miss: anything that needs the subject to see

**Jakob Nielsen, "How to Find Usability Problems by Heuristic Evaluation" (Nielsen Norman Group, 1 Jan 1995)**
(source 5; paraphrase and short quotes from the page reader).
- One evaluator on their own finds a given major problem "at 42 percent on the average", a minor one "only 32
  percent" (six case studies, Nielsen 1992).
- Evaluators are "especially likely to overlook usability problems if the system is highly domain-dependent", and when
  they "have little domain expertise", problems get missed; some domain problems "would have been virtually impossible
  to find without user testing."

**Nielsen's 1992 CHI paper, "Finding usability problems through heuristic evaluation"** (source 6; a course copy of
the PDF, OCR-scanned, so the table figures are as the page reader read them). Same interface, three kinds of
evaluator, average share of the 16 known problems each one found (Table 1):
- novices, who knew neither usability nor the domain: 22%
- usability specialists: 41%
- "double specialists", who knew usability and the domain: 60%, "well over half of the problems on the average"
- what the double specialists added: "especially with regard to certain usability problems that were unique to that
  kind of interface."
Novices did find problems. They found about one in five. The ones only the double specialists found were the ones
that belonged to the subject.

**Schroter and others, "What errors do peer reviewers detect, and does training improve their ability to detect
them?", Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine, 2008** (source 7). The study deliberately put major errors into
papers and counted how many the reviewers found. I could not open it. UNVERIFIED: every number, including how many
errors were planted and how many were found. What I am sure of is the title and the design. If it says what I
remember, even trained experts miss most planted errors, and that would make the point harder, not softer.

**We take that to mean:** a cold reader tells you where the story breaks, not where the sums break. "Sounds right" from
Hana about a number is worth nothing, and it's dangerous, because it feels like a check. Wrong numbers, banned
securities, a fund that isn't on WInS: those need a check built for them (Jun's go-and-see on the raw
file, my rules check). A single reader of any kind misses about half (Nielsen 1995), and a reader who knows neither
the craft nor the subject finds about a fifth (Nielsen 1992), so one cold reader is a sample of one.

## What it means for six Year 11s

**Three jobs, not one list of "outsiders".**

| Job | Who (Keel's world) | Asked to | Catches | Can't catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold reader, no finance | Two people outside the twelve, e.g. Hana (Jun's sister, 20) and Felix's mum Grace (an auditor who reads paperwork for what it leaves out, but not investments) | Read the pitch and IPS once, phone face down, then answer three fixed questions out loud: what does Laura get, when; what could go wrong; can she get money out early? Then circle every word they'd have to look up | Unexplained jargon, a buried point, a missing step, the obvious question we stopped asking | A wrong number, a rule breach, a bad bond match |
| Murder board, hostile insider | Kai as Team Spinnaker, in the Courtroom | Attack the strongest claim before a judge does | Weak reasons, the alternative we didn't beat | What we can't see because we all know the tune |
| Technical check, not an outsider | Jun (numbers, go-and-see), Felix (rules, sources) | Open the raw file and read the line out | Wrong figures, banned securities, wrong dates | Whether anyone else can follow it |

In the real repo the outsiders are simulated: the Courtroom's five mock judges (faculty, practitioner, former finalist,
outsider, finance undergraduate; `.claude/agents/mock-judge.md`) and the co-sponsor reader. They are tests, never quotes.

**Rules I'd write down.**
1. Two cold readers, not one and not six. One is a sample of one (Nielsen); six is a weekend of other people's time
   (Jun: twenty minutes of Hana's is an hour of his).
2. Fixed questions with right answers, as in the leaflet test. Never "is it good?"
3. Never ask a cold reader about a number. "Sounds right" is not a check.
4. The owner answers every outsider note in the log, one line, even if the answer is "no, because". That's the
   handbook's "accept and act", and Kai's owner rule.
5. When: the near-final for review, Sat 31 Oct, so the fixes land before Sat 7 Nov 09:00 Sydney, not after.

**My guess, not research:** the third question ("can she get money out early?") is the one a non-finance reader asks
first, and it is Tess's missing liquidity. If neither cold reader asks it, I'm wrong about outsiders, and Tess still
isn't wrong about liquidity.

**For the work.** For Caplet's IPS: a cold read is the only test of "plain English" that isn't the author marking his
