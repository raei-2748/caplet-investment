## 1. Valve: what the handbook actually says

Source: Valve's own *Handbook for New Employees* (PDF on Valve's server). I read the PDF text directly.

- **No bosses.** "we don't have any management, and nobody 'reports to' anybody else." (section "Welcome to Flatland"; caption
  nearby: "Method to working without a boss").
- **Desks with wheels.** Desks are on wheels as "a symbolic reminder that you should always be considering where you could
  move yourself to be more valuable."
- **You pick your project.** "There's no rule book for choosing a project or task at Valve." And: "Employees vote on projects
  with their feet (or desk wheels)." A project starts by "waiting for someone to decide that it's the right thing to do, and
  then letting them recruit other people" (section "What to Work On"). Also: "you were not hired to fill a specific job
  description."
- **Teams are temporary.** "Cabals are really just multidisciplinary project teams." "They exist to get a product or large
  feature shipped." The handbook adds: "There's no secret decision-making cabal."
- **Peers do the reviewing.** "Peer reviews are done in order to give each other useful feedback on how to best grow as
  individual contributors." and "Stack ranking is done primarily as a method of adjusting compensation." (paraphrase of
  structure: feedback and pay are handled as two separate jobs.)
- **Mistakes.** "Nobody has ever been fired at Valve for making a mistake." and "Even expensive mistakes, or ones which result
  in a very public failure, are genuinely looked at as opportunities to learn."
- **Why no managers.** The handbook says formal structures "inevitably begin to serve their own needs rather than those of
  Valve's customers."

Year of the handbook: the PDF text I fetched carried no date. News coverage dates its public release to 2012 (bit-tech
and TechSpot are in the search results; I did not open them). UNVERIFIED which year exactly; not needed for the argument.

## 2. Valve: the criticism (what an ex-employee said)

- **Jeri Ellsworth**, a hardware developer who was fired from Valve, told the press in July 2013 that the handbook gives
  "a very idealised view" (PC Gamer, Phil Savage, 8 Jul 2013). She called Valve "pseudo-flat" and said "there is actually a
  hidden layer of powerful management structure in the company" (same date, Game Developer headline coverage). Per PC Gamer,
  that layer "felt a lot like High School": some people with power were "the popular kids", some were troublemakers trying to
  make a difference, most were in between (those four-word fragments are PC Gamer's quotation marks; the rest is paraphrase).
- She also argued that "complete latitude" with no oversight is a problem: people tend to shrink their own workload and grow
  their control (paraphrase). And that the bonus system pushed people to highly visible projects, leaving risky work, such as
  augmented reality, without people or resources (paraphrase).
- **Gabe Newell's reply** (reported 26 Mar 2015, MCV): "Cliques or other forms of logrolling tend to fail in the face of
  properly designed markets." and he said he worries "more about making sure our internal market for people's time is working
  correctly." So Valve's answer is that the market of people's time polices cliques. Ellsworth's answer is that the market
  is a popularity contest. I could not find an independent study settling it. UNVERIFIED who is right.
- PC Gamer notes no official statement from Valve on her claims. Ellsworth's account of her own firing is one side only.

What this means for six friends: a flat group of people who like each other is exactly where a "hidden layer" grows,
because the loudest friend decides by default. In our case that friend is me.

## 3. Basecamp's Shape Up: what it actually says

Source: *Shape Up* by Ryan Singer, free online at basecamp.com/shapeup. I read chapters 3, 7 and 14 and the glossary text on
those pages.

- **Appetite.** "You can think of the appetite as a time budget for a standard team size." Glossary: "The amount of time we
  want to spend on a project, as opposed to an estimate." And the key contrast: "Estimates start with a design and end with a
  number." "Appetites start with a number and end with a design."
- **Fixed time, variable scope.** "This principle, called 'fixed time, variable scope,' is key to successfully defining and
  shipping projects." "We use the appetite as a creative constraint on the design process."
- **Cycles and cool-down.** Glossary: a cycle is "a six week period of time where teams work uninterruptedly on shaped
  projects." Cool-down is "a two-week break between cycles to do ad-hoc tasks, fix bugs, and hold a betting table."
- **Betting table.** "we hold a betting table where stakeholders decide what to do in the next cycle." Glossary: "A meeting
  during cool-down when stakeholders decide which pitches to bet on in the next cycle." (The page does not list who the
  stakeholders are.)
- **No backlogs.** "Backlogs are a big weight we don't need to carry." "Backlogs are big time wasters too." And on ideas
  that were not picked: "If we don't, we let it go." (the ideas that matter come back by themselves; paraphrase of the
  section's argument).
- **A bet** is "the decision to commit a team to a project for one cycle with no interruptions and an expectation to finish."
- **Circuit breaker.** "Cancel projects that don't ship in one cycle by default instead of extending them by default."
- **Scope hammering (ch. 14, "Decide When to Stop").** Tasks are split into must-haves and nice-to-haves: "Nice-to-haves can
  linger on a scope after it's considered done." "Those tasks are things to do if the team has extra time at the end and
  things to cut if they don't." "Usually they never get built." And: "Variable scope is not about sacrificing quality." and
  "Making choices makes the product better."

## 4. Shape Up: what went wrong when other teams tried it

- **Desmos Engineering, "One Year of Shape Up" (Cori McElwain, 7 Jan 2021, updated 29 Apr 2021).** Worked: moving design
  earlier; meeting before the betting table so people had time to think; scope hammering got "a resounding 100%
  satisfaction score" in an engineer retrospective. Did not work: rough sketches were "often not enough to truly hit the
  ground running"; the betting table was still a fight over priorities, with the team "revising the solutions while at the betting table";
  and the April 2021 update says the team was re-examining its process to make it more democratic, "given problematic power
  structures at Basecamp" (paraphrase: the update's caveat; the exact wording is not quoted here). That last point matters
  to us: the people who decide which bets get made hold the power, which is Ellsworth's complaint in another coat.
- **Trustpair (French software company), "Why the ShapeUp method just wasn't for us"** (undated in the fetch; adopted 2020).
  Everyone finished at the same time, so "Having everyone in review or QA at the same time caused a real bottleneck." Unpicked
  work got pushed into cycles "just not to lose it" and was poorly defined. Support did not fit the cool-down: "a client
  encountering a bug won't wait weeks before being fixed." They moved to Kanban. (quotes exact; summary is mine.)
- UNVERIFIED: any hard data (not anecdotes) that Shape Up makes teams ship more. I found team retrospectives, not studies.

## 5. Stealable habits, translated for six Year 11s

Constraints I have to respect: Term 4 at Fernleigh; IPS near-final for Caplet's review Sat 31 Oct, final Sat 7 Nov 09:00
Sydney; yearly exams Mon 9 to Fri 13 Nov; my bakery Sat 6 a.m.; Lina Mon/Wed/Thu afternoons; Felix band; Jun runs at 6 a.m.;
Kai phone shop; Amara tutors Tuesdays; Tess does lighting. We meet at lunch, free periods, the 389 bus, the group chat.

### Valve
1. **Desk wheels = "mine" in the group chat.** A question exists when someone types "mine: <question>" and says who they want.
   Nobody, including me, hands out jobs. If nobody says "mine" by the next lunch, it is not worth a person. Cost: nearly
   zero; the group chat already exists. The sting: I stop taking jobs home at midnight, because "mine" means somebody else's.
2. **Cabals = pairs that end.** A "mine" question gets a pair (or three) for a named number of days, then dissolves. Jun and
   Kai on a curve question can finish on the bus; Tess and Amara on a sentence can finish in a free period. Nobody sits in
   a standing sub-team, which is how I'd turn it back into a hierarchy.
3. **Feedback and pay are separate jobs, so feedback gets honest.** We have no pay. What we can copy is the separation of
   "useful feedback to grow" from "who wins the vote". A Friday 389 trip: each person gets one thing that helped and one thing
   to change from whoever sat next to them. It is not a vote. Costs five minutes of a bus ride, nothing for someone with a shift.
4. **"Nobody has ever been fired for a mistake."** We already have value 5. What it adds: say the mistake in the chat the day
   it happens (value 4), with no reply needed. I would not add anything new here.

### Shape Up
1. **Appetite.** Every open question gets a time budget before it starts: "two lunches", "one 389 ride", "Sunday call, 20
   minutes". Appetites start with a number and end with a design. For me this is the cure: I close early because "not
   yet decided" feels like failing. A budget lets me hold a question open until the budget is spent, and no longer. The name
   stays open until Monday with a budget instead of a mood.
2. **Circuit breaker.** When the budget is spent and there is no answer, the question is not extended by default. Someone must
   say "I'm betting another two lunches" out loud. It stops the three-day drift I fear, and it stops me closing it for
   everyone.
3. **Bets and no backlog.** The Sunday whiteboard photo becomes the Sunday bet: pick the two or three things that matter this
   week, drop the rest from the board. Things that matter come back. Costs fifteen minutes of a Sunday evening chat.
4. **Scope hammering for people with shifts.** Each "mine" gets a must-have and a nice-to-have. If Kai's phone shop swallows
   his Thursday, the must-have still ships and the nice-to-have goes to the folder. Time is fixed (a shift cannot move);
   scope bends.
5. **Cool-down = exam week.** The IPS goes in on Sat 7 Nov. Exams run Mon 9 to Fri 13 Nov. Treat that week as the cool-down:
   nothing assigned, no bets; the 6:58 price and the order rules are the "support" that must not stop (Trustpair's lesson,
   with Kai's button rule). Weekend 14-15 Nov is the next betting table (Final Report Sat 5 Dec).

### What I refuse, and why
- **No named person at all.** Valve's own ex-employee says flat groups grow a hidden layer. A friend group is more likely to.
  Someone must own the calendar and the submit button (Sat 7 Nov 09:00). Ownership stays; assignment goes.
- **"Complete latitude" with a deadline.** Ellsworth says people shrink their own work when nobody checks. We have 11 days to
  the IPS. "Mine" comes with a day it will be reported on, in the chat. That is Value 2, not management.
- **Six-week cycles and a betting table of "stakeholders".** We have 11 days, and there are six of us: everyone is a
  stakeholder. The Desmos note says a betting table still turns into a fight over priorities; ours is a Sunday chat.
- **Bonuses and stack ranking.** Not relevant and the ex-employee says visible projects crowd out risky ones. Keel has the
  folder of cut lines for that.

### How it helps the IPS (due Sat 7 Nov 09:00 Sydney)
Appetite plus the circuit breaker keep each hard question open exactly as long as it deserves before the near-final on Sat
31 Oct, so the rewrite stays slow without going loose. "Mine" plus short pairs also fixes Caplet's complaint: ideas bounce
between two people before they reach the group, instead of arriving as one person's one-shot.
