## 1. Girotra, Terwiesch and Ulrich (2010): the one study that measures the best idea
"Idea Generation and the Quality of the Best Idea", *Management Science* (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1100.1172; volume and pages
UNVERIFIED, I read the accepted manuscript, MS-01219-2007.R1, posted by Wharton). Terwiesch and Ulrich teach at
Wharton, which made me laugh.

**Why it is the right study.** It defines a group's effectiveness as "the quality of the best ideas identified by the
group" (abstract), not the count. That is exactly the measure Kai asked for.

**Design (manuscript pp. 13-16, paraphrase with quotes).**
- 44 upper-level product-design students at Penn, "from a wide variety of majors"; teams of four.
- Two product challenges for the student market (sporting goods; dorm/apartment products); 443 ideas in total.
- *Team process:* 30 minutes generating together, then 5 minutes to agree a ranked top 5.
- *Hybrid process:* 10 minutes **alone**, each person ranking their own ideas; then groups of four "given a further 20
  minutes" to share and develop them; then 5 minutes to rank a top 5 together. Same total time.
- Quality was rated by outsiders, not by the teams: 41 MBA students scored "business value" 1-10 ("at least 20"
  raters per idea); 88 target-market students scored purchase intent ("at least 44" raters per idea).

**Results.**
- Count: "the hybrid process generates about three times more ideas than the team process" (p. 19); Table 2, about
  28.5 vs 11.8 ideas per group.
- Average quality: slightly higher for the hybrid (about 0.27 business value, 0.35 purchase intent; Table 2).
- **The best ideas:** "the top 5 ideas from the hybrid process are of better quality than those from the team process"
  (p. 20); the gap is about 0.85 (business value) and 0.90 (purchase intent) in Table 2. This is the finding that
  answers Kai: alone-first won on the best idea, not only on the pile.
- **Picking:** both structures were poor judges of their own ideas: "the absolute value of the correlation for either
  team or hybrid is relatively low" (p. 20). The hybrid's five *selected* ideas were better on purchase intent, but on
  business value "we are not able to reject the hypothesis that both treatments result in top 5 ideas of equal quality"
  (p. 21).
- **"Build on each other" (the "yes, and" rule):** the abstract calls it "counter-productive"; in the results, teams
  built on earlier ideas more, but "we find no support for the often-cited effect of build-up on improving quality of
  ideas" (p. 22).
- Their own caution: "we have to caution the reader about generalizing our results" (p. 23); subjects had "limited
  time, resources, and prior exposure" and "were not placed in teams that had developed a great deal of collective
  experience" (p. 23).

**How solid.** One lab study, 44 students, product ideas, strangers, 35-minute sessions. Well designed (big outside
rating panels, best idea as the outcome), published in a top journal. Solid for the direction, thin for any number.

## 2. Rietzschel, Nijstad and Stroebe (2006): groups pick barely better than chance
"Productivity is not enough: A comparison of interactive and nominal groups in idea generation and selection",
*Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 42, 244-251 (citation from the University of Groningen research page).
Abstract, as returned: "Nominal groups generated more ideas than interactive groups"; "there were no differences
among conditions in quality of the selected ideas"; "idea selection was not significantly better than chance"; "high
productivity in brainstorming is not sufficient to lead to better solutions".

The fuller version (Rietzschel's 2005 Utrecht dissertation, chapter 2, read in the open PDF): groups of three; a blind
trained rater scored originality and feasibility. People working alone, pooled, produced far more high-quality ideas
(mean 13.10 vs 4.18), but did "not select better ideas"; selection was "hardly more effective than taking a random
sample from the available ideas". A follow-up where individuals were given explicit originality and feasibility
criteria did not improve selection either (paraphrase).

**What it changes.** I was right that alone-first makes more *good* ideas, and wrong that "then we pick together" is
the easy bit. Picking is the weak link, in both of the studies that measured it.

## 3. Nemeth and colleagues: debate vs "don't criticise"
"The Liberating Role of Conflict in Group Creativity: A Cross Cultural Study", Nemeth, M. Personnaz, B. Personnaz and
Goncalo; I read the UC Berkeley working-paper version on eScholarship (dated 15 Apr 2003). The journal version
(often cited as *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2004) is UNVERIFIED by me.

- Groups of five, 20 minutes, ideas for reducing traffic congestion (Bay Area in the US, Paris in France); 455 people in
  91 groups. Three instruction sets: none; classic brainstorming ("You should NOT criticize anyone else's ideas");
  debate ("you SHOULD debate and even criticize each other's ideas").
- Abstract: "in general, debate instructions were superior to traditional brainstorming instructions" and the
  findings "hold across both cultures".
- Combined means (Table 5, as returned): ideas per group 17.91 (none), 21.23 (don't criticise), 23.1 (debate).
- **The catch, and it is the same catch Kai found in my round 1:** this paper also counts ideas. It does not rate
  their quality. Several comparisons were marginal or not significant, and the US sample was all female.

**How solid.** Good evidence that criticism does not choke idea flow, which is what "defer judgment" fears. No
evidence here that debate finds the *best* idea. Kai's dissent hunt is backed for "it won't kill the ideas", not for
"it picks the winner". My round-1 line (via the Hult blog) that debate gave "far more creative solutions" overstated
what I could check.

## 4. Alone, then together, then alone (order effects)
- Baruah and Paulus (2008), "Effects of training on idea generation in groups", *Small Group Research* 39(5), 523-541
  (abstract only; full text paywalled). People who went alone first "generated a larger number of ideas than those in
  the group-to-alone sequence"; training raised both count and "average originality". Count, mostly.
- Korde (2014), UT Arlington dissertation, "Hyrbid [sic] Brainwriting: The efficacy of alternating between individual
  and group brainstorming..." (abstract only): starting with an individual phase (AGAG) "produced slightly more ideas"
  than starting with a group phase, beat the group condition, but not the alone condition. Count only. I am reading
  A and G as alone and group from context.
- A properly tested "alone, together, alone" sequence measured on the **best** idea: I did not find one. UNVERIFIED /
  not found. My third "alone" step below is my reasoning from sections 1-2, not a result.

## What it means for six Year 11s
1. **Kai and Jun were right about my sources and wrong about the conclusion.** The one study that rates the best idea
   says alone-first wins on it. But it is one study, of strangers, on product ideas. We are six friends with a
   500-word IPS. Treat it as a direction, not a law.
2. **The alone bit is short and posted at once.** The hybrid's alone phase is 10 minutes and goes straight into the
   group. That is Jun's point exactly: alone is fine, alone and unposted is the problem.
3. **"Yes, and" is not the engine.** Building on each other did not raise quality in Girotra. So the together time is
   for putting six sets of ideas side by side, arguing and merging, not for politely adding. Nora's "give the 'and'
   round the budget" needs one change: give the *together* round the budget, and let it argue (Nemeth).
4. **Picking needs its own step.** Talking it out picks barely better than chance (Rietzschel). Girotra's quality came
   from many separate raters. We are only six, so this is weaker for us (my reasoning, UNVERIFIED for groups of six),
   but scoring alone before anyone argues for a favourite is the closest thing we have.
5. **Then one attack, then one owner.** Kai's dissent hunt goes at the top-scored line only; the owner decides and
   writes the why (Kai's owner rule, Nora's log line).

**The shape I'd propose (about 40 minutes, inside Jun's 60).**
- *Night before, alone (10 min, 389 or bed):* owner posts the question or the UGLY page by 7 p.m. Each of us writes up
  to three lines in our notes app, stars our own best one, and pastes it all at 9 p.m., everyone at once, so nobody
  reads anyone else's first. Shifts and band are safe.
- *Lunch, together (20 min):* Tess's ten silent minutes reading all of it, then ten minutes of arguing, merging and
  "and what if". Criticism allowed. A number line goes to Felix and Jun, never to a vote.
- *Alone again (2 min):* everyone DMs Tess a 1-10 score for each surviving line before anyone says which they love.
- *Then:* Kai attacks the top line (5 min). The owner decides and writes one line of why in the log.

**For the work.** Before Sat 31 Oct, one IPS line (the pin-up line, or the shortfall-timing sentence) goes through this
once. If the top-scored line is not the one any single person starred, that is the compounding Ray asked for, and
it is a true story for the Final Report's Articulation section.
