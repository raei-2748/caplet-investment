## 1. Pixar's Braintrust (Ed Catmull, *Creativity, Inc.*, 2014)

**What it actually is.** A small group of Pixar's strongest storytellers (directors, writers, heads of story) who watch
a director's film while it is still rough and say what is and isn't working. Fast Company's excerpt from the book says
the only requirement for members is "a knack for storytelling" (verbatim, via fetch). Catmull frames his own job in it
as "making sure that the compact upon which the meetings are based is protected and upheld" (verbatim, Fast Company).

**The bits Kai cares about:**
- *Candour, bluntly:* "Candor could not be more crucial to our creative process." and "Because early on, all of our
  movies suck." (both verbatim, Pixar Post excerpt of the book). The goal, per Catmull, is to go "from suck to
  not-suck" (verbatim, Fast Company).
- *"Ugly babies".* Secondary sources (Mindset Online; Christian Science Monitor review) quote Catmull describing early
  work as "truly ugly: awkward and unformed, vulnerable and incomplete" and the CSM reviewer says "Ugly babies" is
  part of the process. UNVERIFIED: the exact "ugly baby" wording in the book itself; I did not get a primary page that
  carries it.
- *The group has no authority.* First Round Review (Catmull interview): the team realised "the Braintrust should have
  no authority" (verbatim), "Our group could not tell the director what to do" (verbatim), and when Catmull and
  Lasseter took over Disney Animation they told directors: "You don't have to listen to anyone's notes, not even
  ours." (verbatim). The same source says the unwritten rule was that "everyone could be extremely candid without
  anyone feeling attacked." Catmull's HBR 2008 summary line: creative authority sits "firmly in the hands of the
  project leaders" (verbatim, HBR abstract).
- *"Notes, not solutions".* UNVERIFIED as a Pixar rule. It is how the habit is often retold, but the Fast Company
  excerpt actually says the group is charged with "identifying and solving problems" (verbatim). What I can support:
  feedback should "move the project forward" and not be "a mere opinion" or personal (Digital Tonto, paraphrasing
  Pixar practice), and the director is free to ignore it. Kai's reading: point at the problem, offer a fix only if
  asked, and the owner decides.
- *Fear never goes away.* Fast Company: "The fear of saying something stupid and looking bad" is the obstacle, and
  "you can't totally eliminate the blocks to candor" (both verbatim).

**Evidence and criticism.**
- Pixar's record of films is the evidence, but it is one company telling its own story in a book by its own president.
  I found no independent study of the Braintrust. UNVERIFIED that it "causes" the hit rate.
- The CSM review (2014) says the book leaves out much of what makes the films distinctive: "not a book for film fans".
- Failure modes of any such group (Jeremy Utley, quoting Isaac Asimov on creative groups): a member with a "much
  greater reputation" or "more commanding personality" can take over and reduce others to "passive obedience"; one
  unsympathetic person can freeze a group; bigger groups mean "the tension of waiting to speak".
- Candour about a film did not stop a people problem: John Lasseter, Catmull's co-founder, left Disney/Pixar in 2018
  after allegations of unwanted hugs (he acknowledged staff felt "disrespected or uncomfortable"; the allegations were
  reported by The Hollywood Reporter and, per the article, not confirmed by Disney or Lasseter). Careful: I am not
  saying the Braintrust failed; I am saying candour about work is not candour about everything.

## 2. Netflix's culture memo (jobs.netflix.com/culture, current page)

**What it actually says** (verbatim from the page unless marked):
- Freedom and responsibility: "It takes an unusually responsible person to thrive on this level of freedom" and
  "You get better outcomes when employees have the information and freedom to make decisions for themselves."
- Context not control: "We expect managers to practice context not control — giving their teams the context and
  clarity needed to make good decisions".
- Farming for dissent: "We call this farming for dissent." and "we expect informed captains to seek out different
  opinions and listen to people at every level". Each decision has "an informed captain who's responsible for making a
  judgment call".
- Candour: "extraordinary candor — ensuring constructive feedback is part of our everyday work".
- Keeper test: "if X wanted to leave, would I fight to keep them?" and "knowing everything I know today, would I hire
  X again?". Fortune (14 Oct 2026) quotes the memo as saying that if the answer is no, "we believe it's fairer to
  everyone to part ways quickly" (quoted from Fortune; I did not see that sentence on the page itself).
- Pay: "we pay personal top of market for the role and location". (This matters: the keeper test comes with
  unusually high pay. Nothing like it exists at a school.)

**Evidence and criticism.**
- Origin: after laying off about a third of staff in the 2001 dot-com bust, Netflix says the remaining people were
  happier and did more (Hastings and Patty McCord, via Fortune). Fortune also reports McCord, who helped build the
  keeper test, was let go by Hastings in 2012.
- 2018: SlashFilm summarising a Wall Street Journal report: some interviewees saw the test as a "guise for ordinary
  workplace politics"; a former employee said "It was very much on our minds: How do I know if I'm about to be
  fired?"; a VP, Karen Barragan, is reported as saying "Good, because fear drives you." (these are second-hand: a
  blog summary of the WSJ; I did not read the WSJ. Treat as reported, not proven.)
- Quartz (Sarah Todd, 28 Sept 2020): the principle means "anyone, no matter how hard-working they are, or how good
  their track record might be, is vulnerable to getting cut" (the page had an en dash; paraphrase of the sense).
- 14 Oct 2026 (Fortune): Netflix is cutting about 1,000 jobs, about 6% of its workforce, with the stock down 22% this
  year; the co-CEOs have not called it a talent-density move. Staff reaction to the 2026 cuts: not reported there.
- Netflix itself says on its page that getting cut "is very disappointing, but there is no shame" (quoted in an AOL
  article on 2022 layoffs, quoted as Netflix's own website).
- Not found: a controlled study that shows the keeper test makes teams better. UNVERIFIED either way. The "fear
  drives you" worry is also a general finding in team research (Jun has Project Aristotle), which is Jun's to check.

## 3. Stealable habits, translated for six Year 11s (Term 4, exams Mon 9 to Fri 13 Nov, IPS due Sat 7 Nov 09:00 Sydney)

| # | Habit | From | What it looks like for us | Cost to someone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ugly-baby post** | Pixar | Whoever owns a piece of the IPS posts their worst, unpolished version in the group chat with "UGLY:" in front and no sorry. Everyone else only answers "where did you stop believing it?" Do it Sun night or on the 389. | Two minutes of pride. Tess hates it; she gets 24 hours before she has to answer. |
| 2 | **Notes about the problem, owner decides** | Pixar | Reviewer says what happened to them reading it ("I lost the plot at the gift line") not "change it to X". The owner can ignore any note and says why in one line. Nobody votes on wording. | Nora can't close it early. Kai can't win by volume. |
| 3 | **A referee who protects the room, not the answer** | Pixar (Catmull's job) | One person per huddle, rotating, whose only job is "was anyone made smaller?". Not Kai when he's hurt. 10 minutes at lunch. | The referee talks less that day. |
| 4 | **Dissent hunt, once, from outside** | Netflix | Before a big vote, each person asks one person who isn't on Keel (a Year 12, Mr Okafor, Hana) to attack the idea. Bring back the best objection, not the group's. | One chat each. Do it between shifts. Skip it for small stuff. |
| 5 | **Context, not checking** | Netflix | Nora gives the whole picture (rubric, 7 Nov, who has a shift/recital that week) once, then people pick their own hours. No "did you do it?" pings; you ask when you're stuck. | Nora has to trust midnight. Jun has to say when he's drowning. |
| 6 | **One captain per decision** | Netflix | Every IPS section has one named person who decides after hearing the dissent. Changing your mind is allowed if you say what did it. | The captain can be wrong in public. |

**Refuse, and why.**
- *The keeper test.* It is a firing rule that works because of top-of-market pay, severance and a market of other jobs.
  For six friends, it just means "who would I drop?" for kids who can't leave and can't be replaced. It would turn Keel
  into Kai's dad's "bold" week: a thrill, then a bill. Netflix itself gets reports of fear.
- *"Fear drives you".* Not even as a joke.
- *Brutal candour about people.* Candour about work, yes. Candour about a friend's worth, no.
- *Pixar's size.* A Braintrust of storytellers works with years of film in a building. We have 11 days and exams.

**What this does for the work (before Sat 7 Nov).** Habits 1 and 2 stop the IPS being written once by one person and
then approved. Habit 4 gets a co-sponsor-style reader's objection into the IPS before the judges give it. For the
Final Report: an honest, Wharton-safe change-of-mind story (a real team event, only once it truly happens).
