Fight A: does the owner decide, or do we vote? My side (round 2): the owner decides wording, strategy goes to a vote,

## 1. Amazon: sort the decision by the door, not by the topic

**2015 letter to shareowners** (filed with the SEC in April 2016; the page itself carries no date line).
- One-way doors: "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible – one-way doors" and
  "these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation." (exact)
  Bezos calls these Type 1. "If you walk through and don't like what you see on the other side" (exact, the start of
  the sentence), you can't get back to where you were (paraphrase).
- Two-way doors: most decisions are changeable and reversible (paraphrase). "If you've made a suboptimal Type 2
  decision, you don't have to live with the consequences for that long." "You can reopen the door and go back
  through." (exact)
- Who decides the reversible ones: "Type 2 decisions can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or
  small groups." (exact)
- The warning: as organisations grow they tend to use the "heavy-weight Type 1 decision-making process on most
  decisions, including many Type 2 decisions" (exact), and the result is "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion,
  failure to experiment sufficiently" (exact), and so less invention (paraphrase).

**2016 letter to shareholders** (published April 2017 per Wikipedia's citation; the page gives no day or month).
- "never use a one-size-fits-all decision-making process." (exact)
- "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had." (exact)
- "you need to be good at quickly recognizing and correcting bad decisions." (exact)
- Disagree and commit: Bezos suggests asking "Disagree and commit?" and, on an Amazon Studios show he doubted, told the
  team "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most watched thing we've ever made." (exact) Tess's round-1 note
  has the same lines; I opened the page myself, so that is go-and-see done on hers.
- "recognize true misalignment issues early and escalate them immediately." (exact)
- The phrase is older than Amazon: Wikipedia credits Andy Grove at Intel, and also Scott McNealy at Sun (paraphrase of
  the article; the attribution is hedged there).

**What it cuts.** Bezos never splits decisions into "wording" and "strategy". He splits them by whether you can walk
back through the door. That kills my round-2 line as I drew it. A wording choice in a submitted IPS can't be undone;
a strategy idea floated on a Tuesday can. And the door that matters most for us changes with the clock: on Tue 27 Oct
almost every IPS sentence is a two-way door; at 09:00 Sat 7 Nov every one of them becomes one-way.

## 2. Bain's RAPID: one person holds the D, and input is not a vote

Source: Charles Ormiston and Paul Rogers, "Manager@Work: Who has the D?", Bain & Company, 26 Feb 2007 (a case about
Wyeth). The fetch tool summarised the role definitions; the quotes below are the ones it returned as exact.
- The five roles are Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide (the article explains them through the case rather than
  as a glossary; paraphrase).
- Decide: "the single point of accountability that commits the organisation to action." (exact) "And when one person
  has the "D", bottlenecks will disappear." (exact)
- Consensus: "Consensus is a worthy goal but can be an obstacle to action or a recipe for lowest-common-denominator
  compromise." (exact)
- Agree is a veto, held by very few (legal counsel is the article's example), and it has a price: "if someone exercises
  a veto, they must offer an alternative or escalate the issue to the person with the "D"." (exact)
- Input: decisions should be "grounded in evaluating facts rather than seeking opinions" (exact); the person
  recommending has to consider input but need not follow it (paraphrase).
- Perform: "A good decision executed well often beats a brilliant one implemented poorly." (exact)
- What the article does NOT say: it does not say small decisions are excluded, and it gives no size test.

**What it cuts.** RAPID has no vote in it. Not one role is "everyone counts hands". The closest thing to our vote is
consultation (Input), and the article is rude about consensus. It also gives Felix a real job: a veto on rules that
must come with an alternative, like legal counsel.

## 3. Netflix: an informed captain for every significant decision

Source: the current Netflix culture memo, jobs.netflix.com/culture.
- "For every significant decision, we identify an informed captain" "who's responsible for making a judgment call on
  the right way ahead." (exact, two fragments of one sentence)
- "We avoid decision-making by committee, which tends to slow companies down and undermine accountability." (exact)
- Captains must farm for dissent: "we expect informed captains to seek out different opinions and listen to people at
  every level." (exact) "Of course, not all opinions are created equal" (exact): the people who know the area carry
  more weight (paraphrase).
- After a decision: everyone, "including the people who argued for a different approach", is expected to "disagree
  then commit" (exact). "Afterwards, when the impact is clear, the informed captain should reflect on their choices"
  (exact) "so everyone can learn how to do better next time." (exact)
- The memo does not talk about reversible vs irreversible decisions at all; "significant" is its only size word.

**What it cuts.** Even Netflix's "significant" decisions get one captain, not a vote. Kai's side, again. What Netflix
adds that Kai didn't: the captain has to go and get the dissent, and has to look back afterwards in public.

## 4. Pixar: the director decides, until the director is the problem

From Kai's round-1 note (his verbatim; I did not reopen these pages): First Round Review's Catmull interview says "the
Braintrust should have no authority" and "Our group could not tell the director what to do", and directors at Disney
Animation were told "You don't have to listen to anyone's notes, not even ours." Catmull's 2008 HBR abstract puts
creative authority "firmly in the hands of the project leaders".

What I added (Empire, on Pixar removing Bob Peterson as director of *The Good Dinosaur*; Animation Magazine's URL dates
Pixar's confirmation to August 2013):
- Catmull's explanation, as Empire quotes it: "Sometimes directors ... are so deeply embedded in their ideas it actually
  takes someone else to finish it up." (exact, with Empire's ellipsis)
- Empire lists earlier swaps: Brad Bird replaced Jan Pinkava on *Ratatouille*, Mark Andrews took over *Brave* from
  Brenda Chapman, and John Lasseter stepped in for Brad Lewis on *Cars 2* (paraphrase).

**What it cuts both ways.** Kai is right that notes have no authority: the Braintrust never takes the pen. But the
pen isn't the director's for ever. When a film is stuck, Pixar changes who holds it. The notes don't decide; the
people running the studio decide who decides. UNVERIFIED: the full reasons for each swap (Empire gives none for
Peterson beyond Catmull's general line).

Where I still disagree with the research, as opinion (UNVERIFIED, not from any source): every source is a company with
a boss above the decider. Amazon has Bezos; Bain's D sits in a hierarchy; Netflix's captains are hired and can be let
go; Pixar can swap a director. Keel has six equals and nobody above us. For the few one-way doors, the only
"single point of accountability" six equal friends have is the six of us. That is the one place a vote survives.

## 6. What it means for six Year 11s

1. **Sort by the door, not the topic.** The five-second test: "If this is wrong, can the owner fix it before
   Sat 7 Nov, 09:00, at the cost of one lunch?" Yes means a two-way door: the owner decides, posts the one-line why,
   done. No means a one-way door: it comes to the six.
2. **One-way doors for us are few, and we can list them now:** anything submitted; any WInS order; anything that changes what Laura is promised or what a
   co-sponsor is told; the one sentence; and, after 09:00 Sat 7 Nov, every word. Everything else is a two-way door.
3. **Even a one-way door has an owner.** RAPID's R: the owner brings the recommendation, Kai's dissent hunt is the
   Input, Felix holds the Agree on rules (a veto that must come with an alternative), and then the six vote, once.
4. **The pen can move; the decision doesn't go to a crowd.** Pixar's lesson: if an owner is stuck past their appetite,
   the fix is a new owner, not a group edit. Whoever is stuck says so with a `CORD:`; anyone can type "mine" to take
   it over.
5. **Disagree and commit, with the receipt.** Netflix and Bezos both want the loser to commit; our round-2 add (the
   dissenter's "what would change my mind" in Felix's log) is the receipt.
6. **What it costs:** the test takes five seconds; the list of one-way doors takes one lunch to agree (Fri 30 Oct, with
   Tess's silent page). Nobody with a shift loses a night to a vote on a comma.

**Tie to the work.** On Sat 31 Oct Caplet gets an IPS draft where every sentence has an owner's reason, and the few
real one-way calls (what Laura is promised, the shortfall timing) arrive as a recommendation plus the dissent, not as
six people's half-edits. For the Final Report's Articulation section: a team that can say which calls were reversible,
and treated them differently, has a true story about how it decided.
