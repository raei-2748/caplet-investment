## 1. Amazon: what it actually does

**The ban (2004).** In June 2004 Bezos emailed his senior team about PowerPoint. I could not find the email itself.
Management Today says it is preserved in Ram Charan and Julia Yang's book *The Amazon Management System* and quotes
two lines: "Well-structured, narrative text is what we're after, rather than just text." and "PowerPoint-style
presentations somehow give permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance,". CNBC
(summarising the same email, paraphrase) says he argued a good four-page memo is harder to write than a 20-page
PowerPoint because the narrative form "forces better thought". The email itself: UNVERIFIED (second-hand only).

**The memo and the silent start (2017 letter, published April 2018).** Bezos's own words, on aboutamazon.com:
"We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon." and "Instead, we write narratively
structured six-page memos." and "We silently read one at the beginning of each meeting in a kind of 'study hall.'"
Paraphrase of the rest: quality varies a lot; great memos are hard to define but readers "know it when they see it";
people think a six-page memo takes a day or two, but a good one takes a week or more, drafted, rewritten, reviewed
by colleagues, set aside, then edited again. Management Today adds (paraphrase) that the silent reading can run up to
half an hour before anyone speaks.

**Disagree and commit (2016 letter, published April 2017, a different letter from the memo one).** Careful: the
brief says "2017 letter" for both; they are two letters. In the 2016 letter, Bezos: "Look, I know we disagree on this
but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?" and "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most
watched thing we've ever made." He also says most decisions should be made with "somewhere around 70% of the
information you wish you had" and "If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow." The phrase is
older than Amazon: Wikipedia traces it to Scott McNealy at Sun and to Andy Grove at Intel (attribution hedged in the
article). Amazon's leadership principle "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" says leaders must "respectfully
challenge decisions when they disagree", "do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion" and "Once a decision is
determined, they commit wholly."

## 2. Stripe: what it actually does

Evidence is thinner and mostly second-hand (a Slab profile, the Pragmatic Engineer, a First Round podcast with a
former Stripe staffer). I found no Stripe primary page on its internal writing. All three are UNVERIFIED against Stripe
itself.
- **Default to writing.** Slab quotes a Stripe documentation lead, Nunez: "From leadership on down, we default to
  writing." and "We don't really have slide decks." Project kickoffs favour a narrative memo over slides (Slab's
  wording, paraphrase).
- **Leaders write in public.** The Pragmatic Engineer quotes CTO David Singleton: "The best way to encourage a
  writing culture is to contribute directly to it." and "I personally try to publish more than one internal blog
  piece per month." CEO Patrick Collison is described as one of the most prolific publishers on the internal blog.
  Nunez on Collison: "The first emails I saw from our CEO [Patrick Collison] literally had footnotes."
- **Anyone can post.** Brie Wolfson (ex-Stripe, First Round podcast): "Anyone can publish to any of these listservs."
  Retrospectives were written by the person who did the work: "the practitioner set the record for the company." Writing
  earned status: "you kind of got org points for doing this stuff."
- **Writing is thinking.** Wolfson: "I cannot think about anything clearly until it's written." (She is describing
  herself, not a Stripe rule.)
- **Templates only where it counts.** Slab (paraphrase): Stripe standardises only "high-leverage" documents (wide
  audience or big business impact) and leaves the rest to teams; it publishes sample docs so "engineers aren't forced
  to stare at a blank page" (Nunez, quote).
- **Kickoff doc.** Wolfson describes a kickoff template (hypothesis, non-goals, prior art, metrics, a named owner).
  She says it is the one she gives clients, not a Stripe artifact. Do not call it Stripe's.

## 3. Evidence and criticism

- **Time cost is real.** Bezos himself says a good memo takes a week or more. Anyone copying the format without
  the writing time gets a rushed memo (Argumentree, paraphrase; note Argumentree sells an argument-mapping tool, so
  its critique serves its pitch).
- **Who it leaves out.** Argumentree, summarising accessibility architect Sheri Byrne-Haber (I did not read her
  original post): silent reading disadvantages slower readers, people with ADHD, dyslexia or autism, and English
  learners; anyone still reading when talk starts falls behind. It also quotes Jesse Freeman, "your ability to write
  a 6-pager directly impacts your status at the table" (via Argumentree, not checked). Plain version: a memo
  culture rewards the people who already write well.
- **Memo theatre.** I found no source that uses the phrase or measures it. The closest is Argumentree's "document-shaped
  alibi" (paraphrase: a document can exist to look rigorous rather than be rigorous). The warning is my inference,
  not a finding.
- **Disagree and commit has a sharp edge.** The principle says do "not compromise for the sake of social cohesion".
  That fits a company of adults paid to fight. UNVERIFIED whether it works for six friends; nobody I read tested it
  on teenagers.
- **Stripe's own caveats (Wolfson):** "it's so uncomfortable to open up a document"; information overload is real;
  channels need "a ton of work and gardening"; practices can outlive their use ("you gotta kill your darlings"); and
  Stripe "is probably very different today" than when she left. The Pragmatic Engineer's writing section lists no downsides.
- **No evidence either company's writing is why it succeeded.** Both are survivor stories told by admirers. UNVERIFIED
  causal claim either way.

## 4. Stealable habits, translated for six Year 11s

All times Sydney. Assumption (mine, not from a source): lunch is short, so anything over ten minutes eats it.

**Amazon**
1. **The ten-minute silent start.** Before any talk about a hard question, one page goes in the Long Room doc and
   everyone reads it in silence, then writes one margin comment each. Then the quietest person on the topic speaks
   first. When: Fri 30 Oct lunch (one open IPS question), then Thu 5 Nov. Who: the page is written by the person who
   owns the question. Cost: ten minutes of lunch, plus whoever wrote it loses an evening. For Nora's shift on the
   bakery Saturday or Kai's phone-shop night, the page goes up the night before and is read on the 389 bus instead.
2. **One page, not six.** Cap it at one page of sentences, no bullets, "what we think, why, what would change our
   mind". Six pages would cost a week we do not have before exams on Mon 9 Nov.
3. **Draft, sleep, edit.** Write it, set it aside overnight, edit it next morning (Bezos's "set aside, then edited
   again"). Thursday night draft, Friday 389 edit, Friday lunch read. Cost: it forces us to start early, which
   cuts against Tess's habit of polishing at midnight.
4. **Disagree and commit, with two changes.** Steal the sentence "I know we disagree, will you gamble with me?" for
   votes that are close and cannot be settled by a fact. Change one: the dissent goes in the journal with the
   dissenter's name and what would change their mind (that is the "disagree" half, and it is the Final Report's
   honest story). Change two: it does not cover a mistake of fact. If someone sees a wrong number or wrong rule
   they say it that day (value 4), whatever was voted.
5. **The 70% rule for small decisions.** Wording, order of sections, which example: decide at 70% of the information.
   Not for numbers, rules or anything Laura's payments depend on.

**Stripe**
1. **Whoever did the work writes the retro.** Kai writes the trade retro, Jun writes the number retro, Amara
   writes the Laura retro. Tess edits and files, but does not write them all. This answers my own fear: a recorder
   writes everyone's minutes; a maker writes her own page. When: within a day of a decision, 10 lines, in the group
   chat doc, not a call.
2. **Leaders write first.** Nora posts the first page, on a question she is tempted to close early, to show nobody
   is exempt. (Singleton: contribute directly to it.)
3. **Non-goals line.** Every task gets one sentence: "what we are not doing". This week it protects the exam
   window: no new research after Sat 7 Nov except what the Final Report needs. Keep it to a line; Wolfson's full
   kickoff template is for companies.
4. **Open posting, with credit.** Anyone can drop a note in the shared doc at any hour; Tess logs who found what
   (the "org points" idea, honest version: names in the journal).
5. **A standard form for the important pages only.** Only decision pages (the sentence, the options, who argued
   what, what we would do differently) get a template; everything else stays free. Tess already keeps this.

**What to refuse**
- Six pages and a half-hour of silence: no time, and it favours fast readers.
- Silent reading as the only way in. Offer read-aloud or the page the night before for anyone who reads slowly or
  is tired after a shift (Tess reads aloud under pressure anyway).
- "Do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion" as a rule. Keel's kindness rule (criticism in private, credit
  in public) stays. Disagreement is a duty, but no one gets crushed to prove it.
- Memo theatre: a page nobody reads or answers. If the page does not change a decision or a margin comment, cut it.

**Tie to the work.** By Sat 7 Nov: one-page memos on the single open IPS question each (for instance the shortfall
line's timing), read in silence on Fri 30 Oct, so the quietest voice is heard before the near-final goes to Caplet on
Sat 31 Oct. For the Final Report: dated retros and written dissents are the honest evidence for Articulation, as
Keel's own practice, never Caplet's experience.
