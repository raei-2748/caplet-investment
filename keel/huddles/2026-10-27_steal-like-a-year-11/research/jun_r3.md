## 1. What Diehl and Stroebe (1987) actually measured
Same PDF Amara read, re-opened for the quality question.
- **Groups and task.** Four-person groups (Experiments 1, 3, 4), 15-minute sessions, problems like improving relations
  between Germans and guest workers, or reducing unemployment in Germany.
- **The main measure** was the "number of nonredundant ideas".
- **Quality was measured once, in Experiment 1 only.** "Ideas were rated for originality and feasibility on two
  5-point scales." A "good idea" was one rated top on one scale and no worse than 2 on the other.
- **Result on quality.** Average originality and feasibility: no significant effects. Number of good ideas: real
  groups made fewer, 3.00 vs 13.33 (personal assessment) and 2.00 vs 5.66 (collective assessment), real vs nominal.
  Their words: "the increase in quantity was associated with an increase in poor as well as in good ideas."
- **Why they stopped measuring quality.** Good ideas correlated r = .82 with total ideas, so "we decided not to conduct
  quality ratings in further studies."

**So I was half wrong.** They did count good ideas, once, and alone-then-pooled won on that too. What they never
measured is the quality of the **best** idea, or whether anyone could pick it out. A count of good ideas is still a count.

## 2. Studies that do measure the best idea, and the picking
- **Girotra, Terwiesch and Ulrich (Wharton), "Idea Generation and the Quality of the Best Idea"** (Management Science;
  the INFORMS DOI is 10.1287/mnsc.1100.1172; year 2010 per the Mack Institute URL; volume and pages UNVERIFIED).
  44 Penn product-design students, teams of four, two product challenges, 443 ideas in all. Two set-ups:
  - *Team*: 30 minutes together, then 5 minutes to agree a top 5.
  - *Hybrid*: 10 minutes alone, then 20 minutes together, then 5 minutes to agree a top 5 (individual ideas included).
  - Quality judged by outsiders: 41 MBA students on business value (1-10, at least 20 ratings an idea) and 88 students on
    purchase intent (1-10, at least 44 ratings an idea).
  - Numbers (as the fetch tool read the table): ideas per group 11.82 team vs 28.45 hybrid. Mean quality 4.52 vs 4.79
    (business value), 4.58 vs 4.93 (purchase intent). **Top 5 ideas generated: 5.18 vs 6.03 and 5.30 vs 6.20.** The
    paper's text says hybrid gives "about three times as many ideas"; the table read gives about 2.4 times. I can't
    settle which basis from the summary. UNVERIFIED which one is right; the direction isn't in doubt.
  - **Picking is the weak step.** "the ability of idea generators to evaluate their own ideas is extremely limited."
    Rank correlation with the outside judges: about 0.16-0.18 hybrid, 0.08-0.10 team (team not significant). On the
    top 5 *selected*, hybrid won on purchase intent (4.95 vs 4.63) but not on business value (4.63 vs 4.77, not
    significant). Paraphrase: the hybrid groups had better ideas in the pile and were only slightly better at finding them.
  - **Building on each other didn't help.** "ideas that build on a previous idea are worse not better, on average."
    Teams built more (build-up score 2.41 vs 2.20) and got nothing for it.
  - Penn repository page (paraphrase): hybrid groups made more ideas, better ideas, and were better at judging them.
- **Rietzschel, Nijstad and Stroebe (2006), "Productivity is not enough"**, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 42,
  244-251. Nominal groups made more ideas, more original and less feasible ones. But there were "no differences among
  conditions in quality of the selected ideas," and selection was "not significantly better than chance."
  (From the Groningen abstract page, read through the fetch tool.)

**What it adds up to:** alone first, then together, beats together from the start on the best ideas, not only on the
count. Amara's side wins that. But nobody, alone or together, is good at choosing the winner. And the "and" round, the
bit we called the only part that compounds, is the part with no evidence for it.

## 3. Numbers: does talking improve an estimate, or wreck it?
- **Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer and Helbing (2011), PNAS, "How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd
  effect."** 144 ETH Zurich students, 12 sessions of 12, separate cubicles, six factual questions (Swiss geography and
  crime), five rounds of estimates, paid for accuracy. Conditions: see nothing; see the group's average; see everyone's.
  - "even mild social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect"
  - Three effects (exact): "The "social influence effect" diminishes the diversity of the crowd without improvements of
    its collective error." "The "range reduction effect" moves the position of the truth to peripheral regions of the
    range of estimates." "The "confidence effect" boosts individuals' confidence after convergence of their estimates".
  - Paraphrase: collective error stayed about the same or fell slightly; diversity fell; confidence rose. Seeing only
    numbers, no reasons, makes people agree and feel sure, not get closer.
  - Side note for me: the plain average was closer than individuals' first guesses in only 21.3% of cases, 77.1% on a
    log scale. The geometric mean beat the arithmetic mean on all six questions. For skewed numbers, average the logs.
- **Navajas, Niella, Garbulsky, Bahrami and Sigman (2018), "Aggregated knowledge from a small number of debates
  outperforms the wisdom of large crowds"** (Nature Human Behaviour per the RePEc listing; arXiv 1703.00045).
  - Abstract (exact): "Participants first answered individually, then deliberated and made consensus decisions in groups
    of five." They "finally provided revised individual estimates." "Remarkably, combining as few as four consensus
    choices outperformed the wisdom of thousands of individuals." Live crowd N = 5,180 at a TEDx event in Buenos Aires.
  - Tom Stafford's write-up (paraphrase): one minute of talk per question; the analysis used 1,400 people in 280 groups
    because of missing data; revised individual answers averaged even better than the group answers; questions that were
    discussed improved more than ones that weren't; a lab follow-up's most-endorsed description was "We shared arguments
    and reasoned together." Caveats he gives: messy live setting, "1 minute isn't long for a group of five to discuss
    anything," and he wants it replicated.
- **Becker, Brackbill and Centola (2017), PNAS, "Network dynamics of social influence in the wisdom of crowds."**
  Couldn't open it (PMC sent a browser check; then the fetch budget ran out). A Penn Engineering story is titled
  "wisdom of crowds can prevail". What it found about which networks help or hurt: UNVERIFIED.
- **Graefe and Armstrong, "Comparing face-to-face meetings, nominal groups, Delphi and prediction markets on an
  estimation task"** (preprint dated 1 Feb 2010, accepted by the International Journal of Forecasting; the RePEc URL
  gives 27(1), 183-195). The closest thing to us: 227 Penn students, 44 groups of mostly 4 to 6, ten almanac
  questions with percentage answers, one hour. Four methods, 11 groups each: face-to-face meeting (FTF); NGT, the
  nominal group technique, which is literally estimate, talk, estimate; Delphi (anonymous rounds with feedback, no
  talk); prediction markets.
  - Mean absolute error, lower is better: Delphi 5.62, NGT 5.92, prediction markets 7.07, FTF 7.21. Exact: "Over all
    ten questions, we did not find statistically significant differences in accuracy between the four methods."
  - Against their own first guesses: "The three structured approaches were more accurate than participants' prior
    individual estimates." All three gains significant.
  - Against just averaging the first guesses (the "staticized group"): error reduction Delphi 0.54 (significant),
    NGT 0.41, prediction markets 0.17 (not significant), as the fetch tool read Table 2. Units of the 0.54: UNVERIFIED
    from the summary. The authors (as returned, cut off by the tool): "The results suggest that NGT and prediction
    markets provide little additional value compared to simple averages of forecasts".
  - FTF vs Delphi, question by question: Delphi significantly better on two questions, never significantly worse.
  - The paper cites Rowe and Wright (1999): Delphi beat unstructured interaction in five studies to one (three to one,
    with two ties, after dropping two papers that didn't test accuracy on number tasks). That's Delphi vs open talk,
    not vs a plain average. Rowe and Wright themselves: not read, UNVERIFIED beyond this citation.
  - Paraphrase of what that means: an open meeting was the worst on the board, but nothing was a big winner. The plain
    average of six sealed guesses is most of the gain, and it costs nothing. Structured rounds add a little; talk without
    structure adds least.

**What it adds up to (my reading of three studies, not a law):** the difference between Lorenz and Navajas is what
gets passed around. Numbers alone: people herd, the spread shrinks, confidence jumps, accuracy doesn't. Reasons in a
small group after everyone has committed alone: the numbers get better, and the second private guess beats the
group's agreed one. Graefe and Armstrong put a size on it for groups our size: a plain average of the sealed first
guesses gets most of the way, structure adds a bit, and an open meeting from scratch is last.

## 4. What it means for six Year 11s
1. **Alone, posted, then together. Amara's order, my condition.** Girotra's split was 10 minutes alone, 20 together. That's
   one 389 ride and one lunch. "Posted" matters because the hybrid's ideas count only if they reach the pile, and
   Navajas only works if the first guess is written down before you hear anyone.
2. **Don't trust the room to pick.** Both Girotra and Rietzschel say the people who made the ideas are bad judges of
   them. So the pick gets an outside check: an outsider reads the top three cold (Kai's dissent hunt), or go-and-see on
   the facts. Not a vote on vibes.
3. **"And" isn't the magic.** Building on ideas made worse ideas in Girotra. Together time goes on comparing, arguing
   and cutting, not stacking.
4. **Numbers split two ways.**
   - *Computed numbers* (the ladder's price on a dated curve, headroom in bp): nobody guesses, nobody votes. Re-run it.
   - *Judgement numbers* (how likely yields fall 2 points and stay down; how big a buffer; what confidence to say on the
     2031 range): sealed guess first, each with one line of reasoning, posted before reading anyone's. Then talk:
     reasons, not numbers. Then each of us guesses again alone. Keep both rounds. Use the median of the second round
     (log-average if it's a skewed number). No time to talk in exam week? The median of the sealed guesses alone is
     most of the gain (Graefe and Armstrong). What we never do is open a call with "so what do we reckon?"
   - *Herding check:* if round two bunched round the first poster's number and nobody gave a new reason, it's
     Lorenz, not Navajas. Confidence up, accuracy unknown. Say so on the sheet.
5. **Cost.** Sealed guess: two minutes, phone, any time before the call. Talk: ten minutes of the Sunday call. Second
   guess: one minute. Fits exam week too.

**For Caplet before Sat 7 Nov:** the IPS's few judgement numbers (the shortfall risk wording, the range's confidence)
come out of a process that can be shown, and the Final Report's Articulation section gets a true account of how a
team estimated a number without herding on it. Caplet would need its own real version of this logged; ours is fiction.
