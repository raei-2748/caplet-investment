## 1. GitLab's handbook culture: what they actually do

**Handbook-first, single source of truth.** GitLab publishes its company handbook on the open web and runs on it.
- "GitLab is intentional about documenting in a manner that creates a single source of truth."
- "It operates handbook-first, and in valuing transparency, makes its handbook publicly accessible to all."
- "Document the solution first, then announce via Slack or email."
- "discussions begin in GitLab Issues and/or Merge Requests – not in Slack." (the page uses a dash; I have kept the sense)
- "any answer or solution that isn't yet documented, should be documented immediately."
(all: handbook-first page, source 1)

**Changes are proposals, and someone else approves.**
- "anyone in the company (and even outside of the company) is empowered to put forth proposals for improvement."
- "GitLab merge requests split the role of submitter and approver."
- "code owners and DRIs (directly responsible individual) are ultimately responsible for reviewing each proposal" (source 1)

**Async by default.** The communication page: "we use asynchronous communication as a starting point and stay as open
and transparent as we can"; "Discussion in issues or Merge Requests is preferred over everything else."; "ensuring that
conclusions of offline conversations are written down"; "At GitLab, we are public by default, but some information is
classified as internal or limited access." (source 2). I could not open the dedicated async page (it redirected to a
GitLab sign-in), so I use nothing from it. UNVERIFIED: anything GitLab says on that page.

**Low-context communication.** "At GitLab, we communicate assuming others have low context." "The goal of low context is to
be considerate of the people or audience you're speaking to." "In colocated environments, high context communication is
the default." Also: "State your intent clearly and early in a conversation - leave nothing up to interpretation" and "By
being specific, you're forcing yourself to think through what you're saying." (source 3). Alongside it: "Lead
conversations with empathy and assume positive intent" and "It costs nothing to be kind, even if you do not believe
someone deserves it." (source 3)

### What the evidence and criticism say about GitLab
- The handbook itself admits the hard part: "There's a very real fear that instilling a culture of 'handbook first' is too
  tall a task to actually accomplish." (source 1). It also says a small team can get by on talk: "With only a few team
  members, it's feasible to keep everyone informed via meetings, Slack, or email threads." (source 1)
- GitLab's Head of Remote, Darren Murph, in a RedMonk write-up (undated in the reader's output, around Aug 2020 going by
  the URL): documentation-first "is not the default" and "it's going to feel awkward until it doesn't". The write-up's
  own point is that synchronous time for family, community and culture is something "even great documentation can't
  replace" (source 4). This is the closest thing to criticism I found; it is a limit, not an attack.
- I found no measured study that handbook-first improves results. The McKinsey piece and an academic PDF showed up in
  search but I did not open them: UNVERIFIED what they claim.
- Felix's own read: the handbook is also very large (the communication page alone is about 140,000 characters in the
  reader). A source of truth nobody can find the one line in is a pile.

## 2. Bridgewater's radical transparency: what they actually do

**In Dalio's words** (TED talk transcript, source 5, a third-party transcript site):
- "we literally tape almost all conversations and let everybody see everything"
- "I wanted to make an idea meritocracy."
- He defines the aim as "people needed to say what they really believed and to see everything."
- "Knowing what people are like also allows us to decide what responsibilities to give them and to weigh our decisions
  based on people's merits. We call it their believability."
- On the dot collector: "The 'Dot Collector' collects these views." Attendees rate colleagues on attributes, "simply note
  the attribute and provide a rating from one to 10."

**Dot collector and baseball cards (second-hand summary, Shortform, 22 Sep 2020, a summary of Dalio's book, not the book):**
a "tool used during meetings where people can award each other dots (positive or negative) on a few dozen attributes";
baseball cards are "summaries of people's strengths and weaknesses and the underlying evidence behind them". Marks from
people judged more credible count for more (paraphrase) (source 6).

**Believability-weighted decisions and recorded meetings (Ron McFarland, Opensource.com, 1 Mar 2018, a favourable review
of the book, relying on Dalio's own account):** "Bridgewater records virtually all meetings." Recordings become case
studies that teams review and role-play again. Review questions include "Did everyone have a chance to speak?" and
"Were the right questions asked?" (source 7). Note: this article gives no outcome data, and does not explain how
believability is measured.

### What the criticism says about Bridgewater
- **Fear and surveillance.** A 2016 New York Times piece syndicated on CNBC (the reader showed no byline or date; the
  URL says 27 Jul 2016): "As is the case with every meeting at Bridgewater, the meeting was recorded." One former
  employee called the firm a "cauldron of fear and intimidation." A former adviser alleged in a complaint (reported as
  unverified allegations) that the culture left him "no one I could trust to keep my experience confidential." The NLRB
  filed a complaint that mandatory confidentiality agreements interfere with employees' rights. Bridgewater said it was
  "confident our handling of this claim is consistent with our stated principles and the law." (source 8)
- **Tough love.** CNBC, 5 Sep 2017 (reader showed no author): Dalio's principles say "tough love is both the hardest and
  the most important type of love to give." Critics say the principles "offer permission to be verbally barbaric." Dalio's
  1993 memo said he "sometimes says or does things to employees which makes them feel incompetent, unnecessary,
  humiliated." The article reports "horror stories of employees who have left in tears" and that "Turnover among new
  employees is high." The same piece questions whether success comes from transparency or from the firm's financial strength. (source 9)
- **An opinion piece with the sharpest claims.** Nathan J. Robinson, Current Affairs, 10 Jun 2018: calls out the dots,
  the baseball cards, taping and public criticism; claims nearly a third of staff leave within two years and that Dalio
  gives little evidence the culture produces returns. Those numbers and the Matt Levine point are SECOND-HAND (Robinson
  citing others): UNVERIFIED by me. It is an opinion piece, not a study. (source 10)
- **Does it travel?** Angelo Calvello, Institutional Investor, 10 May 2018 (op-ed): he searched for a firm using the
  principles the way Bridgewater does and found none: "failed to produce a single organization that adopted the
  principles in a Bridgewater-like manner"; "there can be no idea meritocracy of one." (source 11)
- **Bridgewater's reply** is the Dalio view above: the harshness is self-selection and a way to stress-test ideas. His
  defence in the talk is that thoughtful disagreement is wanted, not hostility: he wants "the smartest people who would
  disagree with me" (source 5, paraphrase of the transcript's sense).
- Honest summary: there is no clean evidence either way. The sympathetic sources rely on Dalio; the critical ones rely on
  anonymous ex-staff and a few lawsuits. What survives both: recording and rating people is the part that is hard to
  defend; asking "did everyone speak, were the right questions asked" is the part that is hard to attack.

## 3. Stealable habits, translated to six Year 11s

Reality check first: it is Term 4, yearly exams are Mon 9 to Fri 13 Nov, the IPS is due Sat 7 Nov 09:00 Sydney, and
nobody has a free week. Nora has the bakery at 6 on Saturdays, I have band with Mr Okafor, Kai has the phone shop, Tess
has lighting. Anything that costs more than a few minutes a day does not survive.

### From GitLab
1. **File first, chat second.** (source: "Document the solution first, then announce via Slack or email.") What it looks
   like: when a decision is made in the Long Room or on a late call, whoever holds the pen writes it in the log in the same
   minute (two minutes; Nora already reads decisions out), and the group chat message is only a link plus one line. Where:
   the decision log. Who: the owner of that decision. Cost: nothing extra; it replaces a chat message. Stops: the 389 bus
   trying to remember what was agreed.
2. **Write for someone who missed the day (low context).** What it looks like: every post is readable cold by someone
   who was at a shift or a recital. Test: Tess, backstage, reads it on her phone and can act on it without asking. First
   line says what the message is for; "State your intent clearly and early". Cost: 30 seconds more writing, minutes saved
   for six readers. When: every post, starting tonight.
3. **Suggest, never overwrite; the owner approves.** What it looks like: when I think Amara's line is wrong, I put my
   change as a suggestion with the source, she accepts or rejects. We already do this in the Google Doc (suggestion
   mode only); extend it to our own files. Cost: nothing. Bonus: it makes me say what I think, in a place where it is
   safe.
4. **Document the answer the second time anyone asks.** (source: "any answer or solution that isn't yet documented,
   should be documented immediately.") What it looks like: the "Where does it say that?" table in my profile already
   does this for rules. Extend it: if a question is asked twice in the chat, it gets a row and the chat gets a link.
   When: ten minutes on Sunday evening, after Nora's whiteboard photo lands.
5. **Say what you actually asked for.** "State your intent" also means my messages start with "I need X from you by Y".
   Cost: none.

**Refuse from GitLab:** *public by default.* Our repo is private on purpose, and there is personal data of teenagers in
the world around it. And the size: GitLab itself says handbook-first can be "too tall a task". We do not build a
handbook. We keep one page per decision and one line per rule.

### From Bridgewater
1. **Two review questions after every call, out loud.** "Did everyone have a chance to speak?" and "Were the right
   questions asked?" (source 7). What it looks like: Nora asks them in the last thirty seconds, after "Decision, owner,
   date". No recording. When: every call; late calls included.
2. **Ask the sharpest disagreer first.** (Dalio, source 5: wants "the smartest people who would disagree with me".) What
   it looks like: before a decision on the IPS near-final for Sat 31 Oct, one named person's job is to argue the opposite
   as hard as they can for five minutes on the bus or at lunch. Kai is already good at this; make it a role that rotates.
   The loser still carries the decision (value 3).
3. **Say what you believe, plainly.** Dalio: "people needed to say what they really believed". This one is aimed at me. I
   quote instead of saying what I think. New habit: after any quote I add one sentence starting "I think". That is the
   "this one matters" practice in words.
4. **Believability, but only as a question, never a score.** What it looks like: when two of us disagree about a claim,
   the first question is "who has actually done this check, and when?" not "who is more senior". We already keep VERIFIED
   and UNVERIFIED and Jun's dated MODEL sheet. Cost: one sentence. No numbers on people.

**Refuse from Bridgewater:**
- **Dot collector and baseball cards.** Rating each other on a few dozen traits, on file. Our repo rule is no rankings of
  teammates. Also I am the one who would be marked "slow".
- **Taping everyone.** "we literally tape almost all conversations": six sixteen-year-olds at home, with parents and Por
  Por in the next room. And the surveillance is the thing the critics point at ("cauldron of fear and intimidation").
- **Public shredding and "tough love".** "permission to be verbally barbaric". Our kindness rule (criticism in private,
  credit in public) stays; when you kill an idea you hand something back.

## 4. For Felix himself

I liked the GitLab handbook far too much. I noticed it on the bus: I was nodding at "documented immediately" the way
Por Por nods at a correct score. If we copy it all we will have a rule for everything, every check will be equal, and the
one check that matters will sit behind thirty-nine that do not. That is already my weakness. Bridgewater is the same
instinct without a brake: rules, records and ratings until nobody can breathe. So the one thing I want in every note we
file: a first line, **"This one matters:"**, naming the single check or decision that cannot be skipped. That is me
learning to rank, with a format that makes me do it.

## 5. How it helps the work before Sat 7 Nov
Decisions logged the minute they are made, with the sharpest disagreement heard first and a "this one matters" line on
top, mean Caplet's near-final review on Sat 31 Oct reads reasons, not chat archaeology. For the Final Report
(Articulation of the competition experience), it leaves a real record of what was argued and why it changed. This is
Keel's own fiction; Caplet's real events must be logged by Caplet itself.
