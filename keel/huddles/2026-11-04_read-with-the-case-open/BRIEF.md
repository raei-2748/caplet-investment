  dates, each maturing before a January payment. A Treasury bond held to maturity pays exactly what it promised,
  whatever markets do. So, to us, high certainty means no payment depends on shares."
- P4 "Shares are called safe over ten years, but time makes a loss rarer, not smaller, and a fixed cheque can't wait

  downturns that hit share prices, so her portfolio shouldn't bet on shares twice."
- P5 "The 2028 deposit first completes the ladder, if needed. Most of the rest buys a Treasury fund maturing in late
  2032: the floor of her gift. What remains, roughly the interest that floor will earn, goes into one world index fund
  holding thousands of companies. Banks sell this as a capital-protected note, but they spend the interest on an option
  that can expire worthless. We would rather she owned the shares. Nor do we copy portfolio insurance, which sells
  shares as they fall and helped deepen the 1987 crash. Our plan changes only on fixed dates, never on a market move."
- P6 "In 2031, Laura gives co-sponsors a range. The bottom is the floor, rounded down: money she already owns. The top
  adds half her shares, which move into Treasuries before she names it, because a number said aloud can't quietly be
  halved after a bad year. The other half stays hers and liquid, as a cushion if the building costs more in Taiwan
  dollars."
- P7 "The ladder's main weakness is that it counts on reinvesting its bonds' interest; if rates fall and stay low, it
  can fall short. Before Laura names her range, we price that shortfall as if every dollar waiting for a cheque earned
  nothing, and move that much from her kept shares into Treasuries. Her spare money shrinks first, and if it can't
  cover the gap, the whole range comes down."
- P8 "From 2033, the ladder and the money set aside become her operating reserve, paying each January's cheque until
  2042. Like the tablet it is named after, the plan works as long as nobody crushes it."
